# Measures dossier: 250 Years of America in the World

Compiled November 22, 2026. Every value below was read from the source named in its row, and every URL in the source list was fetched on that date. Source ids (S1, S2, …) point to the numbered list at the end.

## How to use this

Use these numbers and these sources for these measures and years, so that all ten lessons agree. If a cell is empty, no verified value was found; do not fill it from memory or from another source without adding that source here first.

Status labels: **established** means an official statistic (a census count, an official budget or national-accounts series). **Estimate** means a scholarly reconstruction (Maddison, Bairoch, MeasuringWorth, Fouquin and Hugot). **Computed** means this dossier divided one sourced number by another sourced number; both inputs and their sources are shown, and the result is an estimate. **Contested** flags a value where credible sources disagree or the method is open to dispute.

Rough fit to lessons: 1775–1800 for lesson 1; 1820 and 1860 for lesson 2; 1860–1870 for lesson 3; 1870–1913 for lesson 4; 1913–1940 for lesson 5; 1945–1960 for lesson 6; 1960s–1973 for lessons 7 and 8; 1990–2008 for lesson 9; 2008 and the latest year for lesson 10.

Cautions that apply across sections:

- Share of world output depends on the yardstick. Maddison (S1) and the IMF's purchasing-power series (S12) put the US near 15 percent of world GDP today; at market exchange rates (S12, S13) it is about 26 percent. Say which one you are using, and never put the two in the same comparison.
- Income per person relative to Britain also depends on the yardstick. Maddison's 2011-dollar series gives the US 1.52 times the UK in 2022; the IMF's current purchasing-power series gives 1.30 for the same year (S1, S12). Use Maddison for long-run comparisons and do not splice the IMF number onto it.
- The often-repeated claim that the US produced "half the world's output" in 1945 is not supported by anything in this dossier. Maddison's regional data skip 1945; the nearest years give 20.8 percent (1940) and 27.4 percent (1950). Do not use the "half" figure unless a writer adds a verified source for it here.
- Pre-1930 federal spending, pre-1940 defense spending and pre-1929 trade ratios are computed: official numerators from the Census Bureau's Historical Statistics (S16, S17) divided by MeasuringWorth's nominal GDP (S18), which is itself a scholarly estimate. Government figures before 1940 are mostly fiscal years ending June 30; GDP is calendar years. Treat them as approximate, and round them in prose ("about 2 percent").
- Maddison's citation policy (S1, Notes sheet) requires citing the original country sources, not just the database, when data are shown in any graphical form or when fewer than a dozen countries are used. For the US those are Sutch (2006) for 1790–1870, Prados de la Escosura (2009) for 1800–1830, and the Conference Board's Total Economy Database for 2008–2022; for the UK, Broadberry et al. (2015) for 1700–1870 (S1, Sources sheet). A lesson figure comparing the US and UK from Maddison should carry those citations.
- MeasuringWorth (S18) asks users to contact it before publishing a table of more than five consecutive years of its data. This dossier uses scattered benchmark years only.

## Headline comparisons

These are written to be quoted. Each carries the ids of the sources it rests on.

1. By the Maddison Project's estimates, the United States produced about 2 percent of the world's output in 1820, about 10 percent in 1870, about 17 percent in 1900 and about 27 percent in 1950; in 2022 the figure was about 15 percent (S1).
2. Measured at market exchange rates instead of purchasing power, the US economy was about 26 percent of world GDP in 2024; at purchasing-power parity it was about 15 percent (S12).
3. In 1870 the average American produced about 82 percent as much as the average Briton; by 1913 about 23 percent more; and in 2022 about 52 percent more, by the Maddison Project's 2011-dollar estimates (S1).
4. Americans were about 1 percent of the world's people in 1820, about 6 percent in 1950, and about 4 percent in 2025 (S1, S7).
5. The first census, in 1790, counted 3.9 million people; the 2020 census counted 331.4 million; and the Census Bureau estimated 341.8 million on July 1, 2025 (S2, S3, S6).
6. In 1850, 9.7 percent of the people living in the United States had been born abroad; the share reached 14.8 percent in 1890, fell to 4.7 percent in 1970, and was back to 14.8 percent in 2024 (S8, S11).
7. Federal spending was about 3 percent of GDP in 1930, 41 percent at the wartime peak of 1945, and 23 percent in fiscal 2025 (S14).
8. Before World War II, peacetime military spending ran about 1 percent of the economy; national defense took 36.6 percent of GDP in 1945 and 3.0 percent in fiscal 2025 (S19, S15).
9. In 1860 the US had about 28,000 people on active military duty; in 1913 about 155,000; in 1945 about 12.1 million; and in 2025 about 1.3 million (S16, S29, S30).
10. By Paul Bairoch's estimates, the United States made 7.2 percent of the world's manufactured goods in 1860, when Britain made 19.9 percent; by 1913 the US made 32.0 percent and Britain 13.6 percent (S20).
11. In 2024 the US accounted for about 17 percent of the world's manufacturing value added, and China for about 28 percent, by UN national-accounts data (S13).
12. Exports plus imports equaled about 11 percent of US GDP in 1929, 8 percent in 1950, 13 percent in 1973, 30 percent in 2008 and 25 percent in 2025; for the world as a whole the 2024 figure was about 57 percent (S23, S24).
