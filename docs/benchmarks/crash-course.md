# Benchmark: Crash Course civics and government

*Studied November 2026. The house rules this produced are in
[`STYLE.md` → Teaching moves](../../STYLE.md#teaching-moves-benchmarked-against-crash-course).*

Crash Course is the most widely used free video curriculum for American
government. The goal is to explain everything it covers at least as clearly,
and to beat it on accuracy, currency, balance, and practice. This note
records what they cover, how they teach, where they slip, and what we
should build next.

The 2026 *Big Questions* series is the most direct competitor. Its seven
episodes are: Why are US politics so divisive? Why only two major parties?
Where does campaign money really come from? Why don't more people vote? Is
the Electoral College broken? How big a problem is voter fraud? Is
gerrymandering getting worse? The next episode was announced as why members
of Congress are "old, rich, and white."

## How they teach

- **A cold open with a hook.** The 2015 series opens with a jokey
  misdirect. The 2026 series personifies its subject ("A monster lurks
  among us" for the gerrymander) or opens on a scene (January 6 for voter
  fraud). We already require cold opens, so keep doing it.
- **Define, then name the near miss.** *Judicial Review* defines the term,
  then says what it is *not*: reviewing lower-court decisions is appellate
  jurisdiction.
- **Counter-intuitive reveals.** "Where in the Constitution does judicial
  review come from? Trick question! It's not there."
- **One worked story per hard idea.** The animated segment ("Thought
  Bubble") tells *Marbury v. Madison* as a short narrative with an analogy:
  Marshall weakened the Court in the case to strengthen it for good.
- **Neutral stand-ins for contested mechanics.** A fictional state
  ("Clonesylvania" in 2015, "Koushachusetts" in 2026) and joke parties (the
  "Anti-Arugula Party" against "Make Arugula Great Again") let the viewer
  see packing and cracking without anyone's real team on the line.
- **Paired examples across parties.** The 2026 gerrymandering episode pairs
  North Carolina (Republicans won 10 of 14 seats on about 55% of the vote,
  2024) with Illinois (Democrats won 14 of 17 on about 60%).
- **Advocates in their own voice.** In 2015 the host's clones argued
  liberal and conservative positions in the first person ("Clone Zone").
  That's quick and memorable, if close to caricature.
- **Pacing.** About 1,700–2,200 spoken words in 7–15 minutes,
  conversational, second person, a joke every few sentences. Each episode
  ends by teasing the next question.

## Where they slip, and where we're already better

**Factual errors and dated claims** (all verified):

- The 2015 *Gerrymandering* episode credits *Baker v. Carr* (1962) with
  putting "one person, one vote" into law for House districts. *Baker*
  held that apportionment claims could be heard in court. The
  equal-population rule for House districts came in *Wesberry v. Sanders*
  (1964). The 2026 episode repeats a softened version.
- The same episode says federal election law requires districts to be
  contiguous. The old federal contiguity and compactness requirements were
  not carried into the Reapportionment Act of 1929. *Wood v. Broom* (1932)
  held they had expired, so contiguity is now a matter of state law.
- It lists Montana among the one-seat states. Montana gained a second seat
  after the 2020 census.
- The 2015 *Judicial Review* episode's account of courts deferring to agency
  readings of statutes is out of date: *Loper Bright* (2024) overruled
  *Chevron*. Our *Administrative State* pack covers this.
- Episodes name cases and the occasional study, but give viewers no
  citations to follow up, and nothing marks when a fact was last true.

**Balance.** The 2015 *Political Ideology* episode leans on
generalizations; it even says "it's important to have some kind of data to
back up our generalizations," then offers none. The 2026 series is sharper
and better sourced, but it delivers verdicts on contested questions
("The system is broken," "big, bad, antidemocratic news," and that ending
gerrymandering "will be worth it"). On voter fraud it leans on one
think tank's study (the Brennan Center's), which it calls "an independent
report." Our standard,
to steel-man and then stop, is stricter.

**Depth and currency.** On topics we both cover we are usually far
ahead. Our *Gerrymandering* pack has an interactive map lab, the efficiency
gap computed by hand, and *Louisiana v. Callais* (April 2026). Checkpoints,
assessments, spaced review, and the Socratic coach have no Crash Course
equivalent.

**Where they beat us.**

1. **Breadth of the foundations.** Their 50-episode syllabus covers the
   institutions end to end. Our packs lean toward contested issues and
   skip much of the institutional spine (map below).
2. **Approachability.** Their scripts run 1,700–2,200 spoken words, but
   they're built around one question, loose in register, and funny. Our 25
   intro lessons average about 2,600 words of denser written prose, against
   our own 1,400–2,000 target, in a register aimed at "a college senior in a
   political science seminar." The length isn't the problem; the density
   and register are.

## Proposed foundations track

Intro level, 2–3 lessons per pack, written to the teaching moves in
`STYLE.md`. Listed in suggested order:

1. **`congress`**: (01) why two chambers *(pilot, done)*; (02) who wins
   seats, why incumbents rarely lose, and who serves; (03) committees,
   leaders, and how members decide.
2. **`the-presidency`**: what Article II grants; how presidents actually
   govern (executive orders, the bully pulpit, signing statements); what
   checks them. Contested; needs a strict "as of" date.
3. **`courts-and-the-law`**: civil versus criminal law and the burden of
   proof; how the federal courts are built; how judges decide (precedent and
   methods of interpretation).
4. **`civil-rights`**: equal protection and the tiers of scrutiny; from
   *Plessy* to *Brown* to the Civil Rights Act; sex discrimination;
   affirmative action after *SFFA* (2023). Contested.
5. **`where-political-views-come-from`**: socialization, how voters decide,
   and why turnout is what it is.
6. **`polarization`**: is it real, how do we measure it, and what drives it.
   Contested.
7. **`economics-for-citizens`**: how markets work and fail; the Fed and
   inflation; social insurance (Social Security and Medicare).
8. **Extend `bill-of-rights`** with religion, search and seizure, and the
   rights of the accused, rather than starting a separate civil-liberties
   pack.

That's about 22 new lessons. The pilot, `congress` 01, is the template:
question title, cold open, a comparison table, paired cross-party examples,
a near-miss `QuickCheck`, a magnitude `DataBars`, a steel-manned closing
debate, and a forward pointer.
