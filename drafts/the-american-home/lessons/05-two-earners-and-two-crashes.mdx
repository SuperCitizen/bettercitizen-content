# Two paychecks, two crashes: what changed for the American home from 1970 to 2008?

In the week of October 9, 1981, Freddie Mac's weekly survey of lenders found the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 18.63 percent, the highest it has ever recorded. At that rate a $40,000 loan cost about $623 a month in principal and interest. When the survey began, in the first week of April 1971, the rate was 7.33 percent and the same loan cost about $275. Buyers were not the only ones hurt: the savings and loan associations that had lent at the old rates were going broke. This lesson follows the American home through four changes between 1970 and 2008 and sorts the one-time events from the choices policy can still make.

## How did inflation help owners and break the lenders?

The 1970s brought [[term:stagflation]]: prices rising fast while growth stalled and unemployment climbed. In what the Federal Reserve's own history calls the Great Inflation, from 1965 to 1982, inflation peaked close to 15 percent in March 1980. In 1979 Paul Volcker became chairman of the [[term:federal-reserve]], and the Fed squeezed money until the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge one another overnight, approached 20 percent. A recession from July 1981 to November 1982 pushed unemployment near 11 percent, and by October 1982 inflation was down to 5 percent.

For a family that already owned a home with a fixed-rate mortgage, inflation was a gift: the house's price rose with everything else while the payment stayed the same, so the real weight of the debt shrank every year. In 2000 dollars, the Census median value of an owned single-family home rose from $65,300 in 1970 to $93,400 in 1980, and from 1.8 to 2.4 years of median family income. For a family trying to buy in 1981, the cure for inflation meant borrowing at 18 percent. The decade made owners richer and entry harder.

The lenders paid for the owners' gift. Savings and loan associations, or thrifts, took in savings that could leave at any time and made 30-year loans at fixed rates. Picture a bakery that has promised bread at a fixed price for thirty years while buying flour at that week's price; the picture understates the danger, because a thrift's savers could also walk out. When rates soared, thrifts paid savers more than their old mortgages earned, and by the end of 1982, 415 insured thrifts holding $220 billion in assets were insolvent.

Washington chose to wait and loosen rather than close them. A 1980 law raised federal deposit insurance from $40,000 to $100,000 an account, and the Garn–St Germain Act of 1982 wrote capital forbearance into law and let thrifts lend developers a project's full appraised value. Insolvent thrifts that stayed open bid up deposit rates and poured insured money into speculative real estate, much of which failed. About 1,300 savings institutions failed between 1980 and 1994, and in 1989 Congress abolished the thrifts' regulator and insurance fund. The history published by the [[term:fdic]] puts the cost at just over $160 billion, $132 billion of it from taxpayers, and concludes that the crisis "reflected a massive public policy failure."

The Great Inflation and its cure were a one-time episode, and so was the long slide in mortgage rates that followed, to an average of 5.83 percent in 2003: a tailwind for later buyers that cannot repeat unless rates first climb that high. Deposit insurance, capital rules and how fast regulators close a failing lender are levers that remain.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why was the inflation of the 1970s good news for some households and bad news for others?">
Owners with fixed-rate loans saw prices rise while their payments stayed put, so their debt shrank. Buyers faced the cure, mortgage rates of 18.63 percent in 1981. The thrifts holding the old loans lost most.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did two paychecks become normal?

In 1970, 40.5 percent of married women living with their husbands were in the labor force; by 2000 it was 61.1 percent, where it stayed through 2007. The share of married couples in which both spouses had earnings rose from 45.7 percent to 59.8 percent. Americans also married later, had fewer children and lived in smaller households.

Two more facts frame the debate. Among men who worked full time all year, median income in 2025 dollars was about $69,500 in 1973 and $68,800 in 2007; among women it rose from about $39,300 to $53,900. And the home-value ratio barely moved after 1980, though family income now usually included a wife's paycheck. Nobody disputes these numbers. What caused what is the argument.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Opportunity and choice">
    The economic historian Claudia Goldin, winner of the 2023 Nobel Prize in economics, argues that wives went to work because work and women's expectations changed. She points to a rise that began early in the twentieth century, as service jobs grew and women's schooling climbed, and to the pill, which let women plan careers. In "The Quiet Revolution" (2006) she dates a sharp break to the late 1970s, when young women began to expect long working lives and to count a career as part of who they were. On this view the second paycheck reflects wider choices, not a squeeze.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The two-income trap">
    The bankruptcy scholar Elizabeth Warren and the consultant Amelia Warren Tyagi argue in The Two-Income Trap (2003) that much of the second paycheck never reached the family's pocket. They point to a bidding war: families competed for homes in a shrinking set of neighborhoods with good public schools, and two incomes let them push those prices up. The result, in their account, was a family that earned far more than its one-earner parents yet had less left after fixed costs, and had lost its safety net: a parent at home who could take a job if the other lost one. They propose school choice across a metropolitan area, so a child's school no longer depends on the family's address.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

What the evidence settles is the timing. Married women's work rose from 4.6 percent in 1890 to 23.0 percent in 1950 and 40.5 percent in 1970, and the climb from 1950 to 1970 came while homes got cheaper relative to income, from 2.4 to 1.8 years, which fits Goldin's long story. Warren and Tyagi's comparison starts in the 1970s, and national figures cannot show how much of the later second paycheck went into bidding for houses in particular school districts. Either way the shift was one-time: a household can go from one earner to two only once, and wives' labor force share stopped rising around 2000.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what do the two views of the second paycheck agree on, and where do they part?">
Both accept the numbers. Goldin reads the second paycheck as opportunity grown from long changes in work, schooling and expectations; Warren and Tyagi read much of it as income bid into the price of homes in good school districts.
</Checkpoint>

The trap argument makes a testable prediction about places, so test it on one.

<Checkpoint question="A county lets any child attend its best high school by lottery, whatever the family's address. If Warren and Tyagi are right, what should happen to home prices inside the school's old attendance zone, and why?">
They should fall relative to similar homes nearby, because part of what buyers paid for was a seat in that school. If prices barely moved, the bidding war explained less than the authors argue.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did prices climb so much faster in some places?

In 1971 the town of Petaluma, California, completed 891 new homes. In 1972 it adopted a five-year plan allowing no more than 500 new dwelling units a year, "in order to protect its small town character and surrounding open space," as the appeals court put it. Builders sued and won in a federal district court, but in 1975 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed. A town could ration its own growth.

The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko built a test for what such rules do. Where homes sell for about what it costs to build them, supply is answering demand. Where homes sell far above construction cost, something is stopping builders; Glaeser and Gyourko attribute much of that gap to regulation and call it a regulatory tax (2018). With Raven Saks they argued (2005) that before 1970 rising prices mostly reflected better homes and higher building costs, and after 1970, the growing difficulty of getting approval to build, as residents got better at blocking projects.

Why would a town choose scarcity? The economist William Fischel's homevoter hypothesis (2001) answers that a home is most families' largest asset and cannot be insured against changes in the neighborhood, so homeowners vote, reasonably from where they stand, against development that might lower its value, the logic behind today's [[issue:nimby-and-yimby]] fight. Economists broadly accept that tight rules raise prices where demand is strong; how much of the national rise they explain is still argued, and lesson 6 and the U.S. Housing Policy pack take up today's fight over [[issue:zoning-reform]]. Of every force in this lesson, land-use rules are the most plainly still in policy's hands.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what is Glaeser and Gyourko's test for whether rules are holding prices up?">
Compare what homes sell for with what they cost to build. Near cost, builders are meeting demand; far above it, something is stopping them, usually regulation in Glaeser and Gyourko's account.
</Checkpoint>

## What caused the crash of 2008?

From January 2000 to July 2006 the S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller national home price index rose about 85 percent; then it fell about 27 percent, to a low in February 2012. On the way up, homeownership reached 69.2 percent in 2004 in the Census's quarterly survey, its all-time high. On the way down, in September 2008, the government took Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac into conservatorship and Lehman Brothers failed. By early 2011 about four million families had lost their homes to foreclosure, according to the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, the ten-member panel Congress created to explain the crisis. Its members, appointed by leaders of both parties, split three ways.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Lax private lending and securitization">
    The commission's majority argues that the crisis was avoidable and that "collapsing mortgage-lending standards and the mortgage securitization pipeline lit and spread the flame of contagion and crisis." Lenders made loans with little regard for borrowers' ability to pay and sold them on to be pooled into securities for investors around the world, while regulators heard warnings and did not act. The economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi point to neighborhood data (2009): from 2002 to 2005 mortgage credit grew fastest in ZIP codes heavy with subprime borrowers even as their incomes fell behind, tracking the rise of subprime securitization. Lenders who sold loans soon after making them, they conclude, had too little reason to care whether the loans were repaid. At stake, on this view, is a lender's duty to check that a borrower can pay.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Government housing-finance policy">
    Commissioner Peter Wallison argues in his dissent (2011) that U.S. government housing policy was the essential cause. Beginning with affordable-housing goals Congress imposed on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in 1992, and pressed by the housing department under Presidents Clinton and George W. Bush, policy pushed lenders to lower underwriting standards to reach borrowers at or below their area's median income. He points to his count of 27 million subprime and other risky loans, half of all U.S. mortgages, which he argues were ready to default once the 1997–2007 bubble began to deflate. At stake, on this view, are sound underwriting standards, which a political goal eroded.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="A global saving glut">
    Ben Bernanke, then a Federal Reserve governor, argued in a 2005 lecture that a "global saving glut" had emerged over the previous decade, helping to explain unusually low long-term interest rates. Commissioners Keith Hennessey, Douglas Holtz-Eakin and Bill Thomas built their dissent (2011) on that idea, calling global imbalances "an essential cause of the crisis": China, other developing countries and big oil producers saved more than they spent and sent the surplus to America and Europe. They point to housing bubbles in Britain, Spain, Australia, France and Ireland, some larger than America's, and some in countries that relied little on American-style securitization. At stake, on this view, is an explanation that fits every country the crisis hit.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The boom, the bust, looser lending and the foreclosures are not in dispute; which cause was essential is. The commission's majority found that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac "contributed to the crisis, but were not a primary cause," which Wallison disputes. Two studies complicate every side. Glaeser, Gyourko and Joshua Gottlieb (2010) estimated that lower real interest rates explain only about a fifth of the price rise from 1996 to 2006. Manuel Adelino, Antoinette Schoar and Felipe Severino (2016) found that middle- and high-income borrowers made up most new mortgages even at the peak, and read the boom as a bet, shared by buyers and lenders, that prices would keep rising. The global flood of savings was a condition of the world economy; lending standards, the rules for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and the supervision of lenders are levers that remain.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what are the three explanations of the 2008 crash, and what does each one blame?">
The commission's majority blames private lenders and the securitization pipeline that let them pass risky loans on. Wallison blames government policy that pushed underwriting standards down. Bernanke and the three-member dissent blame global savings that made credit cheap in many countries at once.
</Checkpoint>

Each explanation predicts something different about where bubbles should appear, which gives you a way to test them.

## What this era settled, and what it left open

The rates, the counts and the price swings in this lesson are established. What remains contested is why two paychecks became normal, how much land-use rules explain, and what caused 2008.

Sort the era the way this series sorts every era. The one-time conditions were the Great Inflation and its cure, the long slide in rates that followed, the move of wives into paid work, which a household makes only once, and the flood of global savings. The levers that remain are land-use rules; lending standards and supervision; deposit insurance and how fast failing lenders are closed; the rules for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac; and [[term:monetary-policy]], which broke an inflation in the early 1980s at the price of 18 percent mortgages.

Lesson 6 picks up where the crash left off: today's housing shortage, why the estimates of it differ, and what a home costs in years of income now.
