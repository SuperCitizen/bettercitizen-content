# What could policy realistically recreate today?

On July 15, 1949, Harry Truman signed a housing law and told the country what it was for. The act, he said, "establishes as a national objective the achievement as soon as feasible of a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family." The next year, the typical owner-occupied home was worth about 2.4 years of the typical family's income. In 2025 it was worth about 3.5 years. Seventy-seven years on, Truman's objective still sits in the U.S. Code, and so does the question this series set out to answer: what made the postwar "American dream" possible, and which parts of it could policy realistically build again?

## Which parts of the postwar boom were one-time?

Start by sorting. The boom of roughly 1945 to 1965 rested on two kinds of causes: conditions of the moment, which no law can summon again, and government choices whose instruments still exist.

Four conditions belong in the first pile. The first is land: a continent of it, taken from Native nations and handed out cheaply in the nineteenth century, and in 1950 still open at the edge of nearly every city. The second is America's position after 1945, its factories intact while Europe's and Japan's were being rebuilt. The third is pent-up demand. Builders started about 937,000 nonfarm homes in 1925, only 93,000 in 1933 and 142,000 in 1944, and then 1,952,000 in 1950, as fifteen years of postponed households arrived at once. The fourth is the baby boom: births per woman peaked at 3.77 in 1957, and in 1950 the median man first married at 22.8 and the median woman at 20.3.

The second pile holds the levers: the long, low-down-payment mortgage that the FHA and VA guaranteed, the tax code's treatment of homeowners, the highways that put cheap land within commuting range, and local rules that let a builder put up thousands of houses on one tract. The same programs also wrote racial exclusion into their rules, as lessons 3 and 4 showed, a part nobody proposes to recreate.

The sort matters because the levers worked through the conditions: a mortgage guarantee is powerful when land at the edge is cheap and builders can answer each new buyer with a new house, and weaker when they cannot. The five measures show how much has changed.

Most measures moved far from their 1950 values, and one moved the way the postwar levers aimed: a larger share of households own their homes today than in 1950. The finance levers lasted. What they cannot do is bring back the conditions that once made them cheap to pull.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what separates a one-time condition from a policy lever, and why did the postwar levers work as well as they did?">
  A one-time condition is a fact of its moment that no law can recreate, such as open land at every city's edge. A lever is an instrument government still holds, such as a mortgage guarantee or a zoning rule. The postwar levers worked partly because the conditions let builders answer new demand with new houses.
</Checkpoint>

## What can each lever do, and what does it cost?

Picture a town with 100 houses, 120 families who want them, and a rule that no 101st house may be built. The state gives every family a $20,000 down-payment grant. The same 100 families end up in the same 100 houses, each now selling for more, because every bidder has $20,000 more to bid. Give the grant where builders can add houses, and much of it becomes new homes instead. It is musical chairs: better shoes for every player do not add a chair. The image breaks in one place. Real towns are never perfectly fixed, and aid aimed at particular families decides who gets a chair even when it adds none.

The evidence fits the sketch. When voucher payment limits rose across whole metro areas, Robert Collinson and Peter Ganong (2018) found that rents rose and landlords captured much of the gain, while limits set neighborhood by neighborhood moved families to safer, lower-poverty areas. Edward Pinto of the American Enterprise Institute made the same point about mortgages in his 2021 Senate testimony, subtitled "When Supply Is Constrained, Credit Easing Will Make Entry Level Homes Less Affordable."

So the levers fall into two families. Some change how many homes can exist: land-use rules, permitting, transportation, and public or tax-credit building. Others change who can pay: mortgage finance, the tax code, rent vouchers and cash for families, most of them forms of [[term:subsidy]]. Cities and counties hold most of the first family, through zoning, and Washington most of the second: [[term:federalism]] built into the plumbing.

<Figure caption="Eight levers that still exist. The first three rows, and the building halves of the public-housing and direct-subsidy rows, change how many homes can exist; the rest change who can pay. Each has a cost someone bears." source="Glaeser and Gyourko (2018); JCHS, State of the Nation's Housing 2026; Glaeser and Shapiro (2003); Diamond, McQuade and Qian (2019); Transit Costs Project (2023); Kearney, Levine and Pardue (2022)">

| Lever | What it can do, and what it costs |
| --- | --- |
| Land-use and zoning rules | Let builders answer demand where people want to live, as postwar tract rules did. Cost: neighbors lose some say, and results take years. |
| Permitting | Approve homes by right, or from a catalog of approved designs, as South Bend, Indiana, now does. Cost: less review of each project. |
| Transportation | Put land within commuting range of jobs, as the 1956 interstates did. Cost: roads lengthen commutes, and American rail is slow and costly to build. |
| Mortgage finance and federal guarantees | Make long, low-down-payment loans normal, as the FHA and VA did. Cost: taxpayer risk, and higher prices where supply is fixed. |
| Tax treatment of housing | Reward owning, mainly through the mortgage interest deduction. Cost: the benefit goes mostly to higher-income owners who would own anyway. |
| Tenant protection and public housing | Keep current renters housed, and build homes outside the market. Cost: rent caps can shrink rental supply, and public housing needs funding that lasts. |
| Direct subsidy | Build income-limited homes with tax credits, or pay part of the rent with a voucher. Cost: money; only about one in four eligible very low-income renters get help. |
| Family policy | Lower the cost of raising children, through child credits and childcare. Cost: large budgets, and an uncertain effect on birth rates. |

</Figure>

Three findings sharpen the table. First, the biggest tax lever is poorly aimed. Edward Glaeser and Jesse Shapiro (2003) called the [[issue:mortgage-interest-deduction]] "a particularly poor instrument for encouraging homeownership," because it flows mostly to wealthy households who would own anyway. Second, direct aid is rationed: in 2023 just over one in four very low-income renter households, 5.4 million of 19.1 million, received federal rental help, though the 2025 tax law gave the [[issue:low-income-housing-tax-credit]] a permanent 12 percent increase in its main allocations from 2026, the largest in its history (JCHS, 2026). Third, family cash works fast on poverty. When the 2021 expansion of refundable credits, including the [[issue:child-tax-credit]], ended along with pandemic stimulus payments, the number of children in poverty under the Census Bureau's fuller measure rose by more than 5 million in 2022. Its effect on births is less clear.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why can help with paying for homes raise prices instead of housing more people?">
  Where the number of homes cannot grow, extra buying power lets every bidder offer more for the same houses, so prices rise and the same families end up housed. Where builders can respond, the same money becomes new homes. That is why the paying levers work best alongside the building levers.
</Checkpoint>

Apply that to the lever the section treated most briefly.

<Checkpoint question="Two metros each build a new commuter rail line to an outlying town with cheap farmland. In the first, the town lets builders put up houses and apartments by right. In the second, the town requires two-acre lots and a public hearing for every subdivision. Where is the line more likely to show up as new homes, and where as higher prices near the stations?">
  In the first, the line opens land builders may use, so the new access becomes new homes. In the second, the rules block most new homes, so the access shows up mainly in higher prices for homes near the stations. A transportation lever, like a paying lever, works through the land-use rules beneath it.
</Checkpoint>

## The strongest cases, side by side

Everyone in this argument agrees on three things: homes cost more years of income than in 1950, the one-time conditions are gone, and every lever has a cost that someone bears. They disagree about whose needs come first: the newcomer who cannot find a home, the family that cannot pay for one now, the renter who could lose one, or the couple deciding whether there is room for a child. Four positions answer, each as its strongest advocates would put it, the [[term:steel-man]] standard.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Supply-side and zoning reform">
    The postwar boom was first a building boom, on land the rules let builders use, and building is the part policy can most directly recreate. The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko (2018) find prices near construction cost in most of the country and far above it in a number of tightly regulated, mostly coastal metros, a gap they read as an implicit tax on building. William Fischel (2001) explains why the rules persist: homeowners vote to protect their largest asset. And new homes help further down: Evan Mast (2023) found that a new market-rate building housing 100 people leads 45 to 70 people to move out of below-median-income neighborhoods. The concession: supply takes years, and it never reaches the poorest without subsidy.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Demand-side subsidy and finance">
    The postwar dream was a finance revolution as much as a building one: the guaranteed long mortgage turned renters into owners. Richard Green and Susan Wachter (2005) argue that government shaped the American mortgage's long terms and low down payments, and that those features have served homeowners and the economy's stability. Today the gap is help with paying, which reaches only about one in four eligible very low-income renters. Matthew Desmond (*Evicted*, 2016) argues for a voucher for every poor renting family, and the economist Edgar Olsen (2016) points to studies finding that vouchers provide equally good housing at far lower cost than programs that build projects. Design decides who gains (Collinson and Ganong, 2018). The concession: where supply is fixed, more buying power leaks into prices.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Tenant protection and public housing">
    Supply and subsidy are both promises about the future, and renters are living in the meantime. The sociologists David Madden and Peter Marcuse (*In Defense of Housing*, 2016) argue that the crisis comes from treating housing as real estate before treating it as a home, and that part of it should be taken out of the market, as the 1949 act's [[issue:public-housing]] once was. A modern version runs in Montgomery County, Maryland, whose housing authority built a $100 million revolving construction fund in 2021 and 2022 and keeps majority control of the mixed-income buildings it finances; the first, the 268-unit Laureate, opened in 2023. Protections keep people housed: San Francisco's rent control cut tenants' moves by 20 percent (Diamond, McQuade and Qian, 2019). The concession, from the same study: covered landlords cut rental supply by 15 percent, so caps need careful design.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Family policy">
    The postwar dream was a family arrangement, so housing policy should be judged by whether young adults can form the families they want. Wendell Cox and Lyman Stone of the Institute for Family Studies (2025) argue that high housing costs are a major factor suppressing marriage and births; in their survey, young adults worried about housing costs intended fewer children than they desired. They call for smaller minimum lots and an end to urban growth boundaries. The economists Lisa Dettling and Melissa Kearney (2014) found that rising house prices lift births among owners and lower them among renters. The concession: Kearney, Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue (2022) find that no measurable economic or policy factor explains most of the fall in births since 2007, so policy may ease family life more than it raises births.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The evidence settles some things. Where building is tightly restricted, prices sit far above construction cost; aid to buyers in a fixed market leaks into prices; rent control keeps tenants in place while shrinking rental supply; and cash cuts child poverty quickly. It cannot settle the order of the levers, because that turns partly on whose years count most.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what does each of the four positions treat as the first problem to solve?">
  Supply-side reformers start with how many homes the rules allow; demand-side advocates with who can pay now; tenant advocates with keeping current renters housed; family-policy advocates with whether young adults can afford the families they want. Each grants that its own lever has a cost the others point to.
</Checkpoint>

Now test the positions against a result none of them owns outright.

<Checkpoint question="A city legalizes fourplexes on every lot. Over five years its rents rise less than in neighboring cities, but its poorest renters still pay more than half their income for housing, and its birth rate does not change. Which positions does this result support, and which does it leave standing?">
  It supports the supply-side case, since added building restrained rents. It leaves the demand-side and tenant cases standing, because the poorest households still need help that construction alone did not deliver, and it fits the family-policy concession that lower costs may move births little. One result rarely settles the order of the levers.
</Checkpoint>

## What the series has settled, and what it has not

Return to the question the series began with. What made the postwar dream possible was a combination: a continent of land, an economy without rivals, fifteen years of postponed demand, a generation that married young, and public levers, the guaranteed long mortgage above all, that turned those conditions into houses. It was also a dream with a fence around it, drawn by race through the same programs.

Some of that is settled. The numbers are: a home cost about 2.4 years of family income in 1950 and about 3.5 in 2025. The sort is: the land, the postwar position, the pent-up demand and the baby boom cannot be bought twice, while every lever in this lesson still exists and is in use somewhere. And the measures settle a point the historian Stephanie Coontz pressed in 1992, that the 1950s family was an exception rather than a long tradition: men's median age at first marriage was 26.1 in both 1890 and 1990, and fell as low as 22.5 in 1956 and 1959.

What is not settled is which lever to pull first, whether to aim at ownership or at housing of any kind, how much government rather than private actors built the fence, and whether lower costs would bring back earlier marriage and larger families. Some of that is a question of evidence and some a question of whose needs count first, and no table settles the second kind. Today's policy fight continues, lever by lever, in Better Citizen's contested pack U.S. Housing Policy.

### Further reading

- Stephanie Coontz, *The Way We Never Were: American Families and the Nostalgia Trap* (Basic Books, 1992). Coontz argues that the 1950s breadwinner family was a recent and unusual arrangement, and that American families were never as self-reliant as nostalgia remembers. Its view that the family is changing rather than declining stands against the family-decline thesis of sociologists such as David Popenoe, who argued that children bore the costs of the family's real decline.
- Kenneth T. Jackson, *Crabgrass Frontier: The Suburbanization of the United States* (Oxford University Press, 1985). Jackson traces American suburbs from the early nineteenth century and argues that cheap land, new transportation and the ideal of the detached house drove them, with New Deal housing policy speeding the move and tying it to racial exclusion. His emphasis on federal policy is debated: Amy Hillier (2003) and Price Fishback and colleagues (2021) found that the HOLC's neighborhood maps did little to shape lending, and Robert Bruegmann (*Sprawl*, 2005) argues that suburban spread is old, worldwide and driven mainly by rising affluence.
- Richard Rothstein, *The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America* (Liveright, 2017). Rothstein argues that residential segregation was created and sustained by explicit government policy at every level, which makes it unconstitutional segregation the country is obliged to remedy. Critics such as the geographer Richard Walker (*Jacobin*, 2019) argue that he underweights private actors, above all a real estate industry that had entrenched segregation before 1933; Rothstein, replying in the same magazine, agreed that private racism came first but held that decades of federal policy deepened it.
