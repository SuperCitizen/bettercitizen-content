# What made the postwar American dream possible?

In 1949 Jim and Virginia Tolley waited in a line on Long Island with about 3,000 other former GIs and their families, hoping to buy a house that had not been built yet. Living in a three-room apartment in Jackson Heights, Queens, they signed for a house William Levitt would build on a plot he pointed to on a map. That same year Gene Burnett, also a veteran of the war, saw the same newspaper advertisements and drove out with his fiancée. The salesman would not give him an application form. "The builders have not at this time decided to sell to Negroes," the agent told him.

Two veterans, one subdivision, two answers. Between 1940 and 1960 the share of households owning their home rose from 43.6 percent to 61.9 percent, the largest rise of the twentieth century. What made that possible, who was kept out, and which causes could policy summon again?

## How did a veteran buy a house with nothing down?

The long, amortized mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration under the [[term:new-deal]] was the first engine. The war added a second. On June 22, 1944, days after D-Day, President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, the GI Bill. Its Title III let the Veterans Administration (today's [[term:veterans-affairs]] department) guarantee up to half of a veteran's home loan, to a limit of $2,000, if the loan charged no more than 4 percent interest and was repaid within twenty years.

A loan guaranty is a promise to the lender, not a gift to the borrower. It works like a parent co-signing a first lease: the landlord accepts a tenant he would otherwise refuse, because someone solvent stands behind the debt. The analogy breaks at scale: a parent co-signs for one child, while Washington stood behind millions of loans and wrote their terms. With the government carrying part of the risk, lenders made VA loans with little or, at one time, no down payment. Taxpayers paid only when loans failed, plus the first year's interest on the guaranteed share. By 1955 there were 4.3 million GI home loans worth $33 billion, and veterans had bought 20 percent of all new homes built after the war.

Builders, meanwhile, learned to make houses like cars. On potato farms about 30 miles east of New York City, Levitt and Sons built more than 17,000 houses between 1947 and 1951. The house stood still while specialized crews moved through 27 steps, and William Levitt liked to call his firm the General Motors of housing. His houses sold for $7,990 in 1949, about two and a half times that year's median family income of $3,107.

Roads widened the circle. The Federal-Aid Highway Act, which President Dwight Eisenhower signed on June 29, 1956, expanded the interstate system to 41,000 miles and authorized $25 billion for fiscal years 1957 through 1969. Levittown came first, so the interstates did not start the suburbs, but they carried them farther: the economist Nathaniel Baum-Snow found that each new highway through a central city cut its population by about 18 percent. Inside the cities, the National Archives notes, the highways "cut through neighborhoods and destroyed housing, much of it in poorer sections of the city."

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how did federal policy and private builders together put a house within reach of a veteran like Jim Tolley?">
  The government guaranteed part of his loan, so lenders accepted little or no down payment at a capped rate. Builders mass-produced houses on cheap land at the city's edge, and roads made that land reachable.
</Checkpoint>

A guaranty is easy to confuse with a grant, and the difference matters.

## Why did families form so young?

The family half of the dream shows up in the five measures this series tracks.

Against the longer record, each family measure swings away from the trend rather than back to an old normal. The median man first married at 24.3 in 1940 and at 22.8 in 1950, bottoming out at 22.5 in 1956, yet he married at 26.1 in both 1890 and 1990. Births per woman rose from 2.30 in 1940 to 3.77 at the 1957 peak. The Census Bureau dates the baby boom from 1946 to 1964, when births averaged just under 4 million a year, against 2.6 million in the nineteen years before.

The single-earner household was common but already eroding: in 1950 fewer than one married woman in four was in the labor force, and by 1960 almost one in three was. The historian Stephanie Coontz argues in *The Way We Never Were* (1992) that the breadwinner-and-homemaker family of the 1950s was a new and unusual arrangement, not the traditional one. One caution: tables that show men's participation jumping from 79.0 percent in 1940 to 86.4 percent in 1950 are mixing two sources and age groups; measured the 1940 way, the 1950 figure is 80.2 percent.

Why did so many couples marry and buy at once? Partly because of conditions that came together only once. Americans saved 27.9 percent of their after-tax income in 1944, against 5.4 percent in 1939. American factories came through the war untouched by the fighting, while Western Europe needed more than $12 billion in [[term:marshall-plan]] aid to rebuild. The economists Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo call the 1940s the Great Compression: pay gaps between workers narrowed sharply and stayed narrow until about 1970, partly because of the war and its wage controls and largely because of rising demand for less-skilled workers. Unions, which law can strengthen or weaken, were also at their height, with about one worker in three a member in 1954, against one in ten in 2022 ([[issue:union-rights]]).

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why do the five measures suggest the 1950s family was unusual rather than traditional?">
  Marriage age and births swung far from the long-run trend and back: men married at 26.1 in 1890 and 1990 but at 22.5 in 1956, and births per woman jumped to 3.77. Even the single-earner household was eroding.
</Checkpoint>

That reading changes how anyone can argue from the past.

## Who was kept out, and how?

Gene Burnett met a system with four layers, some public and some private.

The first was the deed. A racial covenant is a clause binding owners not to sell or rent to people of a named race. In 1911 owners on Labadie Avenue in St. Louis signed one barring occupancy "by any person not of the Caucasian race" for fifty years. When a Black family, the Shelleys, bought a house there in 1945, neighbors sued to force them out. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) the Supreme Court held that "in granting judicial enforcement of the restrictive agreements in these cases, the States have denied petitioners the equal protection of the laws." A court enforcing a covenant is the state acting, and the [[term:equal-protection]] clause binds the states. But "the restrictive agreements standing alone," the Court said, broke no right under the Fourteenth Amendment. Covenants became unenforceable, not illegal to write, and *Barrows v. Jackson* (1953) then barred suits for damages too.

The second was federal practice. The FHA's 1938 Underwriting Manual held that "if a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial groups," and it recommended covenants. From February 15, 1950, the FHA would not insure homes with newly recorded covenants, and the VA adopted similar rules. In 1961 the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights found that the change "has not had a great effect in securing equal housing opportunity." By one estimate it reported, fewer than 3,000 of the 200,000 FHA- or VA-backed houses built in the San Francisco Bay Area from 1950 to 1958 were sold to nonwhite buyers, who were 10 percent of the population. Nationally, 2.9 percent of homes with VA loans in 1956 had a nonwhite head of household, while about 7.5 percent of the veterans of World War II and Korea were nonwhite.

The third was the builder's own rule. Levitt's 1947 rental contracts barred occupancy by anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race," and after *Shelley* the exclusion went on as sales policy. The fourth was the neighbors. In August 1957 William and Daisy Myers, a Black couple, bought a house in Levittown, Pennsylvania. About 600 residents petitioned against them, crowds gathered, stones broke their picture window and three crosses burned on the lawn before Governor George Leader sent in the state police.

Washington changed course late and in part. President John F. Kennedy's Executive Order 11063, signed on November 20, 1962, barred discrimination in housing financed by federally insured or guaranteed loans made from then on; for homes already financed it asked agencies only for their "good offices." The Fair Housing Act of 1968 belongs to the next lesson.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did Shelley v. Kraemer change about racial covenants, and what did it leave in place?">
  No court could enforce a covenant after 1948, so no judge could evict a family like the Shelleys. But writing covenants stayed legal, and builders, lenders, agencies and neighbors kept excluding by other means.
</Checkpoint>

Try that distinction on facts the Court never saw.

## Policy, circumstance, or exclusion?

Nobody disputes that ownership soared, families formed young and Black families were largely shut out of the new suburbs. The fight is over weights, and the evidence cuts both ways. The economist Daniel Fetter estimates that GI Bill loans explain 7.4 percent of the 1940 to 1960 rise in ownership, mostly by moving purchases earlier in life, yet by a rough extrapolation the broader change in mortgage terms could explain 40 percent. He also finds that ownership rose about 10 percentage points between 1940 and 1945, while wartime rules severely restricted building, and that wartime rent control explains at least a tenth of that rise.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Built by policy">
    The historian Kenneth T. Jackson argues in *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985) that federal mortgage and highway policy did much to make the suburbs. On this view the boom was designed, from the mortgage to the interstates. Its holders point to Fetter's 40 percent for mortgage terms, to Baum-Snow's 18 percent per highway and to veterans buying a fifth of new postwar homes. It protects the claim that policy can make ownership broad again. It would be moved by evidence that ownership rose as fast among people the programs did not reach.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Built by circumstance">
    Economic historians who stress conditions over programs argue that circumstances that could not last did most of the work, drawing on studies such as Goldin and Margo's and on Robert J. Gordon's argument in *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016) that mid-century productivity came from a one-time wave of great inventions. Its holders point to ownership rising 10 points during the war, before the postwar programs, to Fetter's 7.4 percent, and to wartime savings and ruined rivals. It protects against nostalgia: copying 1950s policy would not bring back 1950s conditions. It would be moved by evidence that similar programs produced similar booms where those conditions were absent.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Built on exclusion">
    Richard Rothstein argues in *The Color of Law* (2017) that residential segregation was *de jure*, imposed by explicit government policy at every level, not *de facto*, the sum of private choices, and so a constitutional wrong that calls for a remedy. Ira Katznelson argues in *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005) that the GI Bill's reliance on local banks and offices let discrimination through. Its holders point to the FHA manual, to VA loans reaching nonwhite households at well under their share of veterans and to a federal order that came only in 1962. It protects an honest account of who got to build wealth through the era's houses. It would be moved by evidence that private builders and neighbors alone would have excluded just as completely.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Each cause can also be sorted by whether it could happen again.

One-time conditions:

- Pent-up demand and savings after fifteen years of Depression and war.
- An economy whose industrial rivals were in ruins.
- The Great Compression in wages.
- A marriage dip and baby boom that ran against the long-run trend.
- Open farmland close to big cities, much of it since built over.

Levers that still exist:

- Federal mortgage guarantees and insurance, still offered by the VA and FHA.
- Public spending on roads and transit that opens land to homes.
- Land-use rules that decide how many homes may go up on a tract ([[issue:zoning-reform]]).
- Labor law, which shapes how strong unions can be.
- Fair housing law and its enforcement.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what makes a cause of the boom a one-time condition rather than a policy lever?">
  A one-time condition came from history, such as pent-up savings or the baby boom, and no law can summon it again. A lever is a choice government can still make, such as guaranteeing loans or setting land-use rules.
</Checkpoint>

The sorting matters most when someone proposes to pull one lever alone.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose Congress offered every first-time buyer a zero-down, federally guaranteed loan in 2027 and changed nothing else. What questions would each perspective raise?">
  The policy view would expect more owners, since the guaranty can be used again. The circumstance view would ask whether builders could add homes fast enough without cheap open land, since more borrowing power can raise prices where supply cannot respond, and would recall that GI loans mostly moved purchases earlier. The exclusion view would ask who could actually use the loans.
</Checkpoint>

## What the postwar boom does not settle

The measures, the laws, the holdings and the Commission's findings are settled. The weighting is not: Fetter's 7.4 percent and his 40 percent give the policy and circumstance views each something to cite. Historians also differ on how much of the exclusion came from government: Rothstein puts policy at the center, while Thomas Sugrue, in *The Origins of the Urban Crisis* (1996), gives great weight to white homeowners' organized resistance, the force the Myers family met. Whether the single-earner family was a freedom many wanted or a constraint many women accepted is still argued. Today's policy fight over supply, subsidy and tenant protection is the subject of the U.S. Housing Policy pack.

By 1960 the ground was already shifting: married women's paid work was climbing, the baby boom would end in 1964, and rivals abroad were rebuilding. The next lesson follows 1970 to 2008, as two earners became the norm and two crashes tested the mortgage system this era built.
