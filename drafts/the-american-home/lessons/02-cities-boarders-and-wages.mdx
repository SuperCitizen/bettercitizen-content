# How did working families pay for a home in the industrial city?

Just past midnight, a New York policeman pounded on a tenement door on Bayard Street, gathering evidence of illegal overcrowding, and Jacob Riis followed him in with a flash camera. In *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) he described what the light caught: "In a room not thirteen feet either way slept twelve men and women, two or three in bunks set in a sort of alcove, the rest on the floor." Most of the men "were lodgers, who slept there for five cents a spot." Later in the book Riis met a Hester Street cloak-maker who earned seven dollars a week and paid nine and a half dollars a month in rent: "A boarder pays about a third of it." The crowded room was a horror. It was also how a working family kept a roof overhead.

## Why did the move to the city change what a family was for?

For most of the nineteenth century the typical American household was a farm, and a farm was a workplace. In 1900, 41 percent of American workers were employed in agriculture; by 1930 the share was 21.5 percent (USDA Economic Research Service, 2005). The 1920 census was the first in which most Americans lived in urban areas, and many newcomers came from abroad during the [[term:gilded-age]] and after: the foreign-born share of the population was 14.8 percent in 1890 and 14.7 percent in 1910 (Census Bureau, 1999).

The sociologist Steven Ruggles has traced what wage work did to the multigenerational home. On the farm, land to inherit kept grown children close, and in the mid-nineteenth century almost 70 percent of Americans aged 65 or older lived with an adult child. Wage work broke that bargain: "Millions of young people left their parents' farms," Ruggles writes, "attracted by the high wages, independence, and excitement of town life." The norm faded slowly, and almost half of elders still lived with adult children in 1920 (Ruggles, 2007), but the multigenerational home was shrinking with the farm economy that had held it together.

A wage household lived on several paychecks. In a federal survey of workers' families in 1901, 95.9 percent had earnings from the husband, 22.2 percent from children, 23.3 percent from boarders or lodgers, and only 8.5 percent from the wife (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2006). The home itself earned money.

Homeownership barely moved nationally, from 47.8 percent in 1890 to 45.6 percent in 1920, but the flat line hides two movements. In 1890, 65.9 percent of farm households owned their home and only 36.9 percent of nonfarm households did, so families leaving the farm left the high-owning group, even as nonfarm ownership crept up to 40.9 percent by 1920 (Historical Statistics, 1975).

## Who earned the rent, and who did the census see?

Before 1940 the census recorded "gainful workers": anyone aged 10 or older who reported a usual occupation. The age floor says something by itself: the census assumed a ten-year-old might have a job. By that count, 79.3 percent of men and 17.4 percent of women aged 10 and up were gainful workers in 1890 (Historical Statistics, 1975).

The 1870 census found 1 in 8 children employed, and between 1890 and 1910 no less than 18 percent of children aged 10 to 15 worked. In one study of Philadelphia families, children supplied 28 to 33 percent of household income in native-born two-parent families, and as much as 46 percent in Irish families (Monthly Labor Review, 2017). The sociologist Viviana Zelizer, in *Pricing the Priceless Child*, traces how between the late 1800s and the 1930s the child became economically "useless" and emotionally "priceless." Federal law came late: only the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 banned oppressive child labor in the industries it covered.

Married women are where the census misleads most. It counted 4.6 percent of married women as gainful workers in 1890 and 9.0 percent in 1920, leaving out the main way they earned money: keeping boarders. The economist Claudia Goldin found that married women were the majority of boardinghouse keepers and that the census missed most of them in cities. In 1880 Philadelphia, 14 percent of white households had boarders, but fewer than 1 percent of dwellings were recorded with a female boardinghouse keeper. Counting the women who kept boarders, Goldin estimated, would add 5.9 to 7.4 percentage points to the census's 4.0 percent for married white women in cities in 1890, more than doubling it (Goldin, 1986).

One figure errs the other way. The census recorded 10.7 percent of married women as workers in 1910, but enumerators that year were specially instructed to record the occupations of women and children, and the Census Bureau's tables warn that they likely counted many women, especially farm laborers, who would not have been counted in other years. The 1910 figure is an artifact, not a trend.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why does the census figure of 4.6 percent for married women in 1890 understate what married women did to pay for housing?">
  The census counted only people who reported a usual occupation. A wife who cooked and cleaned for paying boarders earned real income at home but usually reported none, so she was left out; Goldin's correction more than doubles the count in cities.
</Checkpoint>

## How did a boarder pay the rent, and what did the 1901 law change?

The price of a home in years of income cannot be computed for this era: the Census Bureau's home-value series begins in 1940, and its national median family income in 1947. What survives instead are budgets. In 1901 the federal Bureau of Labor recorded the spending of 2,567 families in 33 states, in "the leading industrial centers," each headed by a wage worker or salaried man earning not over $1,200. Food took 42.5 percent of spending. Rent took about 13 percent, rent plus payments on homes being bought 14.5 percent, and with fuel and lighting, shelter came to about a fifth (Bureau of Labor, 1904).

Shelter took a modest share because families bought little of it and shared it. Riis reported that three-fourths of New Yorkers, "more than twelve hundred thousand persons," lived in 37,000 tenement houses. The housing scholar Richard Harris estimates that in 1900 about a quarter of American households had a lodger or a lodging family, and that almost as many more took one in at some point (Harris, 1994).

New York's Tenement House Act of 1879, the "old law," banned rooms with no access to light and air, so every room needed a window onto the street, the yard or an air shaft. The shafts gave tenements their "dumbbell" shape, and they failed: often too narrow to light lower floors, they collected garbage and carried fire (Landmarks Preservation Commission, 2012). Riis printed the 1888 death rates for the Mulberry Bend block: 35.75 per 1,000 residents, against 26.27 for the whole city, and 139.83 per 1,000 among children under five. In a well-run model tenement across the street, he wrote, "the hearse called that year only twice."

The Tenement House Act of 1901, the "new law," was the [[term:progressivism-political|Progressive]] answer, framed mainly by Lawrence Veiller. It raised light and air requirements so far that building on New York's standard 25-foot lot became economically infeasible, which effectively ended the dumbbell. It required one toilet per apartment, and new-law tenements typically rose six or seven stories around light courts, on lots 35 feet wide or more (Landmarks Preservation Commission, 2012). Chicago, Philadelphia, Boston and other cities copied it, and in 1936 the planning authority James Ford called it "the most significant regulatory act in America's history of housing" (quoted in Beito and Beito, 2016).

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how could a working family in 1901 spend only about a seventh of its budget on rent?">
  It bought little space and shared it, renting part of a few rooms to boarders or lodgers who covered a share of the rent. Food, not shelter, took the largest share of spending.
</Checkpoint>

Rules on crowding reached into the household's budget too.

<Checkpoint question="A family pays $9.50 a month for two rooms, and a boarder pays about a third of it. The city enforces a rule against lodgers in small apartments, and the boarder must go. What can the family do, and what does each choice cost?">
  Pay the full rent from wages and cut food, already the largest line; send a child to work; move to a worse room; or move in with relatives, which is doubling up by another name. The rule buys privacy only if one of those costs is bearable.
</Checkpoint>

## Was the crowded home a danger to regulate or a strategy to protect?

Both sides accept the facts so far: crowding was deadly in the worst blocks, lodging was common, and the 1901 law priced the old cheap form off the standard lot. Both accept that lodging then faded, from 20.6 percent of central-city households in 1910 to 8.5 percent in 1940, as war and the quotas of the 1920s cut off immigration and rising incomes let more people keep a household of their own (Beito and Beito; Harris). The argument is over what the rules did for the families they meant to protect.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Housing reform: a floor under every home">
    Reformers such as Jacob Riis, Lawrence Veiller, and the Chicago investigators Sophonisba Breckinridge and Edith Abbott argued that the tenement was a market failure that killed. One family cannot buy light and air when its windows face a shaft the landlord will never widen; only a rule binding every builder can. They pointed to the death records: in 1888 the Mulberry Bend block lost children under five at a rate of about one in seven a year, while a decently run tenement across the street saw two deaths. Lodging meant strangers beside a family's children; Breckinridge and Abbott found men lodgers sleeping in the same room as a household's young daughters (1911). Standards are how a society says no one may be housed below a floor. What would change their mind: evidence that families pushed out by the rules ended up housed worse, or that health improved no faster where the rules were enforced.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Family strategy: the bottom rung of a ladder">
    Social historians such as John Modell and Tamara Hareven (1973) argued that taking in boarders evened out a family's income over its life cycle. The historians David and Linda Royster Beito (2016) argue that lodging was a strategy for getting ahead. They point to Olivier Zunz's Detroit, where 35 percent of unskilled workers owned homes in 1900, largely thanks to doubling up, and to Roger Simon's Milwaukee, where new owners rented out part of the house to meet the mortgage, then took back the whole building. Every rule that raised quality also raised the price of the cheapest legal home; the Beitos conclude that codes, zoning and density limits "sawed off the bottom rungs of the housing ladder." Paul Groth, writing of cheap hotels and rooming houses, finds that "for at least one hundred years, the keepers of official culture have aimed at eliminating these roles" (1994). What would change their mind: evidence that lodger-keeping families rarely climbed to ownership, or that cheap housing grew scarce for reasons unrelated to the rules.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Beyond those shared facts, the Detroit and Milwaukee studies show lodging was often a temporary path to owning. The evidence cannot settle the counterfactual: how many cheaper homes would have been built without the rules, and how their residents would have fared. Nor can data settle the value question underneath: how high to set the floor, and who should pay to raise it.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what does each side say the 1901 law protected, and what does each say it cost?">
  Reformers say it protected tenants' lives, health and privacy by forcing light, air and sanitation into new buildings, at a cost worth paying. Family-strategy critics say it bought those standards with the cheapest legal housing, raising the bottom rung poor families climbed.
</Checkpoint>

## What the industrial city settles, and what it leaves open

Some things are settled. Working families paid for housing with several earners, including children and wives whose boarders the census rarely saw; about a quarter of households took in a lodger; fewer than half owned their home, and off the farm only about 37 to 41 percent did; and no one can honestly put a price-to-income number on the period. What remains contested is whether the rules that ended the dumbbell and chased the lodger helped poor families on balance.

Some of the era's conditions were one-time: the move off the farm happened once; immigration on the scale of 1890 to 1910 was cut off by war and quotas; and children's wages, largely ended by law and changing values, are something no serious side proposes to bring back. Other conditions are levers that still exist. Building codes are one: each requirement buys some safety and adds to the minimum cost of a legal home, as the 1901 law shows. What housing types the law permits is another. In *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas* (1974) the Supreme Court upheld a village rule limiting a house to a family or to no more than two unrelated people. Renting a room or adding a backyard unit, today's version of taking a boarder, is legal in some places and banned in others; [[issue:accessory-dwelling-units]] and [[issue:zoning-reform]] are where that argument lives now, and the U.S. Housing Policy pack sets out its strongest cases.

One fact points forward. Families often bought homes with savings and lodger money: even in 1920 only 40 percent of nonfarm homes were mortgaged, and in the 1920s the volume of nonfarm residential debt tripled (Beito and Beito, 2016, citing Kenneth Snowden). The next lesson follows that debt: the short, high-down-payment mortgages of the 1920s, the foreclosures of the Depression, and the [[term:new-deal]] system that rebuilt how Americans buy homes.
