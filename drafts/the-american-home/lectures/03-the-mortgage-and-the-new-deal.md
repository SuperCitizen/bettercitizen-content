It is 1933. Some 1,000 American home loans are being foreclosed every day.

[beat]

A brand-new federal agency opens its doors. In under two years, nearly 1.9 million homeowners ask it for help.

The agency is called the Home Owners' Loan Corporation. It makes just over a million loans. Each one becomes a 15-year loan, paid off month by month.

[pause]

Today, that agency is best known for something else. [beat] A set of city maps, with some neighborhoods shaded red.

Both the loans and the maps came out of the same New Deal effort to rebuild the American mortgage. By the end of this lecture, you will be able to explain how the mortgage changed, who the new one left out, and why scholars still argue over the maps.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the 1920s mortgage, and why it was so easy to lose.

Second, the three agencies that built a new kind of loan.

Third, what the maps and the rule book said about race.

And last, the open argument. How much did the maps themselves cause?

## The loan you had to renew

Start with an invented family, buying a 5,000-dollar house in 1926.

A typical home loan of that decade, the economic historian Kenneth Snowden writes, covered no more than half the price. It ran three to five years. And it was what is called a balloon loan. You paid the interest, and maybe a little of the principal. Then the rest came due in one lump.

So our family puts down 2,500 dollars. It borrows the other 2,500, at 6 percent. Each month it pays twelve dollars and fifty cents in interest. [beat] And after five years, it still owes all 2,500.

Nobody planned to pay that lump out of savings. The lender was expected to renew the loan, and it usually did.

> A 1920s mortgage worked only as long as someone renewed it.

Put another way, the family was renting money. It paid rent on the sum, and it owed the whole sum back when the lease ran out, unless the lender renewed.

[beat]

Here is where the comparison breaks. A tenant who cannot renew moves out. A borrower who cannot renew, or repay, loses the house.

There was another kind of loan. A loan paid down in regular installments, so that the last payment clears it, is called fully amortized. Building and loan associations already made loans like that, repaid over eleven or twelve years. But they were the exception.

Then came the crash. From 1929 to 1932, personal income after taxes fell by about two-fifths. Home values fell by about a quarter. But mortgage debt fell only 6.8 percent.

Thousands of banks failed, and with them went the lenders who would have renewed the loans. A family could pay its interest faithfully for years, and still face a lump it could neither pay nor refinance.

[pause]

On January 1, 1934, a federal study of 22 cities found that 43.8 percent of urban homeowners with a first mortgage were in default.

Two different things broke here, and it matters which is which. The collapse itself, a depression that took thousands of banks with it, was a one-time catastrophe. The contract that turned it into mass foreclosure was a design choice. [beat] And design can change.

> The crash was a one-time catastrophe. The balloon loan was a design choice.

Let's test that. It is 1931. A family has never missed an interest payment on its five-year balloon loan. The loan comes due, and the bank that made it has failed. What is the family's real danger? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It owes the whole principal at once, and no lender is left to renew it. Paying interest on time does not help when the full balance falls due, in a year when almost nobody is lending.

## Three agencies, three jobs

So how do you build a mortgage that does not depend on renewal? The New Deal answered with three agencies, and each did a different job.

First, a rescue. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation, created by Congress in 1933, bought mortgages in default from lenders. Then it rewrote each one as a 15-year loan at 5 percent interest, later 4.5 percent. It lent at most 80 percent of the home's value.

By June 1936, it had made just over a million loans. That was about one in five of the nation's mortgaged, owner-occupied homes off the farm.

It was not easy money. Nearly half of all applications were rejected or withdrawn. One in five of its loans still ended in foreclosure. And over its life, its income slightly exceeded its losses.

Second, something meant to last. In 1934, Congress created the Federal Housing Administration. The FHA did not lend money. It insured loans that private lenders made, promising to cover their losses if a borrower defaulted. [beat] In exchange, it set the terms.

> The FHA did not lend. It insured, and in exchange it set the terms.

And the terms are the story. By its 1938 rules, an insured loan was paid down every month until nothing was left. It carried no more than 5 percent interest. It ran as long as 20 years. For a small new house bought with 10 percent down, it could run as long as 25. Borrowers paid for the insurance with a small yearly premium.

Now hear the difference from 1926. In the FHA manual's own worked example, a 5,000-dollar house carries a 3,800-dollar loan for 20 years. The whole monthly bill, taxes and insurance included, comes to about thirty-six dollars. The same house would rent for forty.

[beat]

So the stake up front shrinks. And the balance falls to zero on a schedule, instead of coming due in one piece.

Third, a buyer. A lender still had one worry: its money tied up for 20 years. So in 1938, a federal agency created the Federal National Mortgage Association, later called Fannie Mae. It bought FHA-insured loans from the lenders that made them.

That is called a secondary market. A lender sells a loan, gets its money back, and lends again.

[pause]

Say it once more, in fewer words. The HOLC rescued old loans. The FHA made long new loans safe to make. And Fannie Mae made them easy to sell.

> Rescue, insure, resell. Three agencies, three jobs.

Here is where the one-time and the lasting part company. The HOLC was an emergency agency, wound up in 1951. It lent just after the bottom of the slump, and it collected during a recovery that no law can order up. That timing cannot be repeated on purpose.

The other pieces are still here. Federal mortgage insurance. The long amortized loan. And the secondary market. The FHA has been part of the Department of Housing and Urban Development since 1965, and it says it has insured over 50 million mortgages since 1934.

Did the new system raise homeownership right away? [beat] No.

In 1930, 47.8 percent of households owned their homes. In 1940, the share was 43.6 percent. That is the lowest of any census since the count began in 1890. Households shrank, and births fell. And for what a home cost in years of income, there is no reliable figure. The census valued the median home in 1940, but there is no national median income to divide it by.

[pause]

The machinery existed. [beat] The boom that would use it had not yet come.

Let's test the three jobs. A bank in 1939 likes FHA loans, but will not make more. It does not want its money tied up for 20 years. Which piece of the system answers that worry? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Fannie Mae. The bank is not afraid of going unpaid, because insurance already covers that. It is afraid of being locked in. And a buyer in the secondary market turns a 20-year loan back into cash.

## The grades and the rule book

That is the machine. Now, who it served, and who it left out.

Redlining means refusing mortgage credit to whole neighborhoods, not just to individual borrowers. Starting in September 1935, the HOLC sent agents to cities of 40,000 people or more. By 1940, they had surveyed 239 cities.

Working with local lenders, developers and appraisers, the agents graded neighborhoods. A, colored green, was judged best. D, colored red, was judged hazardous. Each grade came with a written description of the housing, the prices, and the people: their race, their ethnicity and their class.

Black residents, and to a lesser degree immigrants and Jews, were recorded as an infiltration. Most red neighborhoods were white, often immigrant. But Black neighborhoods were graded D almost without exception, whatever their residents earned.

[pause]

Here is one description, from Tacoma, Washington. A small area, otherwise like the B-graded neighborhood around it, was graded D. Listen for the reason the graders gave.

"Three highly respected Negro families own homes and live in the middle block of the area.... While very much above the average of their race, it is quite generally recognized by Realtors that their presence seriously detracts from the desirability of their immediate neighborhood."

[pause]

Highly respected. Above the average. [beat] And still, by the graders' rule, a hazard.

The FHA wrote the same judgment into its rule book. Its 1938 Underwriting Manual told its staff to look for "incompatible racial and social groups" nearby. Here is its reason, in its own words.

"If a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes."

[beat]

The same social and racial classes. The manual counted barriers, natural or artificial, as protection. And among the deed restrictions it recommended, it listed this one: "Prohibition of the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended."

> The FHA's own manual wrote racial exclusion into the rules for insurance.

And the FHA acted on it. The economic historian Price Fishback and his coauthors studied three cities, loan by loan, from 1935 to 1940. In Greensboro, North Carolina, they found exactly one FHA-insured loan to a Black borrower. Greensboro had about 1,300 Black homeowners. In Peoria, Illinois, they found none.

The HOLC's own lending looked different, because its maps came late. It had already made 90 percent of its loans before the survey began, and it refinanced nearly 24,000 African American homeowners.

Yet it asked every applicant's race. And when it resold homes it had foreclosed on, it barred Black buyers from white-majority neighborhoods, until at least 1943.

Let's carry the rule book somewhere new. It is 1938. A builder plans 200 small houses at a city's edge, across a road from an established Black neighborhood. The builder wants FHA insurance, so that buyers can get 20-year loans. Going by the manual, what would most improve the rating? Think it through.

[long pause]

Covenants that limit occupancy to one race, and a barrier between the two areas. The manual counted both as protection. [beat] Access to the long, insured loan ran through exclusion.

## What did the maps themselves do?

All of that is well documented. The open question is narrower. Did the HOLC's maps cause the later decline of the neighborhoods they marked? Or did they record judgments that lenders and the FHA were already acting on?

The historian Kenneth Jackson brought the maps to wide attention in his 1985 book, Crabgrass Frontier. He treated the HOLC as the pioneer of federal redlining. Since then, three readings have emerged. Here is each at its strongest, built the same way: who holds it, what it protects, what it argues, and what it points to.

The first reading: the maps as a cause. It is held by three economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, Daniel Aaronson, Daniel Hartley and Bhashkar Mazumder. It protects a finding: an official grade on a place can itself steer credit for decades. They argue that the maps had "meaningful and lasting effects on the development of urban neighborhoods."

They point to blocks on either side of the lines, checked against comparison lines with matching earlier trends. After the maps, the lower-graded side had less homeownership, lower values and rents, and a rising Black share. That gap grew until about 1970 or 1980. They also point to cities just above the 40,000-person cutoff for a map. Those cities fell behind the ones just below it.

> The maps-as-cause reading: the red lines themselves steered credit for decades.

The second reading: the maps as a record. It is held by the economic historian Price Fishback and his coauthors, and by the scholar Amy Hillier. It protects an accurate account of which agency did what, so that blame lands where it belongs. They argue that the maps recorded a segregated market, and that the FHA did the excluding.

They point to timing. The HOLC made 90 percent of its loans before the maps, and lent to Black homeowners roughly in proportion to their numbers. The FHA's exclusion began before the maps, and changed little after. They also point to Philadelphia, where Hillier found lenders already avoiding the later-red areas. And they point to an estimate that bias in drawing the maps explains at most 4 to 20 percent of the concentration of Black households in the lowest-rated zones.

> The maps-as-record reading: the maps traced lines the FHA and lenders already drew.

The third reading: the maps as one part of a system. It is held by historians such as Todd Michney, who with LaDale Winling has traced the HOLC's place in a wider lending regime. It protects the record of the HOLC's own responsibility, which its late maps could seem to excuse. They argue that the maps are the best-preserved artifact of a regime that made racial risk-rating an industry norm.

They point to an HOLC that asked every applicant's race, and barred Black buyers when it resold foreclosed homes. And they point to the HOLC and the FHA teaching racial appraisal to the industry. They taught it through workshops, trade journals, and a 1937 forum of more than 1,000 people, where HOLC maps were on display.

> The system reading: the maps were one artifact of a whole lending regime.

[pause]

Notice how much the three share. All three accept that the FHA excluded Black neighborhoods by rule. All three accept that the HOLC's graders treated Black residents as a hazard. What they dispute is the weight of one document, against FHA practice and the segregation that came before it.

The loan timing and the boundary comparisons are real evidence, and they point in different directions. Whether a map or a whole regime is the right unit of blame is partly a judgment. [beat] That is a contested question, not a settled one.

You may already lean toward one of these readings. Take the one you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Before we gather this up, pull something back. Near the start, I gave you a comparison for the 1920s mortgage. Can you still name it? And can you say where it broke?

[pause]

Renting money. And it breaks at the end of the lease. A tenant who cannot renew moves out, but a borrower who cannot renew loses the house.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A 1920s mortgage worked only as long as someone renewed it. The crash was a one-time catastrophe. The balloon loan was a design choice.

[beat]

Two. Rescue, insure, resell. The HOLC rescued about a million borrowers. The FHA insured the long amortized loan. Fannie Mae let lenders sell it. Those last levers still exist.

[beat]

Three. The FHA's own manual wrote racial exclusion into the rules for insurance. And Black neighborhoods were graded hazardous almost without exception.

[beat]

Four. How much the maps themselves caused is still argued. A cause. A record. Or one part of a system.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We have the machine and its rules. We do not have agreement on the weight of the maps.

And there is an older argument underneath. The savings associations had a trade group, the Building and Loan League. It saw FHA insurance as a government subsidy for the same risky kind of loan its own members had promoted in the 1920s. Whether insuring long, high-leverage loans is wise is still debated.

The next lecture follows this machine into the postwar boom. The GI Bill, Levittown and the highways ran it at full speed. [beat] And the rules written here traveled with it.

For now, go back to 1933. A million families, each handed a 15-year loan they could pay down month by month. [beat] And a few years later, the same agency, coloring neighborhoods red on a map. You know now what both of those meant, and why people still argue over the second.
