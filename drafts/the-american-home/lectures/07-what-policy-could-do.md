July 15, 1949. Harry Truman signs a housing law, and tells the country what it is for.

The act, he says, "establishes as a national objective the achievement as soon as feasible of a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family."

[beat]

A decent home, for every American family. As soon as feasible.

The next year, the typical owned home was worth about 2.4 years of the typical family's income. In 2025, it was worth about 3.5 years.

[pause]

Seventy-seven years later, Truman's objective is still in the law. And the question this series set out to answer is still open. What made the postwar American dream possible? And which parts of it could policy realistically build again?

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to sort the causes of that dream into two piles. You will be able to say what each remaining tool can do, and what it costs. And you will be able to give four serious answers their best case.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the sort: what happened once, and what can happen again.

Second, the tools that remain, and the catch that runs through half of them.

Third, four positions on which tool comes first.

And last, what this series has settled, and what it has not.

## Two piles

Start with the sort. The boom of roughly 1945 to 1965 rested on two kinds of causes.

Some were conditions of the moment. No law can summon them again.

Others were choices made by governments. The tools behind those choices still exist.

> A condition happens once. A lever can be pulled again.

Four conditions belong in the first pile.

The first is land. A continent of it, taken from Native nations and handed out cheaply in the nineteenth century. In 1950, it was still open at the edge of nearly every city.

[beat]

The second is America's position after 1945. Its factories were intact, while Europe's and Japan's were being rebuilt.

[beat]

The third is pent-up demand. In 1925, builders started about 937,000 homes off the farm. In 1933, they started only 93,000. In 1944, 142,000. And then, in 1950, nearly two million. Fifteen years of postponed households arrived at once.

[beat]

The fourth is the baby boom. Births per woman peaked at 3.77, in 1957. And in 1950, the median man first married at 22.8.

[pause]

Now the second pile: the levers.

The long mortgage with a low down payment, which the FHA and the VA guaranteed. The tax code's treatment of homeowners. Highways that put cheap land within reach of jobs. And local rules that let a builder put up thousands of houses on one tract.

Those same programs also wrote racial exclusion into their rules. Nobody proposes to recreate that part.

Here is why the sort matters. The levers worked through the conditions.

A mortgage guarantee is powerful when land at the edge is cheap, and builders can answer each new buyer with a new house. It is weaker when they cannot.

Put another way, the tools are still in the drawer. The world they were built for is gone.

How much has that world changed? Take three of the five measures this series has tracked.

Price, first. A home went from about 2.4 years of family income in 1950 to about 3.5 in 2025.

[beat]

Marriage, second. The median man married at 22.8 in 1950, and at 30.8 in 2025.

[beat]

And births. Births per woman fell from about three in 1950 to about half that by 2024.

[pause]

One measure moved the way the postwar levers aimed. A larger share of households own their homes today than in 1950. The finance levers lasted. What they cannot do is bring back the conditions that once made them cheap to pull.

Let's test the sort on a new case. In the 1950s, many young couples could buy a house partly because men without college degrees earned rising wages. Those wages came from factories that faced few foreign rivals.

Which pile does that cause belong in? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first pile, mostly. Those wages owed most to an economy with few rivals after the war, and no statute can recreate that.

Labor and trade law can still shape wages at the margin. So the sort is not always clean. A condition can have a smaller lever attached to it.

## The catch

That is the sort. Now the tools that remain, and the catch that runs through half of them.

Picture a town with 100 houses, and 120 families who want them. A rule says no new house may be built.

The state gives every family a grant of 20,000 dollars toward a down payment. What happens?

[beat]

The same 100 families end up in the same 100 houses. And every house sells for more, because every bidder has 20,000 dollars more to bid.

Give the same grant in a town where builders can add houses, and much of the money becomes new homes instead.

It is musical chairs. Better shoes for every player do not add a chair.

> More money for buyers, with no more homes, buys higher prices.

The image breaks in one place. Real towns are never perfectly fixed. And aid aimed at particular families decides who gets a chair, even when it adds none.

The evidence fits the sketch. Robert Collinson and Peter Ganong studied housing vouchers in 2018. When voucher payment limits rose across whole metro areas, rents rose, and landlords captured much of the gain. When the limits were set neighborhood by neighborhood, families moved to safer, lower-poverty areas instead.

Edward Pinto, of the American Enterprise Institute, made the same point about mortgages, in Senate testimony in 2021. His subtitle says it in one line: "When Supply Is Constrained, Credit Easing Will Make Entry Level Homes Less Affordable."

So the levers fall into two families.

The first family changes how many homes can exist. Land-use and zoning rules. Permitting. Transportation. And public building, or building paid for with tax credits.

[beat]

The second family changes who can pay. Mortgage finance. The tax code. Vouchers for rent. And cash for families, like child credits.

Cities and counties hold most of the first family, through zoning. Washington holds most of the second.

Every one of these tools has a cost. Three findings make that concrete.

First, the biggest tax lever is poorly aimed. Edward Glaeser and Jesse Shapiro called the mortgage interest deduction "a particularly poor instrument for encouraging homeownership." It flows mostly to wealthy households, who would own anyway.

[beat]

Second, direct aid is rationed. In 2023, just over one in four very low-income renter households got federal rental help. That is 5.4 million, out of 19.1 million.

[beat]

Third, family cash works fast on poverty. The larger child credit of 2021 ended, along with the pandemic stimulus payments. In 2022, the number of children in poverty rose by more than 5 million, on the Census Bureau's fuller measure. Its effect on births is less clear.

Let's try one. Two metros each build a new commuter rail line, out to a town with cheap farmland.

In the first metro, the town lets builders put up houses and apartments by right. In the second, the town requires two-acre lots, and a public hearing for every subdivision.

Where does the new line show up as new homes? And where as higher prices? Think it through.

[long pause]

In the first metro, the line opens land that builders may use. So the new access becomes new homes.

In the second, the rules block most new homes. So the access shows up mainly as higher prices for the homes already near the stations.

A transportation lever, like a paying lever, works through the land-use rules beneath it.

## Four cases

Now the argument. Start on ground nobody owns.

Everyone in this debate agrees on three things. Homes cost more years of income than in 1950. The one-time conditions are gone. And every lever has a cost that someone bears.

They disagree about whose needs come first. The newcomer who cannot find a home. The family that cannot pay for one now. The renter who could lose one. Or the couple deciding whether there is room for a child.

Four positions answer that question. Each gets the same four things: who holds it, what it protects, its strongest argument, and the evidence it points to.

First, supply-side and zoning reform.

Who holds it? The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko, and William Fischel. What it protects is the newcomer who cannot find a home.

Its argument: the postwar boom was first a building boom, on land the rules let builders use. Building is the part policy can most directly recreate.

Its evidence: Glaeser and Gyourko find prices near the cost of construction in most of the country. In a number of tightly regulated, mostly coastal metros, prices sit far above it. They see that gap as an implicit tax on building.

Fischel argues that the rules persist because homeowners vote to protect their largest asset. And Evan Mast points to chains of moves. A new market-rate building housing 100 people leads 45 to 70 people to move out of below-median-income neighborhoods.

> The supply-side case: let builders build, and prices settle near construction cost.

Its concession: supply takes years, and it never reaches the poorest without subsidy.

[pause]

Second, demand-side subsidy and finance.

Who holds it? Richard Green and Susan Wachter, writing on the history of the American mortgage. Matthew Desmond, in his book Evicted. And the economist Edgar Olsen. What it protects is the family that cannot pay now.

Its argument: the postwar dream was a finance revolution as much as a building one. The guaranteed long mortgage turned renters into owners. Green and Wachter argue that government shaped the American mortgage's long terms and low down payments. Those features, they argue, have served homeowners and the economy's stability.

Its evidence: help with paying reaches only about one in four eligible very low-income renters. Desmond argues for a voucher for every poor renting family. Olsen points to studies finding that vouchers provide equally good housing, at far lower cost than programs that build projects. And design decides who gains, as the voucher study showed.

> The demand-side case: help families pay now, and design the help well.

Its concession: where supply is fixed, more buying power leaks into prices.

[pause]

Third, tenant protection and public housing.

Who holds it? The sociologists David Madden and Peter Marcuse, in their book In Defense of Housing. What it protects is the renter who could lose a home.

Its argument: supply and subsidy are both promises about the future, and renters are living in the meantime. Madden and Marcuse argue that the crisis comes from treating housing as real estate before treating it as a home. Part of it, they argue, should be taken out of the market, as public housing under the 1949 act once was.

Its evidence: a modern version runs in Montgomery County, Maryland. The county's housing authority built a revolving construction fund of 100 million dollars, in 2021 and 2022. It keeps majority control of the mixed-income buildings it finances. The first, the Laureate, has 268 apartments, and opened in 2023.

And protections keep people housed. In a 2019 study, San Francisco's rent control cut tenants' moves by 20 percent.

> The tenant case: renters are living in the meantime, so protect them now.

Its concession, from the same study: covered landlords cut their rental supply by 15 percent. So caps need careful design.

[pause]

Fourth, family policy.

Who holds it? Wendell Cox and Lyman Stone, of the Institute for Family Studies. What it protects is the couple deciding whether there is room for a child.

Its argument: the postwar dream was a family arrangement. So housing policy should be judged by whether young adults can form the families they want.

Its evidence: Cox and Stone argue that high housing costs are a major factor suppressing marriage and births. In their survey, young adults worried about housing costs intended fewer children than they desired. They call for smaller minimum lots, and an end to urban growth boundaries. And the economists Lisa Dettling and Melissa Kearney found that rising house prices lift births among owners, and lower them among renters.

> The family-policy case: judge housing by whether young adults can raise families.

Its concession: Kearney, with Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue, finds that no measurable economic or policy factor explains most of the fall in births since 2007. So policy may ease family life more than it raises births.

[pause]

Four positions. You may already lean toward one of them. So take the one you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

Here is what the evidence settles. Where building is tightly restricted, prices sit far above construction cost. Aid to buyers in a fixed market leaks into prices. Rent control keeps tenants in place while shrinking rental supply. And cash cuts child poverty quickly.

What the evidence cannot settle is the order of the levers. That turns partly on whose years count most.

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## What the series has settled

Before we gather it up, pull something back. Earlier I gave you a first pile: the conditions that happened once. Can you still name all four?

[long pause]

Land. The postwar position. Pent-up demand. And the baby boom.

[pause]

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A condition happens once. A lever can be pulled again. The land, the postwar position, the pent-up demand and the baby boom cannot be bought twice. Every lever still exists, and is in use somewhere.

[beat]

Two. The postwar levers worked through the conditions. Cheap land, and builders free to build, made a mortgage guarantee powerful.

[beat]

Three. More money for buyers, with no more homes, buys higher prices. That is why the paying levers work best alongside the building levers.

[beat]

And four. The four positions disagree about which lever comes first: building, help with paying, tenant protection, or family policy. Each grants that its own lever has a cost the others point to.

[pause]

Now return to the question the series began with. What made the postwar dream possible?

A combination. A continent of land, an economy without rivals, fifteen years of postponed demand, and a generation that married young. And public levers, the guaranteed long mortgage above all, that turned those conditions into houses.

It was also a dream with a fence around it. The same programs drew that fence by race.

Some of this the series has settled. The numbers, for one. And a point the historian Stephanie Coontz pressed in 1992: the 1950s family was an exception, not a long tradition.

Men's median age at first marriage was 26.1 in 1890. It was 26.1 again in 1990. In between, it fell as low as 22.5.

[pause]

What is not settled is which lever to pull first. Whether to aim at ownership, or at housing of any kind. How much government, rather than private actors, built the fence. And whether lower costs would bring back earlier marriage and larger families.

Some of that is a question of evidence. Some of it is a question of whose needs count first. No table settles the second kind.

The argument goes on from here, lever by lever, in the contested pack on today's housing policy. And three books carry the history further: Stephanie Coontz's The Way We Never Were, Kenneth Jackson's Crabgrass Frontier, and Richard Rothstein's The Color of Law. Each one is argued over, and the lesson says where.

For now, go back to that July day in 1949. A decent home and a suitable living environment, for every American family. [beat] The conditions that first brought it within reach are gone.

> The conditions are gone. The levers remain, and each one has a price.
