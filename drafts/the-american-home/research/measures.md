# The American Home: shared measures dossier

Compiled November 22, 2026. Every URL below was fetched on that date and the value was read from it directly (spreadsheets were opened and read; scanned pages of the 1975 *Historical Statistics* were read from the page images).

## How to use this

This file is the single source of numbers for all seven lessons. When a lesson gives one of these measures for one of these years, use the value here and cite the source id shown (S1, S2, and so on, listed at the end). Do not swap in a different figure for the same measure and year from another source, even a good one, because the lessons are meant to be compared side by side.

Each value carries a label. **Established** means an official statistic published by a statistical agency (Census, BLS, NCHS). **Estimate** means a scholarly reconstruction, or an official series that the agency itself describes as estimated from incomplete records. **Derived** means a ratio computed here from two established figures, with the inputs shown so anyone can check the arithmetic. All dollar figures are nominal (current dollars of their year), so every price-to-income ratio is unitless and needs no inflation adjustment.

Empty cells are deliberate. Where no credible national figure exists, or none could be verified, the cell says "no reliable figure" and the lesson should say the same rather than estimate one. The thinnest stretch is before 1890: there are no official national figures for homeownership, marriage age or labor force by marital status, and no national price-to-income ratio exists anywhere before 1950.

The "At a glance" table directly below is built for each lesson's "The five measures, around …" figure. The sections after it give the detail, definitions and alternatives behind every cell.

## 1. Home price in years of income

### Definitions

Two different ratios exist, and they answer different questions. Keep them apart.

**Census value ratio (the dossier's main series).** The Census asks homeowners what their home would sell for. The median of those answers, divided by median income, says how many years of income the typical owned home is worth. From 1940 to 2000 the decennial census value covers "specified" owner-occupied units: single-family homes on less than 10 acres with no business or medical office on the property (S1). From 2010 the American Community Survey (ACS) table B25077 covers all owner-occupied housing units (the universe printed in the table), without the single-family, under-10-acres restriction (S12, S10). Because the group of homes measured changed, say "about" when comparing a ratio from 2000 or earlier with one from 2010 or later.

**Income pairing.** The census asks about income in the previous calendar year, so 1950 home values are divided by 1949 income, 1960 by 1959, and so on. For those income years the dossier uses the Current Population Survey's national median family income (S8), the one consistent national series that runs from 1947 to today. Median household income (S9) starts in 1967. From 2010 on, value and income come from the same ACS survey and period. If a writer instead pairs each census value with same-year income, the ratios come out roughly 0.1 to 0.2 lower (for example 2.2 rather than 2.4 in 1950); the dossier does not use that pairing.

**Market price ratio (JCHS).** Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies divides the median sales price of existing single-family homes (National Association of Realtors) by median household income (Moody's Analytics estimates) (S30, S31). This measures what homes on the market cost buyers that year, not what all owned homes are worth. It runs higher than the Census value ratio. Both are legitimate; they are different measures.

### Before 1940

No national price-to-income figure in this dossier exists for any year before 1950. The Census home value series used here begins in 1940 (S1), and the national median family income series begins in 1947 (S8). No verifiable national median income for an earlier year that could be matched to home values was found. Lessons 1 to 3 should write "no reliable figure" for this measure and may describe the era's housing costs in words instead.

## 3. Who worked outside the home

Labor force participation counts people working for pay or profit or looking for such work, as a percent of the population in the age group. It does not measure unpaid work at home, and it cannot say where the paid work was done. Before 1940 the census counted "gainful workers," people who reported a usual occupation, which is a different concept from the modern labor force.

Other series. The quarterly CPS/Housing Vacancy Survey rate, the figure most often quoted in the news, peaked at 69.2% in the second and fourth quarters of 2004, was 65.6% to 65.7% through 2024, and was 65.5% in the third quarter of 2026 (S13). It jumped to 67.9% in the second quarter of 2020, when pandemic disruptions affected data collection, and no data were collected in October 2025 (S13).

Notes. The census figures (1890 to 2020) are the long comparable series; use them for era comparisons and note when a recent figure comes from the ACS or the HVS instead, because the surveys differ (the 2020 Census found 63.1%, while the HVS ranged from 65.3% to 67.9% across the four quarters of 2020 and the ACS measured 65.4% in 2021). For 1900 the Census's own historical table (S2) prints 46.5% while the 1975 *Historical Statistics* and the 2020 Census brief print 46.7% (S17, S3); the dossier uses 46.7%, the figure in the most recent Census publication. There are no national homeownership figures before 1890.

## Headline comparisons

These are written to be quoted. Each keeps its source ids.

1. In 1950 the median owner-occupied home was valued at about 2.4 years of median family income; in 2025 it was valued at about 3.5 years of median family income, or about 4.4 years of median household income. (S1, S8, S10; the 1950 figure covers single-family homes on less than 10 acres, the 2025 figure all owner-occupied homes.)
2. Measured by what homes actually sold for, the median existing single-family home cost about 4.7 times the median household income in 2025, down from a record 5.0 in 2022 but up from 4.1 in 2019 and an average of 3.2 in the 1990s. (S31)
3. The average household shrank from 5.79 people in 1790 (counting free people only) to 3.37 in 1950 and 2.50 in 2025. (S14, S6)
4. In 1890 fewer than 1 in 20 married women (4.6 percent) were in the labor force; by 1950 it was 23.0 percent, by 2000 61.1 percent, and in 2025 58.2 percent. (S16, S25, S26)
5. In 1967 both husband and wife had earnings in 43.6 percent of married couples; by 2000 the share was 59.8 percent. (S28)
6. The median American man first married at 22.8 and the median woman at 20.3 in 1950; in 2025 the figures were 30.8 and 28.4. (S7)
7. Men's median age at first marriage was 26.1 in both 1890 and 1990; in between it fell as low as 22.5, in 1956 and 1959. (S7)
8. The total fertility rate was about 7 births per woman in 1800 (white population, estimated), 3.77 at the 1957 peak of the baby boom, and 1.60 in 2024, a record low. (S18, S15, S22)
9. Fewer than half of American households owned their home in every census from 1890 to 1940, when the share hit a low of 43.6 percent; it reached 55.0 percent in 1950, 66.2 percent in 2000, and was 63.1 percent in the 2020 Census. (S3)
10. In 1950, 33.9 percent of women and 86.4 percent of men 16 and older were in the labor force; in 2025 the figures were 57.3 percent and 67.8 percent. (S27)
