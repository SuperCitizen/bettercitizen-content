# Review notes: The American Home

What each lesson's writer could not fully verify, and what they did with it, for the editor's review. Every lesson passed `pnpm validate-lesson` against lesson spec 1.0.0 before it was saved here.

## 01 · Was the frontier's free land really free?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,393 words; listening 2,761 words (about 20 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: free land as opportunity (Turner; Edwards, Friefeld and Wingo; Ferrie); a subsidy the well-placed captured (Gates, Danhof, Shannon, Land Office commissioner Sparks); dispossession at the foundation (Wolfe, Frymer, Carlos, Feir and Redish, Allen).

- Founders Online blocked automated fetching; Franklin's 1751 wording was checked against another copy of the text, and the source is listed without a URL.
- Wolfe (2006) is paraphrased ("a continuing structure, not a single event"); his famous line is not quoted because the article itself was not read. Gates, Shannon, Danhof and Allen are described through other scholars' accounts of them; Frymer from the publisher's description; Edwards, Friefeld and Wingo kept general.
- How many homesteaders succeeded is presented as disputed: the Park Service's "more than half proved up" against 1.6 million titles from 4 million claims (40 percent).
- The 4,000 to 5,500 Black patentees under the Southern Homestead Act are attributed to Keri Leigh Merritt (Aeon, 2016).
- The writer's own work, not a source: the Otoe-Missouria treaty total (about $405,000 over forty years, summed from Article 4 plus the $20,000 relocation payment); the line that farm-household work "rarely got reported" as an occupation (inferred from the 1910 census note); and the gloss of Turner's "competency" as "a modest independent living".
- Cut: the midnight-filing story (the Park Service calls it a legend); a 46 million descendants figure (the Park Service's 93 million is used); a "1.5 billion acres" figure.

## 02 · How did working families pay for a home in the industrial city?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,384 words; listening 2,776 words (about 20 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: housing reform, a floor under every home (Riis, Veiller, Breckinridge and Abbott); family strategy, the bottom rung of a ladder (Modell and Hareven; the Beitos drawing on Zunz and Simon; Groth).

- Several figures are secondhand through David and Linda Beito (2016) and attributed to them: Zunz's Detroit figure (35 percent of unskilled workers owned homes in 1900), Simon on Milwaukee, Breckinridge and Abbott on lodgers, James Ford's 1936 quotation ("quoted in Beito and Beito"), lodging shares for 1910 and 1940, and Kenneth Snowden on 1920s mortgages.
- Modell and Hareven (1973) is paywalled; the life-cycle claim is softened to "social historians such as…". Harris (1994): only its quarter-of-households-had-a-lodger figure (1900) is used. Zelizer's thesis is checked against the publisher's description; the Philadelphia children's-income figures come from BLS's Monthly Labor Review (2017).
- The Tenement House Act of 1901: provisions come from the Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2012 report ("one toilet per apartment"); the Beitos, citing Lubove, say "water closets on each floor". Check against the statute.
- BLS Report 991 (2006) says 19.0 percent of families owned a home in 1901, apparently the surveyed workers' families, which conflicts with the census's 46.7 percent; it was left out.
- The cold open infers Jacob Riis's presence at the raid from his "we"; "pounded" paraphrases his "thundering rap".
- The assessment's q3 explanation uses a 1920 farm ownership figure (58.1 percent) that is not in the lesson.

## 03 · How did the New Deal remake the American mortgage?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,397 words; listening 2,776 words (about 20 minutes, near the top of its band); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: the maps as a cause (Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder); the maps as a record (Fishback and coauthors; Hillier); the maps as one part of a system (Michney and Winling).

- Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder: the published article (AEJ: Economic Policy, 2021) is paywalled, so three details come from their August 2020 Chicago Fed working paper, which is cited separately; the published version could differ slightly.
- Fishback and coauthors (2024): read as the 2021 NBER working paper (the Greensboro and Peoria figures); journal details confirmed through Crossref.
- Hillier (2003): only what the Crossref abstract states. Winling and Michney (2021) is paywalled and not cited; the third perspective rests on Michney's Mapping Inequality essay.
- Kenneth Jackson's account of the HOLC as the pioneer of redlining follows Fishback and coauthors' description of the literature; the book itself was not read.
- The Tacoma area description comes from Mapping Inequality's script file, not the scanned original. FHA Underwriting Manual quotations come from FRASER's scan of the February 1938 edition.
- The 1920s loan example ($2,500 at 6 percent, $12.50 a month) is labelled as invented. Early HOLC loans' interest-only first three years is left out.

## 04 · What made the postwar American dream possible?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,393 words; listening 2,758 words (about 21 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: built by policy; built by circumstance; built on exclusion.

- Levittown's $7,990 price (1949) is cited to American Heritage (Boulton, 1993); other sources give $7,000 (1948) or $7,500. Monthly payments were left out because sources disagree.
- William Levitt's often-quoted line about solving the housing problem was left out: only weak secondary sources, with varying wording.
- The 1938 FHA Underwriting Manual is quoted as the 1961 Civil Rights Commission report quotes it, not from the manual itself.
- "The VA adopted similar rules" (1950) simplifies: the VA still guaranteed such loans but removed the lender's right to assign the foreclosed property to it.
- "American factories came through the war untouched by the fighting" has no fetched source behind it. Check or soften.
- The San Francisco Bay Area figures are an estimate the Commission reported, and the lesson says so. The common "less than 2 percent of FHA homes went to minorities" figure and a Mississippi statistic from Katznelson were dropped as unverifiable.
- Fetter's wartime rent-control paper is cited as NBER Working Paper 19604 (2013, revised 2016); its journal publication was not confirmed.
- Six books (Jackson, Rothstein, Katznelson, Coontz, Gordon, Sugrue) are paraphrased from their well-known theses, not quoted, and cited without URLs.

## 05 · Two paychecks, two crashes

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,392 words; listening 2,779 words (about 21 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: on two incomes, opportunity and choice (Goldin) and the two-income trap (Warren and Warren Tyagi); on 2008, lax private lending and securitization (FCIC majority, Mian and Sufi), government housing-finance policy (Wallison), and a global saving glut (Bernanke; the Hennessey, Holtz-Eakin and Thomas dissent).

- Monthly payments ($623 against $275 on a $40,000 loan) are the writer's own amortization arithmetic on Freddie Mac's verified weekly rates (18.63 percent, week of October 9, 1981; 7.33 percent, April 1971). The 1981 average ("above 16 percent") and 2003 average (5.83 percent) were computed from Freddie Mac's weekly file.
- Petaluma: facts verified from the vlex copy of the opinion, which is the URL cited (Justia refused automated fetches).
- The Two-Income Trap: argument checked against 2003–2004 reviews, not the book; nothing quoted. Cited "Basic Books, 2003 (paperback 2004)"; the 2003 edition's publisher is unconfirmed.
- Fischel, The Homevoter Hypothesis (2001): confirmed through library records and reviews; cited without a URL.
- FCIC report: only "about four million families" is used, because the PDF's numerals did not survive extraction. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's conservatorship is written as "September 2008" without a confirmed day.
- Each PerspectiveTabs "At stake, on this view" line is the writer's summary, not a quotation. Worth an editor's read.

## 06 · Is there really a housing shortage?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,393 words (just under the 2,400 ceiling, so any addition needs a cut); listening 2,448 words (about 18.5 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: land-use rules hold back supply; interest rates, investors and finance; incomes and inequality.

- Elena Patel's Boston Fed paper (November 6, 2026; vacancy averages of 10.7 and 13.2 percent, a 3.7 million swing) is a conference draft with an unfinished appendix; cited as a draft and attributed to her by name.
- Manuel Aalbers's book (Routledge, 2016) is confirmed only through publisher listings; paraphrased, cited without a URL.
- Left out as seen only secondhand: Brookings's 4.9 million and Realtor.com's 4.03 million shortage estimates. NLIHC's gap (7.1 or 7.2 million, by source) replaced by JCHS's own wording.
- FHFA's lock-in paper: its current page gives 1.33 million prevented sales and a 5.7 percent price effect, while the published `housing` pack quotes 1.7 million and 7 percent, probably from an older version. Only the 18.1 percent figure both share is used. **Corrected on November 22, 2026:** the published `housing` pack (lesson 1, its assessment and, on the spoken-lectures branch, its lecture) now gives 1.33 million sales between the second quarter of 2022 and the end of 2023, and 5.7 percent, as FHFA Working Paper 24-03 reads; pack version 1.4.0.
- The Department of Labor childcare release is cited at the address that resolved (`dol.gov/index.php/newsroom/releases/wb/wb20241119`).
- The NAHB regulation-cost figure (2026) is labelled as the builders' own count.

## 07 · What could policy realistically recreate today?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,387 words; listening 2,635 words (about 20 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: supply-side and zoning reform; demand-side subsidy and finance; tenant protection and public housing; family policy.

- It has two figures: the five-measures table and a table of eight levers with what each can do and costs. The second can come out if each lesson should have exactly one figure.
- Coontz: no review critiquing the book directly was found; her view is set against David Popenoe's family-decline thesis, confirmed through Arlie Hochschild's 1991 review. Popenoe's book title was not confirmed. Her self-reliance argument rests on a chapter title and her essays.
- Rothstein: Richard Walker's critique (Jacobin, June 2019) is confirmed from secondary summaries, not the article itself; Rothstein's reply from the open part of his response.
- Jackson: his thesis is taken from catalog descriptions and summaries, not the book. The debate is characterized from Fishback and coauthors (NBER) and reviews of Bruegmann (2005).
- Mast's "45 to 70" finding comes from the journal abstract as reported in search results; no URL is listed for it.
- Child poverty: only the "more than 5 million children" rise in 2022 is used, because Census reissued the supplemental poverty thresholds in July 2026 and revised rates will follow.
- Dettling and Kearney: effect sizes differ between versions of the paper, so only the direction is given.
- Cut as unconfirmed: the 1949 act's 810,000 public housing units; Austin rent figures; a CBPP voucher page. "One in four" comes from the JCHS 2026 report instead.
