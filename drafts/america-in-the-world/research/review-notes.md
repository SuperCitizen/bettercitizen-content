# Review notes: 250 Years of America in the World

What each lesson's writer could not fully verify, and what they did with it, for the editor's review. Every lesson passed `pnpm validate-lesson` against lesson spec 1.0.0 before it was saved here.

## 01 · How did thirteen colonies become a country in a world at war?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,398 words; listening 2,773 words (about 21.5 minutes, near the top of its band); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: the ideological reading (Bailyn); the economic reading (Beard, with Brown, McDonald and McGuire); slavery at the center (Morgan, Waldstreicher). Wilentz (2018) appears as the live dispute.

- Abigail Adams: only "remember the ladies" is quoted, as archives.gov and the Library of Congress print it; Founders Online refused automated fetches, so the original's capitalization is unchecked.
- Beard: only "essentially an economic document" is quoted, confirmed through the National Constitution Center's excerpt. The lecture paraphrases it without quotation marks.
- Bailyn, Morgan, Waldstreicher, Wilentz, McGuire, Brown and McDonald are paraphrased from catalogs, reviews and profiles, never quoted. Goldin and Lewis (1980) is named without a result.
- From the writer's own knowledge, without a fetched source: that most states limited the vote by property or taxes (Keyssar, The Right to Vote, 2000), and that "with one partial exception" (New Jersey) the new states did not give women the vote. Check both.
- The 718 soldiers of 1789 and other census-chapter values come from the dossier's scanned tables, which the writer could not read directly.
- Dropped rather than softened: Dunmore's Proclamation, Black Loyalist evacuation numbers, colonial export values, and Becker's "who should rule at home".
- `sovereignty` is listed in concepts but not linked in the text.

## 02 · How did the young republic grow so fast, and who paid for it?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,389 words; listening 2,774 words (about 21 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: on expansion, national vision (Turner, Haynes) and conquest (Limerick, Ostler); on slavery and growth, the new history of capitalism (Baptist, Beckert) and the economic historians' critique (Olmstead and Rhode, Olmstead and Rousseau).

- Livingston's April 11, 1803 report of Talleyrand's offer comes from the Discovering Lewis & Clark transcription (citing American State Papers, Foreign Relations, vol. 2, p. 552); Founders Online refused automated requests.
- Jefferson's "one single spot… It is New Orleans" is quoted as the Office of the Historian quotes it, with no date or recipient.
- Baptist's "driving force" sentence is as Pritchett's review quotes it (p. 413); Baptist and Beckert are otherwise checked through reviews. Olmstead and Rhode (2018) is paywalled; its points come from Olmstead's 2015 review. No percentage is given for the US share of British cotton imports.
- Haynes, Limerick and Turner are checked through reviews, blurbs and an excerpt. The 1845 "Annexation" essay is "usually credited to" O'Sullivan.
- Removal toll: Ostler's estimates, attributed (about 88,000 removed, 12,000 to 17,000 dead); the House history page says "roughly 100,000" removed.
- Erie Canal cost left out (sources give $7.1 or $7.5 million). Railroad mileage (about 3,000 miles in 1840, 30,000 in 1860) from Rodrigue's Geography of Transport Systems, drawing on Rand McNally (1898).
- Computed by the writer from census tables: "about seven in ten" foreign-born in 1860 were Irish- or German-born; "one American in eight" was enslaved in 1860.
- Inference, not documented parcel by parcel: the 1850 railroad land grant was "much of it acquired from Native nations".

## 03 · What did the Civil War settle?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,396 words (at the ceiling); listening 2,778 words (about 22 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: on why Britain stayed out, the battlefield decided it (Office of the Historian; Howard Jones), slavery made the South hard to back (Office of the Historian; Don Doyle) and Britain's own interests ruled (Frank Owsley); on judging Reconstruction, the Dunning School (described, then judged in the National Park Service's words as "challenged and often discredited"), Du Bois's Black Reconstruction and Foner's synthesis. The Lost Cause is treated as a settled finding, from the secession declarations themselves.

- The amendments' constitution.congress.gov URLs refused automated fetches; their text and dates were checked against archives.gov's transcription.
- Beckert's "77 percent of 800 million pounds" was read in a Portside reprint of his Atlantic essay (December 12, 2014); cited without a URL.
- Jones, Doyle, Owsley and Dew: confirmed to exist, not read; arguments from publisher pages and reviews. The usual "Antietam stopped intervention" claim is left out, following Jones. Owsley is cited only for diplomatic history (he was also a Southern Agrarian whose other writing fed the Lost Cause).
- Du Bois: chapter titles and "a general strike against the slave system" checked against a scan of the 1935 text; that his last chapter indicts the Dunning historians rests on the Park Service study. Foner described in the writer's own summary.
- Reported secondhand from Ransom's 2001 EH.Net entry and attributed to him: 625,000 deaths, Goldin and Lewis's $6.6 billion, the close to $3 billion value of enslaved people, white Southern income falling from $125 (1857) to about $80 (1879).
- Robert Smalls: no passenger count (the Park Service says 16 freedom seekers; the House biography, a crew of eight plus women and children).
- Cut: a challenge question on birthright citizenship (its 2026 legal status unconfirmed), the Cornerstone Speech, Gladstone's 1862 speech, Lincoln's letter to the Manchester workers.

## 04 · How did America become the world's industrial giant?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,391 words; listening 2,786 words (about 21.5 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives, in two tab sets: on the tariff, builder (Chang, O'Rourke, Head) and burden (Irwin); on the industrialists, robber barons (Josephson) and industrial statesmen (Nevins, Chandler).

- "By 1914 European powers ruled almost all of Africa, Ethiopia and Liberia excepted" is cited to Britannica without a URL; the writer saw its wording only in a search excerpt.
- The army (154,914 in 1913), defense (about 0.7 percent of GDP) and federal spending (about 2.5 percent in 1900) figures are the dossier's estimates from scanned census tables, and the text calls them rough estimates.
- "Around 40 percent" duties on imported capital goods around 1890 is J. Bradford DeLong's calculation as Irwin reports it; DeLong's paper was not read.
- Ha-Joon Chang's "literally the most heavily protected economy in the world" is from his History & Policy essay, not checked against the book.
- Read only in abstract or catalog: Head (1994), Wright (1990), David and Wright (1997), O'Rourke (1997). Chandler is paraphrased from David Landes's review; Nevins and Josephson from reference entries; Richard White's Railroaded from the publisher's description.
- Standard Oil: the "90 per cent" charge is quoted from the government's suit as recited in the 1911 opinion.
- Cut as unverifiable: Bairoch's 1900 figure, railroad mileage, rebate claims against Standard Oil, and Anti-Imperialist League details.

## 05 · How did America become the world's creditor, and why did it turn inward?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,394 words (at the ceiling); listening 2,792 words (about 21 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: on the Depression's causes, monetary contraction (Friedman and Schwartz, Bernanke), the gold standard (Eichengreen, Eichengreen and Sachs, Kindleberger) and spending and debt (Temin, Fisher, Bernanke 1983); on the New Deal, recovery and reform (Romer, Fishback, Darby) and prolonged the slump (Cole and Ohanian). Social Security's exclusion of farm and domestic workers is presented as a debate in prose (Lieberman, Katznelson, Poole against Davies and Derthick, DeWitt), with the shared facts: 65 percent of Black and 27 percent of white workers were in the excluded jobs, yet 74 percent of excluded workers were white.

- DeWitt (2010) was read through a Wayback Machine copy of the ssa.gov page, which is the URL cited. Poole (2006) is credited only with arguing that race shaped the exclusions, as DeWitt groups her; her book was not checked directly.
- Not read in full, described from catalogs, abstracts and reviews: Friedman and Schwartz, Golden Fetters, Temin, Kindleberger, Lieberman, Katznelson, Davies and Derthick, E. Cary Brown. Kindleberger's and Brown's famous lines are not quoted. Cole and Ohanian's "about 60 percent" figure was left out (working paper only).
- Debtor-to-creditor figures ($3.5 billion abroad against $7.2 billion foreign-held in 1914; $7.0 billion against $3.3 billion in 1919, private holdings) were read by eye and OCR from the scanned Historical Statistics Series U 26–39; the lesson calls the pre-1919 values rough.
- Bairoch's 1928 figure (39.3 percent) is from a secondary site and labelled so. The 1938 defense share is "an estimated 1.2 percent".
- Stock exchange closure, July 31 to December 12, 1914, from an interview summarizing Silber's book (other sources give December 11, or November 28 for bonds). No total for the 1924 quota ceiling (sources give 186,437 or about 165,000).
- The 1890 census "taken before the largest arrivals from southern and eastern Europe" is the writer's inference.
- Not covered: Mexican repatriation in the 1930s, for lack of a verified source. Worth adding.
- Numbers from outside the dossier: real GDP down 26 percent 1929–33 (BEA), unemployment by year (Census HS-29), about 9,000 bank suspensions (FDIC), money supply down nearly 30 percent (Federal Reserve History), new permanent residents by year (DHS 2023 Yearbook).
- For the dossier: Maddison's original sources for 1913–1940 are Maddison (1995) for the US and Feinstein (1972) for the UK.

## 06 · How did America come to lead the postwar world?

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,390 words and listening 2,792 words, both just under their ceilings, so any addition needs a cut; 5 assessment questions. Perspectives, in two tab sets: on the Marshall Plan, generosity and strategy (De Long and Eichengreen, Hogan) and an overstated effect (Milward, Crafts); on the Cold War's origins, orthodox (Schlesinger, Gaddis 1997), revisionist (Williams) and post-revisionist (Gaddis 1972, Leffler).

- Henry Luce's Life editorial (February 17, 1941): two short fragments are quoted, matched across three reproductions; the Life page itself could not be read.
- "Half the world's output in 1945" appears only as a common misconception, against Maddison's about 21 percent (1940) and 27 percent (1950).
- Milward is described only as Crafts's survey and the publisher describe him; the common line that recovery was under way before the aid was dropped as unconfirmed. Leffler, Gaddis 1997, Williams and Katznelson are characterized from publisher pages and reviews. Schlesinger 1967 and Gaddis 1983 are confirmed bibliographically only.
- Kennan's "X" article is dated "July 1947" from its standard citation; history.state.gov gives only the year.
- Marshall Plan total: $13.3 billion appropriated, from archives.gov (other sources give $12 to $13 billion).
- Stated without a cited source: that the 48 states saw no invasion or bombing campaign, that America entered the war more than two years after it began in Europe, and that Bracero contracts were temporary.

Perspectives: the classical narrative (Branch; Cha-Jua and Lang); the long civil rights movement (Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, with Korstad's "civil rights unionism"); the conservative and libertarian critique (Murray, Friedman, Sowell).

The brief names only "the Secretary of State"; the lesson names Acheson, who held the office then.
- Johnson's 1965 signing remarks read "This bill that we will sign today is not a revolutionary bill" in both the American Presidency Project and the LBJ Library. **Corrected on November 22, 2026:** the published `immigration` pack (lesson 1 and, on the spoken-lectures branch, its lecture) now quotes "This bill that we will sign today", checked against the American Presidency Project; pack version 1.4.0.
- The Voting Rights Act is described as barring literacy tests "in the places it covered" (archives.gov says "outlawed"; in 1965 it suspended them only in covered jurisdictions).
- Cut as unreachable: Mississippi Black registration rising from 6.7 to 59.8 percent, the Vietnam troop peak of about 543,000, and the Kerner Commission quotation.
- Hall is paraphrased from an excerpt and listings, not the full article. Branch, Murray, Friedman, Sowell, Cha-Jua and Lang, and Gavin Wright are paraphrased from reviews and listings, cited without URLs.

Perspectives: on the Great Inflation, the monetary explanation (Bryan, Meltzer, Barsky and Kilian) and the supply-shock explanation (Blinder and Rudd); on the decade's meaning, an extraordinary era ended (Levinson, Gordon, Abramovitz) and choices ended it (Stein, Meltzer).

- 1970's 4.7 percent foreign-born as the smallest share since 1850 rests on the dossier's table, which the writer did not open.

- The figure uses only IMF purchasing-power shares of world output (no Maddison), and China's market-rate share is a separate, labelled sentence. - Autor, Dorn and Hanson's findings were checked against the papers themselves.
- Quoted from copies, not the publishers' pages, and cited without URLs: Huntington (a course transcript), Fukuyama (an OCR scan in the Thatcher archive), Campbell and Ratner (a USC-hosted copy). Winston's 1998 deregulation figures are from a third-party copy.
- Kotkin, Schweizer and Rodrik: headline theses only, from publisher descriptions and reviews.

Validator: 0 errors, 0 warnings. Reading 2,384 words; listening 2,756 words (about 21 minutes); 5 assessment questions. Perspectives: relative decline (National Intelligence Council 2008; Zakaria); enduring advantages (Beckley; Brooks and Wohlforth); divided at home (Haass; Levitsky and Ziblatt; Levin). Every policy example is paired across parties.

- Haass: the line "The biggest threat to the United States comes not from abroad but from within" turned out to be the Council on Foreign Relations' summary of his book, not his words; it is paraphrased without quotation marks in both versions.
- Stanford's AI Index: the "89 percent drop since 2017" in AI scholars moving to the US comes from a HAI takeaways article, paraphrased with attribution. Counts of notable AI models were left out because Stanford's own pages disagree.
- Beckley, Levitsky and Ziblatt, and Brooks and Wohlforth are paraphrased from reviews and summaries, not quoted.
- The February 2024 Senate vote (49 to 50) was on cloture on H.R. 815; calling it a bipartisan border deal rests on general knowledge and Senate floor logs found in search.
- "The CHIPS Act subsidizes chipmaking at home" is stated as common knowledge; the roll calls confirm only the votes.
- The Fed's broad dollar index averaging about 91 in 2008 and 123 in 2025 is the writer's own averaging of FRED data, labelled as such.
- Pew's views-of-China figures change method (phone to online panel), and the lesson says they are not strictly comparable.
- The closing refers to lessons 6, 8 and 9 from the brief's description of them, written before those lessons existed. Read the series end to end for continuity.
- Cut as unverified or paywalled: Tooze's Crashed on swap lines, Huntington's 1988 "waves of declinism", Paul Kennedy, and Christopher Layne (2012).
