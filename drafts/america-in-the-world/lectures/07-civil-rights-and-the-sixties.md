December 1952. The Justice Department files a brief in a lawsuit about the public schools of Topeka, Kansas.

A school case. And inside it, a letter from the secretary of state, Dean Acheson. [beat] Listen for where his sentence ends.

"The segregation of school children on a racial basis is one of the practices in the United States that has been singled out for hostile foreign comment in the United Nations and elsewhere."

[pause]

The United Nations, and elsewhere. A dispute over which school a Black child could attend had become a question about America's standing in the world.

Within thirteen years, Congress had outlawed segregation in public places. It had protected the Black vote. And it had scrapped the immigration quotas of 1924, which ranked newcomers by birthplace.

So why did so much change at once? [beat] And how much of the push came from abroad?

By the end, you will be able to explain how legal segregation fell, and why the world cared. Four stops.

First, how protest and law broke Jim Crow.

Second, why the world's opinion mattered in Washington.

Third, a quiet immigration law that changed who came.

And last, the decade's two wars, one on poverty and one in Vietnam, and the three ways historians read it all.

## How segregation fell

Start in the South, at midcentury. Jim Crow was still the law. Statutes separated Black and white Americans in schools, buses and restaurants, and poll taxes and literacy tests kept Black Southerners from voting.

It fell to two pressures, working together.

The first came from the courts. On May 17, 1954, in Brown versus Board of Education, a unanimous Supreme Court ruled on public schools. Its key sentence was short. "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal."

[pause]

Inherently unequal. But a ruling is not a result. Much of the white South resisted it.

The second pressure came from the people segregation was aimed at.

On December 1, 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, Rosa Parks was arrested. She had refused to give up her bus seat to a white passenger.

Four days later, 90 percent of Montgomery's Black residents stayed off the buses. A carpool of about 300 cars kept the boycott going for thirteen months, led by a young minister named Martin Luther King Jr.

Here is the part people forget. [beat] The boycott ended only after federal courts struck down bus segregation, in a case called Browder versus Gayle.

Ending the boycott, King said the community had come to see that "it is more honorable to walk in dignity than ride in humiliation."

[pause]

Walk in dignity. Montgomery ran its own carpools. And it still needed federal judges.

> Organized protest made the crisis. Federal power made the rule.

That pairing came back in the 1960s.

In 1964, Southern senators, led by Richard Russell of Georgia, held the civil rights bill in a filibuster. A filibuster is an attempt to talk a bill to death. This one ran for 60 working days.

On June 10, the Senate voted to end debate, 71 to 29, with 44 Democrats and 27 Republicans voting yes. It was the first time the Senate had ever cut off debate on a civil rights bill.

Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act on July 2. It outlawed segregation in businesses such as theaters, restaurants and hotels, and banned discrimination in employment.

Then the vote itself. On March 7, 1965, state troopers attacked peaceful marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama.

On August 6, Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act. It did three things. [beat] It barred literacy tests in the places it covered. [beat] It sent federal examiners to register voters. [beat] And it made those places get federal approval before they changed their voting rules. That approval is called preclearance.

By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had been registered. One-third were registered by federal examiners.

Put another way, the movement made the crisis, and Washington supplied the force.

Let's test preclearance. It is 1966. A county covered by the act moves its only polling place far from its Black neighborhoods. What had to happen before that move could take effect? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Federal approval. [beat] Under preclearance, the change could not take effect until federal officials approved it. The approval came first, not after an election.

## The world was watching

That is how segregation fell at home. Now the view from abroad.

Go back to the brief from 1952. In its own voice, it told the Court this.

"Racial discrimination furnishes grist for the Communist propaganda mills, and it raises doubts even among friendly nations as to the intensity of our devotion to the democratic faith."

[pause]

Grist for the propaganda mills. That filing was an amicus brief, from someone not a party to the case: the United States government.

> In 1952, the government told the Court segregation hurt America abroad.

Why did that matter so much? Because of the Cold War. The United States and the Soviet Union were competing for the loyalty of a changing world.

Between 1945 and 1960, three dozen new states in Asia and Africa won autonomy or independence. The United Nations had 35 members in 1946. By 1970, it had 127.

In 1957, mobs in Little Rock defied a court order to admit Black students to Central High School. President Dwight Eisenhower sent troops, and told the nation why it mattered beyond Arkansas.

"Our enemies are gloating over this incident and using it everywhere to misrepresent our whole nation."

[pause]

The legal historian Mary Dudziak built this evidence into an argument, in a book called Cold War Civil Rights. She argues that foreign policy helped push Washington toward reform. American racism damaged the country's image in Africa, Asia and Latin America, just when Washington wanted their allegiance.

But she argues that the pressure cut both ways. It often rewarded changes to the image over changes in substance. And anticommunism, in the McCarthy era, narrowed what activists could safely demand.

> Dudziak's argument: the Cold War pushed reform, and it also narrowed it.

How much the world's gaze changed outcomes, against the movement's own pressure, is something historians still weigh.

One more thing made the world's opinion matter. [beat] America's lead was narrowing.

By the Maddison Project's estimates, American output per person kept rising. Yet America's share of world output fell. It was 27.4 percent in 1950, and 22.4 percent in 1970. A rebuilt Western Europe, among others, was growing faster.

[pause]

The lead from the ruin of rivals was wearing off.

## A quiet law that changed who came

Now the third stop. It is October 3, 1965, on Liberty Island in New York Harbor. Johnson is signing amendments to the Immigration and Nationality Act.

"This bill that we will sign today is not a revolutionary bill. It does not affect the lives of millions."

[pause]

Not revolutionary. He also called the national-origins quotas it replaced "un-American in the highest sense."

Here is what the law did. It wrote a rule that would have fit in the civil rights acts. No person, it said, "shall receive any preference or priority or be discriminated against in the issuance of an immigrant visa because of his race, sex, nationality, place of birth, or place of residence."

[beat]

Place of birth. The quotas that had ranked people by birthplace ended in 1968.

> The 1965 law stopped choosing immigrants by birthplace.

What took their place? Ceilings. [beat] A ceiling of 170,000 visas a year from outside the Western Hemisphere. [beat] A limit of 20,000 for any one country. [beat] And a first ceiling of 120,000 on the Western Hemisphere.

Within those ceilings, about three-quarters of the visas were set aside for relatives. And 6 percent went to refugees, defined in Cold War terms. A refugee was someone fleeing "any Communist or Communist-dominated country or area," or the Middle East.

And the visas went in the order the petitions were filed.

Put another way, where you were born no longer set your place in line. [beat] When you filed did.

So did Johnson's prediction hold? [beat] It did not.

In 1970, the share of Americans born abroad hit 4.7 percent, its lowest since counting began in 1850. Then it climbed, and the newcomers came from new places.

In 1960, among the foreign-born who reported a birthplace, 75 percent had been born in Europe. By 1990, Europe's share was down to 22.9 percent. Latin America's share was 44.3 percent. And Asia's was 26.3 percent.

[pause]

Now, pull something back from earlier. A while ago I gave you a pattern for how segregation fell. It had two parts. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Organized protest made the crisis. [beat] Federal power made the rule. The pull between what people do for themselves and what government does for them runs through the next stop too.

## Two wars, one budget

On January 8, 1964, Johnson told Congress this.

"This administration today, here and now, declares unconditional war on poverty in America."

That May, he named the larger goal. "The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time."

[pause]

The Great Society included Medicare, health insurance for people 65 and older, and Medicaid, coverage for people with limited incomes. Federal spending grew, from 15.3 percent of GDP in 1950 to 18.2 percent in 1973.

And poverty fell fast in those boom years. The official poverty rate was 22.4 percent in 1959. [beat] It was 19.0 percent in 1964. [beat] And it was 12.1 percent in 1969.

For Black Americans, the rate fell from 55.1 percent to 32.2 percent between 1959 and 1969. That was still more than two and a half times the national rate.

One caution, because it matters later. The official measure counts cash income before taxes. It leaves out noncash help, such as Medicaid and food stamps.

Now the turn. [beat] The same government was fighting a war.

On August 7, 1964, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, unanimously in the House and with two votes against in the Senate. It became the legal basis for the Vietnam War.

In 1968, national defense took 9.1 percent of GDP. The National Archives counts 58,220 American military dead. Vietnamese losses ran far higher.

> The war on poverty and the war in Vietnam drew on one budget.

They drew on one stock of public trust, too.

On April 4, 1967, at Riverside Church in New York, King argued that the war was draining resources needed to fight poverty at home. The NAACP and leading newspapers criticized him for tying the two causes together.

A year later to the day, King was assassinated in Memphis.

[pause]

Johnson's coalition began to split. It split over the war, over riots in Northern cities, and over how far government should go.

Let's test King's claim the way a historian would. Which of three findings, if true, would most directly support it?

One. Poverty fell between 1964 and 1969. [beat] Two. Defense reached 9.1 percent of GDP in 1968. [beat] Three. Budget records showing anti-poverty funds cut to pay for the war.

[long pause]

Number three. [beat] A claim about a trade-off needs evidence of the trade-off. The size of war spending shows the war was costly. It does not show what the war displaced. And the fall in poverty during a boom cuts against King's claim, not for it.

## Three readings, and what to keep

So what did the decade settle? Segregation by law ended. The immigration law stopped sorting people by birthplace. And measured poverty fell sharply, then stalled.

Now sort the decade's advantages into the series' three kinds.

Geography and timing. [beat] A long boom paid for both wars, for a while. And an ocean kept the fighting far away.

Choices and policy. [beat] The civil rights laws, the 1965 immigration law, Medicare, and escalation in Vietnam were all choices. The economic historian Gavin Wright argues that civil rights brought Black Southerners large gains, without making white Southerners poorer.

The troubles of other nations. [beat] The lead from the ruin of rivals was fading. And refugees from communist countries gained a door of their own.

Historians disagree about the Cold War itself. Dudziak counts the Soviet rivalry as pressure that helped end segregation. The historian Jacquelyn Dowd Hall counts its anticommunism as a force that broke the movement's labor wing, in the late 1940s.

[pause]

Which brings us to the readings. They answer one question. What was the civil rights movement, and what counts as its success? Here is each reading, as its strongest advocates put it.

First, the classical narrative. It lives in much public memory, and in narrative histories of the King years, such as Taylor Branch's Parting the Waters. The historians Sundiata Keita Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang defend the years from 1954 to 1965 as a distinct era.

It protects credit and cause. It argues that the tactics and alliances of those years explain why Jim Crow fell when it did. A movement with no fixed beginning or end, it argues, blurs the differences between eras.

It points to the record. Brown. Montgomery. A vote to end the filibuster, won by both parties. And a quarter of a million new voters.

> The classical narrative: protest and landmark law ended Jim Crow in a decade.

Second, the long civil rights movement. Its best-known voice is Jacquelyn Dowd Hall. It protects the movement's full agenda: jobs, wages, housing and union rights, alongside the ballot and the lunch counter.

Hall argues that the movement took root in the labor and left politics of the late 1930s, and grew in the 1940s as what Robert Korstad called civil rights unionism. It reached far beyond the South, and it did not end in 1965. A short story, she argues, lets the country celebrate a finished victory, and set aside the economic goals that remained.

It points to the late 1940s, when an anticommunist drive helped break the alliance of Black workers and unions. And it points to King's last campaigns, against poverty and against the war.

> The long civil rights movement: rooted in the 1930s, about jobs too.

Third, the conservative and libertarian critique. Writers such as Charles Murray, Milton Friedman and Thomas Sowell hold it, in different ways. It protects equal treatment under law, and the effort of the poor. It honors the laws of 1964 and 1965, and quarrels with parts of the Great Society's design.

Murray argues that anti-poverty policy from the mid-1960s made it sensible, in the short run, for poor young people not to work or marry. Friedman had argued for giving the poor cash, through a negative income tax, instead of a patchwork of agencies. Sowell argues that Black incomes were rising before 1964, through education and migration.

It points to the official poverty rate. That rate was already falling before most of the Great Society was law. It reached 11.1 percent in 1973, and did not drop below that again until 2019.

> The conservative and libertarian critique: parts of the anti-poverty design backfired.

Remember, though: the official rate leaves out noncash help, such as Medicaid and food stamps, so the same numbers can be read both ways.

[pause]

Three readings. You may already lean toward one. So take the reading you find least convincing, and state the best case for it, the way the people who hold it would.

[pause 5s]

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Organized protest made the crisis. Federal power made the rule. Montgomery ended with a court ruling, and the new voters of 1965 came with federal examiners.

[beat]

Two. In 1952, the government told the Court segregation hurt America abroad. And in Dudziak's argument, the Cold War pushed reform, and also narrowed it.

[beat]

Three. The 1965 law stopped choosing immigrants by birthplace. By 1990, most foreign-born Americans had been born in Latin America or Asia.

[beat]

Four. The war on poverty and the war in Vietnam drew on one budget, and one public's trust. Poverty fell fast. The war cost 58,220 American lives, and it split Johnson's coalition.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Which reading of the decade is right? The evidence cannot settle it, because the readings disagree about what counts as the movement, and what counts as success.

The decade ended with its largest commitments still being paid for. Equal citizenship at home. And a war abroad.

The next lecture follows the bill: the end of the gold-linked dollar in 1971, oil shocks in 1973 and 1979, and rivals in Europe and Japan. How much of that turn began in the 1960s, and how much arrived from abroad?

For now, go back to that brief, in December 1952. A case about schools in Topeka, carrying a letter about the United Nations. [beat] You know now why the secretary of state cared. And you know how much changed, in public places, at the ballot box and at the border, in the thirteen years that followed.
