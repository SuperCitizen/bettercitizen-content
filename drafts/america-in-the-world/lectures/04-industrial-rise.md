Promontory, Utah. Monday, May 10, 1869.

Two railroads have just been joined into one line across the continent. At 12:47 that afternoon, a Western Union telegrapher sends one word down the wire, letter by letter. [beat] Done.

Think about who laid that track. The Union Pacific had employed more than 8,000 Irish, German and Italian immigrants. The Central Pacific's workforce included over 10,000 Chinese laborers.

[pause]

Thirteen years later, Congress barred Chinese laborers from entering the country.

Hold both halves of that picture. A continent joined by immigrant hands. [beat] And a door shut on some of those hands.

In the decades after that telegram, the United States became the largest industrial economy in the world. By the end of this lecture, you will be able to say how. And you will be able to sort its advantages into three kinds: geography and timing, choices and policy, and the troubles of other nations.

We will take it in four steps. First, how a continent became one market, and the fight over the tariff. Second, the people who did the work, and the people shut out. Third, the giant companies and the great strikes. And last, the turn outward, in 1898.

## One market

Start with where America stood. The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated each country's share of the world's manufactured goods.

In 1860, the United States made 7.2 percent of them. Britain made 19.9 percent.

By 1913, the American share was about a third. [beat] That was more than Britain and Germany put together.

> By 1913, America made about a third of the world's manufactured goods.

Estimates of total output tell the same story, more gently.

Output per person shows how close the race was. In 1870, the average American produced about 82 percent as much as the average Briton. By 1913, the average American produced about 23 percent more.

But there was no single day of overtaking. America first edged ahead in 1880. It fell behind again in several years after that. [beat] It stayed ahead for good only from 1899.

[pause]

So why here? Three reasons.

First, the ground. Between 1870 and 1910, the United States became the world's leading producer of minerals.

Second, size. The population grew from 38.6 million in 1870 to about 97 million in 1913. And all of it sat inside one market.

That one market was not a gift of geography. It was a choice, made in 1787. The Constitution said no state could tax goods coming in or going out without the consent of Congress. [beat] A Pittsburgh mill met no customs house at the Ohio line.

Third, federal help, the part the story of self-made men leaves out. The Pacific Railway Act of 1862 gave the railroad builders public land, and federal bonds of 16,000 dollars a mile.

In all, Congress granted 174 million acres of public land for four transcontinental railroads. Put another way, these private companies were built on public land and public credit.

Let's test the single market. Imagine a country in 1880. It has rich coal and iron, a large population, and a high tariff against foreign goods. But each of its provinces charges duties on goods from the others.

Which American advantage does it lack? Take a moment.

[long pause]

One national market. [beat] It has the resources, the people and the outer wall. What it lacks is free trade inside the wall.

Now the other great policy: the protective tariff. A tariff is a tax on imports, meant to shelter home industry. Around 1890, by one calculation that the economist Douglas Irwin reports, duties on imported capital goods averaged around 40 percent.

Did that wall build American industry? Start on ground nobody owns. Tariffs were high, and the economy grew fast. The argument is over whether the first caused the second.

Each of two readings gets the same four things: who holds it, what it protects, its case, and its evidence.

The first reading is the tariff as builder. It is held by the economist Ha-Joon Chang, in a book called Kicking Away the Ladder, from 2002. It protects young industries, in an argument that goes back to Alexander Hamilton.

Its case is that a young industry needs time behind a wall, to learn what its foreign rivals already know. Chang writes that from the Civil War to the Second World War, the United States "was literally the most heavily protected economy in the world." [beat] And it became the richest.

Its evidence: Kevin O'Rourke found that tariffs were positively correlated with growth across ten countries, from 1875 to 1914. And Irwin's own study found that the McKinley tariff of 1890 brought tinplate making to America about a decade sooner.

> The tariff as builder: a wall buys young industries time to learn.

The second reading is the tariff as burden. It is held by the Dartmouth economist Douglas Irwin, and by economists who share his doubts. It protects the buyers, everyone who paid more for goods and machines because of the wall.

Its case is that tariffs and growth arriving together do not prove that one caused the other. Growth hinged more on population and investment than on productivity. And by raising the price of imported machinery, the tariff may have slowed investment.

Its evidence: productivity grew fastest in transportation, utilities and services, which faced little foreign competition. And Irwin's tinplate study concludes that protection "does not pass a cost-benefit test."

> The tariff as burden: growth came from people and investment, not the wall.

[pause]

Notice that both readings cite the same studies. So what does the evidence settle? It settles that a tariff can give an industry a head start. It cannot settle whether the head start was worth the higher prices everyone else paid.

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## The people

That is the market. Now the people.

In 1870, 14.4 percent of the country's people had been born abroad. In 1890 the share peaked, at 14.8 percent. In 1910 it was still 14.7 percent. [beat] Put another way, about one resident in seven.

In 1892, a federal immigration station opened on Ellis Island, in New York harbor. The first person processed there was Annie Moore, of Ireland. More than 12 million immigrants passed through it.

The economists Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson count about 55 million Europeans who left for the New World between 1850 and 1914. Why did they go?

The pull was American wages and land. The push was hardship at home.

And here is the turn. [beat] Some of that hardship was made in America.

Falling transport costs let cheap grain from the prairies and elsewhere flood into Europe. The economic historian Kevin O'Rourke found that it lowered land rents throughout Europe, and real wages outside Britain.

So cheap grain helped push Europeans off the land. [beat] And many of them came to America.

Then a third force: law. For Europeans, the door stood open. Congress set no quotas on them until the 1920s.

For the Chinese, the door closed. The Chinese Exclusion Act of May 6, 1882, banned Chinese laborers for ten years. It barred the courts from making Chinese immigrants citizens. And it lasted, in one form or another, until 1943.

The National Archives notes that, for the first time, federal law barred an ethnic group of workers from entering.

> Europe's hardship pushed, American wages pulled, and American law chose.

Others were already inside, and still left out. Under the Dawes Act of 1887, land that tribes held in common was broken into individual allotments, and Native communities lost vast acreage.

And in 1896, in a case called Plessy versus Ferguson, the Supreme Court upheld a Louisiana law. It required "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races." [pause] That ruling left the South's Jim Crow laws in place for more than half a century.

Let's put push, pull and law to work. A farming family in Sweden, in 1887, sells its land after grain prices collapse. It buys passage, and it settles in Minnesota.

What is the push? What is the pull? And what policy let them in? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The push is the fall in grain prices, which cheap American grain helped cause. [beat] The pull is land and wages. [beat] And the policy is the open door: no quota on Europeans, and no exclusion law aimed at Swedes.

## Giants and strikers

Andrew Carnegie came from Dunfermline, Scotland, in 1848. He worked as a bobbin boy, for a dollar twenty a week. In 1875 he built a steel plant near Pittsburgh. And in 1901 he sold his company to J. P. Morgan, for 480 million dollars.

John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil grew so large that the federal government's suit charged it with "controlling 90 per cent of the business of producing, shipping, refining, and selling petroleum."

What should a government do about giants like these? Washington's answers were new, and weak.

In 1890 came the Sherman Act, the start of antitrust law. It defined none of its terms. And in 1895, the Supreme Court held that a company refining about 98 percent of the country's sugar had not broken it.

In the great strikes, government acted, and it sided with the owners.

First, 1877. A 10 percent wage cut on the Baltimore and Ohio set off a railroad strike. By some estimates it drew 100,000 workers. Federal troops helped end it, and dozens died.

[beat]

Then 1892, at Homestead. Carnegie's manager, Henry Frick, locked out 3,800 workers and brought in about 300 Pinkerton guards. Nearly a dozen people died. The governor sent 8,500 troops, and the union was broken.

[beat]

And 1894. The American Railway Union refused to handle Pullman cars. A federal injunction, and thousands of marshals and Army troops, ended the boycott. Dozens died.

The Supreme Court upheld the jailing of the union's leaders, in a case called In re Debs. Listen for the first six words.

"The entire strength of the nation may be used to enforce in any part of the land the full and free exercise of all national powers."

[pause]

The entire strength of the nation.

Compare Germany. It insured workers against sickness in 1883, against accidents in 1884, and against old age and disability in 1889. Washington offered industrial workers nothing of the kind. By rough estimates, federal spending was about 2.5 percent of the economy in 1900.

> Government regulated the giants lightly, and sided with owners against strikers.

How should we read the giants themselves? Two readings again.

The first is the robber barons. It is held by the writer Matthew Josephson, whose book The Robber Barons, from 1934, gave the era its lasting label, and by historians who stress power and corruption. It protects competition, and a government that serves the public.

Its case is that the great fortunes were taken more than made, through bribery, monopoly, and the crushing of rivals and workers.

Its evidence: in 1867, Oakes Ames gave shares in Crédit Mobilier, the company building the Union Pacific, to two senators and nine representatives. Standard Oil's grip was such that the Supreme Court upheld its breakup in 1911. And the defeat at Homestead brought twelve-hour days, wage cuts, and 500 lost jobs.

> The robber-baron reading: the great fortunes were taken more than made.

The second is the industrial statesmen. It is held by the historian Allan Nevins, whose biography of Rockefeller appeared in 1940. It is held too by the business historian Alfred Chandler, whose book The Visible Hand won the Pulitzer Prize. It protects the credit due to those who organized the new economy.

Its case is that the giant firm was a new tool. Chandler traced how big business, beginning with the railroads, built hierarchies of professional managers. Modern enterprise, he argued, took over wherever that kind of coordination did better than the market.

Its evidence is the output itself: nearly a third of the world's manufacturing by 1913. And Carnegie rose from bobbin boy to steel master, then gave away some 350 million dollars.

> The industrial-statesman reading: the giant firm was a new tool.

[pause]

Try both readings on one firm. A meatpacker builds refrigerated rail cars and cold-storage warehouses. It sells beef more cheaply in every city. And it drives thousands of local butchers out of business.

How would each reading describe it? Think it through.

[long pause]

The robber-baron reading sees power: a combination that destroyed small rivals, and could later raise prices. [beat] The industrial-statesman reading sees organization: mass production joined to national distribution, at costs small butchers could not match.

The facts are the same. The dispute is over who captured the gains.

You have now heard two arguments, one about the tariff and one about the giants. Take the side you find less convincing, in either one. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## The turn outward

That is the economy at home. Now the world.

Europe's empires were at their height. By 1914, European powers ruled almost all of Africa. Ethiopia and Liberia were the exceptions. In China, the European powers and Japan guarded their spheres of influence and trading privileges.

Americans began to look outward too. In 1890, the naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that the economy would soon produce more than the country could absorb. So the United States should seek markets abroad, guarded by a battleship navy and bases.

In 1893, the historian Frederick Jackson Turner quoted the census finding that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." [beat] He called it "the closing of a great historic movement."

[pause]

Then, in 1898, the country acted. The battleship Maine sank in Havana harbor on February 15. In April, the United States went to war with Spain. By August 12 there was a cease-fire. On that same day, a joint resolution made Hawaii a U.S. territory.

The Treaty of Paris that December guaranteed Cuba's independence. Spain ceded Guam and Puerto Rico. [beat] And it sold the Philippines, for 20 million dollars.

> In 1898, the United States took colonies of its own.

Filipinos who wanted independence, not a new ruler, fought on. The Philippine-American War ran from 1899 to 1902. It killed over 4,200 American combatants, and over 20,000 Filipino combatants. And as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died, from violence, famine and disease.

[pause]

Still, the American empire stayed smaller than Europe's. In China, Secretary of State John Hay's Open Door notes asked only for equal trading opportunity.

And the new world power kept a small army. In 1913, 154,914 Americans were on active duty. Defense took roughly 0.7 percent of the economy.

At the start, I promised you three kinds of advantage. Can you still name them?

[pause]

Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] And the troubles of other nations.

So let's sort. The minerals look like geography. The single market, the land grants, the tariff and the open door were choices. And the troubles of others? Cheap grain squeezing Europe's farms. The hardship that sent 55 million Europeans abroad. And a Spanish empire that the war of 1898 ended in the Americas.

But the edges are disputed. Paul David and Gavin Wright argue that American mineral abundance was "socially constructed," the product of law, institutions and technology. On that reading, even the ground was partly a choice.

And the tariff was a policy. Whether it helped at all is the argument you heard earlier.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. By 1913, America made about a third of the world's manufactured goods. And it pulled ahead of Britain in output per person not on one day, but for good from 1899.

[beat]

Two. One market, from 1787, and rails built on public land and credit. And a tariff still in dispute. The tariff as builder: a wall buys time to learn. The tariff as burden: growth came from people and investment.

[beat]

Three. Europe's hardship pushed, American wages pulled, and American law chose. The door stayed open to Europeans, and shut on Chinese laborers in 1882.

[beat]

Four. Government regulated the giants lightly, and sided with owners against strikers. Robber barons, or industrial statesmen: the same facts, read two ways.

[beat]

And five. In 1898, the United States took colonies of its own. But it kept a small army.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Historians still argue over the edges of that sorting. Were the minerals nature or choice? Did the tariff help? And were the railroads, in Richard White's reading, public subsidy and private corruption as much as enterprise?

The next lecture asks how Europe's war of 1914, the boom of the 1920s, the closing of the immigration door, and the Depression changed where America stood.

For now, go back to Promontory, at 12:47 on that Monday in 1869. One word on the wire. [beat] The line was done. [beat] Who would share in what it built was still being decided.
