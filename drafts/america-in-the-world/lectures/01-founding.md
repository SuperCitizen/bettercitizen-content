Philadelphia. June 7, 1776. A Virginian named Richard Henry Lee rises in the Continental Congress with a resolution of three sentences.

The first declares that "these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States."

[beat]

The second says it is "expedient forthwith to take the most effectual measures for forming foreign Alliances."

[beat]

The third calls for a plan of confederation.

[pause]

Be free. Find allies. Form a union. Four days later, Congress named one committee to draft a declaration, and another to plan the alliances.

> Independence and the search for allies began in the same resolution.

That resolution opens the question this whole series follows, across 250 years. How did the United States become what it became? And how much of that did the rest of the world decide?

By the Maddison Project's estimates, the country produced about 2 percent of the world's output in 1820. By 1950, it produced about 27 percent.

To explain a climb like that, the series sorts America's advantages into three kinds. Listen for them, because I will ask for them later.

First, geography and timing: what the land and the moment handed Americans. [beat] Second, choices and policy: what Americans and their governments decided. [beat] And third, the troubles of other nations: what other peoples' wars, debts and revolutions made possible.

In every era there is one more question. Who shared in what was built, and who was left out?

By the end, you will be able to explain how thirteen colonies became a country in a world at war. We will take it in four steps. First, what the colonies produced, and for whom. Second, the ideas that made breaking away thinkable, and the people they left out. Third, why France fought Britain's war. And last, how a weak republic survived Europe's wars.

## An economy on the edge of an empire

Start in 1775. The thirteen colonies were farms, forests and fishing ports, on the western edge of the British Empire.

The Chesapeake grew tobacco. The Carolina low country grew rice, and indigo, a blue dye. New England sold fish, timber and ships. And British trade laws steered that commerce through British ports.

It was a prosperous place for those who owned it. [beat] Much of the work behind the most valuable crops was done by people who owned nothing, not even themselves.

> The colonies supplied an empire, and enslaved people did much of the work.

Put another way, the colonial economy ran on two things it did not pay for in full. One was enslaved labor. The other was land.

The land that made farms cheap was Native land. When settlers poured over the Appalachians after 1763, Native nations fought back. A war led by the Ottawa leader Pontiac took all but two of Britain's western forts. London answered by forbidding settlement west of the mountains.

[pause]

Now a few numbers. The first census, in 1790, counted just under four million people. Nearly seven hundred thousand of them were enslaved. [beat] That is about 18 percent.

By the Maddison Project's estimates, output per person in the colonies in 1775 was about 84 percent of Britain's. In 1790, exports plus imports came to about 31 percent of the economy. And in 1789, the United States Army had 718 soldiers.

So picture a small country. Not far behind Britain in output per person. Living on foreign trade. Nearly unarmed.

Here is a question those numbers raise. In 1775, American output per person was about 84 percent of Britain's. By 1800, it was about 76 percent. Does that mean Americans got poorer? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. [beat] A ratio can fall while both sides grow. In these estimates, output per person rose in both countries. Britain's rose faster, so the gap widened.

Now the crisis, which came out of a victory. In the Seven Years' War, which ended in 1763, Britain beat France and took all French territory east of the Mississippi.

The war had been enormously expensive, so Parliament decided the colonists should help pay, with taxes like the Stamp Act of 1765. The colonists answered with riots, with boycotts, and with a slogan. "No taxation without representation."

The economy explains why that quarrel mattered to merchants and planters. [beat] It does not explain why so many people would die over it.

## Ideas, and the people they left out

Plenty of people paid taxes they disliked without founding a country. What turned a tax quarrel into a revolution was a set of ideas, from the movement called the Enlightenment.

From the English philosopher John Locke, the colonists took a claim. Government rests on consent, and a government that attacks the rights it exists to protect may be replaced. From the French writer Montesquieu, they took a warning. Liberty survives only where power is divided.

On July 4, 1776, the Declaration of Independence put those ideas in a sentence. "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal."

Governments, it went on, derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed." That principle is called popular sovereignty. Power rises from the people, rather than coming down from a king.

The Declaration also faced outward, claiming the power to make war, make peace and contract alliances. That mattered abroad. French officials would not consider an alliance until the colonies declared themselves a country.

[pause]

Now, who was in "all men"? The founding's own documents answer plainly.

Thomas Jefferson, who drafted the Declaration, was a Virginia slaveholder. In 1790, nearly one American in five was Black, and almost all of them were enslaved.

The Declaration accused the king of turning "the merciless Indian Savages" against the frontier. Those were the words the founding document used for Native nations.

In March 1776, Abigail Adams asked her husband John, then in Congress, to "remember the ladies" in the new laws. With one partial exception, the new states did not give women the vote.

And the vote followed property, as well as sex. Most states limited it to men who owned property or paid taxes. So many free men were shut out too.

> In practice, the governed who counted were free white men, mostly with property.

The partial exception was New Jersey. Its 1776 constitution gave the vote to "all inhabitants" worth fifty pounds. So a widow who owned a farm could vote. A married woman could not, because she could not own property in her own name. Neither could a laborer with nothing. The line ran through property, until 1807, when the legislature limited the vote to tax-paying white men.

## France's war

So the colonies had declared themselves a country. Now they had to win, and for that, they needed France.

France had its own reasons. It had lost Canada, and its lands east of the Mississippi, in 1763. Its ministers saw the American rebellion as a chance to profit from British troubles. From late 1775, France secretly shipped gunpowder and arms to the rebels.

Then a battle turned secret help into an alliance. In December 1777, news reached Paris that a British army had surrendered at Saratoga. On February 6, 1778, Benjamin Franklin and his fellow commissioners signed a treaty of alliance with France. Neither side could make peace with Britain without the other's consent.

The alliance turned a rebellion into a world war.

In 1780, 5,500 French troops landed in Rhode Island. In 1781, a French fleet drove off a British fleet, blockaded the Chesapeake, and trapped General Cornwallis at Yorktown. About 17,600 American and French soldiers besieged 8,300 British troops. And on October 19, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered.

[pause]

Britain was fighting in India, Gibraltar, the West Indies and Ireland as well. In March 1782, Parliament voted against carrying on the American war. And French loans to the Americans passed two million dollars.

> France fought for its own reasons, and its fleet made Yorktown possible.

Put plainly, independence was not won alone. It was won inside a war between empires.

The peace showed American choices at work. Franklin, John Adams and John Jay negotiated directly with Britain, and signed preliminary terms without France at the table. The Treaty of Paris, in 1783, acknowledged the United States "to be free sovereign and independent states," with a western boundary at the Mississippi.

No Native nation signed it, though Britain signed away land that Native nations held. And listen to one line of Article 7. British forces were to leave "without causing any destruction, or carrying away any Negroes or other property of the American inhabitants."

[pause]

Negroes, or other property. The treaty that recognized American independence listed enslaved people among the property to be left behind.

Now apply this section's logic to another country. Spain went to war against Britain in 1779. But Spain never allied with the United States, and its ministers were wary of American independence. Why would Spain fight Britain, and still keep its distance? Think it through.

[long pause]

Like France, Spain fought for its own interests. It hoped to win back territory lost in earlier wars. An independent republic next to its own American empire was no prize. So Spain joined France's war, without joining America's cause.

## Surviving Europe's wars

That is how independence was won. Now, how was it kept?

Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress could not tax, and it could not regulate trade. States ignored the peace treaty's promise to British creditors, so Britain kept its forts on the Great Lakes. Spain closed the Mississippi to American shipping. And in 1785, the United States stopped paying interest on its French loans.

The Constitution, in force from 1789, answered with power. Congress could now tax, and regulate commerce with foreign nations. And treaties became "the supreme Law of the Land."

> The Constitution gave the republic power to tax trade and keep its treaties.

The new government lived on the tariff, a tax on imports. Tariffs supplied 80 to 90 percent of national revenue in the nineteenth century. In other words, the government's income rose and fell with foreign trade.

The Constitution also protected slavery, without naming it. It counted "three fifths of all other Persons" toward each state's seats in the House. It barred Congress from stopping the importation of enslaved people before 1808. And it required that a person held to labor who escaped be delivered up.

At the Convention, James Madison "thought it wrong to admit in the Constitution the idea that there could be property in men." And the word slave appears nowhere in the text.

[pause]

Then Europe went to war. Revolutionary France executed its king in January 1793, and was soon fighting Britain and Spain. The 1778 treaty had guaranteed France's American islands forever, and now France wanted help.

On April 22, 1793, Washington proclaimed something else. The United States would be friendly and impartial toward the powers at war. [beat] Neutrality.

Holding it was hard. Britain seized American ships and sailors anyway. John Jay's treaty with Britain, approved in 1795, got the British out of the Great Lakes forts, but settled little else.

Spain gave more, because Spain was losing. Beaten in Europe, it gave Thomas Pinckney a treaty in 1795 that opened the Mississippi and New Orleans to American trade. And Pinckney got it without the alliance Spain asked for.

France, angry at Jay's treaty, seized American ships, and from 1798 the old allies fought an undeclared naval war. The Convention of 1800 ended the war, and the alliance with it. The United States would not sign another formal alliance for nearly a century and a half.

In September 1796, Washington's Farewell Address turned the decade into advice. Listen for two words: commercial, and political.

"The great rule of conduct for us in regard to foreign nations is in extending our commercial relations," [beat] "to have with them as little political connection as possible."

[pause]

Commercial relations, yes. Political connection, as little as possible. And he grounded the rule in geography. "Our detached and distant situation invites and enables us to pursue a different course."

## Three readings, three kinds

Historians agree on these events far more than on what drove them. Three readings answer that question.

The first is the ideological reading. Its best-known voice is the historian Bernard Bailyn, in a book of 1967. It insists on taking the founders' own words seriously. It argues that ideas made the Revolution. And it points to the pamphlets colonists published before independence.

In them, Bailyn found a sincere and coherent fear that British ministers were plotting against American liberty. Taxes mattered as evidence of the plot. Then the same arguments spread, to slavery, to established churches, and to social rank. Bailyn called that spread the contagion of liberty.

> The ideological reading: ideas made the Revolution, then outran their authors.

The second is the economic reading. Its best-known voice is the historian Charles Beard, in a book of 1913. It insists on following the money. It argues that the Constitution was, at bottom, an economic document. And it points to the framers' holdings: money, public securities, trade and shipping.

Men like these, Beard argued, wanted a government strong enough to pay debts and enforce contracts. Robert Brown and Forrest McDonald, in the 1950s, concluded that the records did not bear Beard out. But Robert McGuire's statistical study, in 2003, revived a narrower claim. Delegates' economic interests help predict their votes on particular clauses, alongside their ideas.

> The economic reading: interests, not only ideas, shaped the Constitution.

The third reading puts slavery at the center. Its voices include the historians Edmund Morgan, in 1975, and David Waldstreicher, in 2009. It insists on looking at who was left out. It argues that the exclusions were part of the design: a republic of free men, built on enslaved labor and on Native land.

And it points to the evidence. In Virginia, Morgan found freedom for white men and slavery for Black people growing up together, each making the other possible. In the 1787 text, Waldstreicher finds slavery at its heart, protected though never named.

> Slavery at the center: the founding's exclusions were part of its design.

What can the evidence settle? The clauses are in the text. The pamphlets survive. The delegates' finances can be counted. What the evidence cannot settle is weight. The historian Sean Wilentz, in 2018, grants that the Convention protected slaveholders. But he argues that keeping property in man out of the text gave later antislavery politicians ground to stand on.

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

[pause]

Three readings. You may already lean toward one. Take the one you find least convincing, and state the best case for it, the way its strongest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

Now pull back something from the start. At the beginning, I gave you three kinds of advantage. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] The troubles of other nations.

Geography and timing gave the republic an ocean between it and Europe's armies, and abundant land, which was Native land. Choices and policy gave it early allies, a Constitution that could tax trade, and neutrality. The troubles of other nations gave it France's wish to undo 1763, Britain's other wars, and Spain's defeats.

The sorting is itself argued. The historian Samuel Flagg Bemis subtitled his 1926 study of Pinckney's treaty, A Study of America's Advantage from Europe's Distress. The State Department's history of the same treaty dwells on Pinckney's threat to walk out. And land is harder still. Americans counted it a gift of geography. But it became American by war and treaty.

[pause]

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Independence and the search for allies began in the same resolution.

[beat]

Two. The colonies supplied an empire, and enslaved people did much of the work, on Native land.

[beat]

Three. In practice, the governed who counted were free white men, mostly with property.

[beat]

Four. France fought for its own reasons, and its fleet made Yorktown possible.

[beat]

And five. The Constitution gave the republic power to tax trade and keep its treaties. Then neutrality kept it out of Europe's wars.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. How much weight belongs to ideas, to interests, and to slavery. And how much of the founding was American choice, and how much was Europe's distress.

Europe's distress paid its largest dividend just after this lecture ends. In 1803, Napoleon's army in Saint-Domingue was decimated by yellow fever, and war with Britain was looming. Napoleon sold the whole Louisiana territory to the United States, for 15 million dollars.

The next lecture follows the country across that land, from 1800 to 1860. And it asks who paid for the expansion.

For now, go back to Philadelphia, to that June day in 1776. One resolution. Three sentences. Be free. Find allies. Form a union.

[beat]

Freedom was the first sentence. The world was the second.
