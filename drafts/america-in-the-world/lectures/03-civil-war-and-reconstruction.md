Charleston harbor, at about four in the morning, on May 13, 1862.

A Confederate steamer called the Planter comes up to Fort Sumter. Its pilot gives the proper signal. [beat] The sentry lets it pass.

The pilot's name is Robert Smalls. He is enslaved. So are the crewmen, the women and the children aboard.

Past the fort, Smalls turns the ship over to the USS Onward, one of the Navy ships blockading the port.

[pause]

Thirteen years later, Smalls takes a seat in the United States House of Representatives.

Twenty years after that, at South Carolina's constitutional convention of 1895, he is one of six Black delegates. He speaks against changes designed to remove Black South Carolinians from the voter rolls.

Slavery. War. Freedom. The vote. [beat] And its loss. One life ran that whole arc.

That arc is our subject, from 1860 to 1877. By the end, you will be able to say what the Civil War settled, and what it left open.

We will take it in four steps.

First, why the South left, in its own words.

Second, what the Union built while it fought.

Third, why Britain and France stayed out.

And last, Reconstruction: what it built, how it ended, and how historians have judged it.

## Why the South left

South Carolina left the Union in December 1860, a few weeks after Abraham Lincoln's election. Ten more states followed it into the Confederacy.

Four of them published formal declarations of their causes. These are the reasons secessionists gave at the time, to each other, and, in Georgia's phrase, to "the world."

South Carolina's declaration blamed "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery."

Mississippi's made its case in its second sentence. Listen for how it ends. "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world."

[pause]

The greatest material interest of the world.

Georgia put a price on the stake. It spoke of 3 billion dollars "of our property." By property, it meant people.

The economic historian Roger Ransom puts the value of the four million people enslaved in 1860 at nearly 3 billion dollars, as property.

> The seceding states said, in their own words, that slavery was their cause.

After the war, a different story took hold. A book by Edward Pollard, in 1866, gave it a name: the Lost Cause. In that story, the Confederacy was a noble, doomed defense of states' rights, and slavery was a side issue.

The documents overturn that story twice.

First, states' rights. South Carolina's grievance was not federal power. It was Northern states' rights.

It named thirteen Northern states whose laws, it said, "either nullify the Acts of Congress or render useless any attempt to execute them." Those acts of Congress were the federal laws for returning people who had escaped slavery.

[beat]

So the grievance ran the other way: Northern states stood in the way of a federal law.

Second, the tariff, the tax on imports. Georgia's declaration says more about economics than any of the others. And it says the South had already won the tariff fight.

After 1846, in its words, "free trade, low duties, and economy in public expenditures was the verdict of the American people."

What Georgia feared was something else. It feared a party whose "mission and its purpose," it said, was "anti-slavery."

[pause]

Historians treat this as a finding, not an opinion.

The historian Charles Dew found the same message in the speeches of the seceding states' commissioners. And the National Park Service's study of the era, in 2017, describes a Lost Cause "mythology," whose memorials obscured "the cause and nature of the conflict."

Let's test the method, because it works on any claim about motives.

A school textbook from the 1910s tells you the South seceded over the tariff. How would you test that claim? And what would you expect to find? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Go to what the secessionists wrote when they acted, not to accounts written after defeat. There, South Carolina and Mississippi rest their case on slavery. And Georgia says the tariff fight was already won.

A reason first offered decades later, in defense of a side's memory, is weaker evidence than the reasons given in the act itself.

## What the Union built

That is why the war came. Now, what the war did.

It lasted four years. By Roger Ransom's count, it killed about 625,000 soldiers.

The Union fought first to restore the Union. The war against slavery grew out of it.

Enslaved people fled to Union lines by the thousands. W. E. B. Du Bois would later call that movement "a general strike against the slave system."

Then, on January 1, 1863, came the Emancipation Proclamation. It declared that people held as slaves in the rebelling states "are, and henceforward shall be free."

It had limits. It exempted the loyal border states, and the areas the Union already held. And its promise depended on victory.

But it opened the army and the navy to Black men, and almost 200,000 fought for the Union.

The proclamation ends by invoking "the considerate judgment of mankind." [beat] It was written for a watching world.

Meanwhile, with Southern members gone, Congress remade the economy. Four laws matter most.

One. The Homestead Act, in 1862, offered 160 acres of public land to settlers who lived on it for five years and paid a small fee.

[beat]

Two. The Pacific Railway Act gave federal bonds and land grants to two railroad companies. More than 8,000 European immigrants worked on one line, and more than 10,000 Chinese laborers on the other. The lines met in Utah, in May 1869.

[beat]

Three. The Morrill Act gave the states land to endow colleges of agriculture and the mechanic arts.

[beat]

Four. The national banking acts of 1863 and 1864 created a uniform national currency, backed by Treasury bonds.

[pause]

Here is what that means for the myth of self-reliance. The families who made it on their own did so on land the government gave away, much of it taken from Native nations. And railroads the government financed carried their crops.

Most public land never reached homesteaders at all. Of some 500 million acres the government disposed of, about 80 million did.

Let's test the myth. A family claimed 160 acres in Nebraska in 1870. Years later, it says it owed nothing to anyone. What most directly complicates that claim? Think it through.

[long pause]

The land itself. It came from the federal government, for five years on the land and a small fee. And federally backed railroads carried the family's crops.

The family's work was real. [beat] But it stood on a public foundation.

> Saving the Union built a far stronger national government.

Put a number on that. In 1860, about twenty-eight thousand Americans were on active military duty. By 1865, more than a million were.

But the war also cost the country ground. By the Maddison Project's estimates, American output per person was about 87 percent of Britain's in 1860. By 1870, it had slipped to about 82 percent. [beat] In the war decade, America lost ground to Britain instead of gaining it.

## Why Britain stayed out

That is the war at home. Now the world.

The Confederacy's best hope lay across the Atlantic. By the late 1850s, the historian Sven Beckert writes, cotton grown in the United States made up 77 percent of the cotton Britain's mills consumed. Southern leaders expected that dependence to bring Britain in.

The bet failed, step by step.

In April 1861, Lincoln proclaimed a blockade of Southern ports. That May, Britain answered with neutrality. It treated the Confederacy as a belligerent, able to buy and borrow abroad. [beat] But not as a nation.

That November, a United States captain seized two Confederate envoys from a British mail ship, the Trent. Britain sent troops to Canada. And Lincoln's government released the envoys, rather than risk a second war.

Textile districts in Britain and France suffered high unemployment. Yet neither government recognized the Confederacy. And Napoleon the Third, busy installing an emperor in Mexico, would not act without Britain.

[pause]

So why did cotton diplomacy fail? That is still argued.

Here is the ground nobody disputes. Britain needed Southern cotton. And Britain never recognized the Confederacy. Historians weigh three explanations for the gap between those two facts.

The first is the battlefield view. The State Department's historians hold it, and so does Howard Jones, the author of Union in Peril, from 1992. What it keeps in view is power.

Its argument is that Europe recognizes winners, and the Confederacy never won enough victories in a row to convince Europe it could last. Its evidence is how seriously Britain's cabinet weighed offering mediation in 1862.

> The battlefield view: Europe recognizes winners, and the South never won enough.

The second is the slavery view. The State Department's historians hold this one too, and so does Don Doyle, the author of The Cause of All Nations, from 2015. What it keeps in view is ideas, and public opinion.

Its argument is that once the Union tied the war to freedom, no British government could back a slaveholders' republic without trouble at home. Its evidence is the abolitionist feeling that restrained the prime minister, Lord Palmerston, who sympathized with the South.

> The slavery view: once the war meant freedom, Britain could not back the South.

The third is the interests view. Frank Owsley held it, in King Cotton Diplomacy, from 1931. What it keeps in view is Britain's own calculation.

Its argument is that Britain stayed out because staying out paid. Its evidence is what a war would have cost. A war would have exposed Canada. And by respecting the blockade, the leading naval power kept a precedent it might need itself.

Europe also had its own crises, in Poland and Denmark. And later in the war, Britain found new cotton in Egypt and India.

> The interests view: Britain stayed out because staying out paid.

Notice how they sort. The first two credit Union choices. The third credits the circumstances of other nations.

The evidence fits all three in part. [beat] So the argument is over the weighting, not the facts.

Three views. You may already lean toward one. So take the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its strongest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## Reconstruction

That is the war and the world. Now the peace, and the hardest question the war left behind. What would freedom mean, for some four million people?

Part of the answer went into the Constitution, in three amendments.

The Thirteenth, in 1865, abolished slavery, with one exception: punishment for a crime, after a conviction.

The Fourteenth, in 1868, made "All persons born or naturalized in the United States" citizens. And it barred any state from denying "the equal protection of the laws."

The Fifteenth, in 1870, forbade denying the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

[beat]

Each one let Congress enforce it. That is the change. A Constitution that had mostly limited the national government now made it a guarantor of rights against the states. The historian Eric Foner calls the amendments the Second Founding.

Put another way: the Constitution had mostly said what the national government could not do. Now it also said what that government must protect.

Reconstruction then built a democracy the South had never had. Black men in the South voted from 1867. About 2,000 held local office before the century ended. Seventeen were elected to Congress. [beat] Robert Smalls was one of them.

Republican governments created the South's first public school systems.

What freedpeople wanted most was land. Land mostly never came. Most worked for white landowners, for wages or for a share of the crop.

[pause]

White Democrats answered with fraud and terror. Federal prosecutions broke the Ku Klux Klan in South Carolina, for a time.

But on Easter Sunday, 1873, at Colfax, Louisiana, a white militia killed scores of Black men, most after they had surrendered. Then, in a case called United States versus Cruikshank, the Supreme Court ruled that the enforcement law reached too far. [beat] The killers went free.

After the disputed election of 1876, a commission gave every contested electoral vote to Rutherford B. Hayes. It did so by a single vote.

In April 1877, Hayes withdrew the troops guarding the statehouses of South Carolina and Louisiana. The last Republican governments fell. In time, Jim Crow followed.

Words on paper were not enough. In 1898, in a case called Williams versus Mississippi, the Supreme Court accepted the South's disfranchisement. In other words, enforcement, and not the text alone, decided what the amendments meant.

[pause]

How should we judge those years? Historians have judged them three ways.

The first is the Dunning School. William Dunning of Columbia University and his students held it, in the early twentieth century. What it defended was the standing of white Southerners as the wronged party.

Its argument was that Reconstruction put carpetbaggers and Black Southerners over loyal white Southerners. Its evidence was corruption in the new governments. And its premise was that Black Americans were at best second-class citizens.

> The Dunning School: Reconstruction as a catastrophic error, driven by Northern vindictiveness.

That view echoed through the film The Birth of a Nation. Later scholarship, the Park Service study records, "challenged and often discredited" it.

The second is Du Bois's Black Reconstruction, from 1935. W. E. B. Du Bois held it, and his last chapter, The Propaganda of History, indicted the historians who had told the story otherwise. What it defended was freedpeople's place as the story's actors.

Its argument is that their flight to Union lines was a general strike, and their votes built a democracy. Its evidence is what that democracy did, in one chapter title: Founding the Public School.

> Du Bois: freedpeople were Reconstruction's actors, undone by a counter-revolution of property.

Modern scholars often cite their debt to him.

The third is Foner's synthesis. Eric Foner holds it, in a book from 1988 called Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution. What it defends is the era's lasting achievements.

Its argument dates the era from emancipation and puts Black Southerners' aims at the center. And it explains the defeat by Southern terror and a Northern loss of will, sped by the depression after 1873.

> Foner: Reconstruction was an unfinished revolution, not a failure.

Du Bois stresses class conflict. Foner stresses the meaning of freedom. But both rest on evidence the Dunning School ignored.

[beat]

The factual quarrel with Dunning is settled. Why the nation let Reconstruction go is not.

## What to keep

Before we gather this up, pull something back. Earlier I gave you three explanations for why Britain stayed out. Can you still name them? And which kind of advantage does each one credit?

[long pause]

The battlefield. Slavery. And Britain's own interests. [beat] The first two credit Union choices, and the third, other nations' circumstances.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The seceding states said, in their own words, that slavery was their cause. The Lost Cause came later, and the documents undercut it.

[beat]

Two. Saving the Union built a far stronger national government. It freed and armed enslaved people, gave away land, backed railroads, endowed colleges and created a national currency.

[beat]

Three. Britain needed Southern cotton, but it never needed the Confederacy. Why is still argued.

[beat]

And four. The amendments made the national government a guarantor of rights. Then the nation stopped enforcing them, and by 1877 Reconstruction was over. Unfinished, in Foner's word, not failed.

[pause]

Here is what the war did not settle. It settled two questions by force. A state cannot leave the Union. [beat] And a person cannot be owned.

A third question, whether the freed would be equal citizens, it settled only on paper. The end of Reconstruction left equality unenforced for most of a century.

One more argument stays open. Did the wartime laws make America's industrial rise, or ride it? Charles and Mary Beard called the war a Second American Revolution. The economic historian Thomas Cochran argued that it slowed industrial growth.

The next lecture begins on the railroads. The depression of 1873 started when Jay Cooke's banking house, dragged down by railroad bonds, failed. Eighty-nine of the nation's 364 railroads went bankrupt.

The industrial giant of 1870 to 1914 would be built on the war's land, laws and money. Was that rise the war's dividend, or the continent finally paying out?

For now, go back to Charleston harbor, before dawn. A steamer passes Fort Sumter with the proper signal, carrying people toward their own freedom.

Robert Smalls lived to sit in Congress, and to speak, in 1895, against changes designed to take the vote away. [beat] The war settled that he was free. It left the rest to be fought for.
