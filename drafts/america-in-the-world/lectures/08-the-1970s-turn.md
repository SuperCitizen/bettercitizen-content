Sunday, August 15, 1971. Richard Nixon is on national television, and he makes three announcements at once.

A freeze on all prices and wages, for 90 days. [beat] A tax of 10 percent on imported goods. [beat] And one more line. He says he has "directed Secretary Connally to suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold."

[pause]

Temporarily. [beat] The suspension was never lifted.

Within a decade, Americans would wait in lines for gasoline. They would see prices rise more than 14 percent in a year. And they would hear a president speak of "a crisis of confidence."

[pause]

So here is the question. The long postwar boom ended in the 1970s. Was it bound to end? Or was it ended by choices?

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to explain why the boom ended. And you will be able to sort its causes into three kinds: geography and timing, choices and policy, and the troubles of other nations.

We will take it in four steps. First, the dollar and its gold. Second, oil, and a new word for an old fear. Third, who was really falling behind. And last, how Washington changed its tools, and what the turn meant.

## The promise the dollar could not keep

Start with the promise. The system built at Bretton Woods in 1944 pegged other currencies to the dollar, and tied the dollar to gold at 35 dollars an ounce.

A foreign government holding dollars could trade them for American gold. And the United States held about three-quarters of the world's official gold reserves. So, in the words of the Federal Reserve's historians, "the system seemed secure."

The trouble was arithmetic. The world needed dollars to trade and to hold in reserve, and it got them as America sent dollars abroad, in aid, military spending and investment. [beat] But America's gold did not grow.

In 1960, the economist Robert Triffin warned Congress about the bind. Without American deficits, the world would run short of reserves. With them, confidence in the dollar would erode.

Picture a coat check that has handed out more tickets than it holds coats. It works until people doubt it and all come back at once. The picture breaks in one place: people wanted the tickets for their own sake, because the world traded in dollars.

> By 1971, the world held more dollars than America had gold.

Part of the cause was a success. Europe and Japan had rebuilt from the war, and their goods now competed with America's.

So Nixon went to Camp David with fifteen advisers. Among them was a Treasury undersecretary named Paul Volcker. Hold on to that name. He comes back.

In December 1971, the Smithsonian Agreement set new fixed rates around a cheaper dollar. It did not hold. In March 1973, six European countries floated their currencies together against the dollar, and the fixed rates of Bretton Woods were over.

In the Fed historians' words, "the international monetary system turned into a fiat one." Fiat money is money backed by trust in the government that issues it. [beat] Not by metal.

And the dollar lost its gold but not its place. In 2024, dollars still made up 58 percent of the foreign reserves that governments disclose.

Try the idea on a new case. A small country pegs its currency to the dollar, and promises to swap its notes for dollars on demand. It has issued far more notes than it holds in dollars, and its exporters start asking for dollars. What are its choices? And what does 1971 suggest about waiting?

[long pause]

It has three. It can make its money scarcer by raising interest rates, at the cost of a slump. It can devalue. Or it can stop swapping, as Nixon did.

And waiting narrows the choices, because each new doubt sends more holders to the window.

## Oil, and a new word

That is the dollar. Now the oil.

Geography had given the United States an advantage few rivals matched: its own oil. American crude production set a record in 1970 that stood until 2018. But demand outran it, and by 1973 the economy had, in the State Department historians' words, "grown increasingly dependent on foreign oil."

In October 1973, during the Arab-Israeli war, the United States resupplied Israel's military. On October 19, the Arab oil producers' organization placed an embargo on the United States.

The price of oil nearly quadrupled. Before the embargo, a barrel cost two dollars and ninety cents. [beat] By January 1974, it cost eleven dollars and sixty-five cents.

Then in 1979 it happened again. Revolution in Iran cut that country's output, and between April 1979 and April 1980 the price of oil more than doubled.

[pause]

Now watch prices and jobs together. In 1964, inflation was 1 percent, and unemployment was 5 percent. Ten years later, inflation was over 12 percent. And unemployment was above 7 percent.

Prices rising fast, while growth was slow and jobs were scarce. [beat] That combination has a name. It is called stagflation.

> Stagflation means rising prices and high unemployment, at the same time.

Economists had expected the opposite: that a little more inflation would buy a little less unemployment. The Federal Reserve historian Michael Bryan put it plainly. "The trade-off that policymakers hoped to exploit did not exist."

So the usual tools fought each other. Scarcer money fights rising prices, but it costs jobs. Easier money saves jobs, but it feeds the rise. And meanwhile, inflation quietly shrank savings and incomes fixed in dollars.

[pause]

Those facts are not in dispute. [beat] Their cause is.

Here is the ground both sides share. Both accept what the economists Robert Barsky and Lutz Kilian call "the striking coincidence of major oil price increases and worsening stagflation." The argument is over which way the cause ran.

The first answer is the monetary explanation. It is held by the Federal Reserve's own historians, and by economists in the line of Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps. What it protects is a principle: central banks are answerable for inflation.

Its argument is that the Great Inflation came from "policies that allowed for an excessive growth in the supply of money." The Fed, on this view, kept accommodating large and rising government deficits. The economist Allan Meltzer documents what he calls "the role of Keynesian thinking in creating the Great inflation."

Its evidence: inflation began climbing in the mid-1960s, before any oil shock. And Barsky and Kilian show that swings in money can produce stagflation "even in the absence of supply shocks."

> The monetary explanation: the Fed let money grow too fast.

The second answer is the supply-shock explanation. It is held by economists such as Alan Blinder, who revisited the case with Jeremy Rudd in 2013. What it protects is a different point: some shocks are beyond any central bank's control.

Its argument is that the worst bursts of inflation came from shocks. Two oil shocks. [beat] Two food-price shocks. [beat] And the end of Nixon's wage and price controls. Testing that account against new data, Blinder and Rudd conclude that the old-fashioned explanation holds up quite well.

Its evidence: the two worst bursts of inflation arrived with the two oil shocks. And no central bank can print, or prevent, an embargo.

> The supply-shock explanation: oil and food shocks drove the worst bursts.

So the evidence settles the timing. It does not settle the direction of cause. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

The two explanations predict different things, so let's use them. Suppose a drought doubles food prices in one year, and the central bank holds money growth steady. What would each explanation predict for inflation over the next five years?

[long pause]

Both predict a jump in prices that first year. The supply-shock view treats that jump as the main event.

The monetary view expects it to fade. Lasting inflation, on that view, needs a central bank that goes along with it.

## Who was falling behind?

Now a question about standing in the world.

On July 1, 1980, the Treasury Secretary, G. William Miller, sent President Carter a memo on the auto industry. In 1978, imports had held 17.7 percent of the American car market. By early 1980, they held 26.4 percent.

Buyers were turning to small cars, and Japan supplied 80 percent of those imports. Layoffs in the industry had reached 239,000, most of them in the industrial Midwest. And steel had gone first. In 1977, the closing of the Youngstown Sheet and Tube mills in Ohio put about 5,000 people out of work.

[pause]

It sounds like decline. The longer record tells a more exact story.

Take America's share of the world's output, by the Maddison Project's estimates. In 1950 it was about 27 percent. In 1970, about 22 percent. [beat] And in 1980, about 21.

Listen to where the fall happened. Most of it came between 1950 and 1970, before either oil shock. Across the stormy 1970s, the share slipped only a little.

The economic historian Moses Abramovitz studied a century of this kind of evidence. He found that countries further behind tend to grow faster than the leader, and he called the pattern catching up. A follower can adopt methods the leader has already proved.

> Most of America's relative decline came before 1970, as rivals rebuilt.

In plainer words, the gap narrowed mostly because others rose. The Marshall Plan had set out to rebuild Western Europe, and the narrowing gap was partly its success.

But the 1970s did bring real losses. In manufacturing, America's share of the world's value added fell from 28 or 29 percent to 20 or 21 percent. Part of that fall is the cheaper dollar itself, so the number comes with a caution.

There was a loss at home too. Productivity is the output of an hour of work. From 1947 to 1973, it grew 2.8 percent a year. From 1973 to 1979, it grew 1.2 percent. And that slowdown lasted until about 1995.

Why does that matter? Because productivity is what lets pay rise without prices rising.

Two more facts complete the picture. Trade had grown to 13 percent of the economy by 1973, so shocks abroad reached home faster. And in 1970, only 4.7 percent of residents had been born abroad, the smallest share since the count began in 1850.

Let's try the distinction on a new case. Country A's share of world output falls from 30 to 25 percent over twenty years. Which added fact would best show that others are catching up, rather than that Country A is failing?

[long pause]

The fact that its output per person kept rising the whole time. A falling share with rising output means the others grew faster.

A cheaper currency, more imports or a rescued firm could happen either way. So none of those tells the two stories apart.

## How Washington changed its tools

Now the politics. Washington's tools changed in the 1970s, though not in a straight line.

In August 1971, a Republican president froze wages and prices. The Fed's historians judge that the controls "only temporarily slowed the rise in prices while exacerbating shortages." That same month, Nixon also backed a federal guarantee of a 250 million dollar bank loan to Lockheed.

In 1978, a Democratic president, Jimmy Carter, signed the Airline Deregulation Act. "For the first time in decades," he said, "we have deregulated a major industry." And in January 1980, Carter signed a law allowing federal guarantees of up to 1.5 billion dollars in Chrysler loans, if its unionized workers gave up wages and benefits.

[pause]

So under presidents of both parties, two industrial giants leaned on Washington. And the promise of self-reliance kept coming back.

In July 1979, Carter pledged that "this Nation will never use more foreign oil than we did in 1977." [beat] And then one more word. "Never."

That address is remembered as the malaise speech. [beat] But the word malaise never appears in it. What Carter said was, "It is a crisis of confidence."

Now pull something back. Earlier I asked you to hold on to a name from that weekend at Camp David. Can you still name him?

[pause]

Paul Volcker. In 1979, Carter named him chairman of the Federal Reserve. That October, Volcker said the Fed would steer by the reserves of banks, to "get firmer control over the growth in money supply."

> In 1979, the Federal Reserve put fighting inflation first.

Now sort the decade into the three kinds. First, geography and timing. Domestic oil peaked in 1970 and fell behind demand. And the postwar head start was partly timing: an economy untouched by war, beside rivals in ruins. That gap was bound to narrow.

[beat]

Second, choices and policy. The dollar's role was built by agreement in 1944, and remade by decision in 1971. The Marshall Plan chose to rebuild rivals. The Fed, the controls, the rescues and deregulation were all choices.

[beat]

Third, the troubles of other nations. In 1950, other countries' ruin had flattered America's share. In the 1970s, their troubles cut the other way. War in the Middle East, and revolution in Iran, reached Americans through the price of oil.

[pause]

Two items refuse to stay in one box. One is the Great Inflation, which we have heard argued. The other is the end of the boom itself. Was it timing, or choice?

The first answer says an extraordinary era ended. It is held by the writer Marc Levinson, in a 2016 book called An Extraordinary Time. It is held by the economist Robert Gordon, and by the catch-up tradition of Abramovitz. What it protects is a caution: do not blame policy for what history did.

Its argument is that the quarter century after 1945 was the exception. Levinson treats 1973 as the year that broadly shared, rapid growth gave way to slower growth. Gordon argues that the inventions of 1870 to 1970 changed daily life on a scale that cannot be repeated.

Its evidence: most of America's relative decline came before 1970. And the productivity slowdown outlasted two decades of different policies, under both parties.

> The extraordinary-era view: the postwar boom was the exception, not the rule.

The second answer says choices ended it. It is held by scholars who disagree about which choices were wrong, but agree that they were choices. It protects a principle too: the people who decided could have decided otherwise.

Its argument comes from two directions. The historian Judith Stein, in a 2010 book called Pivotal Decade, argues that Washington chose to fight inflation rather than unemployment, and a balanced budget over growth. Her subtitle says the country traded factories for finance. From the other side, Meltzer faults a Fed that let money grow too fast, for too long.

Its evidence: when the Fed changed course in 1979, the stagflation cycle broke. The price, in the Fed historians' words, was the most severe recession since the Great Depression. And Washington chose which firms to rescue, and which industries to deregulate.

> The choices view: the decade's outcomes were decided in Washington.

The evidence can tell us when the decline came, and what the policies were. It cannot tell us whether other choices would have kept the boom alive.

So take the view you find less convincing. Timing, or choices. And state the best case for it, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. By 1971, the world held more dollars than America had gold. Nixon stopped trading dollars for gold, and the dollar became fiat money, though it kept its place.

[beat]

Two. Stagflation means rising prices and high unemployment, at the same time. The monetary explanation says the Fed let money grow too fast. The supply-shock explanation says oil and food shocks drove the worst bursts.

[beat]

Three. Most of America's relative decline came before 1970, as rivals rebuilt. The 1970s added real losses, in manufacturing and in productivity.

[beat]

And four. In 1979, the Federal Reserve put fighting inflation first. Washington's tools had changed.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Economists still argue whether central bankers or shocks from abroad made the Great Inflation. Historians still argue whether the postwar boom had to end, or whether choices let it slip.

The costs are not in doubt. They fell hardest on the autoworkers and steelworkers of the industrial Midwest, and on anyone whose savings or income was fixed in dollars.

The next lecture follows what Americans did with the tools the 1970s left them, from 1980 to 2008. Deregulation. Open markets. The end of the Cold War. And China's entry into the World Trade Organization.

For now, go back to that Sunday in August 1971. A president, a freeze, a tax, and one word. [beat] Temporarily. You know now why the promise could not be kept, and how much changed once it was broken.
