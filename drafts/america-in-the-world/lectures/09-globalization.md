March 8, 2000. President Bill Clinton is speaking at the Paul H. Nitze School of Advanced International Studies. He is asking Congress for one thing: to make China's normal trade status permanent.

"Economically," he says, "this agreement is the equivalent of a one-way street."

[beat]

China would cut its tariffs. America, in his words, would only "maintain the present access which China enjoys." And for the first time, he promises, "we'll be able to export products without exporting jobs."

[pause]

Congress agreed. China joined the World Trade Organization in December 2001. And by 2007, American factories employed about 3.4 million fewer people than in 2000.

So here is the puzzle. The deal changed none of America's own tariffs. What did it have to do with those jobs?

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to answer that. You will also be able to say who gained from this era, from 1980 to 2008, and who paid. We will take it in four steps.

First, a recession in the early 1980s, and a wave of deregulation.

Second, the end of the Cold War, and the argument over what it meant.

Third, open trade, the China shock, and three ways to judge it.

And last, the new economy, and who shared in it.

## The cure and its cost

Start in March 1980. Prices are rising at nearly 15 percent a year.

Paul Volcker has chaired the Federal Reserve since August 1979. He sets out to break the inflation Americans have come to expect. By early 1981, the Fed has let the rate banks charge one another overnight approach 20 percent.

[beat]

Money gets scarce, and a recession follows, in 1981 and 1982. Unemployment rises to nearly 11 percent. It hits hardest the people who build things. By the end of 1982, unemployment is 22 percent in home building. In auto manufacturing, it is 24 percent.

And inflation breaks. It falls under 5 percent, and through the late 1980s it averages 3.5 percent.

> Volcker broke inflation, and a deep recession was the price.

Think of a thermostat that everyone has learned to cheat. When workers and firms expect prices to rise 10 percent a year, they write contracts that make it so. Only a large shock breaks the loop.

[beat]

The picture fails in one place. A thermostat throws no one out of work.

[pause]

That was the first remaking. The second was quieter, and it crossed party lines.

Since 1940, a federal board had set airline fares and routes. Jimmy Carter, a Democrat, signed the Airline Deregulation Act in 1978. In 1980 he signed the Motor Carrier Act, loosening rules that had, in his words, "insulated the trucking industry from competition since 1935."

Ronald Reagan, a Republican, carried the idea into banking. In 1982 he signed a law that let savings and loans make commercial loans. "All in all," he said, "I think we hit the jackpot."

[beat]

The results split by industry.

In transportation, the economist Clifford Winston found real airfares roughly 33 percent lower, and truckload rates at least 75 percent lower. But consumers, he wrote, "have not shared equally," and truckers lost significant wages.

In finance, it went worse. Weak savings and loans used their new powers, as a Federal Reserve history puts it, "to gamble unsuccessfully for recovery." The cleanup cost taxpayers as much as an estimated 124 billion dollars.

[pause]

Put another way, deregulation was government stepping back. The cleanup was government stepping back in.

Let's test that record on a new case. A city has long set every taxi fare, and capped the number of licenses. It lifts both rules. Judging by airlines and trucking, what happens next? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Average fares most likely fall, and service grows. [beat] But drivers who held the scarce licenses lose income, just as truckers did.

## What the end of the Cold War meant

So much for the economy at home. Now the world.

The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989. On December 25, 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as Soviet president.

The rival that had organized American strategy for four decades was gone. Many scholars have a name for the position it left America in: hegemony.

The country cashed part of the peace. Defense spending fell from 5.1 percent of the economy in 1990 to 2.9 percent in 2000.

[pause]

What did the victory mean? Two answers became famous. Each gets its turn.

The first came from the political scientist Francis Fukuyama, in a 1989 essay called The End of History. What it protects is a claim about ideas: that the contest over how to govern was over. Listen for the words "end point."

We may be witnessing, he wrote, "the end of history as such: that is, the end point of mankind's ideological evolution."

[beat]

The end point. Fukuyama points to fascism's defeat, and to communism losing its believers. Big wars, he argues, need large states still caught in the grip of history, and those seemed to be passing. He did not call it a happy ending. "The end of history," he wrote, "will be a very sad time."

> The end-of-history view: liberal democracy may be the end point of ideology.

The second answer came in 1993, from the political scientist Samuel Huntington, in an essay called The Clash of Civilizations. What it protects is the weight of culture. Huntington argues that ideology had been one phase of conflict, not its end.

"The great divisions among humankind and the dominating source of conflict," he wrote, "will be cultural."

Huntington points to Western ideas, like democracy and free markets, that often have little resonance elsewhere. Many states, he argues, would try "to modernize but not to Westernize." On this view, 1991 was a moment of Western power, not a universal civilization's arrival.

> The clash view: the next great conflicts would run along lines of culture.

[pause]

Now test the two views. From 1978 on, China's economy grew by an average of more than 9 percent a year. And its Communist Party kept its monopoly on power. Which view does that record trouble more? Think it through.

[long pause]

It troubles Fukuyama's more. He expected mounting pressure for political change as China's economy opened. His defenders answer that he claimed a victory of ideas, which would govern the material world in the long run.

[beat]

Huntington's phrase fits the case: to modernize, but not to Westernize. Though China grew inside Western trade rules.

## Open trade, and who paid

Between 1990 and 2008, exports plus imports grew from 19.9 percent of the American economy to 29.8 percent.

The country's share of the world's output, measured at purchasing-power parity, fell from 21.7 percent to 17.8. But the American economy did not shrink. [beat] Others, above all China, grew faster. In 2010, China passed the United States in manufacturing.

The first step was regional. George H. W. Bush signed the North American Free Trade Agreement in 1992, and in 1993 the House approved it, 234 to 200. Republicans supplied 132 of those votes, and Democrats 102, while 156 Democrats voted no.

One study finds that NAFTA raised America's welfare by 0.08 percent, and Mexico's by 1.31 percent. Another finds it dramatically lowered wage growth for blue-collar workers in the most exposed places.

[pause]

China was the bigger shock. And here is the answer to the puzzle we started with.

Since 1980, Chinese goods had paid the low tariffs that most trading partners paid. In 1999 that was about 4 percent, on average. But only as long as Congress renewed the status, every year. Without renewal, tariffs would jump to rates first set in 1930, about 36 percent.

Making the status permanent changed no rate anyone paid. [beat] What it changed was the risk.

Picture land leased by the year, at a rent that might jump ninefold at any renewal. A permanent lease changes nothing on the bill. And it changes everything about whether anyone builds a factory there.

The economists Justin Pierce and Peter Schott find that the industries most exposed to the removed threat lost the most jobs. The picture fails in one place. China's own reforms, which moved over 150 million workers from farm to city, were pushing exports up regardless.

Now the damage, as three economists measured it: David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson. Chinese goods rose from 0.6 percent of American spending on goods in 1991, to 4.6 percent in 2007.

> In their main estimate, Chinese imports explain a quarter of lost factory jobs.

Put another way, three-quarters of the decline had other causes.

And the losses were local. Tennessee, with its furniture makers, was far more exposed than Alabama, with its heavy industry. In the exposed places, wages stayed depressed for at least a full decade.

The federal program for trade's losers was Trade Adjustment Assistance. In 2007 it paid about 2 dollars a person. In hard-hit places, the rise in disability payments was over thirty times the rise in that assistance.

[pause]

So who gained, and who paid? Three views answer, and each gets the same four things: who holds it, what it protects, its argument, and its evidence.

The first is the gains-from-trade view. Most trade economists hold it. What it protects is the income and choice that open markets create. It argues that the gains are large but spread thin, while the losses are visible.

It points to the economists Gary Hufbauer and Zhiyao Lu. They find that trade expansion from 1950 to 2016 paid the United States 2.1 trillion dollars, about 18,000 dollars per household. It points to a finding that trade typically favors the poor, who spend more on traded goods. And abroad, China's growth lifted almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty.

The remedy, on this view, is to cushion the displaced, not to close markets.

> The gains view: trade made the country richer, and helped poorer shoppers most.

The second is the concentrated-costs view. Economists who study trade's local effects hold it. What it protects is the workers and towns that bore the adjustment. It argues that gains spread across a nation of shoppers never reached the town whose plant shut.

It points to the economist Dani Rodrik, who warned in 1997 that open markets divided people with the skills and mobility to flourish from people without them. And it points to the evidence that came later: losses lasting a decade, and trade assistance so small that disability payments did the cushioning. On this view, the winners never compensated the losers.

> The costs view: the losses landed on particular towns, and stayed there.

The third is the strengthened-rival view. Security strategists hold it. What it protects is America's position against a peer. It argues that trade policy is also power policy.

It points to Clinton's promise, that China would "import one of democracy's most cherished values, economic freedom." It points to China's share of world manufacturing, 14.5 percent by 2008. And it points to 2018, when President Trump's trade office told Congress that "the United States erred in supporting China's entry into the WTO."

Two former Obama officials, Kurt Campbell and Ely Ratner, wrote that "diplomatic and commercial engagement have not brought political and economic openness."

> The rival view: engagement made China stronger without making it freer.

[pause]

What does the evidence settle? That the gains were broad, and the losses concentrated. It cannot settle what the country owed the losers, or whether engagement with China was a mistake. Those are questions of value.

You may already lean toward one of these views. So take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

Now one more case. A trade deal ends tariffs on imported bicycles. Bike prices fall nationwide. And a county whose two bicycle plants employ most of its workers loses both. What does the China-shock evidence predict for that county, ten years on? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Its wages and jobs will most likely still be depressed, a decade later. [beat] That is how the places hit by Chinese imports looked, ten years on.

## Who shared in the new economy

One more revolution ran through these years, and it began inside government.

The internet started as a Defense Department experiment in the late 1960s. By 1993 the National Science Foundation's network linked more than 2 million computers. The World Wide Web came from Europe: a British scientist at CERN, Tim Berners-Lee, invented it in 1989.

Households with internet access went from 18 percent in 1997 to 42 percent in 2000. And economists at the Federal Reserve credited information technology with about two-thirds of the late-1990s step-up in productivity growth.

[beat]

So the self-made story is half the story. The foundations were public, and some of them foreign. The companies were private.

The country also changed who lived in it. In 1970, 4.7 percent of residents had been born abroad. By 2010 the share was 12.9 percent, the highest since 1920.

In 1986, Ronald Reagan signed a law pairing penalties on employers of unauthorized workers with a path to legal status, which nearly 2.7 million people took.

What did immigrants do to other workers' wages? A National Academies panel, in 2016, found that over ten years or more, the effect on native-born workers' wages overall "is very small." Any losses fall mostly on earlier immigrants, and on native-born high-school dropouts.

[pause]

So who shared in all this growth?

The gains were large, and lopsided. Between 1979 and 2012, the top 1 percent's share of household income rose from 10 percent to 22.5 percent. Over the same years, the earnings gap between college-educated and high-school-educated men working full time doubled.

[beat]

From 1980 to 2012, the real hourly earnings of college-educated men rose 20 to 56 percent. Men with only a high-school diploma saw theirs fall 11 percent. David Autor points to two forces: computers taking over routine tasks, and trade with low-wage countries.

> College graduates pulled ahead, while men without degrees lost ground.

And Raj Chetty's team found a change across generations. Of children born in 1940, about 90 percent came to earn more than their parents. Of children born in the 1980s, 50 percent did.

[pause]

Places pulled apart too. Poorer states used to catch up with richer ones. The economists Peter Ganong and Daniel Shoag document a dramatic decline in that catching up, over thirty years. Much of it, they find, comes down to housing. High prices in rich places kept less-skilled workers from moving there.

And when workers cannot follow the jobs, the gap between places stops narrowing.

## What to keep

Now sort the era's advantages into the series' three kinds.

Geography and timing gave two friendly neighbors, along what Bush called "the two largest undefended borders of the globe," and a computer revolution that arrived as the Cold War's end let defense spending fall.

Choices and policy gave Volcker's disinflation, deregulation under both parties, NAFTA and permanent trade status for China, and public research.

And the troubles of other nations gave the Soviet collapse, and a poor China whose low wages made its goods cheap.

[pause]

Before we gather it up, pull something back. Earlier I gave you a picture for the China deal: a piece of land, and a lease. Can you still say what the permanent lease changed?

[pause]

Nothing on the bill. Everything about whether anyone builds. [beat] Permanent status changed no tariff. It removed the risk.

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Volcker broke inflation, and a deep recession was the price. Deregulation, under both parties, made travel and shipping cheaper, and in finance it left taxpayers a bill.

[beat]

Two. The Cold War's end removed America's great rival. Fukuyama saw liberal democracy as the end point of ideology. Huntington saw the next great conflicts running along lines of culture.

[beat]

Three. In the main estimate, Chinese imports explain a quarter of lost factory jobs. And the losses stayed in particular towns for a decade.

[beat]

Four. Three views judge it. The gains view: trade made the country richer, and helped poorer shoppers most. The costs view: the losses landed on particular towns, and stayed there. The rival view: engagement made China stronger without making it freer.

[beat]

And five. College graduates pulled ahead, while men without degrees lost ground. And poorer places stopped catching up.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Historians still argue over whether the Soviet collapse came from American pressure or from within. Economists still argue over whether the China shock was an American choice, or something that happened to America. And who should have cushioned the losers is a question of values.

The era ended in the financial crisis of 2008. The next lecture takes up that crisis, China's rise, and the places this era left behind. It also asks what 250 years do and do not tell us about what comes next.

[pause]

Go back to that speech in March 2000. A president calls a trade deal a one-way street. [beat] He was right about the tariffs. The traffic, it turned out, ran both ways.
