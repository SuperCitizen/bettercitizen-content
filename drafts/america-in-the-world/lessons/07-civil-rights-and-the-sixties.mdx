# Why did the 1960s rewrite who belonged in America?

In December 1952, the Justice Department filed a brief in a lawsuit over the public schools of Topeka, Kansas, quoting a letter from Secretary of State Dean Acheson: "The segregation of school children on a racial basis is one of the practices in the United States that has been singled out for hostile foreign comment in the United Nations and elsewhere." A dispute over which school a Black child could attend had become a question about America's standing in the world. Within thirteen years, Congress had outlawed segregation in public places, protected the Black vote and scrapped the birthplace quotas of 1924. Why did so much change at once, and how much of the push came from abroad?

## How did legal segregation fall?

At midcentury, [[term:jim-crow]] was still the law across the South: statutes that separated Black and white Americans in schools, buses and restaurants, backed by poll taxes and literacy tests that kept Black Southerners from voting. It fell to two pressures working together.

The first came from the courts. On May 17, 1954, in [[term:brown-v-board-of-education]], a unanimous Supreme Court ruled: "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." Much of the white South resisted it.

The second came from the people segregation was aimed at. On December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks was arrested in Montgomery, Alabama, for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white passenger. Four days later, 90 percent of Montgomery's Black residents stayed off the buses, and a carpool of about 300 cars carried the boycott through thirteen months under a young minister, Martin Luther King Jr. It ended only after federal courts struck down bus segregation in Browder v. Gayle, affirmed by the Supreme Court in November 1956. Ending the boycott, King said the community had come to see that "it is more honorable to walk in dignity than ride in humiliation."

The same pairing returned in the 1960s. In 1964, Southern senators led by Richard Russell of Georgia held the civil rights bill in a [[term:filibuster]], an attempt to talk it to death, for 60 working days. On June 10 the Senate voted 71 to 29 to end debate, with 44 Democrats and 27 Republicans in favor, the first time it had ever cut off debate on a civil rights bill. The Civil Rights Act, signed July 2, outlawed segregation in businesses such as theaters, restaurants and hotels, and banned discrimination in employment.

On March 7, 1965, state troopers attacked peaceful marchers crossing the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama. The Voting Rights Act, signed August 6, barred literacy tests in the places it covered, sent federal examiners to register voters, and required those places to get federal approval, called preclearance, before changing their voting rules. By the end of 1965, a quarter of a million new Black voters had been registered, one-third by federal examiners. Self-reliance cuts both ways here: Montgomery ran its own carpools, and still needed federal judges.

## Why did the world's opinion matter in Washington?

In its own voice, the 1952 brief told the Court: "Racial discrimination furnishes grist for the Communist propaganda mills, and it raises doubts even among friendly nations as to the intensity of our devotion to the democratic faith." It was an [[term:amicus-brief]], filed by someone not a party to the case.

The audience was growing. In the [[term:cold-war]], the United States and the Soviet Union competed for the loyalty of a changing world: between 1945 and 1960, three dozen new states in Asia and Africa won autonomy or independence, and the United Nations grew from 35 members in 1946 to 127 by 1970. When mobs in Little Rock, Arkansas, defied a court order to admit Black students to Central High School in 1957, President Dwight Eisenhower sent troops and told the nation: "Our enemies are gloating over this incident and using it everywhere to misrepresent our whole nation."

The legal historian Mary Dudziak built this evidence into an argument in Cold War Civil Rights (2000). She argues that foreign policy helped push Washington toward reform, because American racism damaged the country's image in Africa, Asia and Latin America just when it wanted their allegiance. The pressure cut both ways, she argues: it often rewarded changes to the image over changes in substance, and McCarthy-era anticommunism narrowed what activists could safely demand. How much the world's gaze changed outcomes, against the movement's own pressure, historians still weigh.

The pressure arrived as America's lead was narrowing.

American output per person kept rising, from about $15,200 in 1950 to about $26,600 in 1973 in 2011 dollars, yet the share of world output fell five points as a rebuilt Western Europe, among others, grew faster. The lead that rivals' ruin had handed America after 1945 was wearing off, and a country no longer alone at the top had more reason to court other nations.

## How did a "not revolutionary" law change who came?

On October 3, 1965, on Liberty Island in New York Harbor, Johnson signed amendments to the Immigration and Nationality Act: "This bill that we will sign today is not a revolutionary bill. It does not affect the lives of millions." He called the national-origins quotas it replaced "un-American in the highest sense."

The law wrote a rule that would have fit in the civil rights acts: no person "shall receive any preference or priority or be discriminated against in the issuance of an immigrant visa because of his race, sex, nationality, place of birth, or place of residence." The quotas ended in 1968. In their place came a ceiling of 170,000 visas a year from outside the Western Hemisphere, a limit of 20,000 for any one country, and a first ceiling of 120,000 on the Western Hemisphere. Spouses, children and parents of citizens came outside the ceilings. Within the 170,000, the law set aside about three-quarters of visas for relatives, a fifth for professionals and workers in short supply, and 6 percent for refugees, defined in Cold War terms as people fleeing "any Communist or Communist-dominated country or area" or the Middle East. Visas went in the order petitions were filed.

Johnson's prediction did not hold. In 1970 the foreign-born share hit 4.7 percent, its lowest since counting began in 1850; then it climbed. Of foreign-born residents in 1960 who reported a birthplace, 75 percent had been born in Europe, 9.4 percent in Latin America and 5.1 percent in Asia. By 1990 those shares were 22.9, 44.3 and 26.3 percent, and the foreign-born share of the country was back to 7.9 percent.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did the 1965 law change about how America chose its immigrants?">
  It stopped choosing by birthplace. Quotas gave way to ceilings, a per-country limit and preferences for family ties, skills and refugees, so where you were born no longer set your place in line.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did the Great Society and Vietnam pull against each other?

On January 8, 1964, Johnson told Congress: "This administration today, here and now, declares unconditional war on poverty in America." That May he named the larger goal: "The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time." The [[term:great-society]] included Medicare, health insurance for people 65 and older, and Medicaid, coverage for people with limited incomes, both signed into law on July 30, 1965. Federal spending rose from 15.3 percent of GDP in 1950 to 18.2 percent in 1973.

Poverty fell fast in those boom years. The official rate, 22.4 percent in 1959, was 19.0 percent in 1964 and 12.1 percent in 1969. For Black Americans it fell from 55.1 to 32.2 percent between 1959 and 1969, still more than two and a half times the national rate. One caution matters later: the official measure counts cash income before taxes and leaves out noncash help such as Medicaid and food stamps.

The same government was fighting a war. On August 7, 1964, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, unanimously in the House and with two votes against in the Senate; it became the legal basis for the [[term:vietnam-war]] under Johnson and Nixon. National defense took 9.1 percent of GDP in 1968. The National Archives counts 58,220 American military dead, 16,899 of them in 1968; Vietnamese losses ran far higher.

Both wars drew on one budget and one stock of public trust. On April 4, 1967, at Riverside Church in New York, King argued that the war was draining resources needed to fight poverty at home; the NAACP and leading newspapers criticized him for tying the causes together. A year later to the day he was assassinated in Memphis. Johnson's coalition began to split over the war, over riots in Northern cities, and over how far government should go.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why did the Great Society and the Vietnam War compete with each other?">
  Both drew on one budget and one public's trust. As the war's cost rose, critics such as King argued it was starving the fight against poverty, and the quarrel split the people who had backed both.
</Checkpoint>

## What the decade settled, and what is still argued

Sort the decade's advantages into the series' three kinds. Geography and timing: a long boom paid for two wars, abroad and on poverty, for a while, and an ocean kept the fighting far away. Choices and policy: the civil rights laws, the 1965 immigration law, Medicare, and escalation in Vietnam; the economic historian Gavin Wright argues in Sharing the Prize (2013) that civil rights brought Black Southerners large gains without making white Southerners poorer. The troubles of other nations: the lead from rivals' ruin was fading, while refugees from communist countries gained a door of their own. Historians disagree about the Cold War itself. Dudziak counts the Soviet rivalry as pressure that helped end segregation; Jacquelyn Dowd Hall counts its anticommunism as a force that broke the movement's labor wing in the late 1940s.

Three readings of the decade compete.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Classical narrative">

**Who holds it:** much public memory; narrative histories of the King years, such as Taylor Branch's Parting the Waters (1988); and the historians Sundiata Keita Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang, who in 2007 defended the 1954 to 1965 movement as a distinct era.

**What it protects:** credit and cause: a specific movement of nonviolent protest, allied with courts, presidents and a bipartisan Senate, ended legal segregation in about a decade.

**Its argument:** it argues that the tactics and alliances of those years explain why Jim Crow fell when it did, and Cha-Jua and Lang argue that a movement with no fixed beginning or end blurs differences between eras and regions.

**Its evidence:** it points to Brown, Montgomery, a cloture vote won by 44 Democrats and 27 Republicans, and a quarter of a million new voters by the end of 1965.

  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Long civil rights movement">

**Who holds it:** Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, whose 2005 article set it out, and the historians who followed her.

**What it protects:** the movement's full agenda: jobs, wages, housing and union rights alongside the ballot and the lunch counter.

**Its argument:** Hall argues that the movement took root in the labor and left politics of the late 1930s, grew in the 1940s as what Robert Korstad called civil rights unionism, reached far beyond the South, and did not end in 1965. A short story, she argues, lets the country celebrate a finished victory and set aside the economic goals that remained.

**Its evidence:** it points to the late 1940s, when an anticommunist drive helped break the alliance of Black workers and unions, and to King's last campaigns against poverty and the war.

  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Conservative and libertarian critique">

**Who holds it:** writers such as Charles Murray, Milton Friedman and Thomas Sowell, in different ways, drawing on conservatism and [[ideology:libertarianism]].

**What it protects:** equal treatment under law and the effort of the poor. It honors the laws of 1964 and 1965; its quarrel is with parts of the Great Society's design.

**Its argument:** Murray argues in Losing Ground (1984) that anti-poverty policy from the mid-1960s made it sensible in the short run for poor young people not to work or marry. Friedman had argued in Capitalism and Freedom (1962) for cash through a negative income tax instead of a patchwork of agencies. Sowell argues in Civil Rights: Rhetoric or Reality? (1984) that Black incomes were rising before 1964, through education and migration.

**Its evidence:** it points to the official poverty rate, which fell from 22.4 to 19.0 percent between 1959 and 1964, before most of the Great Society was law, reached 11.1 percent in 1973, and did not drop below that again until 2019.

  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The evidence settles some of this: segregation by law ended, the immigration law stopped sorting people by birthplace, and measured poverty fell sharply and then stalled. It cannot settle which reading is right, because the three disagree about what counts as the movement and what counts as success.

The decade ended with its largest commitments, equal citizenship at home and a war abroad, still being paid for. Lesson 8 follows the bill: the end of the gold-linked dollar in 1971, oil shocks in 1973 and 1979, inflation with unemployment, and rivals in Europe and Japan. How much of that turn began in the 1960s, and how much arrived from abroad, is its question.
