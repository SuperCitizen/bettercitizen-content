# How did the young republic grow so fast, and who paid for it?

On April 11, 1803, Robert Livingston, the American minister in Paris, was trying to buy New Orleans; he and James Monroe could pay up to $10 million for the city and the Floridas. That day the French foreign minister, Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand, changed the question. "M. Talleyrand asked me this day, when pressing the subject, whether we wished to have the whole of Louisiana," Livingston reported to Secretary of State James Madison. Less than three weeks later, on April 30, the Americans signed for all of it: about 828,000 square miles for $15 million, roughly 4 cents an acre. The country doubled in size in one treaty, and the reason lay on a Caribbean island where enslaved people had risen against slavery and were destroying a French army.

The census counted 9.6 million Americans in 1820 and 31.4 million in 1860, by which time the country reached the Pacific. Where did that growth come from, and who paid for it? This lesson sorts the era's advantages into three kinds: geography and timing, choices and policy, and the troubles of other nations.

## Why did Napoleon sell half a continent?

France had acquired Louisiana from Spain in 1800. Napoleon Bonaparte's plan was to revive a French empire in the Americas: retake the sugar colony of Saint-Domingue from the enslaved people who had risen there in 1791, and use Louisiana as the granary that fed it. Americans watched with alarm, because the produce of the West reached the ocean through New Orleans. "There is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural and habitual enemy," Jefferson wrote. "It is New Orleans."

The plan died in Saint-Domingue. The army Napoleon sent under his brother-in-law, General Leclerc, fought a revolution led first by Toussaint Louverture and then by Jean-Jacques Dessalines, and it fought yellow fever, which at one point was killing 100 to 120 men a day. With his army dying and a new war with Britain threatening, Napoleon gave up on Louisiana. In 1804 the colony became independent Haiti. The United States, a slaveholding republic that had just profited from the Haitian Revolution, did not recognize Haiti until 1862.

So the Purchase is the purest case of the troubles of other nations. It also took a choice: Jefferson, who read the Constitution strictly, could find no clause empowering him to buy territory, and set his doubts aside. And what France sold was only a claim, made under a European legal theory called the Doctrine of Discovery, to land that Native nations had lived on for millennia. The Purchase left the United States to deal with those nations directly for their land.

## Whose land was the West?

East of the Mississippi, much of the land that cotton planters wanted in the Southeast belonged to the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole nations. The Indian Removal Act, which President Andrew Jackson signed on May 28, 1830, authorized the president to negotiate removal treaties with nations living east of the river and send them to land in the west. The House passed it 102 to 97, with northern members almost all opposed.

Between 1831 and 1838 all five nations were forced from their homelands toward Indian Territory, in today's Oklahoma. In May 1838 the army began rounding up about 19,000 Cherokees. A missionary doctor, Elizur Butler, estimated that more than 4,000 of them, a fifth of the Cherokee people, died along what became known as the [[term:trail-of-tears]]. Counting every nation moved west in the 1830s and 1840s, the historian Jeffrey Ostler estimates that about 88,000 people were forced out and that 12,000 to 17,000 died.

Further west, the United States annexed Texas in 1845 and declared war on Mexico in May 1846. In the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo of 1848, Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles, 55 percent of its territory, for $15 million. The phrase for all of it came from an unsigned 1845 essay in the *Democratic Review*, usually credited to its editor, John L. O'Sullivan, who wrote of "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions." Whether [[term:manifest-destiny]] was a national vision or a name for conquest is one of the oldest arguments in American history.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Expansion as national vision">

The reading's founding text is Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 address to the American Historical Association: "The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development." Turner argued that cheap land on a moving frontier let ordinary newcomers own farms, and that this gave Americans a distinct character and a more democratic politics. The reading protects what its holders see as a real achievement: a republic of landowning farmers. Its holders also point to the world beyond: the historian Sam Haynes has shown how fear of Britain shaped the drive to annex Texas. In a continent still ringed by empires, this reading argues, a continental republic was a matter of security too.

  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Expansion as conquest">

The New Western History, whose landmark is Patricia Nelson Limerick's *The Legacy of Conquest* (1987), argues that the West was never "free land." It was other people's country, taken by treaties signed under pressure, by removal and by war, and the contest over its land did not end when Turner declared the frontier closed. The reading protects the people the national story left out. Its holders point to the record: a removal act that passed the House by five votes, tens of thousands driven from their homes, and a war that took more than half of Mexico. Ostler goes further and argues that removal meets the definition of genocide, because the government kept pursuing it after its deadly effects were known. On this reading, "manifest destiny" was the language a conquering people used to describe a choice as fate.

  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## How much of the economy rested on slavery?

The land taken from Native nations became cotton land, worked by enslaved people. The census counted 1,538,022 enslaved people in 1820 and 3,953,760 in 1860, about one American in eight. Many were sold from the older states to the new cotton country, through markets such as New Orleans, the largest in the Southwest. Their cotton fed the mills of Britain, the heart of the [[term:industrial-revolution]], which by Paul Bairoch's estimates made about a fifth of the world's manufactured goods by 1860. Southern planters captured a dominant share of the Atlantic cotton trade, and cotton was the key American export. The amount an enslaved worker picked in a day rose three to four times between 1800 and 1860.

Both sides of the current debate accept those facts, and that slavery was profitable to slaveholders and rested on violence. They argue over how central it was to growth.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="New history of capitalism">

Historians of what they call the new history of [[term:capitalism]], among them Edward Baptist (*The Half Has Never Been Told*, 2014) and Sven Beckert (*Empire of Cotton*, 2014), argue that slavery was an engine of modern capitalism. Baptist argues that "slavery's expansion was the driving force of US history between the framing of the Constitution and the beginning of the Civil War," and that rising picking rates came from an escalating system of torture. He puts cotton's direct and indirect role at almost half of American economic activity in 1836. Beckert calls the system "war capitalism": the violent seizure of land and labor, combined with new machines and wage workers. He notes that enslaved people, counted as property, were worth more than all the North's industrial capital. The reading protects the place of the enslaved at the center of the story of American wealth, and its holders point to the testimony of formerly enslaved people, slave-market records and the credit planters raised on human collateral.

  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Economic historians' critique">

Economic historians, led here by Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode (2018), argue that the new history gets the numbers wrong. Baptist's own figure puts the cotton crop around 1836 at about $77 million, some 5 percent of the economy's output; reaching "almost half" required adding sales of land and of enslaved people, which are not output, and inputs already counted in cotton's price. The economist Peter Rousseau puts cotton at 5 to 6 percent of output. On productivity, Olmstead and Rhode, drawing on more than 600,000 daily picking records, argue that the gains came mainly from new cotton varieties with larger bolls that were easier to pick. The reading protects the standard of evidence; Olmstead reports a consensus in his field that slavery was detrimental to economic development. Its holders point out that capitalism survived emancipation and cotton output soared after the war.

  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## Who built the market revolution, and who came to work in it?

Before the Erie Canal opened in October 1825, moving a ton of wheat from New York's Genesee Valley to Albany by wagon took 20 days and cost $100. After it, a ton could reach New York City in 10 days for $5. The canal was a state-owned waterway, built by New York, and public help ran through the era's other networks too. Railroads grew from about 3,000 miles of track in 1840 to about 30,000 by 1860. In 1843 Congress paid $30,000 for Samuel Morse's experimental telegraph line, over which he sent "What hath God wrought" from the Capitol to Baltimore on May 24, 1844. In 1850 Congress granted Illinois, Mississippi and Alabama alternate sections of public land along the route of a railroad from Chicago to Mobile: a [[term:subsidy]] paid in land, much of it acquired from Native nations. The self-reliant farmer was real, and he shipped his wheat on a public canal.

Workers came from abroad. A potato blight reached Ireland in the fall of 1845, and the famine that followed killed about one million people, an eighth of the population. Hard times in the German states sent others. By 1860 the census counted 1,611,304 Irish-born and 1,276,075 German-born residents, about seven of every ten people born abroad. The reaction was fierce and largely anti-Catholic. In Philadelphia in 1844, a riot lasting four days burned two Catholic churches and left at least 14 dead. A secret society whose members answered questions with "I know nothing" grew into the American Party, which held 51 of the House's 234 seats in the Congress of 1855 to 1857 before the fight over slavery broke it apart.

## What 1860 settled, and what it left open

All three kinds of advantage appear. Geography and timing: fertile land, the Mississippi, and Britain's mills wanting ever more cotton just as the United States gained land to grow it. Choices and policy: a purchase made despite constitutional doubts, a removal act passed by five votes, a war with Mexico, a state canal, a federal telegraph, railroad land granted by the section. The troubles of other nations: Haiti's revolution and the threat of war with Britain, which put Louisiana on the market, and Ireland's famine and Germany's hard times, which sent workers.

Historians argue about the sorting itself. Turner's "free land" treats the West as geography; Limerick's conquest treats it as a choice. The new historians of capitalism treat slave-grown cotton as the engine of growth; Olmstead and Rhode credit its gains largely to better seed and count slavery a drag. The costs are not in dispute. Settlers gained farms, planters and merchants fortunes, and immigrants work along with hostility. Native nations lost their homelands and thousands of lives, Mexico lost more than half its territory, nearly four million people lived enslaved in 1860, and Haitians won their freedom by revolution.

The war with Mexico also reopened the most dangerous question in American politics. In 1846 the Wilmot Proviso proposed that slavery never exist in land taken from Mexico; Southern senators blocked it, and whether slavery could spread west stayed unresolved until the Confederacy's defeat in 1865. The next lesson takes up that war, what it settled and what it did not, and why Britain and France chose not to intervene, though Britain's mills ran on Southern cotton.
