# What did the Civil War settle?

At about four in the morning on May 13, 1862, a Confederate steamer called the *Planter* gave the proper signal to the sentry at Fort Sumter and was allowed to pass. Its pilot, Robert Smalls, was enslaved, and so were the crewmen, women and children aboard. Past the fort he surrendered the ship to the USS *Onward*, one of the Navy ships blockading Charleston. Thirteen years later Smalls took a seat in the U.S. House of Representatives. Twenty years after that, at South Carolina's constitutional convention of 1895, he was one of six Black delegates, speaking against changes designed to remove Black South Carolinians from the voter rolls. One life ran the arc of this lesson: slavery, war, freedom, the vote, and its loss.

## Why did the South leave? Read what it said at the time

South Carolina left the Union in December 1860, weeks after Abraham Lincoln's election, and ten more states followed it into the Confederacy. Four published formal declarations of their causes: the reasons secessionists gave at the time, to each other and, in Georgia's phrase, to "the world."

South Carolina's, adopted on December 24, 1860, blamed "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery." Mississippi's second sentence reads: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world." Georgia's priced the stake at "$3,000,000,000 of our property," meaning people; the economic historian Roger Ransom values the four million people enslaved in 1860 at "close to $3 billion."

After the war a different story took hold. Edward Pollard's *The Lost Cause* (1866) gave it a name: a noble, doomed defense of states' rights, with slavery a side issue. The documents overturn it. South Carolina's grievance was Northern states' rights: it named thirteen Northern states whose laws "either nullify the Acts of Congress or render useless any attempt to execute them," the federal laws for returning people who escaped slavery. [[term:tariff]] grievances fare no better. Georgia's declaration, the one most concerned with economics, says the South had already won that fight: after 1846, "free trade, low duties, and economy in public expenditures was the verdict of the American people." What it feared was a party whose "mission and its purpose" was "anti-slavery."

Historians treat this as a finding. Charles Dew's *Apostles of Disunion* (2001) found the same message in the speeches of the commissioners the seceding states sent to other slave states, and the National Park Service's 2017 study of the era describes a Lost Cause "mythology" whose memorials obscured "the cause and nature of the conflict."

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what do the secession documents show about why the war came?">
The seceding states said at the time that they were leaving to protect slavery from a national majority hostile to it. The states'-rights and tariff explanations came later, and the declarations undercut both.
</Checkpoint>

The method works on any claim about motives.

<Checkpoint question="A school textbook from the 1910s tells you the South seceded over the tariff. Using this section's method, how would you test that claim, and what would you expect to find?">
Go to what secessionists wrote when they acted, not to accounts written after defeat: South Carolina and Mississippi rest their case on slavery, and Georgia says the tariff fight was already won. A reason first offered decades later, in defense of a side's memory, is weaker evidence.
</Checkpoint>

## What did the Union build while it fought?

The war lasted four years and killed about 625,000 soldiers, by Ransom's count. The economists Claudia Goldin and Frank Lewis put its direct cost at $6.6 billion, one and a half times the nation's whole output in 1860.

The Union fought first to restore the Union, and the war against slavery grew out of it. Enslaved people fled to Union lines by the thousands, in what W. E. B. Du Bois would call "a general strike against the slave system." On January 1, 1863, the [[term:emancipation-proclamation]] declared that those held as slaves in the rebelling states "are, and henceforward shall be free." It exempted the loyal border states and areas the Union already held, and its promise depended on victory. But it opened the army and navy to Black men, and almost 200,000 fought for the Union. The proclamation ends by invoking "the considerate judgment of mankind," a line written for a watching world.

With Southern members gone, Congress also remade the economy. The Homestead Act (May 1862) offered 160 acres of public land for five years' residence and a small fee. The Pacific Railway Act (July 1862) gave federal bonds and land grants to the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific, whose crews, more than 8,000 European immigrants on one line and more than 10,000 Chinese laborers on the other, met at Promontory, Utah, in May 1869. The Morrill Act gave each state 30,000 acres per member of Congress to endow colleges of agriculture and the mechanic arts. The national banking acts of 1863 and 1864 created a uniform currency backed by Treasury bonds.

These laws complicate the myth of self-reliance. Families who "made it on their own" did so on land the government gave away, much of it taken from Native nations, reached by railroads it financed. And most public land never reached homesteaders: of some 500 million acres disposed of between 1862 and 1904, about 80 million did.

Two things stand out. A small government put more than a million people in uniform by 1865, and in 1870 more than two-fifths of its spending was interest on the war's debt. And by the Maddison Project's estimates, American output per person slipped from about 87 to about 82 percent of Britain's in the war decade.

It freed and armed enslaved people, raised an army of a million, and through Congress gave away land, backed railroads, endowed colleges and created a national currency. Federal spending nearly tripled as a share of the economy between 1860 and 1870.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did Britain and France stay out?

The Confederacy's best hope lay across the Atlantic. By the late 1850s, the historian Sven Beckert writes, cotton grown in the United States made up 77 percent of the 800 million pounds Britain's mills consumed, and Southern leaders expected that dependence to bring Britain in.

The bet failed step by step. Lincoln proclaimed a blockade on April 19, 1861, and Britain answered on May 13 with neutrality, treating the Confederacy as a belligerent, entitled to buy and borrow abroad, but not as a nation. When a U.S. captain seized two Confederate envoys from the British mail ship *Trent* that November, Britain sent troops to Canada, and Lincoln's government released the envoys rather than risk a second war. Textile districts in Britain and France suffered high unemployment, yet neither government recognized the Confederacy; Napoleon III, busy installing an emperor in Mexico, would not act without Britain. British-built raiders such as the *Alabama* sank more than 150 Northern ships, and in 1872 an arbitration ordered Britain to pay the United States $15.5 million.

Why cotton diplomacy failed is still argued.

<PerspectiveTabs>
<Perspective name="The battlefield decided it">

Europe recognizes winners. The State Department's historians judge that the most important factor "rises from the battlefields": the Confederacy never won enough victories in a row to convince Europe it could last. Howard Jones's *Union in Peril* (1992) traces how seriously Britain's cabinet weighed mediation in 1862. On this reading, Union staying power kept recognition out of reach.

</Perspective>
<Perspective name="Slavery made the South hard to back">

Once the Union tied the war to freedom, no British government could back a slaveholders' republic without trouble at home. The State Department's historians credit "the success of Union efforts to link the Confederacy with the institution of slavery," and the abolitionist feeling that restrained a prime minister, Lord Palmerston, who sympathized with the South. Don Doyle's *The Cause of All Nations* (2015) shows Union diplomats winning Europe's public opinion.

</Perspective>
<Perspective name="Britain's own interests ruled">

Britain stayed out because staying out paid. War with the United States would have exposed Canada, and by respecting the blockade the leading naval power kept a precedent it might need itself. Europe had its own crises in Poland and Denmark, and later in the war Britain found new cotton in Egypt and India. Frank Owsley's *King Cotton Diplomacy* (1931) concluded that neither Britain nor France would risk a clash with the United States.

</Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The first two readings credit Union choices, the third other nations' circumstances; the evidence fits all three in part, so the argument is over the weighting.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why did the Confederacy's bet on cotton fail?">
Britain needed Southern cotton but never needed the Confederacy: it accepted the blockade, found other suppliers and judged recognition not worth a war. Historians weigh the reasons differently, but none sees cotton forcing Europe's hand.
</Checkpoint>

## Reconstruction: a second founding, and its undoing

Victory left open what freedom would mean for some four million people. Part of the answer went into the Constitution as the [[term:civil-war-amendments]]. The Thirteenth (1865) abolished slavery "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." The Fourteenth (1868) made "All persons born or naturalized in the United States" citizens and barred any state from denying "the equal protection of the laws." The Fifteenth (1870) forbade denying the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Each let Congress enforce it, so a Constitution that had mostly limited the national government now made it a guarantor of rights against the states: in Eric Foner's phrase, *The Second Founding* (2019).

[[term:reconstruction]] built a democracy the South had never had. Black men there voted from 1867; about 2,000 held local office before the century ended, and seventeen were elected to Congress, Smalls among them. Republican governments created the South's first public school systems. Land, what freedpeople wanted most, mostly never came; most worked for white landowners for wages or a share of the crop.

White Democrats answered with fraud and terror. Federal prosecutions broke the Ku Klux Klan in South Carolina for a time, but at Colfax, Louisiana, on Easter Sunday 1873, a white militia killed scores of Black men, most after they surrendered, and the Supreme Court's ruling in *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876), that the enforcement law reached too far, let the killers go free. After the disputed election of 1876 a commission gave every contested electoral vote to Rutherford B. Hayes, by one vote, and in April 1877 Hayes withdrew the troops guarding the statehouses of South Carolina and Louisiana. The last Republican governments fell, and in time [[term:jim-crow]] followed.

Historians have judged those years three ways.

<PerspectiveTabs>
<Perspective name="The Dunning School">

In the early twentieth century William Dunning of Columbia University and his students cast Reconstruction as a catastrophic error, driven by Northern vindictiveness, that put carpetbaggers and Black Southerners over loyal white Southerners. They pointed to corruption in the new governments; their premise was that Black Americans were at best second-class citizens. The view echoed through the film *The Birth of a Nation* (1915). Later scholarship, the Park Service study records, "challenged and often discredited" it.

</Perspective>
<Perspective name="Du Bois's Black Reconstruction">

W. E. B. Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction* (1935) made freedpeople the story's actors: their flight to Union lines was a general strike, and their votes built a democracy whose work included, in one chapter title, "Founding the Public School." It fell to a "Counter-Revolution of Property," and his last chapter, "The Propaganda of History," indicted the historians who had told it otherwise. Modern scholars often cite their debt to him.

</Perspective>
<Perspective name="Foner's synthesis">

Eric Foner's *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988) gathered the scholarship since the 1960s into the synthesis the Park Service study cites most. It dates the era from emancipation, puts Black Southerners' aims at the center, credits lasting achievements, and explains the defeat by Southern terror and a Northern loss of will, sped by the depression after 1873. Its verdict is its title: unfinished, not failed.

</Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Du Bois stresses class conflict and Foner the meaning of freedom, but both rest on evidence the Dunning School ignored. The factual quarrel with Dunning is settled; why the nation let Reconstruction go is not.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did Reconstruction build, and what ended it?">
It wrote freedom, citizenship, equal protection and Black men's vote into the Constitution, and built biracial governments and the South's first public schools. Terror, narrow court rulings, a depression and a North that stopped enforcing the law ended it by 1877.
</Checkpoint>

Now test the amendments' words with no one enforcing them.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose a Southern state in 1890 lets a man vote only if he pays a poll tax and passes a reading test, and its law never mentions race. Does the Fifteenth Amendment's text forbid it, and what does your answer show?">
Not on its face: the law names none of the grounds the amendment forbids, race, color or previous condition of servitude. Whether it falls depends on courts and Congress weighing purpose and effect: enforcement, not text alone, decided what the amendments meant. In *Williams v. Mississippi* (1898) the Supreme Court accepted the South's disfranchisement.
</Checkpoint>

## What the war settled, and what it left open

The war settled two questions by force: a state cannot leave the Union, and a person cannot be owned. A third, whether the freed would be equal citizens, it settled only on paper. The amendments wrote equality into the Constitution, and the end of Reconstruction left it unenforced for most of a century.

Sorted, the era's advantages look like this.

| Advantage | Kind |
| --- | --- |
| An ocean between the war and Europe's armies; a continent of western land | Geography and timing |
| Union diplomacy, emancipation and almost 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors | Choices and policy |
| Free land, federally backed railroads, land-grant colleges, a national currency | Choices and policy, on land taken from Native nations |
| Europe's crises in Poland and Denmark; France's venture in Mexico; Britain's new cotton from Egypt and India | Troubles of other nations |

Two sortings are disputed. One is Britain's neutrality, argued in the tabs above. The other is older: did the wartime laws make the industrial rise, or ride it? Charles and Mary Beard (1927) called the war a "Second American Revolution" that put Northern industry in command, while the economic historian Thomas Cochran argued in 1961 that the war slowed industrial growth, as this lesson's Maddison estimates suggest for the decade. The costs fell unevenly: on the dead, on freedpeople left without land, on Native nations whose land endowed colleges and railroads, and on the white South, whose income per person, by an estimate Ransom reports, fell from $125 in 1857 to just over $80 in 1879.

The next lesson begins on the railroads. The depression of 1873 started when Jay Cooke's banking house, dragged down by railroad bonds, failed; 89 of the nation's 364 railroads went bankrupt. The industrial giant of 1870 to 1914 would be built on the war's land, laws and money, and its corporations would claim the Fourteenth Amendment's protection as legal "persons." Whether that rise was the war's dividend or the continent finally paying out is lesson 4's question.
