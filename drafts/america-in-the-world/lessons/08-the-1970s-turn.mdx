# Why did the postwar boom end in the 1970s?

On Sunday, August 15, 1971, Richard Nixon went on national television with three announcements. He ordered "a freeze on all prices and wages throughout the United States for a period of 90 days." He put a tax of 10 percent on imported goods. And he said he had "directed Secretary Connally to suspend temporarily the convertibility of the dollar into gold." The suspension was never lifted. Within a decade Americans had waited in lines for gasoline, seen prices rise more than 14 percent in a year, and heard a president speak of "a crisis of confidence." Was the boom bound to end, or was it ended by choices?

## Why couldn't the dollar stay as good as gold?

Lesson 6 described the Bretton Woods system of 1944. Other currencies were pegged to the dollar, and the dollar was tied to gold at $35 an ounce, a price Congress had set. A foreign government could trade its dollars for American gold. Because the United States held about three-quarters of the world's official gold reserves, the Federal Reserve's historians write, "the system seemed secure."

The trouble was arithmetic. The world needed dollars to trade and to hold in reserve, and it got them as America sent dollars abroad in aid, military spending and investment, while America's gold did not grow. In 1960 the economist Robert Triffin warned Congress of the bind: without American deficits the world would run short of reserves, but with them, confidence in the dollar would erode. Picture a coat check that has handed out more tickets than it holds coats: it works until people doubt it and all come back at once. The image breaks in one place: people wanted the tickets for their own sake, because the world traded in dollars.

By 1971 there were more foreign-held dollars than American gold, and the State Department's historians find "mounting evidence that the overvalued dollar was undermining the nation's foreign trading position." Part of the cause was a success: Europe and Japan had rebuilt, and their goods now competed.

Nixon decided at Camp David with fifteen advisers, among them Fed Chairman Arthur Burns and a Treasury undersecretary named Paul Volcker. The December 1971 Smithsonian Agreement set new fixed rates around a devalued dollar, but in March 1973 six European Community members "jointly floated against the U.S. dollar," and the fixed rates of Bretton Woods were over. In the Fed historians' words, "the international monetary system turned into a fiat one." [[term:fiat-currency]] is money backed by trust in its issuer, not by metal. The dollar lost its gold but not its place: in 2024 it still made up 58 percent of the foreign reserves governments disclose.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why could the United States not keep turning foreign governments' dollars into gold at $35 an ounce?">
  The world needed ever more dollars, and America supplied them by spending abroad, but its gold did not grow to match. Once foreign-held dollars outnumbered the gold, the promise held only while nobody tested it.
</Checkpoint>

Any country that promises to swap its money for something scarce can meet the same squeeze.

<Checkpoint question="A small country pegs its currency to the dollar and promises to swap its notes for dollars on demand. It has issued far more notes than it holds in dollars, and its exporters start asking for dollars. What choices does it have, and what does 1971 suggest about waiting?">
  It can make money scarcer by raising interest rates, at the cost of a slump; it can devalue; or it can stop swapping, as Nixon did. Waiting narrows the choices, because each doubt sends more holders to the window.
</Checkpoint>

## How did oil and inflation become one story?

Geography had given the United States an advantage few rivals matched: its own oil. American crude production set a record in 1970 that stood until 2018, but demand outran it, and by 1973, the State Department's historians write, the economy had "grown increasingly dependent on foreign oil."

During the Arab-Israeli war of October 1973 the United States resupplied Israel's military, and on October 19 the Arab oil producers' organization imposed an [[term:embargo]] on the United States, lifted in March 1974. Oil nearly quadrupled in price, from $2.90 a barrel to $11.65 by January 1974. Then revolution in Iran cut its output by 4.8 million barrels a day, about 7 percent of world production, and from April 1979 to April 1980 the price more than doubled.

[[term:stagflation]] is slow growth and high unemployment with rising prices, all at once. In 1964 [[term:inflation]] was 1 percent and unemployment 5 percent; ten years later inflation was over 12 percent and unemployment above 7 percent. Economists had expected more inflation to buy less unemployment. "The trade-off that policymakers hoped to exploit did not exist," writes the Federal Reserve historian Michael Bryan. Scarcer money fights rising prices but costs jobs; easier money saves jobs but feeds the rise. Meanwhile inflation shrank savings and incomes fixed in dollars. Those facts are not in dispute. Their cause is.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="The monetary explanation">
    Held by the Federal Reserve's own historians and by economists in the line of Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps, whose warnings, Bryan writes, proved right. It holds central banks answerable for inflation. The Great Inflation came from "policies that allowed for an excessive growth in the supply of money," as the Fed "accommodated large and rising fiscal imbalances." The economist Allan Meltzer's history of the Fed (2009) documents "the role of Keynesian thinking in creating the Great inflation." The evidence: inflation began climbing in the mid-1960s, before any oil shock, and Robert Barsky and Lutz Kilian (2002) show that swings in money can produce stagflation "even in the absence of supply shocks."
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The supply-shock explanation">
    Held by economists such as Alan Blinder, who made the case in earlier work and revisited it with Jeremy Rudd in 2013. It keeps in view the shocks no central bank controls. The worst bursts of inflation came from two OPEC oil shocks, two food-price shocks, and the end of Nixon's wage and price controls in 1973 and 1974. Testing that account against new data and models, Blinder and Rudd conclude that the "old-fashioned" explanation holds up quite well. The evidence: the two worst bursts of inflation arrived with the two oil shocks, and no central bank can print or prevent an embargo.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Both sides accept what Barsky and Kilian call "the striking coincidence of major oil price increases and worsening stagflation." Which way the cause ran is still argued.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what is stagflation, and why did it leave policymakers without an easy move?">
  Stagflation is rising prices together with slow growth and high unemployment. The usual cure for each problem worsened the other: scarcer money fights inflation but costs jobs, and easier money saves jobs but feeds inflation.
</Checkpoint>

The two explanations predict different things.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose a drought doubles food prices in one year, while the central bank holds money growth steady. What would each explanation predict about inflation over the next five years?">
  Both predict a jump in prices that year, which the supply-shock view treats as the main event. The monetary view expects it to fade, because lasting inflation needs a central bank that accommodates it.
</Checkpoint>

## Was America declining, or were others catching up?

On July 1, 1980, Treasury Secretary G. William Miller warned President Carter that imports' share of the American car market had risen from 17.7 percent in 1978 to 26.4 percent in early 1980, as buyers turned to small cars, and that Japan supplied 80 percent of those imports. Indefinite layoffs had reached 239,000, about 70 percent of them in Michigan, Ohio, Missouri and Indiana. Steel had gone first: in 1977 the closing of Youngstown Sheet and Tube's mills in Ohio put about 5,000 people out of work. The longer record tells a more exact story.

Most of the fall in America's share of world output came between 1950 and 1970, before either oil shock, from about 27 to about 22 percent; across the 1970s it slipped only to about 21. Over those years, output per American went from 2.1 times the Western European average to 1.5 times. Studying a century of evidence in 1986, the economic historian Moses Abramovitz found that countries further behind tend to grow faster than the leader, a pattern he called catching up: a follower can adopt methods the leader has proved. The Marshall Plan had set out to rebuild Western Europe; the narrowing gap was partly its success.

The 1970s loss was concentrated in manufacturing, where America's share of world value added fell from 28 or 29 percent to 20 or 21 percent. These figures use market exchange rates, so part of that fall is the cheaper dollar itself, and they cannot be joined to lesson 5's figure for 1938, which used Paul Bairoch's different measure. A real loss at home ran alongside: productivity, the output of an hour of work, grew 2.8 percent a year in nonfarm business from 1947 to 1973 and 1.2 percent from 1973 to 1979, a slowdown that lasted until about 1995. Productivity is what lets pay rise without prices rising. Trade grew from 8 to 13 percent of the economy between 1950 and 1973, so shocks abroad reached home faster. And only 4.7 percent of residents in 1970 were foreign-born, the smallest share since the count began in 1850, a figure the 1965 law from lesson 7 was beginning to change.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what is the difference between America declining and others catching up, and which does the record from 1950 to 1980 fit better?">
  Catching up means others growing faster from further behind, so America's share shrinks while its own output rises. Most of the fall came between 1950 and 1970, as Europe and Japan rebuilt; the 1970s added real losses in manufacturing and productivity.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did Washington change its tools?

Washington's tools changed, though not in a straight line. In August 1971 a Republican president froze wages and prices; the controls, the Fed's historians judge, "only temporarily slowed the rise in prices while exacerbating shortages." That month Nixon also backed a federal guarantee of a $250 million bank loan to Lockheed. In 1978 a Democratic president, Jimmy Carter, signed the Airline Deregulation Act, crediting Senator Ted Kennedy: "For the first time in decades, we have deregulated a major industry." In January 1980 Carter signed a law allowing federal guarantees of up to $1.5 billion in Chrysler loans, if its unionized workers gave up at least $462.5 million in wages and benefits.

Under presidents of both parties, two industrial giants leaned on Washington, and the promise of self-reliance kept returning. Nixon announced Project Independence in November 1973. In July 1979 Carter pledged that "this Nation will never use more foreign oil than we did in 1977—never." That address is remembered as the "malaise speech," but the word malaise never appears in it. What Carter said was: "It is a crisis of confidence."

The deepest turn came at the [[term:federal-reserve]]. Carter named Paul Volcker, the undersecretary from Camp David, its chairman, and on October 6, 1979, Volcker said the Fed would steer by bank reserves to "get firmer control over the growth in money supply." Fighting inflation, in Bryan's account, was now seen as necessary for both of the Fed's goals. Abroad, a decade opened by Nixon's visit to China in 1972 closed with Soviet troops in Afghanistan.

Two items resist the sorting: was the Great Inflation a trouble from abroad or a choice at home, and was the end of the boom timing or choice?

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="An extraordinary era ended">
    Held by the writer Marc Levinson in *An Extraordinary Time* (2016), the economist Robert Gordon in *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016) and the catch-up tradition of Abramovitz. It guards against blaming policy for what history did. The quarter century after 1945 was the exception. Levinson treats 1973 as the year broadly shared, rapid growth gave way to slower growth, driven by trends largely immune to economic reforms. Gordon argues that the inventions of 1870 to 1970 changed daily life on a scale that cannot be repeated. The evidence: most of America's relative decline came before 1970, and the productivity slowdown outlasted two decades of different policies under both parties.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Choices ended it">
    Held by scholars who disagree about which choices were wrong but agree they were choices. It insists that the people who decided could have decided otherwise. The historian Judith Stein's *Pivotal Decade* (2010), subtitled *How the United States Traded Factories for Finance in the Seventies*, argues that Washington chose to fight inflation rather than unemployment, and a balanced budget over growth. From the other side, Meltzer faults a Fed that let money grow too fast for too long. The evidence: when the Fed changed course in 1979, the stagflation cycle broke, at the price of what the Fed's historians call the most severe recession since the Great Depression; and Washington chose which firms to rescue and which industries to deregulate.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did American economic policy turn away from in the 1970s, and what did it turn toward?">
  Away from controlling prices directly and from putting full employment first; toward markets setting prices in industries like the airlines, and a central bank that put inflation first. It was not a straight line: the same years brought rescues of Lockheed and Chrysler.
</Checkpoint>

The 1971 tools come back whenever prices rise fast.

<Checkpoint question="A future president, facing fast-rising prices, proposes freezing all prices and wages for 90 days. Drawing on this lesson, what would you expect, and what would each side of the inflation argument want to know first?">
  The 1971 controls slowed prices only for a while and worsened shortages, so expect a pause, then a rebound. The monetary side would ask whether money growth is being reined in; the supply-shock side, whether a one-time shock is driving prices, which a freeze cannot cure.
</Checkpoint>

## What the 1970s settled, and what they left open

The gold link ended for good, though the dollar kept its place. Foreign oil became a lasting concern. Productivity growth slowed for two decades. The Great Inflation, which Bryan dates from 1965 to 1982, ended only after the Fed changed course in 1979, at the price of a deep recession. The costs fell hardest on the autoworkers and steelworkers of the industrial Midwest, and on anyone whose savings or income was fixed in dollars.

It did not settle whether central bankers or shocks from abroad made the Great Inflation, or whether the postwar boom was an extraordinary era that had to end or an achievement that choices let slip. The next lesson follows what Americans did with the tools the 1970s left them, from 1980 to 2008: deregulation, open markets, the end of the Cold War and China's entry into the World Trade Organization.
