# How did America become the world's creditor, and why did it turn inward?

On July 31, 1914, the New York Stock Exchange did not open. War had broken out in Europe, and foreign investors were rushing to sell American stocks and bonds and turn the proceeds into gold. Treasury Secretary William McAdoo kept the exchange shut until December 12. That summer the United States still owed the world more than the world owed it. Five years later, Europe's governments owed the US Treasury more than $10 billion. This lesson follows the reversal to 1941, when a country that had turned inward passed a bill numbered H.R. 1776.

## How did a war in Europe make America the world's creditor?

In mid-1914 foreigners held about $7.2 billion of investments in the United States, roughly twice the $3.5 billion that Americans owned privately abroad. The country was a net debtor, owing the world more than it was owed.

The war turned the flow around. For more than two years the United States stayed neutral while, in the State Department historians' words, "supplying munitions and financial assistance to the Allies." Germany's renewed submarine attacks brought it into the war in April 1917, after Woodrow Wilson told Congress that "The world must be made safe for democracy." Active-duty forces grew from about 155,000 in 1913 to nearly 2.9 million in 1918, and the Treasury lent the Allies more than $10 billion. By 1919 Americans' private investments abroad had doubled to about $7.0 billion, while foreign holdings in the United States had fallen to about $3.3 billion. The pre-1919 figures are rough, but the reversal is not in doubt.

The peace did not follow Wilson's design. The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles in November 1919 and again in March 1920, and the United States never joined the League of Nations. Washington refused to cancel the war debts, and the Fordney-McCumber Tariff Act of 1922 raised tariffs, which made it harder for Europeans to earn the dollars to pay them. A circle kept the system running: American banks lent to Germany, Germany paid reparations to Britain and France, and they paid the United States. When the lending stopped, the circle broke, and by mid-1933 every European debtor except Finland had defaulted.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how did the United States go from debtor to creditor between 1914 and 1919, and why did the war debts become a trap?">
  Europe sold American assets and borrowed American money to fight, so by 1919 Americans owned far more abroad than foreigners owned in America. The Allies could repay only with dollars earned by selling to America or borrowed from it, and high tariffs blocked the first route.
</Checkpoint>

## Who shared the boom of the 1920s, and who was shut out?

By the Maddison Project's estimates, the average American produced about 1.36 times as much as the average Briton in 1929. The economic historian Paul Bairoch put the American share of world manufacturing at 32.0 percent in 1913, and a secondary source gives his figure for 1928 as 39.3 percent, about four times Britain's. Maddison gives no world total for 1913 or 1929; the nearest, for 1920, puts the United States at 22.5 percent of world output. All are scholarly estimates.

The boom had a creed. Campaigning in October 1928, Herbert Hoover praised "the American system of rugged individualism" against European "doctrines of paternalism and state socialism."

Not everyone was let in to share it. The Immigration Act of 1924 capped each nationality at 2 percent of its foreign-born count in the 1890 census, taken before the largest arrivals from southern and eastern Europe, which limited what the State Department's historians call "newer immigration." It barred anyone "ineligible for citizenship" by race or nationality, which shut out immigrants from Asia, and it left the Western Hemisphere outside the quotas. Its "most basic purpose," those historians conclude, was "to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity." About 1.2 million people became permanent residents in fiscal 1914, about 294,000 in fiscal 1925, and about 23,000 in fiscal 1933, when the Depression deterred them too. The share of residents born abroad fell from 13.2 percent in 1920 to 8.8 percent in 1940.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did the Immigration Act of 1924 change, and how did its formula work?">
  It set each nationality's quota at 2 percent of its foreign-born count in the 1890 census, which shrank quotas for southern and eastern Europe. It barred Asians as ineligible for citizenship and left the Western Hemisphere outside the quotas.
</Checkpoint>

The base year looks like a technical detail. It was the policy.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose the 1924 law had kept the 2 percent rate but counted from the 1920 census instead of 1890. Who would have gained the most, and who would have gained nothing?">
  Southern and eastern Europeans, because far more of them lived in the United States by 1920 than in 1890. Asians would have gained nothing: the citizenship bar excluded them whatever the base year.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did a crash become a worldwide depression?

The crash of October 1929 began the slump; it does not explain its depth. Between 1929 and 1933 real output fell by about 26 percent, and unemployment rose from 3.2 to 24.9 percent of the labor force. About 9,000 banks suspended operations from 1930 to 1933, with no deposit insurance behind them, and the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent from late 1930 to early 1933. Prices fell too. [[term:deflation]], a general fall in prices, made every debt heavier, because incomes fell while the sums owed did not.

Measured against Britain, the fall was stark.

By these estimates output per American fell by a third from 1929 to 1933, and for two years the average Briton was ahead again. The world turned inward at the same time. A thousand economists asked President Hoover to veto the Smoot-Hawley [[term:tariff]]; he signed it on June 17, 1930, as trading partners were already raising tariffs of their own. Scholars disagree about how much harm it did, but the State Department's historians conclude that it "did nothing to foster cooperation among nations." World trade fell by some 66 percent from 1929 to 1934, and American exports and imports together fell from 11.0 percent of GDP in 1929 to 7.7 percent in 1938.

Underneath ran the gold standard. A dollar was a promise of a fixed weight of gold, $20.67 an ounce, and the system tied countries' interest rates together. When Britain left gold on September 21, 1931, the [[term:federal-reserve]] Bank of New York raised its discount rate to stop gold leaving the country, in the middle of a banking panic. The United States suspended gold in April 1933 and set a new price of $35 an ounce in 1934.

Economists agree on most of these facts and argue about which mattered most.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Monetary contraction">
    Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, in A Monetary History of the United States (1963), argued that the Federal Reserve turned a recession into a catastrophe by letting bank failures shrink the money supply instead of acting as lender of last resort. Their evidence is the size and timing of the monetary collapse, and it won over the Fed itself: in 2002 Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor, told Friedman, "Regarding the Great Depression. You're right, we did it. We're very sorry."
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The gold standard">
    Barry Eichengreen, in Golden Fetters (1992), argued that the gold standard spread the slump worldwide and kept central banks from fighting it, since to protect their gold they raised interest rates. His evidence is a natural experiment: Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs (1985) found that countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner. Charles Kindleberger (1973) argued that the system needed a leader to keep markets open and lend in a crisis, and that Britain no longer could while the United States would not.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Spending and debt">
    Peter Temin, in Did Monetary Forces Cause the Great Depression? (1976), argued that the first blow was a fall in spending, not in money, in the tradition of John Maynard Keynes. Irving Fisher argued in 1933 that debts and falling prices fed each other, as debtors' forced sales pushed prices down and real debts up. Bernanke's 1983 study argued that bank failures also cut off lending to small firms and farmers. Their evidence is that spending and credit collapsed in ways the money supply alone does not explain.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The argument is over the first push and the weights.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why did the slump that began in 1929 become a worldwide depression?">
  Bank failures shrank money and credit, falling prices made debts heavier, the gold standard tied countries together and pushed central banks to tighten in a slump, and tariffs cut trade. Economists weigh these causes differently.
</Checkpoint>

The gold-standard reading makes a testable prediction.

## What did the New Deal build, and who did it leave out?

By 1933, five years after Hoover's speech, a quarter of the labor force was out of work. Franklin Roosevelt, inaugurated that March, closed the banks for a national holiday, and the Banking Act of June 1933 created federal deposit insurance. The [[term:new-deal]] added work relief, farm programs and the National Industrial Recovery Act, which let industries set prices and wages together until the Supreme Court struck it down. The Social Security Act of August 1935 created old-age insurance funded by payroll taxes, unemployment insurance and aid to the needy, and the Wagner Act of 1935 protected workers' right to organize. Federal outlays rose from 3.4 percent of GDP in 1930 to 9.6 percent in 1940.

Real output passed its 1929 level in 1936, but unemployment, down to 14.3 percent in 1937, rose to 19.0 percent in the relapse of 1938 and was still 14.6 percent in 1940.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Recovery and reform">
    Defenders point to fast growth after 1933, the year Roosevelt took the dollar off gold. Christina Romer (1992) found that nearly all the recovery before 1942 came from monetary expansion, fed by gold flowing in after the 1933 devaluation and by capital fleeing Europe after 1935. Price Fishback's 2017 survey found that relief and public works spending raised state incomes about one for one and reduced crime and several causes of death. Michael Darby (1976) argued that counting people on work relief as employed cuts the official 1934 to 1941 unemployment rates by 4 to 7 points.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Prolonged the slump">
    Critics point to how slowly jobs came back. Harold Cole and Lee Ohanian (2004) argued that New Deal policies toward industry and labor, which let firms coordinate prices as long as they paid high wages, held real wages well above trend, so firms hired fewer workers; in their model this was an important reason the economy failed to return to its normal path. Critics also cite Fishback's finding that farm programs mostly helped large owners at the expense of tenants and farm workers.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The economist E. Cary Brown (1956) criticized from the other side: deficit spending was never tried at full scale.

The protections also had edges. Social Security's old-age insurance excluded "agricultural labor" and "domestic service in a private home," and the Wagner Act left out the same groups. By the 1930 census about 65 percent of Black workers were in those jobs, against 27 percent of white workers, though about 74 percent of the workers excluded were white. Robert Lieberman (1998), Ira Katznelson (2005) and Mary Poole (2006) argue that race shaped where the line fell; in Lieberman's and Katznelson's accounts, Southern Democrats' price for a national program was leaving the South's segregated farm and household labor outside it. Gareth Davies and Martha Derthick (1997) argue that Southern Democrats were not the source, and Larry DeWitt (2010), a Social Security Administration historian, traces the exclusions to Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau, who told Congress in February 1935 that payroll taxes could not practically be collected from farms and households. Most of these workers were covered in 1950, the rest in 1954. Both sides agree on the result: the workers with the least to fall back on were the last to be covered.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did the New Deal change about the federal government's job, and who did its first protections leave out?">
  Washington took on deposit insurance, old-age and unemployment insurance, union rights and work relief, and its spending roughly tripled as a share of the economy. Its insurance and labor laws left out farm and domestic workers, among whom Black Americans were concentrated.
</Checkpoint>

The same questions can be asked of any rule.

<Checkpoint question="A new benefit covers only workers whose employers file payroll reports every quarter. Who is most likely to be left out, and what would each side of the 1935 debate ask about the rule?">
  Workers paid informally by households, farms and very small employers. One side asks whether the rule is truly needed to run the program; the other asks who drew the line, who knew where it would fall, and who gained. Both can agree on the effect while disagreeing about intent.
</Checkpoint>

## From neutrality to Lend-Lease: what was settled, and what is still argued

[[term:isolationism]] became law in the 1930s. Persuaded by critics who blamed bankers and arms traders for drawing the country into the last war, Congress banned arms sales to nations at war in 1935 and loans to them in 1936. Defense took an estimated 1.2 percent of GDP in 1938. After war broke out in Europe in 1939, the rules bent: arms sales for cash in November 1939, more than 50 old destroyers traded for British bases in September 1940, and on December 29, 1940, Roosevelt's declaration that "We must be the great arsenal of democracy." On March 11, 1941, he signed H.R. 1776, "An Act to Promote the Defense of the United States," known as Lend-Lease. Over the war it sent some $50 billion in aid to more than 30 countries.

Some items fit more than one box. The economist William Silber argues that McAdoo's choices in 1914 began the dollar's rise toward a world currency, which makes the creditor position partly a policy achievement. Romer's evidence makes the recovery partly a product of Europe's troubles.

Settled here: the reversal from debtor to creditor, the scale of the collapse, and the exclusions written into the laws of 1924 and 1935. Still argued: what made the Depression so deep, whether the New Deal sped or slowed recovery, why its protections stopped where they did, and how much of America's position was earned. In December 1941 the attack on Pearl Harbor brought the United States into the war it had been arming. The next lesson asks how that war, and the ruin of every rival, made an American century.
