# Slavery and emancipation

Slavery existed in British North America for more than a century and a half before the United States declared independence. When the founders drafted a constitution in 1787, they built compromises around it that shaped American law for generations. Undoing those compromises would require a civil war and three constitutional amendments.

## Slavery and the Constitution

By the time of the Revolution, enslaved people of African descent lived in every one of the thirteen colonies, with the institution most deeply rooted in the plantation economies of the South. Slavery was a system of *chattel* bondage: enslaved people were treated in law as property, to be bought, sold, and inherited.

The Constitution of 1787 never used the words "slave" or "slavery," but it protected the institution in three key clauses. The **three-fifths clause** (Article I, Section 2) counted "three fifths of all other Persons" toward a state's population for representation and taxation, inflating the political power of slaveholding states. The **fugitive slave clause** (Article IV, Section 2) provided that a "Person held to Service or Labour" who escaped to another state must be "delivered up" to the person claiming them. And a clause in Article I, Section 9 barred Congress from prohibiting "the Migration or Importation of such Persons as any of the States now existing shall think proper to admit" before 1808 — protecting the Atlantic slave trade for twenty years. Congress banned the importation of enslaved people effective January 1, 1808, but slavery within the United States continued to expand.

## The sectional crisis and Dred Scott

As the nation grew, the question of whether slavery would spread into new western territories became the central conflict in American politics. A series of compromises — the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the Compromise of 1850 — postponed but did not resolve it.

In 1857, the Supreme Court attempted to settle the question in **Dred Scott v. Sandford**. Dred Scott, an enslaved man, sued for his freedom on the ground that he had lived in free territory. Chief Justice Roger Taney's opinion held that Black people, whether enslaved or free, were not and could not be citizens of the United States, and that Congress had no power to prohibit slavery in the federal territories — striking down the Missouri Compromise. Taney wrote that people of African descent had "no rights which the white man was bound to respect." Far from settling the crisis, the decision inflamed it and pushed the nation toward war.

## Civil War and emancipation

The election of Abraham Lincoln in 1860 prompted eleven Southern states to secede. The Civil War that followed (1861–1865) became, in time, a war over slavery itself. On January 1, 1863, Lincoln issued the **Emancipation Proclamation**, declaring that enslaved people in the rebelling states "are, and henceforward shall be free."

> "That on the first day of January, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and sixty-three, all persons held as slaves within any State... in rebellion against the United States, shall be then, thenceforward, and forever free."

The Proclamation was a war measure; it freed enslaved people only in Confederate-held territory and rested on the president's wartime powers. But it transformed the meaning of the war and authorized the enlistment of Black soldiers. At Gettysburg later that year, Lincoln framed the conflict as a test of whether a nation "conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could endure.

## The Reconstruction Amendments

Permanent abolition required changing the Constitution itself. In the years after the war, three amendments — what the historian Eric Foner calls the "second founding" — remade it.

The **Thirteenth Amendment** (1865) abolished slavery: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States." The **Fourteenth Amendment** (1868) established birthright citizenship and barred states from denying any person "the equal protection of the laws" or depriving them of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." The **Fifteenth Amendment** (1870) provided that the right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged... on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

## The rise and fall of Reconstruction

During Reconstruction (roughly 1865–1877), the federal government enforced these guarantees in the South. Black men voted in large numbers and held office at every level, and Congress passed civil rights legislation to protect the freed population. For a brief period, the promise of equal citizenship seemed within reach.

That period did not last. White Southern resistance — including organized violence by groups such as the Ku Klux Klan — combined with waning Northern political will. The disputed presidential election of 1876 was resolved by a compromise that withdrew the last federal troops from the South in 1877. With federal enforcement gone, Southern states moved to strip Black citizens of the rights the amendments had promised — setting the stage for the long era of segregation and disenfranchisement that the next lesson takes up.
