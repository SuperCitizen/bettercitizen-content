December 17, 2024. Fifty-one rooms. One in each state capitol, and one in Washington, D.C.

In those rooms, on that day, 538 people gathered. You have almost certainly never heard of any of them. [beat] And they elected the President of the United States.

[pause]

So what was the election in November? The one you watched on television, with the maps and the concession speech?

[beat]

Constitutionally speaking, that was an enormous, elaborate opinion poll. A poll about how those 538 people should vote.

The Electoral College is not a building. It is not a college. And it is not a single body that ever meets in one place.

The phrase is shorthand, coined in the nineteenth century, for a procedure. And the procedure is precise.

So before any argument about whether it should exist, the machinery. By the end, you will be able to follow a presidential election from the ballot in November to the count in January. And you will be able to say which parts are the Constitution, and which parts were added later, by state law and by the Supreme Court.

We will take it in four steps.

First, what the Constitution says, and what it leaves out.

Second, how the states filled the gap.

Third, what became of the elector.

And last, the two points where the machine is under the most strain: when nobody wins, and when the votes are counted.

## What the Constitution says

Start with the text. Article Two, section one, says that each state appoints its electors "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct."

Keep that phrase in your ear. The manner is whatever the legislature directs. We will hear it again.

How many electors does a state get? As many as it has seats in Congress. Its seats in the House, plus its two senators.

Then, in 1961, the Twenty-Third Amendment gave the District of Columbia three electors of its own.

So the arithmetic is fixed. 435 for the House. [beat] 100 for the Senate. [beat] Three for the District. That makes 538, and a candidate needs a majority. A majority is 270.

Notice what the plus two does. Every state gets that pair of electors, whatever its population. So Wyoming's roughly 580,000 people share three electoral votes. California's 39 million people share 54.

Now, three things follow from the constitutional text, and each of them routinely surprises people.

First. The Constitution does not require a popular vote. Not anywhere, and not for anyone.

Today, all fifty states and the District choose their electors by some form of popular election. But South Carolina's legislature picked its electors directly until the Civil War. And in 1892, in a case called McPherson v. Blacker, the Supreme Court confirmed what the text implies. The manner of choosing is the legislature's call.

> How a state chooses its electors is up to its legislature.

Second. The Electoral College never meets as one body. It is 51 separate events.

Each state's electors gather in their own state capital, on a December date set by federal statute. They cast one ballot for President, and a separate ballot for Vice President. They sign certificates, and they go home.

The certificates travel to Washington, where Congress counts them in a joint session on January 6.

Third. The candidates' names on your ballot are, technically, a convenience.

When a voter marks a candidate for President, that voter is legally choosing a slate of electors. They are party loyalists, activists and dignitaries, nominated through the state party.

The constitutional act is the elector's vote in December. [beat] Not yours in November.

[pause]

Let's test that. Your state has 8 seats in the House. How many electors does it get? And who decides how they are chosen? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Ten. Eight for the House seats, plus two for the senators. And the state legislature decides how they are chosen.

That second answer matters more than it sounds. Suppose your legislature wanted to award its electors by congressional district, or to appoint them itself. The Constitution would not stand in the way.

## How the states filled the gap

So the Constitution leaves the method to each state. What have the states done with it?

Nearly all of them have chosen the same rule. Whoever wins the statewide popular vote, even by a single ballot, gets every one of that state's electors. [beat] That rule is called winner-take-all. Forty-eight states and the District use it.

Two states do it differently. Maine, starting in 1972, and Nebraska, since 1992, use what is called the congressional-district method. Two electors go to the statewide winner. One goes to the winner of each congressional district. So those two states can split their electoral votes, and they do.

[pause]

Here is the structural point. [beat] None of this is in the Constitution.

Winner-take-all spread in the early 1800s, and the reasons were nakedly strategic. A state that splits its electors dilutes its own influence. So once a few big states had consolidated, the rest followed in self-defense.

James Madison disliked the development. In 1823 he wrote that the district method "was mostly, if not exclusively in view when the Constitution was framed".

[beat]

Districts, in other words, were what the Framers mostly expected. Winner-take-all came later, and a legislature could switch methods tomorrow, with no constitutional amendment required.

One caution before we go on, because two mechanisms here get blamed for each other constantly.

The plus two tilts the count toward small states. That part is in the Constitution.

Winner-take-all is what produces swing-state campaigning. It also produces most of the splits between the popular vote and the electoral vote. And that part is an artifact of state law, which the Framers never voted on.

> The plus two is in the Constitution. Winner-take-all is state law.

Keep those two apart. I will ask you for them later.

## From deliberator to messenger

That is how electors are awarded. Now the electors themselves. Why have them at all?

The original theory comes from Hamilton, in Federalist 68. He imagined electors as independent deliberators. Electors, he wrote, would be "most likely to possess the information and discernment requisite to such complicated investigations."

[beat]

Information and discernment. On this theory the elector exercises judgment. Think of a search committee of local notables, convened once, and insulated from "tumult and disorder."

That theory was dead within a dozen years.

Here is how it died. In the original design, each elector cast two votes for President, with nothing to tell the two apart. The runner-up became Vice President. That is workable only in a world without parties.

By 1796, the country had parties. The procedure delivered a President, Adams, saddled with his chief rival, Jefferson, as his Vice President.

By 1800, it delivered an exact tie, between Jefferson and his own running mate, Aaron Burr. Every elector of their party had dutifully cast both votes for the ticket. The House took 36 ballots to untangle it.

So in 1804 came the Twelfth Amendment: separate ballots for President and for Vice President. That amendment is the Constitution formally absorbing the existence of parties. And it quietly buried the deliberating elector.

> The elector was designed to deliberate. What remained was a messenger.

But could a messenger still refuse to deliver the message? An elector who votes for someone other than the candidate they pledged to support has a name: a faithless elector. Through the twentieth century, faithless electors were rare, and legally untested.

Then came 2016. Seven faithless votes were actually counted, the most in a century. They came amid an organized effort to persuade electors to defect, and so to deny Donald Trump a majority. The effort failed. But it produced the test case.

Washington State fined three of its faithless electors a thousand dollars each, and they sued. In 2020, in a case called Chiafalo v. Washington, the Supreme Court ruled against them, nine to nothing. States may enforce an elector's pledge, by fine or by replacement.

Justice Kagan's reasoning ran through the phrase I asked you to keep. The power to appoint electors in the manner the legislature directs includes the power to demand a binding promise first.

And listen to how she closed. A state that binds its electors, she wrote, "instructs its electors that they have no ground for reversing the vote of millions of its citizens." That direction accords, in her words, "with the trust of a Nation that here, We the People rule."

[pause]

Here, We the People rule. Those lines are the epitaph for Hamilton's deliberator.

Most states, and the District, now require an elector's pledge. A growing number void a faithless ballot automatically, and swap in an alternate. The institution is more mechanical than it has been at any point in its history.

So try this one. It is December 2028. An elector pledged to Candidate X has a crisis of conscience, and votes for Candidate Y. What actually happens? Think it through.

[long pause]

It depends entirely on state law. In a state with an enforcement statute, the vote is voided, and the elector is replaced or fined. Chiafalo says that is constitutional. In a state with no binding law, the faithless vote counts when Congress opens the certificates.

The Constitution itself is silent. The discipline comes from the states.

## When nobody reaches 270

Now the part of the machine that most people have never looked at. It is also the part with the highest stakes. What happens if no candidate wins 270?

The Twelfth Amendment moves the choice to Congress, under rules that have nothing to do with population.

The House picks the President, from the top three finishers in electoral votes. But the House does not vote member by member. It votes by state. [beat] One state, one vote.

California's delegation has 52 members, and together they cast a single vote. Wyoming has a lone member, and that member casts one vote too. It takes 26 state delegations to make a majority.

[beat]

The Senate picks the Vice President, from the top two. There, each senator votes individually, and 51 votes win.

And what if the House is still deadlocked on Inauguration Day, January 20? Then, under the Twentieth Amendment, the Senate's choice for Vice President becomes acting President, until the House resolves it.

That whole fallback is called a contingent election. In a contingent election, the question is not which party won more votes, or even more House seats. The question is which party controls more state delegations.

> In a contingent election, the House votes by state: one state, one vote.

Has it ever happened? Twice, for President.

Once in 1800, with the tie between Jefferson and Burr. And once in 1824, when four candidates split the electors. Andrew Jackson led in both the popular vote and the electoral vote, and the House chose John Quincy Adams.

And the near-misses are nearer than most people realize. In 1968, George Wallace won five Southern states and 46 electoral votes. He was hoping to deny both parties a majority, and then to bargain from the deadlock.

Nixon cleared 270. But had roughly 56,000 voters in two states switched, the presidency would have been settled by state delegations in the House.

[pause]

That remains the realistic trigger: a serious regional candidate from a third party, who wins a few states and denies everyone 270.

Let's run it once. It is January 2029. No candidate has reached 270, because a regional third-party candidate holds 3 electoral votes.

In the House, Party A holds more seats, by a margin of one. But Party B controls 26 state delegations, and Party A controls 24.

Who picks the President? And which party is favored?

[pause 5s]

The House picks, from the top three, voting one state, one vote. And Party B is favored, even though it has fewer members. It controls 26 delegations, which is exactly the majority it needs.

The total count of seats is irrelevant. Control of delegations is everything.

## The count

One stop is left: the count itself.

On January 6, 2021, the certification of the electoral votes became a crisis. And the crisis exposed how much of the count ran on a vague statute from 1887.

Congress responded with the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. It tightened the procedure at four points.

One. Objections got harder. It used to take one representative plus one senator for an objection to be heard. Now it takes one-fifth of each chamber, and the grounds are narrower.

[beat]

Two. The Vice President's role is explicitly ministerial. The presiding officer opens the envelopes and announces the results, and nothing more. The statute now forecloses the theory that a Vice President may reject or delay a state's electors.

[beat]

Three. One conclusive slate for each state. The state's executive must certify its electors by a hard deadline. Disputes go to an expedited panel of three federal judges. And Congress must treat the certificate that the court validates as conclusive.

[beat]

Four. No more "failed election" loophole. The old statute let a legislature appoint electors after Election Day, if the state had "failed to make a choice." That language is gone.

[pause]

Now notice what the act did not do. It changed nothing about the structure. The 538 electors stand. Winner-take-all stands. The contingent election stands.

> The 2022 act armored the counting, not the institution.

## Not yet an argument

Before we gather this up, pull something back. A while ago I asked you to keep two mechanisms apart, the two that get blamed for each other. Can you still name them? And which one is in the Constitution?

[long pause]

The plus two, and winner-take-all. The plus two is in the Constitution. Winner-take-all is state law, and a legislature could change it tomorrow.

Hold on to that distinction, because the argument comes next. The mechanics are politically charged, but they are not the argument. The argument is whether any of this should exist. It has three serious positions, and here is each one in brief.

Originalist defenders argue that the institution was a deliberate compromise of the Founding era. They argue that it has served stability, federalism, and the protection of small-state interests. And they argue that abolishing it would unsettle constitutional balances that most reformers do not fully understand.

> The originalist defender: a deliberate compromise, which has served stability and federalism.

Democratic-majority reformers argue that the institution has made a winner of the popular vote's loser five times. They argue that it distorts campaigning into a handful of swing states. And they argue that it is anti-democratic, in a country whose every other office is now elected directly.

> The democratic-majority reformer: an anti-democratic distortion, where every other office is elected directly.

Federalist preservationists are distinct from originalist defenders. They argue that the institution is not a quirk, but a part of the federal compact. They argue that presidential authority derives from the states acting as states, and not from a national electorate. And they argue that reformers underestimate how much constitutional structure rests on that premise.

> The federalist preservationist: part of the federal compact. Authority derives from the states, as states.

[pause]

Three positions. You may already lean toward one of them. So take the one you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

A defensible position takes one of those views, says why, and names the evidence that would change it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. 538 electors, and 270 to win. The Electoral College is not one meeting. It is 51 of them, in December, and that vote is the constitutional act.

[beat]

Two. How a state chooses its electors is up to its legislature. The plus two is in the Constitution. Winner-take-all is state law.

[beat]

Three. The elector was designed to deliberate. What remained was a messenger. And since the Chiafalo ruling of 2020, a state may hold that messenger to the pledge.

[beat]

Four. If nobody reaches 270, the House chooses the President in a contingent election. One state, one vote, and 26 delegations to win.

[beat]

And five. The 2022 act armored the counting, not the institution.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We have the machine: who votes, when, and under whose rules. We have not asked whether the machine should exist.

A deliberate compromise. An anti-democratic distortion. A part of the federal compact. [beat] Three answers, and all three are still standing.

The next lecture takes those three positions one at a time, and gives each one its strongest case.

For now, go back to those rooms in December. Fifty-one of them. 538 people, signing certificates and going home. [beat] You know now who sent them, what they are permitted to do, and what happens when their votes are not enough.
