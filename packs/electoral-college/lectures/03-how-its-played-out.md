A few minutes past four in the morning. March 2, 1877. In Washington, the president of the Senate announces that Rutherford B. Hayes has been elected President of the United States.

The count is 185 electoral votes to 184.

[beat]

Inauguration is two days away. And the man who won the popular vote, by three points, is not Hayes.

[pause]

Who settled that count? Not the House of Representatives. Not the Senate. Not anything described in the Constitution.

It was a commission of fifteen members, which Congress had invented six weeks earlier. And it had resolved every disputed question by the same vote. [beat] 8 to 7, along party lines.

[pause]

From 1788 through 2024, sixty presidential elections have been run under the Electoral College. Most produced a clean winner. This lecture is about the ones that did not.

Why spend our time on the exceptions? Because the exceptions are where any theory of the institution gets tested. And because each major failure left a scar, in a statute or an amendment, that still governs how the machine runs today.

> Each major failure of the Electoral College left a scar in the law.

Last time, three positions argued in the seminar room. This time, the record they argue over.

One word before we start. When the loser of the popular vote wins the presidency, I will call that an inversion. It has happened five times. By the end, you should be able to name the five, and say what actually decided each one.

We will take it in four stops. First, the founding generation. Second, the Gilded Age. Third, the years since 2000. And last, how three camps read the same record.

## The founding generation

Start with a design that broke almost at once.

The original procedure gave each elector two votes for President, with nothing to tell them apart. Most votes won the presidency. Second place became Vice President. The design had no concept of a party ticket.

Parties arrived almost immediately, and the procedure failed in both available directions.

In 1796, it produced a split executive. John Adams won, and his bitter rival, Thomas Jefferson, finished second. So Jefferson became Vice President of the administration he intended to oppose.

In 1800, it produced something worse. Jefferson's party had learned discipline. Every one of its electors cast one vote for Jefferson, and one for his running mate, Aaron Burr. The result was a perfect tie, at 73 votes each. [beat] A tie between the ticket and itself.

That threw the choice into a House controlled by the opposing party, the Federalists. The House deadlocked through 35 ballots, over six days. On the 36th, Jefferson was elected.

Then came the repair. In 1804, the Twelfth Amendment separated the ballot for President from the ballot for Vice President. The party ticket was now part of the Constitution.

The original design had lasted four elections.

[pause]

The amendment kept a fallback. If no candidate wins a majority of the electors, the House chooses among the top three, and each state's delegation casts a single vote. That fallback is called the contingent procedure. It sat unused until 1824.

That year, the vote splintered. Andrew Jackson led, with 99 electoral votes. [beat] But a majority was 131.

So the House chose. Henry Clay had finished fourth, which put him out of the running. But Clay was also the Speaker of the House. He threw his support to John Quincy Adams. And on the first ballot, Adams won 13 of the 24 state delegations.

Adams then appointed Clay Secretary of State. In that era, the office was the recognized stepping-stone to the presidency.

Jackson's supporters called it the "Corrupt Bargain." Whether any explicit bargain occurred is still disputed. But the accusation did its work. Jackson won the rematch in 1828, decisively.

[pause]

Now be careful with what 1824 proves. It appears on every list of popular-vote losers who won. And Jackson did lead the recorded popular vote, with about 41 percent.

But two things qualify it. First, in six of the 24 states, the legislature appointed the electors, and no popular vote was held at all. New York, the largest state, was one of them. So the national tally is incomplete by construction.

Second, the final choice was made by the House, under the contingent procedure. It was not made by Electoral College arithmetic.

> 1824 is the first entry on the inversion list, and the one with an asterisk.

Put another way, 1824 indicts the system's fallback. The clean tests of its arithmetic come later.

## The Gilded Age

Second stop. We go back to where we began: the election of 1876. It is the institution's darkest stress test, and its mechanics are botched in retellings more than any other's. So let's walk through them precisely.

The Democrat, Samuel Tilden, won the popular vote by about three points. He stood at 184 undisputed electoral votes. [beat] One short of a majority.

The Republican, Rutherford B. Hayes, had 165.

The remaining 20 votes were disputed, and nearly all of them came from three Southern states: Florida, Louisiana and South Carolina. There, Republican-controlled boards had thrown out the returns from counties where Black voters had been violently intimidated. And rival state authorities mailed Congress competing certificates. One set for Hayes. One set for Tilden.

So the question was not who had a majority. The question came before that one. Which certificates were real?

[beat]

And on that question, the Constitution was silent.

Stop there, and think back to the fallback. In 1824, the House chose the President, with one vote for each state. Why didn't the House simply do the same in 1876? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because the contingent procedure has a trigger, and the trigger was never pulled. It applies only when no candidate has a majority of the appointed electors. In 1876, the question was which electors had been validly appointed in the first place.

With the 20 disputed votes, Hayes had exactly a majority. Without them, Tilden did. That earlier question had no constitutional answer. So Congress improvised.

In January 1877, it created a tribunal of fifteen. Five senators. Five representatives. And five justices of the Supreme Court. The act named four of the justices, two from each party, and those four were to choose the fifth.

Everyone expected the fifth to be David Davis, a genuine independent. Then, days before the commission convened, the legislature of Illinois elected Davis to the United States Senate, and he withdrew. The four justices chose Joseph Bradley, a Republican.

The commission awarded every disputed vote to Hayes. [beat] 8 to 7. On party lines.

[pause]

Democrats acquiesced. They did not obstruct the count. In exchange came what history calls the Compromise of 1877. The new Hayes administration withdrew federal troops from the South, and that ended Reconstruction.

So state the trade plainly. The disputed returns had been generated by terror against Black voters. The resolution traded the contested presidency for the abandonment of federal civil-rights enforcement. [beat] That enforcement would not seriously resume for eight decades.

[pause]

The procedural scar arrived ten years later: the Electoral Count Act of 1887. It set the rules for counting electoral votes in a joint session of Congress. Under those rules, one senator plus one representative could lodge an objection. Remember that threshold. It lasted until 2022, and we will meet it again.

The very next election is the opposite kind of case. In 1888 there was no commission, no House vote, and no disputed slate. If you want the system's structural tendency in isolation, this is the laboratory.

Grover Cleveland, the Democratic incumbent, won the popular vote, by roughly 89,000 votes. And he lost the presidency, 233 electoral votes to 168.

How? Benjamin Harrison's votes were simply distributed better. Cleveland ran up enormous, useless margins across the South. Harrison won New York, Cleveland's own state, by about one percent, and took all 36 of its electors. [beat] Flip New York, and Cleveland wins.

> In 1888, winner-take-all arithmetic rewarded efficient distribution over total support.

## Since 2000

Third stop: our own era. Three elections, and three different kinds of stress.

In 2000, George W. Bush defeated Al Gore, 271 electoral votes to 266. That is one vote above the majority line. Gore won the national popular vote, by about half a million.

Everything turned on Florida. Its electors were certified for Bush by 537 votes. [beat] Out of roughly six million cast.

Florida's courts ordered manual recounts. The United States Supreme Court halted them, in Bush v. Gore. The structure of that holding is misstated constantly, so let's get it right.

Seven justices concluded that the recount Florida's high court had ordered violated the guarantee of equal protection. It had no single standard. Different counties were divining "voter intent" by different rules.

The decision was five to four only on the remedy. The majority held that no constitutionally adequate recount could be completed by December 12, a deadline that Florida law was understood to require. So the certified count stood.

And the Court limited its own ruling, in a sentence argued about ever since. "Our consideration is limited to the present circumstances."

[pause]

Gore conceded the next day.

Now test your grip on that. A friend tells you that the Supreme Court ruled, five to four, that Bush won Florida. That sentence needs two corrections. What are they?

[long pause]

First, the vote. On equal protection, the holding was seven to two. Only the remedy, stopping the recount, was five to four.

Second, the Court never declared a winner. It halted the recount, and that left Florida's certified margin of 537 votes in place. Bush won Florida by certification. And then Gore conceded.

> In Bush v. Gore, the Court halted a recount. It never declared a winner.

How do the three camps see 2000?

The democratic-majority reformer sees the system's failure mode dragging the judiciary into raw politics.

The federalist preservationist sees containment working. A national margin of half a million votes produced a dispute bounded to one state, and resolved before the electors met.

The originalist defender sees a result that can mostly be accepted, while wincing at the equal-protection reasoning that carried it.

What nobody disputes is this. It was the first time a ruling of the Supreme Court effectively settled a presidential election.

[pause]

Then 2016. It is the modern clean inversion: 1888 again, at internet speed. Hillary Clinton won the popular vote by nearly 2.9 million. Donald Trump won the Electoral College.

The decisive margins came in three states: Michigan, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Together, they came to fewer than 78,000 votes. [beat] And they outweighed a national margin 37 times larger.

It was the second inversion in five elections, after a gap of 112 years. Both modern inversions favored the same party. And that converted an institutional argument into a partisan one.

[pause]

2020 stressed a different joint. The question was not who won. It was whether the count would be honored.

Joe Biden won the Electoral College, and he won the popular vote by about seven million. But the Electoral College kept it tight. Flip three states, which Biden carried by a combined 43,000 votes or so, and the count is a tie. That would have meant a contingent election, despite the national margin.

After every state certified, objections were lodged at the joint session of January 6, 2021, under the old threshold from 1887. One senator, plus one representative. The Capitol was attacked. And the count finished under guard, near four in the morning.

Congress answered with the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. It raised the threshold for an objection to one-fifth of each chamber. It made the Vice President's role expressly ministerial. It required one conclusive certificate from each state, open to review by a court. And it deleted a loophole for a so-called failed election.

2024 supplied the coda. Donald Trump defeated Kamala Harris, winning all seven battlegrounds and the popular vote. The first count under the new act took about half an hour. Vice President Harris presided over the certification of her own defeat. And no objection was raised.

The defender sees the system correcting itself. The reformer sees one aligned election, which changes nothing structural.

## Reading the record

So lay the five inversions side by side: 1824, 1876, 1888, 2000, 2016. They are not five instances of one failure. They are three different mechanisms, misfiring.

Before I sort them, try it yourself. Take the five years in order. For each one, what actually decided it?

[pause 5s]

1824: the House, in a contingent election. Thirteen of 24 delegations.

[beat]

1876: a commission invented for the occasion, with every dispute resolved 8 to 7.

[beat]

1888: winner-take-all arithmetic, and New York by about one percent.

[beat]

2000: one state's certification, by 537 votes, with the recount halted by the Court.

[beat]

2016: winner-take-all arithmetic again. Fewer than 78,000 votes, across three states.

[pause]

So count the mechanisms. The fallback in the House, once. Winner-take-all arithmetic, twice. And twice, a disputed count that was settled from outside the Electoral College: by a commission, and then by a court.

Notice, too, how the failures cluster. Two in the founding generation. Two in the Gilded Age. Two since 2000. And each cluster forced a procedural invention: the Twelfth Amendment, the Electoral Count Act of 1887, and the reform of that act in 2022.

> Each cluster of failures forced a procedural fix.

That is the what. What the record means is the contested part, and each camp has an honest version. Put the same question to all three. Is this record an indictment, or a track record?

Here is the originalist defender's reading. Five anomalies in sixty elections. Every one of them absorbed, without the constitutional order collapsing. And the worst of them, 1876, was a crisis of Reconstruction-era violence, which no allocation rule would have sanitized.

> The defender's reading: five anomalies in sixty elections, and every one absorbed.

Here is the democratic-majority reformer's reading. The failure rate is concentrating: two inversions and one near-tie, in the seven elections since 2000. And the near-misses cut both ways. In 2004, had about 60,000 voters in Ohio switched sides, John Kerry would have taken the presidency, while George W. Bush won the popular vote by three million.

> The reformer's reading: two inversions and a near-tie, in the last seven elections.

Here is the federalist preservationist's reading. The statutes of 1887 and 2022 show the system metabolizing its crises. Each one hardened the count, and left the federal structure intact. And in 2000, as this camp sees it, the dispute stayed bounded to one state.

> The preservationist's reading: the count was hardened, and the federal structure left intact.

[pause]

One shelf of facts, and three readings. Choose the reading you find least convincing. Make its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Five times, the loser of the popular vote won the presidency. 1824, 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016.

[beat]

Two. They were not one failure repeated. The House decided 1824. A commission decided 1876. A halted recount settled 2000. And winner-take-all arithmetic decided 1888 and 2016.

[beat]

Three. 1824 is the entry with an asterisk. Six states held no popular vote, and the House made the choice.

[beat]

Four. In Bush v. Gore, seven justices found the equal-protection problem. Only the remedy was five to four. And the Court never declared a winner.

[beat]

And five. Each cluster of failures forced a procedural fix: the Twelfth Amendment, the Electoral Count Act of 1887, and its reform in 2022.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It settles the what: five inversions, two contingent elections, one invented commission, one judicial intervention, and a statute after each crisis. It does not settle whether that record is an indictment or a track record.

What to replace, what to repair, and whether to do either: that is the next lecture. It takes up the reform proposals on the table today, from the National Popular Vote compact to district allocation, and the fights over each.

For now, go back to four in the morning, in March of 1877. A count of 185 to 184, settled by a commission the Constitution never mentioned. [beat] You know now how it came to that. And you know the scar it left in the law.
