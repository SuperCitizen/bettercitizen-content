September 18, 1969. The House of Representatives votes to abolish the Electoral College, and to elect presidents by a direct national vote.

The tally is lopsided. 339 to 70.

President Nixon endorses it. And Gallup has just measured public support for direct election at 80 percent.

[pause]

Then the proposal meets the Senate. A filibuster holds, anchored by Southern segregationists and by senators from small states. Two votes to cut off debate fail. And the closest the United States has ever come to abolishing the institution dies, without ever receiving an up-or-down vote.

[pause]

That is the pattern of this entire subject. The National Archives counts more than 700 proposals in Congress to reform or eliminate the Electoral College. No other topic in American history has drawn more proposed amendments.

And how many were enacted that touched how the states award their electors?

[beat]

Zero.

> More than 700 proposals in Congress. Enacted, on how states award electors: zero.

Which is why the energy for reform moved away from amendment long ago, toward paths that need no amendment at all.

Three lectures have given you the machine, the positions, and the record. This last one gives you the menu: what could be changed, and who would have to agree. By the end, you should be able to take any reform on that menu and say who has the authority to enact it.

We will take it in four stops. First, a compact among the states. Second, awarding electors by district. Third, two larger ideas that no state can carry alone. And last, how three camps sort the whole menu.

## The compact

The dominant path today routes around that graveyard of amendments. Its full name is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact. I will call it the compact.

It began as an idea. After Bush v. Gore, two brothers, Akhil and Vikram Amar, published a proposal. States, they argued, could achieve direct national election without amending anything. They would use a power the states already hold: full authority over how electors are chosen, under a ruling of 1892 called McPherson v. Blacker. John Koza and the National Popular Vote organization turned that idea into model legislation.

Here is the mechanism. A state enacts a law that commits its entire slate of electors to the winner of the national popular vote. Not the winner in that state. [beat] The winner in the country.

But the commitment is suspended. It stays suspended until the states that have enacted the same law control at least 270 electoral votes.

Below that threshold, nothing changes anywhere. [beat] At the threshold, the member states alone can guarantee that the national popular-vote winner becomes President, however the other states award their electors.

> The compact stays dormant until its members control 270 electoral votes.

So where does it stand? As of mid-2026, 18 states and the District of Columbia have joined. Together they control 222 electoral votes. [beat] That leaves the compact 48 short.

The newest member is Virginia, which joined in April 2026. And one member has put the question to its voters. In 2020, Colorado kept the compact, 52 to 48.

The unsolved problem is visible on the membership map. Every member is a state that has voted Democratic in recent presidential cycles. The remaining 48 votes must come from states that benefit from battleground status, or lean Republican, or both.

The lesson page has a tool that lets you feel that problem. It asks you to assemble 270 electoral votes without the seven battlegrounds of 2024. The arithmetic turns out to be easy. The politics is incoherent, because the coalitions that clear the line sweep deep-blue and deep-red states together, which no real candidate does. That is why campaigns treat the safe states as settled, and pour everything into the seven.

And it is the compact's recruitment problem in miniature. The votes it still needs sit disproportionately in exactly the states whose leverage the current system creates. Joining would mean legislating away the only thing that makes presidential candidates visit.

[pause]

Let's test the mechanism. It is 2027. The compact stands at 222 electoral votes. A state with 10 electoral votes enacts it. What changes for the election of 2028? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Nothing. At 232, of the 270 required, every member's statute stays dormant. The election of 2028 runs under the existing rules, everywhere.

The design is all-or-nothing on purpose. Suppose a member acted early, while the non-members stayed winner-take-all. It could simply hand its votes to the candidate its own voters opposed, with no guarantee in return.

## The compact in court

Now the legal fight, which is the central one. Article One, section ten, forbids a state to "enter into any Agreement or Compact with another State" without the consent of Congress. The compact has no such consent. [beat] Does it need it?

In 1893, in a case called Virginia v. Tennessee, the Supreme Court gave that clause a functional meaning. Consent is required for combinations that increase the political power of the states, in a way that may encroach on the supremacy of the United States.

The compact's supporters, the Amar brothers and John Koza among them, argue that it does not meet that test. Each state is exercising an appointment power that it independently, and unquestionably, possesses. Any state could award its electors to the national winner tomorrow, alone. So coordinated timing adds no power that the states lack.

[beat]

The compact's critics, in a formulation by Norman Williams, argue that it restructures the presidency itself. They call it the most consequential electoral change since the Seventeenth Amendment. Article Five reserves a change of that kind to supermajorities. And to achieve it through legislatures holding 270 electoral votes is, they argue, exactly the kind of end-run that the Compact Clause, rightly understood, forbids.

[beat]

If the compact ever reaches 270, that question goes to the Supreme Court immediately. And nobody wants it resolved for the first time in the middle of an election.

[pause]

There are operational questions as well. There is no official national vote total, so each member's chief election official would have to add up every other state's certified results. And membership is reversible. A state could repeal, in the run-up to an election it expects to regret.

The compact's text has a blackout clause against that. Whether one legislature can bind its successor that way is itself contested. Supporters reply that every interstate compact carries a risk of defection, and that the answer everywhere else is ordinary legal enforcement.

## By district

The second path needs no one's permission. Any state can adopt it alone, today, by ordinary statute. Two electors go to the statewide winner. One goes to the winner of each congressional district.

Maine has done it that way since 1972, and Nebraska since 1992. Both delivered split results in 2024.

This reform has the oldest intellectual pedigree of any. James Madison, writing in August 1823, claimed that the district method was mostly what the Constitution's authors had in view. He wanted a constitutional amendment to mandate districts, and to kill the statewide ticket.

[beat]

In 1958, a history of the Electoral College by Lucius Wilmerding extended the point. Winner-take-all, on his account, was a corruption the Framers never chose. And the system's defenders are mostly defending the corruption, not the design.

Modern reformers accept that diagnosis, and reject the cure. They reject it for one decisive reason, which Madison could not have foreseen at this scale.

> The district method inherits the congressional map, and politicians draw the map.

Under district allocation nationwide, presidential elections would run through every gerrymandering fight in the country.

The counterfactual is stark. In 2012, Barack Obama won the national vote by nearly four points, and he won the Electoral College, 332 to 206. Yet Mitt Romney carried more congressional districts. [beat] 226 of the 435.

Run that election again under a nationwide district method, and the landslide all but vanishes. On the National Popular Vote organization's tally, it becomes a squeaker. [beat] 270 to 268.

So district allocation can make inversions more likely, not less, whenever the district maps are biased relative to the electorate.

[pause]

Nebraska's recent history shows the partisan stakes in miniature. In 2024, Trump allies pushed the legislature to switch to winner-take-all. They were blocked when a single state senator refused to provide the deciding vote. A bill in 2025 died when a vote to end debate failed. Two more attempts, aimed at the ballot of November 2026, fizzled. And legislators in Maine, watching the siege, openly discussed a retaliatory switch of their own.

Allocation rules, the episode teaches, are not neutral plumbing. They are contested terrain, and the terrain moves with whoever expects to profit.

[pause]

Try this one. In some state, a party has lost the last three statewide votes. But it holds a majority in the legislature, and a comfortable majority of the state's congressional districts. It proposes district allocation, "so every part of the state counts." What is the disciplined first question? Think it through.

[long pause]

Who drew the districts?

Under the district method, the presidential outcome inherits the congressional map. A party that loses statewide, but controls the drawing of districts, can convert control of the map into electoral votes. That is why such proposals have historically surfaced in exactly this configuration. The merits of the reform cannot be separated from the redistricting process beneath it.

## Ideas that need everyone

Third stop: two larger ideas. Neither one works for a state acting alone.

The first is proportional allocation. Split each state's electors in proportion to its popular vote. Take a state with 10 electoral votes. If it votes 60 to 40, its electors go 6 to 4.

This reform had the grandest near-miss. In 1950, a proportional amendment passed the full Senate, 64 to 27. Then it died in the House.

Its problems are arithmetic before they are political. With whole electors, small states cannot meaningfully split. Picture a state with 4 electors that votes 58 to 42. It rounds to two electors each. [beat] A sixteen-point landslide, producing a tie.

And for one state acting alone, the reform defeats itself. Colorado's voters saw the trap clearly in 2004. They rejected a proportional plan, 65 to 35. The argument that carried the day: splitting Colorado's electors, while its neighbors stayed winner-take-all, would simply delete the state's influence.

So proportional allocation works only as a coordinated national rule. And the only vehicles for coordination are an amendment or a compact.

That makes this a good moment to pull something back. A reform group argues: "Proportional allocation is fairer, so our state should adopt it now and lead by example." You have just heard the flaw in that. Here is the harder half. Why doesn't the same flaw sink the compact?

[long pause]

Because the compact is engineered around that trap. Think back to its trigger. Every member's commitment stays dormant until states holding 270 electoral votes have joined. So no state ever moves alone.

One reform is self-defeating piecemeal. The other is all-or-nothing by design.

> Alone, proportional allocation defeats itself. The compact never asks a state to move alone.

The second idea is the maximal one: direct election, by constitutional amendment. It is also the one with the clearest verdict from history.

You heard how 1969 ended. The Senate tried again, in July 1979. A direct-election amendment won 51 votes, with 48 against. That is a majority. [beat] But it is 15 short of two-thirds.

And the coalition that beat it is the most instructive fact in the whole reform literature. Alexander Keyssar's history documents it. Alongside the small-state and Southern senators stood several urban civil-rights and Jewish organizations. They had concluded that their communities' pivotal position, in big winner-take-all states, was worth more than arithmetically equal votes in a national pool.

The Electoral College's distortions create stakeholders everywhere. And not always the ones you would guess.

## Three ways to sort the menu

Now step back from the paths. One sentence organizes all of them.

> One state alone can do only small things. Large things, the system's beneficiaries can veto.

A single state can bind its electors, or split them by district. The compact needs states holding 270 electoral votes, and it has to survive a challenge under the Compact Clause. Direct election needs Article Five: two-thirds of each chamber, and 38 states.

Three camps sort that menu, each in its own way. Each one gets the same treatment here: what it accepts, and what it argues.

First, the NPVIC supporter. Those five letters are the compact's initials, so I will say the compact supporter. This camp works the compact map, state by state. It argues that the states already hold the power to appoint electors as they choose, so that coordinated timing adds nothing they lack. And it argues from the record of amendment. Article Five, reformers answer, hands a veto to the exact actors the reform would take power from, even with public support at 63 percent. So to insist on an amendment or nothing is to choose nothing, and call it principle.

> The compact supporter's case: states already hold the power, and amendment-or-nothing chooses nothing.

Second, the federalist preservationist. This camp accepts what a state does for itself. It accepts statutes that bind electors, which the Supreme Court upheld in 2020, and which more than 30 states now have. It accepts experiments with districts, like those of Maine and Nebraska. What it opposes is the compact. It argues that the compact is federalism dissolved by workaround. Put another way, the state-by-state election would be gone, and no amendment would have ended it.

> The preservationist's case: the compact is federalism dissolved by workaround.

Third, the constitutional-amendment purist. This camp accepts only Article Five, and bets that the country can be persuaded. It argues that Article Five is not an obstacle. It is the point. A change this fundamental should require two-thirds of each chamber, and three-quarters of the states. The compact would transform the system by simple majorities, in the legislatures of a minority of states, and it could be reversed the same way. That, the purist argues, is building on sand. Better no reform than a reform whose own legitimacy can be litigated.

> The purist's case: Article Five is not the obstacle. Article Five is the point.

[pause]

Three camps. Take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. More than 700 proposals in Congress, and none enacted on how states award electors. So reform moved to paths that need no amendment.

[beat]

Two. The compact commits a state's electors to the national winner, and it stays dormant until its members control 270 electoral votes. As of mid-2026, it stands at 222. The legal question is the consent of Congress, under the Compact Clause.

[beat]

Three. Any one state can award electors by district. But the district method inherits the congressional map, and politicians draw the map. So ask who drew the districts.

[beat]

Four. Proportional allocation defeats itself for one state alone. And direct election by amendment passed the House once, in 1969, and fell 15 votes short in the Senate, in 1979.

[beat]

And five. One state alone can do only small things. Large things, the system's beneficiaries can veto. The compact supporter, the preservationist and the purist each sort that menu differently.

[pause]

Here is what these four lectures have not settled, and have deliberately not settled. Three things are yours to decide. Which frame you find most defensible. Which reform path follows from it, including none. And what evidence would change your mind. The record constrains all three. It dictates none.

Some of it is still moving, as of mid-2026. Whether the compact finds 48 electoral votes outside the blue column. Whether the Supreme Court would bless it. Whether Nebraska's district method survives its annual siege. And whether another inversion, or another clean, aligned election like 2024, shifts the public opinion that everything else stands on.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position, and then to run a steel-man drill against the position you hold most strongly.

For now, go back to 1969. A House vote of 339 to 70. A president's endorsement. Eight Americans in ten in favor. And still, nothing changed. [beat] Defended positions are cheap. Refined ones are earned.
