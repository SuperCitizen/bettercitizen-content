March 1956. The United States Senate has spent days debating a constitutional amendment. It would dismantle winner-take-all, and divide every state's electoral votes.

The most effective voice against it belongs to a 38-year-old Democrat from Massachusetts. [beat] His name is John F. Kennedy.

He warns his colleagues that they are not tinkering with an isolated mechanism. Here is his sentence.

"It is not only the unit vote for the Presidency we are talking about, but a whole solar system of governmental power."

[pause]

A whole solar system of governmental power.

The reform died. Four years later, Kennedy won the presidency. [beat] A proportional system might well have denied it to him.

[pause]

That is the lesson of 1956, and of this whole debate. The argument over the Electoral College scrambles partisan instincts.

> On the Electoral College, serious people have landed on every side.

Last time, the machinery. This time, the argument. You will hear three positions, each presented the way its smartest adherent would present it, because engaging a caricature teaches you nothing.

Before the positions, the ground under them. The three camps disagree in two different ways, and it helps to hear the difference now.

One disagreement is about values. Should the President answer to a single national electorate? Or to the states, as constitutional units? [beat] No data settles that.

The other disagreement is about evidence. Who actually gets protected, courted and counted under the current rules? Data bears on that directly. And the data is not kind to every claim, on either side.

> The camps largely agree on the arithmetic. They disagree about what it means.

So, four stops. First, the originalist defender. Second, the democratic-majority reformer. Third, the federalist preservationist. And last, where the disagreement actually lives.

Each position gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, the evidence it points to, its hardest problem, and what would change it.

## The originalist defender

Begin with the originalist defender, and with what this position protects: the design the Founders chose.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The Electoral College was not a quirk, and it was not an accident. How to elect the executive was among the most heavily debated questions at the Convention of 1787. Direct popular election was proposed that summer, and rejected. [beat] Repeatedly.

The elector was a deliberate solution to a cluster of problems: an informed choice across a continent with no mass communication, a president independent of Congress, and states with very different voting rules.

And the design builds something in. A candidate has to win states, so a candidate cannot simply pile up margins in the largest metropolitan areas. The path to 270 runs through farm states, industrial states and desert states, with different economies and different grievances.

Defenders reach for a sports analogy. A World Series is won by games, not by total runs. The rules test distributed strength, not raw accumulation.

Tara Ross, in a 2019 book called Why We Need the Electoral College, makes the full version of the case. The system, she argues, is a guardrail. It forces majorities to be federal majorities before they can govern.

> The originalist defender's case: majorities must be federal majorities before they can govern.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, the record of the Convention, and the text it produced. Madison, in Federalist 39, described the Constitution as "neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both." The presidency's arithmetic, on this view, is that composition in miniature.

[beat]

Second, the arithmetic itself, run honestly. Every state gets its House seats plus two, so the floor is three electors. On the 2020 census, Wyoming has one elector for roughly every 192,000 residents. California has one for roughly every 732,000. [beat] A vote in Wyoming carries nearly four times the weight.

Reformers call that indefensible. The defender calls it the knowing price of union, identical in logic to the Senate's rule of two seats for every state.

[beat]

Third, the track record. From 1788 through 2024, the country held 60 presidential elections. In 55 of them, the winner also led the popular vote. One caveat travels with that number: the earliest contests had no meaningful national popular vote to lead.

And the modern misfires, in 2000 and 2016, were photo finishes. In each, the defender argues, the system's tiebreaker favored breadth over depth. That is the design doing what it says. It is not the design failing.

[pause]

Let's test this position. Take three things: the plus-two floor, winner-take-all, and the state legislature's control over how electors are appointed. Which one is the originalist defender not committed to defending? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Winner-take-all. The plus-two floor and the legislature's control are in the constitutional text. Winner-take-all is an artifact of state law. It spread in the early 1800s for partisan advantage, and Madison himself wanted it amended away.

So a defender of the Founders' design can consistently favor awarding electors by district, or in proportion. Keeping the constitutional skeleton apart from the state-law muscle is the first discipline of this debate.

Now this position's hardest problem. Campaign attention goes to closely divided states, not to small ones. The strongest defenders concede that the data on campaign attention is uncomfortable. They fall back on the values claim: federal composition matters, even if no campaign consultant prices it in.

And what would change this position? A showing that small-state protection does not operate in modern campaigning, because campaigns ignore most small states anyway. And with it, evidence that the coalition incentive produces swing-state pandering, not breadth.

## The democratic-majority reformer

Now the democratic-majority reformer, built the same way. What this position protects is equality. In choosing the one official who represents the whole country, every citizen's vote should weigh the same.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The starting move is moral, not historical. The Senate is malapportioned by design. The judiciary is appointed. The presidency is the single office plausibly accountable to a national majority. [beat] And the Electoral College distorts exactly that office.

Robert Dahl, in a 2001 book, put the comparative point bluntly. Among established democracies, no other country elects its chief executive through anything like it. And almost none of the Framers' stated reasons for it survived even their own generation.

> The democratic-majority reformer's case: for a national office, every vote should weigh the same.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, where the campaigns go. In the general election of 2024, 94 percent of campaign events took place in seven battleground states. Together, those seven hold a bit under 20 percent of the country's population.

Here the reformer draws on the scholar George Edwards. Attention follows competitiveness, not size. Candidates ignore small safe states exactly as thoroughly as they ignore large safe ones. So whatever the Electoral College is protecting, the reformer argues, it is not the small state as such. It is the closely divided state, a category with no constitutional significance whatsoever.

And that reframes the arithmetic you heard a few minutes ago. The nearly four-to-one weight of a Wyoming vote is, the reformer argues, the smaller distortion. The larger is that a vote in any safe state has effectively zero chance of changing the outcome.

[beat]

Second, the misfires. Five elections have put the loser of the popular vote in the White House, and two of those came in the seven elections since 2000. The public has noticed. In 2024, Pew found 63 percent of Americans in favor of a national popular vote.

[beat]

Third, the record of the Convention itself. On July 19, 1787, Madison called "the people at large" the fittest to choose the executive. But he saw a difficulty. The right to vote was much wider in the Northern states than in the Southern, where enslaved people could not vote. And here is his answer. "The substitution of electors obviated this difficulty."

[pause]

Electors resolved it, because the three-fifths ratio counted enslaved people toward seats in the House, and therefore toward electors. Akhil Amar and others press the implication. The original allocation rewarded slaveholding states. That rationale died with the suffrage amendments. And what it left behind, on this view, is inertia where the reason used to be.

Defenders respond that slavery was one thread among several. The quotation, at least, is not in dispute.

[pause]

Let's test this position. A defender says: "Under direct election, candidates would just run up margins in New York and Los Angeles and ignore everyone else." What is the reformer's strongest reply from the evidence? Take a moment.

[long pause]

That the current system already writes off most of the country. In 2024, 94 percent of campaign events went to seven states, holding under a fifth of the population. Under direct election, every marginal vote counts the same, wherever it is cast. And a campaign that only ran up its margins in cities would leave most votes to its opponent: suburban, rural, small-town.

That reply concedes nothing about values. It attacks the defense's factual premise: that the Electoral College produces geographic breadth.

Now this position's hardest problem. Direct election at national scale has failure modes of its own: recounts, and incentives for fraud. And the cause carries a tension in its own history. In 1979, civil-rights organizations helped sink a direct-election amendment, reasoning that pivotal blocs in big winner-take-all states would lose leverage in a national pool.

And what would change this position? A showing that direct national election would generate worse pathologies at scale: nationwide recount chaos, sharper incentives for fraud in one-party strongholds, campaigns that abandon retail politics. All of it large enough to outweigh the gains in equality and legitimacy.

## The federalist preservationist

Now the federalist preservationist, built the same way. This position is regularly confused with the first, and the confusion matters.

The originalist defends the Electoral College because the Founders' design was wise. The preservationist argues something narrower. Even if the original reasons are dead, the state-by-state structure now does constitutional work, and reformers have not priced that work in.

So what this position protects is the federal structure, as it operates today.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The President is the executive of a federal union, not of an undifferentiated national mass. And the states run elections. Registration, ballot design, and the triggers for a recount all vary by state law.

So a national popular vote, on this view, is not a thing that exists. It is a sum of 51 separately administered elections, conducted under 51 different rule books. [beat] The Electoral College is honest about that. It adds up outcomes in the states, which are real legal facts. It does not pretend that raw totals, cast under different rules, can be measured on one scale.

> The federalist preservationist's case: the state-by-state structure does work nothing else does.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, a principle. Judith Best, in a 1996 book, gives this camp its sharpest formulation. The federal principle, she argues, requires presidential majorities to be distributed majorities: concurrent majorities, across many states. The Constitution asks the same of an amendment, which needs supermajorities of states, and not just of people. A majority concentrated in one region is exactly the kind the federal design is built to filter.

[beat]

Second, containment, which is the most practical point. In 2000, the national margin was half a million votes. But the legal fight was bounded. One state. One set of recount rules. One certified margin, of 537 votes.

Under direct election, an equally tiny national margin would make every precinct in every state worth litigating, all at once. [beat] Fifty Floridas, run under fifty different recount statutes.

The federal structure quarantines the trouble. Nationalize the count, the preservationist argues, and you nationalize the crisis.

[beat]

Third, experimentation. Maine and Nebraska award electors by district. Some states use ranked-choice ballots for President. Those are exercises of the state-level authority that reform would extinguish.

And from there follows this camp's closing demand. If the country wants direct election, the honest path is an amendment under Article Five, with the supermajorities that entails. Not a workaround.

[pause]

Let's test this position. A senator argues: "Even if Hamilton's reasons are obsolete, abolishing the Electoral College would turn every close national election into a fifty-state recount war." Which camp is she in? And which words in her sentence tell you? Take a moment.

[long pause]

She is a federalist preservationist, and the giveaway is how her sentence opens: even if. She has surrendered the ground of the Founders' wisdom, which is the originalist's home turf. She is defending the institution on its structural consequences today.

The two defenses often travel together in politics. But they rest on different premises, and they fail under different evidence.

Now this position's hardest problem. It has to explain why this structure carries federalism, and not the Senate and the Tenth Amendment.

And what would change this position? Evidence from other federations. Brazil, Mexico, Argentina and Nigeria all elect their presidents directly. A showing that they combine direct election with robust authority below the national level, and with recount procedures a country can survive, would change it. The strongest preservationists concede that this is an open empirical question, not a settled one.

## Where the disagreement lives

Three positions. Before we sort them, pull something back. Near the start, I said these camps disagree in two different ways. Can you still name the two?

[long pause]

Values, and evidence. On one kind of question, data bears directly. On the other, no data settles anything.

So sort what you have heard, starting with what is settled, or nearly so.

Five elections put the loser of the popular vote in the White House. [beat] Campaign attention is overwhelmingly concentrated: 94 percent of events, in seven states, in 2024. [beat] States hold full authority over how electors are appointed. [beat] And slavery's arithmetic shaped the original design, alongside other rationales. Madison's remarks of July 19 are on the record.

Now what is contested. Whether direct election would produce worse failure modes than the ones it cures. [beat] Whether the coalition incentive produces breadth, or merely moves the narrowness from big states to swing states. [beat] And whether federalism needs this institution at all, given the Senate, the Tenth Amendment, and state control of election machinery.

Underneath all of those sits the question of values.

> A national electorate, or the states as states: no dataset resolves that.

Now choose the position you find least congenial. State its best case, the way its smartest holder would, and name what would change that holder's mind. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The camps largely agree on the arithmetic. They disagree about what it means. Part of that disagreement is about evidence, and part is about values.

[beat]

Two. The originalist defender's case: majorities must be federal majorities before they can govern. Its evidence: the Convention's choice, the plus two as the price of union, and 55 elections of 60. Its hardest problem: campaigns go to closely divided states, not small ones.

[beat]

Three. The democratic-majority reformer's case: for a national office, every vote should weigh the same. Its evidence: 94 percent of campaign events in seven states, five misfires, and the Convention's own record on slavery. Its hardest problem: direct election has failure modes of its own.

[beat]

Four. The federalist preservationist's case: the state-by-state structure does work nothing else does. Its evidence: the federal principle, 51 rule books, and one Florida in place of fifty. Its hardest problem: explaining why federalism needs this institution, and not only the Senate.

[beat]

And five. A national electorate, or the states as states: no dataset resolves that.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not chosen among the three, and it will not. A defensible position takes one of these frames, says why, and names what would change its holder's mind. You should be able to do the same for the position you like least.

The next lecture leaves the seminar room for the historical record. What has this machine actually done, across 60 elections? Five times, it overrode the popular vote. Twice, it nearly broke.

For now, go back to the Senate in 1956. A young Democrat from Massachusetts, speaking against reform, and warning of a whole solar system of governmental power. [beat] The argument scrambled partisan instincts then. It still does. And you can now state all three sides of it.
