## Three hundred thirty-nine to seventy

On September 18, 1969, the House of Representatives voted 339 to 70 to abolish the Electoral College and elect presidents by direct national vote. President Nixon endorsed it. Gallup had measured public support at 80 percent.

- House, 1969: 339–70

## Then the Senate

Then the proposal met the Senate. A filibuster held through two failed cloture votes, and the proposal died without ever receiving an up-or-down vote.

- Senate: filibustered, never voted on

## The road around the amendment

So the reform energy shifted to paths that need no amendment. The largest is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact. A state commits its electors to the winner of the national popular vote, and the commitment stays suspended until states holding 270 electoral votes have done the same.

- Dormant until 270

As of mid-2026 the compact holds 222 electoral votes, 48 short. Supporters argue each state is using an appointment power it already has. Critics argue the compact does what the Constitution reserves to an amendment.

- 222 electoral votes, 48 short

> Below 270, the compact changes nothing anywhere.

## What one state can do alone

Any single state can award electors by congressional district, as Maine and Nebraska do. The catch is that the district method inherits the congressional map, and the map is drawn by politicians.

- District method: inherits the map

In 2012 Barack Obama won the national vote by about five million ballots. Mitt Romney carried 226 of the 435 congressional districts to Obama's 209.

- 2012: Obama by 5 million, Romney 226 districts

> District allocation can make inversions more likely, not less.

## Small alone, large together

Read the whole menu and a pattern shows.

> What one state can do alone is small. What is large needs coordination.

And the coordination can be vetoed by those the system benefits. Each perspective sorts the menu its own way. The compact's supporter works the map state by state. The federalist preservationist accepts district experiments and opposes the compact as a workaround. The amendment purist accepts only Article Five, and bets the country can be persuaded.

- Compact supporter: state by state
- Preservationist: experiments, not the compact
- Amendment purist: only Article V

The lesson has all five paths, with proportional allocation, the Senate votes of 1970 and 1979, and the legal fight over the compact.
