## A whole solar system

In March 1956, the Senate debated an amendment that would have divided every state's electoral votes. The most effective voice against it was a 38-year-old Democrat named John F. Kennedy. "It is not only the unit vote for the Presidency we are talking about," he warned, "but a whole solar system of governmental power." The reform died.

- Senator John F. Kennedy, 1956

Serious people have landed on every side of this argument. Three positions follow, each as its smartest adherent would put it.

## The originalist defender

The originalist defender argues that the Founders deliberately rejected direct election, and that the design forces broad, federal coalitions. The defender points to the record: 55 of 60 elections aligned with the popular vote.

Every state gets its House seats plus two electors. So a Wyoming vote carries nearly four times the electoral weight, per person, of a California vote. The defender calls that the knowing price of union, identical in logic to the Senate.

- Wyoming vs California: nearly 4 to 1

> The defender's case: the knowing price of union.

## The democratic-majority reformer

The democratic-majority reformer argues that in choosing the one official who represents the whole country, every citizen's vote should weigh the same. The reformer points to the campaign record: in 2024, 94 percent of general-election events took place in seven battleground states.

- 94% of 2024 events in 7 states

Attention follows competitiveness, not size. Candidates ignore small safe states exactly as thoroughly as large safe ones.

> The reformer's case: the system protects swing states, not small ones.

## The federalist preservationist

The federalist preservationist argues that even if the original reasons are dead, the state-by-state structure now does load-bearing work. The preservationist points to 2000: the national margin was half a million votes, but the legal fight was bounded to one state. Under direct election, an equally tiny national margin would make every precinct in every state worth litigating.

- 2000: one Florida, not fifty

> The preservationist's case: the structure quarantines a disputed election.

## Values and evidence

The camps largely agree on the arithmetic. They disagree about what it means. Whether the President should answer to one national electorate, or to the states as states, is a question of values that no data settles.

- Agreed: the arithmetic
- Contested: what it means

The lesson gives each position at full length, its hardest problem, and the evidence its holder admits would change their mind.
