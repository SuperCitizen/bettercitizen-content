## The election in December

On December 17, 2024, 538 people gathered in fifty-one separate rooms, one in each state capitol and one in Washington, D.C., and elected the President of the United States.

[beat]

The November election you watched on television was, constitutionally speaking, an enormous opinion poll about how those 538 people should vote.

> The Electoral College is a procedure, not a place.

## Where 538 comes from

Each state gets a number of electors equal to its total seats in Congress. That is 435 for the House and 100 for the Senate. Washington, D.C. has three more. A majority wins, and a majority of 538 is 270.

- 435 House + 100 Senate + 3 D.C.
- 270 to win

[check q1]

## Who decides how electors are chosen

The Constitution says each state appoints its electors in the manner its legislature directs. It does not require a popular vote, anywhere, for anyone.

- The state legislature decides

Today forty-eight states and D.C. use winner-take-all: whoever wins the statewide vote, even by one ballot, gets every elector. Maine and Nebraska award some of theirs by congressional district instead.

- Winner-take-all: 48 states + D.C.
- By district: Maine and Nebraska

None of that is in the Constitution. A legislature could switch methods tomorrow, with no amendment required.

> Winner-take-all is a state-law choice, not a constitutional command.

[check q2]

## If nobody reaches 270

Now the part of the machine with the highest stakes. If no candidate wins a majority, the Twelfth Amendment moves the choice to Congress. The House picks the President from the top three, and it votes one state, one vote. California's delegation of 52 members casts a single vote. Wyoming's one member casts one too. The Senate picks the Vice President from the top two.

- House: top three, one vote per state
- Senate: Vice President, from the top two

> In the House, 26 state delegations make a President.

It has happened twice for President, in 1800 and in 1824.

[check q3]
