## Two Presidents who lost

Two days before Christmas in 2020, President Donald Trump said no to a bill. Congress had passed it, but the President refused to sign it. Congress voted again, and the bill became a law without his name on it.

Four years before that, the same thing happened to President Barack Obama. He said no to a bill, and Congress passed it anyway.

- 2020: President Trump
- 2016: President Obama

One President was a Republican and the other was a Democrat, and both of them lost.

> A President can say no to a bill, and still lose.

## The veto, and how Congress can beat it

When Congress passes a bill, it goes to the President. The President can sign it, and then it is a law. Or the President can send it back, which is called a veto. A veto is the President's way to check Congress. To check a branch means to stop it or slow it down.

- Veto: the President sends a bill back

Congress has a check of its own. It can vote on the bill again. If two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote yes, the bill becomes a law anyway. That is called an override.

- Override: two-thirds of House and Senate

Two-thirds is a lot. If all 100 senators vote, it takes 67 of them. Presidents have vetoed bills 2,599 times since 1789, and Congress has overridden 112 of those vetoes.

- 2,599 vetoes, 112 overrides

> A veto is strong, but it is not always the last word.

## Each branch can check the others

Every branch has at least one way to limit the other two. Congress decides how money is spent. The President picks the judges. The courts can rule that a law breaks the Constitution, and that check is called judicial review.

- Congress: the override, and the money
- The President: the veto, and the judges
- The courts: judicial review

A plan like this one, where each branch can limit the others, is called checks and balances.

## Accused is not removed

Congress has one check that it rarely uses. It can remove a President or a judge from office, and that takes two steps. First, the House votes to impeach. To impeach means to formally accuse an official of serious wrongdoing. Second, the Senate holds a trial. The official is removed only if two-thirds of the senators at the trial vote guilty.

- Step 1: the House accuses
- Step 2: the Senate holds a trial

> To impeach is to accuse. Removing takes a trial.

## A tool someone has to pick up

The checks are tools, and a tool only works when someone picks it up. Americans argue about whether the branches use their checks too little or too much. That argument is still going on.

- Too little, or too much?
