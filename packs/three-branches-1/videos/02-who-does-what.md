## Three buildings

Three buildings in Washington, D.C., tell the story of the three branches. Congress works in the Capitol, the big building with the white dome. The President lives and works in the White House. The Supreme Court meets in a building of its own, right across the street from the Capitol.

- The Capitol: Congress
- The White House: the President
- The Supreme Court building: the Court

> Each branch has its own home, and its own job.

## Hundreds of people, and one

Congress makes the laws, and it has two parts. The House of Representatives has 435 members. States with more people get more members, and a House member serves for two years. The Senate has 100 members. Each state gets two senators, no matter how big or small it is, and a senator serves for six years.

- House: 435 members, 2 years
- Senate: 2 per state, 6 years

The President carries out the laws. The country elects a President every four years, and a person can be elected President only twice.

- President: 1 person, 4 years

Making a law takes hundreds of people, but carrying out the laws is led by just one. That is on purpose.

> A big group argues over the rules. One leader acts fast.

## Nine justices, and no set term

The courts decide what the laws mean. The highest court is the Supreme Court. Its judges are called justices, and there are nine of them. Here is a surprise. The Constitution does not say how many justices there should be. Congress chose the number nine in 1869.

- 9 justices: chosen by Congress, 1869

[check q2]

The judges of the national courts are called federal judges, and they are not elected. The President picks them, and the Senate has to approve them. After that, they have no set term. Why so long? A judge sometimes has to make a ruling that many people dislike. The writers of the Constitution wanted judges to do that without fear of losing their jobs.

> Judges have no set term, so they can rule without fear.

[check q4]

## Three branches that need each other

Each branch has its own job and its own way of choosing people. Even so, the branches cannot stay out of each other's way. A bill, which is an idea for a law, needs both Congress and the President. A new judge needs both the President and the Senate.

- A bill: Congress and the President
- A judge: the President and the Senate
