You know what it feels like when someone in charge says no. Most of the time, that is the end of the story.

[beat]

Now picture a much bigger no. It is two days before Christmas, in 2020. Congress has passed a huge yearly bill, one that sets plans and pay for the military. And the president, Donald Trump, refuses to sign it.

[pause]

This time, no was not the end of the story. Members of Congress came back to Washington over the holidays. On December 28, the House voted to pass the bill anyway. On New Year's Day, the Senate agreed, 81 to 13. The bill became a law without the president's name on it.

[pause]

Four years before that, in 2016, the same thing happened to a different president. President Barack Obama said no to a bill. It was about lawsuits over the attacks of September 11, 2001. Congress passed it anyway. The Senate vote was 97 to 1.

One president was a Republican. The other was a Democrat. [beat] Both of them lost.

[pause]

So what rule let Congress win? Last time, we ended on a bigger question: what happens when the branches disagree? Today we answer both, in three steps. First, the president's no, and how Congress can beat it. Second, the tools each branch can use against the others. And third, the strongest tool of all.

## The veto, and how Congress can beat it

When Congress passes a bill, it goes to the president. The president can sign it, and then it is a law. Or the president can send it back, with a list of reasons. That is called a veto, from a Latin word that means "I forbid."

A veto is the president's way to check Congress. To check a branch means to stop it, or slow it down.

But Congress has a check of its own. It can vote on the bill again. If two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote yes, the bill becomes a law anyway. That is called an override.

> A veto sends the bill back to Congress. It does not end the story.

Two-thirds is a lot. If all 100 senators vote, it takes 67 of them. Most of the time, Congress cannot get there.

How rare is an override? Count every veto since 1789. By November of 2026, presidents had vetoed bills almost 2,600 times. [beat] Congress had overridden 112 of those vetoes.

So a veto is strong. But it is not always the last word.

[pause]

Now you can explain that bill, two days before Christmas. Congress passed it. The president vetoed it. It went back to Congress. Two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate voted yes. Only then did it become a law, without the president signing it.

## Each branch can check the others

The veto and the override are only two of the checks. Every branch has at least one way to limit the other two.

Start with Congress. It can override a veto. It can decide how money is spent. It can say no to the president's picks for judges and top jobs. And it can remove a president or a judge.

Next, the president. The president can veto a bill, and can pick the judges.

Last, the courts. They can rule that a law breaks the Constitution. They can also rule that a president broke the law.

> Every branch has tools. No branch has all of them.

The courts' check has a name, judicial review, and it comes with a surprise. [beat] The Constitution never spells it out. The Supreme Court said it had this power in 1803, in a case called Marbury v. Madison. Courts have used it ever since.

A plan like this one has a name too.

> Checks and balances means each branch can limit the others.

Let's test that. Congress passes a law, and the president signs it. A year later, the Supreme Court rules that the law breaks the Constitution. Is that the end of the story? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not always. The law can no longer be used. But Congress can write a new law that fixes the problem. The country can even change the Constitution itself, though that is very hard to do. So even the Supreme Court can be answered.

## The strongest check: impeachment

Congress has one check that it rarely uses. It can remove a president, a judge, or another top official from office. Removing someone takes two steps.

First, the House votes to impeach. To impeach means to formally accuse an official of serious wrongdoing. More than half of the House members voting must agree.

Second, the Senate holds a trial. The official is removed only if two-thirds of the senators at the trial vote "guilty."

> Impeached does not mean removed.

Let's test it. The House votes to impeach a president. What happens next?

[long pause]

The president does not leave office that day. The Supreme Court does not take a vote. The country does not hold a new election. [beat] The Senate holds a trial. Impeaching is only the accusation. People often say impeached when they mean removed, but the two are not the same.

Three presidents have been impeached. Andrew Johnson, in 1868. Bill Clinton, in 1998. And Donald Trump, in 2019, and again in 2021. [beat] The Senate did not find any of them guilty.

In its whole history, the Senate has found eight officials guilty. [beat] All eight were federal judges.

## Do the checks always work?

One last idea. None of these checks works by itself. Congress has to choose to override a veto. The Senate has to choose to turn down a pick. A court has to be asked to rule.

> A check is a tool. It only works when someone picks it up.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A veto sends a bill back to Congress. It does not end the story.

[beat]

Two. Congress can override a veto. Can you still say how many votes that takes?

[long pause]

Two-thirds of the House, and two-thirds of the Senate. That is a lot, and most of the time Congress cannot get there.

[beat]

Three. Checks and balances means each branch can limit the others. Congress decides how money is spent. The president can veto a bill. The courts can rule that a law breaks the Constitution. Every branch has tools. No branch has all of them.

[beat]

Four. Impeached does not mean removed. The House accuses. The Senate holds the trial, and it takes two-thirds of the senators there to remove an official.

[pause]

Remember, a check only works when someone picks it up. So here is what is not settled. Americans argue about whether the branches use their checks too little, or too much. That argument is as old as the country, and it is still going on. Now that you know what the tools are, you can judge for yourself.

Remember where we started. A president said no, two days before Christmas. Congress picked up a tool. [beat] And no was not the last word.
