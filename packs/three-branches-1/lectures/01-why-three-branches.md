Picture a kickball game at recess. One kid makes up all the rules. The same kid decides who broke them. And the same kid hands out the punishments.

Now picture that kid's team starting to lose. [beat] What happens next?

[long pause]

You can probably guess. The rules change. The calls all go one way. And nobody can stop it, because one person holds all the power.

[pause]

The people who wrote the United States Constitution worried about this same problem. Their answer was to split the government's power into three parts. We call those parts the three branches.

Today we will find out why. We will take three steps. First, how the country got here. Second, the three jobs that every government does. And third, why no one group should do all three.

## First a king, then a plan that was too weak

Before 1776, the American colonies were ruled by Great Britain and its king. The colonists believed the king had too much power over them. So they fought a war to be free. And they won.

Then came the hard part. They had to build a government of their own.

Their first plan was called the Articles of Confederation. It set up a Congress. A Congress is a group of people, sent by the states, to make decisions.

And that was almost all it set up. [beat] There was no president to carry out the laws. There were almost no national courts to settle fights. Congress could ask the states for money. But it had no way to make them pay.

The plan did not work well.

So here is a question for you. That first plan had a Congress. It had no president, and almost no national courts. Which jobs do you think went undone?

[long pause]

Two jobs. Nobody was in charge of carrying out the laws that Congress passed. And no national court could settle most arguments, such as a fight between two states.

A government needs someone to make the rules, someone to carry them out, and someone to settle disputes. The first plan had only one of the three.

So in the summer of 1787, leaders from the states met in Philadelphia to try again. They had a hard puzzle to solve. The new government had to be two things at once.

> Strong enough to get things done. But not so strong that it could act like a king.

## Three jobs, three branches

The leaders in Philadelphia wrote a new plan. It is called the Constitution. And it is still the highest law in the country.

The writers saw that every government does three main jobs. [beat] It makes laws. It carries out those laws. And it decides what the laws mean when people disagree.

The Constitution gives each of those jobs to a different group.

The first three parts of the Constitution are called articles. Each article sets up one branch.

Article One sets up Congress. Congress makes the laws. This is called the legislative branch.

Article Two sets up the president. The president carries out the laws. This is called the executive branch.

Article Three sets up the courts. The courts decide what the laws mean. This is called the judicial branch.

[pause]

Three branches. Three jobs. They are worth knowing by heart.

> Make the laws. Carry them out. Decide what they mean.

Splitting up the jobs this way has a name. It is called the separation of powers. A power, here, means the right to do one of those three jobs.

> Separation of powers means that no single branch gets to do all three.

## Why not let one group do it all?

One of the writers was a man named James Madison. He explained the plan in a set of essays, which were printed in newspapers. In one of them, he wrote a line that people still quote today.

"If men were angels, no government would be necessary."

[pause]

If men were angels. What did he mean by that?

He meant that people are not perfect. Even a good leader can be tempted to grab more power. So the plan does not count on leaders being good.

> The plan counts on power being split up.

In another essay, Madison gave a warning. Putting all three powers "in the same hands," he wrote, is "the very definition of tyranny."

Tyranny is a big word. It means cruel and unfair rule by someone with too much power. And Madison said it did not matter whose hands they were. One person, or a large group. Too much power in one place was the danger either way.

Now think back to the kickball game. The fix is simple. One kid makes the rules. A different kid calls the plays. And a third kid settles the arguments. With the jobs split up like that, no one can cheat alone.

Let's try Madison's idea on a new case. A city has one leader. That leader writes the laws, runs the police, and acts as the only judge. What would Madison say is wrong?

[long pause]

Start with what he would not say. He would not say the leader was chosen in an unfair vote. He would not say the city has too many laws. [beat] He would say that all three powers are in the same hands.

Madison's worry was about where power sits. Even a leader who won a fair vote would hold all three powers at once. Nothing would stop that leader from making an unfair law, enforcing it, and then ruling that it was fine.

## Where the kickball picture breaks down

One last thing. A kickball game is not a country, and our picture has one flaw.

On the playground, the three kids could do their jobs without ever talking to each other. The real branches cannot work that way. They have to work together. And each one can also block the others, in certain ways.

## What to keep

So let's gather it up. Three things to keep.

One. The first plan, the Articles of Confederation, was too weak. It had a Congress, but no president, and almost no national courts.

[beat]

Two. The Constitution splits the government's three jobs among three branches. Can you still name who does what? [long pause] Congress makes the laws. The president carries them out. The courts decide what they mean.

[beat]

Three. That split is called the separation of powers. It is there because, as Madison warned, all the powers in the same hands is the very definition of tyranny.

[pause]

That leaves two questions. Who does what inside each branch? And what happens when the branches disagree? The next two lectures take those questions one at a time.

For now, remember the kickball game. One kid with all the jobs can cheat. [beat] Three kids, with one job each, cannot cheat alone.
