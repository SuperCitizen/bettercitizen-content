Imagine your class has a big idea. You want a new national holiday. So you sit down to write a letter.

[beat]

But where do you send it?

Picture three buildings in Washington, D.C. The first is the Capitol, the big building with the white dome. Congress works there. The second is the White House, where the president lives and works. The third stands right across the street from the Capitol. The Supreme Court meets there.

[pause]

Three buildings, for three branches.

Last time, we gave each branch a job. Today we go inside. We will walk to each building in turn, and ask three things. What is the job? Who gets it? And how long do they keep it?

By the end, you will know where your letter goes, and why.

## Congress makes the laws

We start at the Capitol.

Congress is the legislative branch, and legislative means law-making. Congress has two parts, and one rule ties them together.

> A new law has to pass both parts of Congress.

The first part is the House of Representatives. It has 435 members. States with more people get more members, and every state gets at least one. A House member serves for two years, and then has to run again.

The second part is the Senate. It has 100 members. Each state gets two senators, no matter how big or small it is. A senator serves for six years.

[pause]

So the House is the big part, and its members serve for two years. The Senate is the small part, and its members serve for six.

A law begins as an idea, and an idea for a law is called a bill. Members of Congress write bills, argue about them, and vote on them. A bill has to win that vote twice, once in the House and once in the Senate.

Congress also decides how the government collects money, and how it spends that money. And only Congress can declare war.

[pause]

Now think about the size of Congress. Making a law takes hundreds of people, who argue over what the rules should be. Carrying out the laws is led by just one person. [beat] That is on purpose.

> A big group is good at arguing. One leader is good at acting fast.

## The president carries out the laws

So let's meet that one leader. Our next stop is the White House.

The president leads the executive branch. Executive means the part that carries things out. The country elects a president every four years, and a person can be elected president only twice.

The president does not do the work alone. Millions of people work in the executive branch, including soldiers, park rangers and food inspectors. The president is their boss.

The president has other big jobs, too. Here are four.

One. The president leads the armed forces. That makes the president the commander in chief.

Two. The president deals with other countries, and can make agreements called treaties. The Senate has to approve them.

Three. The president chooses people for top jobs, and that includes judges. The Senate has to approve many of these picks.

Four. The president signs or rejects bills. A bill from Congress goes to the president before it can become a law.

[pause]

Notice that the Senate came up twice in that list. Hold on to that.

Now, one caution. You may hear someone say, "The president makes the laws." [beat] Not quite. The president can ask Congress for a law.

> The president can sign a bill or reject it. Only Congress can pass one.

So, back to your letter. Your class wants a new national holiday. Which branch should you write to first, and why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Write to Congress. [beat] A national holiday is set by a law, and Congress is the branch that makes laws. After Congress passes the bill, the president can sign it or reject it. But Congress comes first.

## The courts decide what the laws mean

One building is left. Cross the street from the Capitol, and you are at the Supreme Court.

This building is newer than you might think. [beat] It was finished in 1935. For more than a hundred years before that, the Court met in rooms inside the Capitol.

The courts are the judicial branch, and judicial means having to do with judges. Sometimes people disagree about what a law means. Sometimes a person says that a law breaks the Constitution. Courts settle those fights.

The country has many national courts, which are called federal courts. The highest one is the Supreme Court. Its judges are called justices, and there are nine of them.

Here is a surprise. [beat] The Constitution does not say how many justices there should be. Congress chose the number nine, in 1869, and it has not changed since.

Who gets this job? Federal judges are not elected. The president picks them, and the Senate has to approve them.

And for how long? Judges have no set term, which means no fixed number of years. The Constitution says they keep their jobs "during good Behaviour." Most of them serve until they retire or die.

[pause]

Why so long? Here is a hint. A judge sometimes has to make a ruling that many people dislike. Think it through.

[long pause]

The writers of the Constitution wanted judges to make that ruling without fear of losing their jobs.

> Judges have no set term, so they can make rulings people dislike without fear.

## Three branches that need each other

That is the end of our walk. Each branch has its own job, its own building, and its own way of choosing people. Even so, the branches cannot stay out of each other's way.

Remember the Senate, coming up twice in the president's list. That was a clue. A new judge needs both the president and the Senate. And a bill needs both Congress and the president.

> The three branches need each other.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Congress makes the laws. It has two parts, the House and the Senate, and a new law has to pass both.

[beat]

Two. The president carries out the laws. The president can sign a bill or reject it. Only Congress can pass one.

[beat]

Three. The courts decide what the laws mean. The president picks the judges, and the Senate has to approve them.

[beat]

Four. Each job is held for a different length of time. Can you still say how long? A House member. A senator. A president. A Supreme Court justice.

[long pause]

A House member serves two years. A senator serves six. A president serves four, and can be elected at most twice. And a justice has no set term, so that a judge can rule without fear.

[pause]

Here is what we have not answered. What happens when the branches disagree? That is the subject of the next lecture, the last one in this series.

For now, picture the Capitol once more, with its white dome. [beat] That is where your letter goes. And now you know why.
