# Why does the government have three branches?

Picture a kickball game at recess where one kid makes up all the rules. The same kid decides who broke them, and then hands out the punishments too. What do you think happens when the rule-maker's own team starts to lose?

You can probably guess. The rules change, the calls all go one way, and nobody can stop it, because one person holds all the power.

The people who wrote the United States Constitution worried about this same problem. Their answer was to split the government's power into three parts, which we call the three branches.

## First a king, then a plan that was too weak

Before 1776, the American colonies were ruled by Great Britain and its king. The colonists believed the king had too much power over them, so they fought a war to be free, and they won.

Then they had to build a government of their own. Their first plan, called the Articles of Confederation, set up a Congress, which was a group of people sent by the states to make decisions. That was almost all it set up. There was no president to carry out the laws, and there were almost no national courts to settle fights. Congress could ask the states for money, but it had no way to make them pay.

The plan did not work well, so in the summer of 1787 leaders from the states met in Philadelphia to try again. They had a hard puzzle to solve. The new government had to be strong enough to get things done, but not so strong that it could act like a king.

Nobody was in charge of carrying out the laws that Congress passed, and no national court could settle most arguments, such as a fight between two states. A government needs someone to make the rules, someone to carry them out, and someone to settle disputes.

</Checkpoint>

## Three jobs, three branches

The leaders in Philadelphia wrote a new plan called the Constitution, and it is still the highest law in the country.

The writers saw that every government does three main jobs. It makes laws, it carries out those laws, and it decides what the laws mean when people disagree. The Constitution gives each of these jobs to a different group.

Splitting up the jobs this way has a name: the **separation of powers**. A *power* here means the right to do one of those three jobs. [[term:separation-of-powers]] means that no single branch gets to do all three.

## Why not let one group do it all?

One of the writers was James Madison, who explained the plan in a set of essays that were printed in newspapers. In one essay he wrote a line that people still quote today.

> **In the primary sources:** "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." James Madison, 1788

Madison meant that people are not perfect, and that even a good leader can be tempted to grab more power. So the plan does not count on leaders being good. It counts on power being split up among them.

In another essay, Madison gave a warning. Putting all three powers "in the same hands," he wrote, is "the very definition of tyranny." *Tyranny* means cruel and unfair rule by someone with too much power. Madison said it did not matter whether those hands belonged to one person or to a large group, because too much power in one place was the danger either way.

Now think back to the kickball game, where the fix is simple. One kid makes the rules, a different kid calls the plays, and a third kid settles the arguments. With the jobs split up like that, no one can cheat alone.

## Where the kickball picture breaks down

A kickball game is not a country, and the picture has one flaw. On the playground, the three kids could do their jobs without ever talking to each other. The real branches cannot work that way. They have to work together, and each one can also block the others in certain ways.

That leaves two questions. Who does what in each branch? And what happens when the branches disagree? The next two lessons take those questions one at a time.
