# Who does what in the three branches?

Three buildings in Washington, D.C., tell the story of the three branches. Congress works in the Capitol, the big building with the white dome. The President lives and works in the White House. The Supreme Court meets in a building of its own, right across the street from the Capitol.

That last building is newer than you might think. It was finished in 1935. For more than a hundred years before that, the Court met in rooms inside the Capitol. Today each branch has its own home, and each one has its own job.

## Congress makes the laws

Congress is the legislative branch, and *legislative* means "law-making." Congress has two parts, and a new law has to pass both of them.

The first part is the **House of Representatives**, which has 435 members. States with more people get more members, and every state gets at least one. A House member serves for two years and then has to run again.

The second part is the **Senate**, which has 100 members. Each state gets two senators, no matter how big or small it is, and a senator serves for six years.

An idea for a law is called a *bill*. Members of Congress write bills, argue about them, and vote on them. Congress also decides how the government collects money and how it spends that money, and only Congress can declare war.

Look at those numbers again. Making a law takes hundreds of people, but carrying out the laws is led by just one person. That is on purpose. A big group is good at arguing over what the rules should be, and one leader is good at acting fast.

## The President carries out the laws

The President leads the executive branch, and *executive* means "the part that carries things out."

The country elects a President every four years, and a person can be elected President only twice.

The President does not do the work alone. Millions of people work in the executive branch, including soldiers, park rangers and food inspectors, and the President is their boss.

The President has other big jobs, too.

- **Leading the armed forces.** The President is the commander in chief.
- **Dealing with other countries.** The President can make agreements called treaties, which the Senate has to approve.
- **Choosing people for top jobs.** That includes judges, and the Senate has to approve many of these picks.
- **Signing or rejecting bills.** A bill from Congress goes to the President before it can become a law.

<Callout type="misconception">

"The President makes the laws." Not quite. The President can ask Congress for a law, and can sign a bill or reject it, but only Congress can pass one.

</Callout>

<Checkpoint question="Your class wants a new national holiday. Which branch should you write to first, and why?">

Write to Congress. A national holiday is set by a law, and Congress is the branch that makes laws. After Congress passes the bill, the President can sign it or reject it.

</Checkpoint>

## The courts decide what the laws mean

The courts are the judicial branch, and *judicial* means "having to do with judges."

Sometimes people disagree about what a law means, and sometimes a person says that a law breaks the Constitution. Courts settle those fights. The country has many national courts, which are called *federal* courts, and the highest one is the Supreme Court. Its judges are called *justices*, and there are nine of them.

Here is a surprise: the Constitution does not say how many justices there should be. Congress chose the number nine in 1869, and it has not changed since.

Federal judges are not elected. The President picks them, and the Senate has to approve them. After that, they have no set term, because the Constitution says they keep their jobs "during good Behaviour." Most of them serve until they retire or die.

Why so long? A judge sometimes has to make a ruling that many people dislike, and the writers of the Constitution wanted judges to do that without fear of losing their jobs.

## Three branches that need each other

Each branch has its own job, its own building and its own way of choosing people. Even so, the branches cannot stay out of each other's way. A bill needs both Congress and the President, and a new judge needs both the President and the Senate.

So what happens when the branches disagree? That is the subject of the last lesson.
