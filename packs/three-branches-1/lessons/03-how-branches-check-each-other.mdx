# What stops one branch from taking over?

Two days before Christmas in 2020, President Donald Trump said no to a bill. It was a huge yearly bill that sets plans and pay for the military. Congress had passed it, but the President refused to sign it.

Members of Congress came back to Washington over the holidays. On December 28, the House voted 322 to 87 to pass the bill anyway, and on New Year's Day the Senate agreed, 81 to 13. The bill became a law without the President's name on it.

Four years before that, the same thing happened to a different President. In 2016, President Barack Obama said no to a bill about lawsuits over the attacks of September 11, 2001. Congress passed it anyway, by votes of 348 to 77 in the House and 97 to 1 in the Senate.

One President was a Republican and the other was a Democrat, and both of them lost. This lesson is about the rule that let Congress win, and about the other ways the branches hold each other back.

## The veto, and how Congress can beat it

When Congress passes a bill, it goes to the President. The President can sign it, and then it is a law. Or the President can send it back with a list of reasons, which is called a **veto**. The word comes from Latin and means "I forbid."

A veto is the President's way to *check* Congress. To check a branch means to stop it or slow it down.

Congress has a check of its own, though. It can vote on the bill again, and if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote yes, the bill becomes a law anyway. That is called an **override**.

Two-thirds is a lot. If all 100 senators vote, it takes 67 of them, and most of the time Congress cannot get there.

## Each branch can check the others

The veto and the override are only two of the checks. Every branch has at least one way to limit the other two.

The courts' check has a name: **judicial review**. [[term:judicial-review]] comes with a surprise, which is that the Constitution never spells it out. The Supreme Court said it had this power in 1803, in a case called *Marbury v. Madison*, and courts have used it ever since.

[[term:checks-and-balances]] is the name for a plan like this one, where each branch can limit the others.

<Checkpoint question="Congress passes a law, and the President signs it. A year later, the Supreme Court rules that the law breaks the Constitution. Is that the end of the story?">

Not always. The law can no longer be used, but Congress can write a new law that fixes the problem. The country can even change the Constitution itself, though that is very hard to do. So even the Supreme Court can be answered.

</Checkpoint>

## The strongest check: impeachment

Congress has one check that it rarely uses. It can remove a President, a judge or another top official from office, and removing someone takes two steps.

First, the House votes to **impeach**. To impeach means to formally accuse an official of serious wrongdoing, and more than half of the House members voting must agree.

Second, the Senate holds a trial. The official is removed only if two-thirds of the senators at the trial vote "guilty."

Three Presidents have been impeached: Andrew Johnson in 1868, Bill Clinton in 1998, and Donald Trump in 2019 and again in 2021. The Senate did not find any of them guilty. In its whole history the Senate has found eight officials guilty, and all eight were federal judges.

## Do the checks always work?

The checks are tools, and a tool only works when someone picks it up. Congress has to choose to override a veto. The Senate has to choose to turn down a pick. A court has to be asked to rule.

Americans argue about whether the branches use their checks too little or too much. That argument is as old as the country, and it is still going on. Now that you know what the tools are, you can judge for yourself.
