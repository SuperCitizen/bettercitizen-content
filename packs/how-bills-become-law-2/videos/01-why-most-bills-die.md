## Two in a hundred

In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced 16,202 bills. Over the same two years, 274 measures became law. That is fewer than two for every hundred.

- 16,202 bills introduced
- 274 became law

The usual diagram makes the process look like a conveyor belt. It works more like an obstacle course.

> Where do bills stop, and who built the places where they stop?

## Win four times, or lose once

Picture a student who wants her school to allow phones at lunch. Four groups must say yes, one after another. She has to win four times in a row. Anyone who opposes her needs to win only once, at whichever stop is easiest.

Political scientists call each of these stops a veto point, meaning a place where a proposal can be halted.

- Veto point: where a proposal can be halted

## Three stops in the Constitution

The Constitution's own instructions are short. A bill must pass the House, pass the Senate, and be presented to the President. That creates three veto points and no more.

- The House, the Senate, the President

Everything else rests on a single clause: "Each House may determine the Rules of its Proceedings." Committees, the House Rules Committee and the Senate filibuster all come from that clause. None of them is mentioned anywhere in the Constitution, and each chamber could change them.

> The Constitution creates three veto points. Congress built the rest.

## The number 60

The Senate allows debate to continue until senators vote to end it. Refusing to end it is called a filibuster. The vote that ends debate is called cloture, and since 1975 it has taken three-fifths of all senators, which is 60.

- Cloture: 60 votes to end debate

Here is the part that trips up most people. Passing a bill in the Senate takes a simple majority. It is reaching the vote on passage that can take 60.

In 2021 a Democratic House passed a sweeping elections bill. In the Senate, the motion to take it up drew 50 votes when it needed 60.

> Passing a bill takes a majority. Reaching that vote can take 60.

## A flaw, or the design?

Alexander Hamilton defended difficulty openly. Stopping a few good laws, he judged, was a fair price for preventing a number of bad ones.

- Hamilton: the trade is worth making

Critics answer that Hamilton was defending the veto points in the Constitution, and that Congress has since added many more. When Congress cannot act, they argue, problems go unaddressed.

- Critics: problems go unaddressed

The lesson does not settle that argument. It walks every stop, from the committee chair to the veto, sets a Republican bill beside a Democratic one, and lets you slide a bill's support from 50 senators to 60.
