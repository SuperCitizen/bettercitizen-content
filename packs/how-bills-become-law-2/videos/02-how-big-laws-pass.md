## Fifty-one to fifty, twice

On August 7, 2022, the Senate passed a major climate, health and tax law by 51 votes to 50, with a Democratic Vice President casting the deciding vote. On July 1, 2025, it passed a major tax and spending law by 51 votes to 50, with a Republican Vice President casting the deciding vote.

- 2022: Inflation Reduction Act, 51–50
- 2025: One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 51–50

Most major bills need 60 votes to get past a filibuster. Neither of these had anything close.

> Neither law had 60 supporters. Both became law.

## The 51-vote route

Both bills traveled through a process called budget reconciliation, which Congress created in 1974. Its power comes from a single feature: the law limits Senate debate on a reconciliation bill to 20 hours.

- Senate debate limited to 20 hours

A debate with a time limit cannot be stretched out forever. So a filibuster is impossible, and the bill needs only a simple majority.

> A debate with a time limit cannot be filibustered.

## The fence around it

A route that easy would swallow every other kind of bill, so the Senate fenced it in. Any senator may challenge a provision as extraneous, meaning that it is not really about the budget. Keeping a challenged provision takes 60 votes.

- Not about the budget: 60 votes to keep

In practice, taxes and spending can travel this route. Most other policy cannot.

- Taxes and spending only

## The bill that has to pass

The second route runs through the government's own budget. Congress is supposed to pass a dozen spending bills before October 1, and it almost never manages to.

So leaders reach for two tools. A continuing resolution is a stopgap law that keeps money flowing for a few more weeks or months. An omnibus rolls many spending bills into a single enormous package.

- Continuing resolution: a stopgap
- Omnibus: many spending bills in one

A bill of that kind has to pass, because the alternative is a government shutdown. That makes it valuable to anyone with an unrelated proposal that could not pass by itself.

> A bill that has to pass can carry what could not pass alone.

[check q3]

## Adaptation, or decline?

People who study Congress disagree about what this means, and not along party lines. One view holds that Congress has adapted sensibly: the government keeps running, and an elected majority can enact some of its program.

- One view: a sensible adaptation

The other view holds that something has been lost: a package assembled by a few leaders, under a deadline, gets less scrutiny.

- The other view: less scrutiny

The lesson does not settle which view is right. It sets the two paths side by side, shows who writes these bills, and gives each view its argument. Then it reads Hamilton, who can be taken in support of either side.
