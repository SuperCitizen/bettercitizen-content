# How do big laws get passed anyway?

On August 7, 2022, the Senate passed a major climate, health and tax law by 51 votes to 50, with a Democratic Vice President casting the deciding vote. On July 1, 2025, the Senate passed a major tax and spending law by 51 votes to 50, with a Republican Vice President casting the deciding vote.

The first was the Inflation Reduction Act, and the second was the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Neither had anything close to 60 supporters. The previous lesson explained that most major bills need 60 votes to get past a filibuster in the Senate, yet both of these became law, because their sponsors did not take the path in the textbook diagram.

## The 51-vote route

Both bills traveled through a process called budget reconciliation, which Congress created in the Congressional Budget Act of 1974. [[term:reconciliation]] began as a practical idea. Each year Congress adopts a budget plan, and reconciliation was meant as a quick way to change tax and spending laws so that they matched the plan.

What makes reconciliation powerful is a single feature: the law limits Senate debate on a reconciliation bill to 20 hours. A debate with a time limit cannot be stretched out forever, so a filibuster is impossible, and the bill needs only a simple majority to pass.

Both parties have used the route for their largest priorities, as those two votes show. A route that easy would swallow every other kind of bill if it had no limits, however, and so the Senate fenced it in. Under a rule adopted in 1985, made permanent in 1990 and named for Senator Robert Byrd, any senator may challenge a provision in a reconciliation bill as "extraneous," meaning that it is not really about the budget. Keeping a challenged provision takes 60 votes, which defeats the purpose of using reconciliation at all. In practice, taxes and spending can travel this route, while most other policy cannot.

## One enormous bill in place of twelve

The second route runs through the government's own budget. Each year Congress is supposed to pass a dozen separate appropriations bills, which fund the federal agencies, before the new fiscal year begins on October 1. It almost never manages to. Since the current budget system took effect, Congress has passed all of its spending bills on time in only four years, and the most recent of those was fiscal 1997.

When the deadline approaches with the work unfinished, leaders reach for one of two tools. The first is a continuing resolution, a stopgap law that keeps money flowing at roughly the current level for a few more weeks or months. The second is an omnibus bill, which rolls many spending bills into a single enormous package that each chamber passes in one vote. [[term:continuing-resolution]] is usually shortened to CR, and you will see both tools named in the news every autumn.

A bill of that kind has to pass, because the alternative is a government shutdown. That makes it valuable to anyone with an unrelated proposal that could not pass by itself. The spending package of December 2022, for example, also carried the Electoral Count Reform Act, which rewrote the rules for counting presidential electoral votes. Lawmakers call such attachments riders.

The deadlines are real. In the fall of 2025 the fiscal year began with no agreement in place, and much of the federal government shut down for 43 days, the longest shutdown in American history.

<Checkpoint question="A senator has a popular bill on drug prices that cannot reach 60 votes. She tries to attach it to the year-end spending package. Why might that work when the bill alone failed?">

Because the package is a bill that has to pass. Senators who would filibuster the drug bill by itself may be unwilling to block the funding of the entire government in order to stop it. The strategy has costs, though. Leaders decide what gets attached, and every addition gives some member a new reason to vote no.

</Checkpoint>

## Who writes the bill?

These routes change more than the vote count. In the textbook, a bill is written by the committee that knows its subject, in public sessions where any member may propose a change. A reconciliation bill or an omnibus is usually assembled differently. Party leaders and a few committee chairs negotiate the text, often in private and often against a deadline, and the finished package reaches the floor with little room for amendment.

For the leaders, the advantages are clear. A single large package allows them to trade one group's priority for another's until enough members have a reason to vote yes, and a deadline forces a decision that might otherwise be postponed forever. For an ordinary member, the choice becomes all or nothing, since a vote against one provision is a vote against everything else in the package.

## Two paths compared

Put these routes together and the way major laws are made looks quite different from the diagram in the last lesson. The political scientist Barbara Sinclair spent years documenting the change and gave it a name, *unorthodox lawmaking*.

## Adaptation, or decline?

People who study Congress disagree about what all this means, and the disagreement does not follow party lines, since both parties use every one of these tools.

One view holds that Congress has adapted sensibly. The filibuster made the textbook path nearly impassable for anything controversial, and the new routes allow a government to keep running and an elected majority to enact at least some of its program.

The other view holds that something has been lost. A package assembled by a few leaders and voted on under deadline pressure gets less scrutiny than a bill that went through committee, and an ordinary member may have little time to read it and almost no chance to change it. Voters lose something as well, because it is hard to hold a representative responsible for one provision buried in a package that funded the whole government.

Hamilton's essay from the last lesson can be read in support of either side.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Federalist No. 73 (1788)">

"They will consider every institution calculated to restrain the excess of law-making, and to keep things in the same state in which they happen to be at any given period, as much more likely to do good than harm; because it is favorable to greater stability in the system of legislation."

</Callout>

Hamilton valued stable laws and distrusted "the excess of law-making." A law passed by 51 votes can be undone by the next 51, which is the kind of instability he feared. Yet the roadblocks that he praised are also the reason these routes exist, because a Congress that cannot use the front door will find a side one.

This lesson does not settle which view is right. It should change how you read the news about any major bill. Ask which route the bill is on, how many votes that route requires, and what deadline is forcing a decision.
