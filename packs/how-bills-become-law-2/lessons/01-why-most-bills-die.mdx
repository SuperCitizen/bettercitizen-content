# Why do most bills never become law?

In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced 16,202 bills. Over the same two years, 274 measures became law. That works out to fewer than two for every hundred bills introduced, and the count of new laws includes dozens that did nothing more than name a post office.

The usual diagram of how a bill becomes a law shows the steps correctly and hides those odds. It makes the process look like a conveyor belt, when it works more like an obstacle course on which nearly every runner is eliminated. A more useful question than "what are the steps?" is where bills stop, and who built the places where they stop.

## The problem on neutral ground

Start with a case that has no politics in it: a student who wants her school to allow phones at lunch. The rule can change only if four groups say yes, one after another: a student council committee, the full student council, the faculty senate and the principal. In the faculty senate, any teacher may keep the discussion going, and it takes three-fifths of the teachers to end it and hold a vote.

Notice the shape of the fight. The student has to win four times in a row, while anyone who opposes her needs to win only once, at whichever stop is easiest. A committee chair who never puts the proposal on the agenda has defeated it without casting a vote, and a determined minority of teachers can defeat it even if most of the faculty would have said yes.

Political scientists call each of these stops a *veto point*, meaning a place where a proposal can be halted. Congress has more of them than the school does, and while some are written into the Constitution, others were added later by Congress itself.

## What the Constitution requires

The Constitution's own instructions for making a law are short.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Article I, Section 7">

"Every Bill which shall have passed the House of Representatives and the Senate, shall, before it become a Law, be presented to the President of the United States; If he approve he shall sign it, but if not he shall return it, with his Objections to that House in which it shall have originated, who shall enter the Objections at large on their Journal, and proceed to reconsider it."

</Callout>

That sentence creates three veto points and no more: the House, the Senate and the President. The two chambers must pass the same bill, and a President who objects can be overridden only by two-thirds of each chamber.

Everything else that a bill runs into rests on a single clause two sections earlier: "Each House may determine the Rules of its Proceedings." Committees, the House Rules Committee and the Senate filibuster all come from that clause, and none of them is mentioned anywhere in the Constitution.

<Checkpoint question="Using only the passage from Section 7, list what must happen before a bill becomes law. Which familiar steps does the passage leave out?">

The bill must pass the House, pass the Senate, and be presented to the President, who either signs it or returns it with objections for another vote. The passage says nothing about committees, hearings, the Rules Committee or the filibuster. Each chamber created those for itself under its power to set its own rules, which also means each chamber could change them.

</Checkpoint>

## The veto points Congress built

**Committees.** Every bill is referred to a committee, and the House alone has 20 standing committees. A committee that takes up a bill usually begins with a public hearing, where witnesses with different views testify, and then holds a mark-up session, in which members amend the text before voting on whether to send it to the full chamber. For most bills none of that ever happens, because the chair simply never schedules it. The House does have an escape route called a discharge petition: if a majority of the whole House, 218 members, sign it, the bill goes to the floor without the committee's consent. It rarely succeeds, partly because signing means publicly defying one's own leaders. In November 2025, however, a petition collected its 218th signature and forced a vote on a bill that House leaders had not scheduled, and the bill then passed 427 to 1.

**The House floor.** A bill that leaves committee still needs time on the floor, and in the House that time is rationed by the Rules Committee. For each major bill the committee writes a special rule that sets the terms of debate, including how long it lasts and which amendments may be offered. The committee has nine members from the majority party and four from the minority, and it is known as "the Speaker's Committee" because it is how the Speaker keeps control of the floor. A bill that the majority's leaders do not want debated does not receive a rule.

**The Senate floor.** The Senate allows debate to continue until senators vote to end it. [[term:filibuster]] is the name for refusing to end it. Before 1917 the Senate had no way at all to cut off debate. That year it adopted a rule allowing two-thirds of senators to do so, and in 1975 it lowered the bar to three-fifths of all senators, which is 60. [[term:cloture]] is the name of the vote that ends debate, and it is the reason the number 60 appears in so many news stories about Congress.

The near miss here trips up most people: passing a bill in the Senate takes a simple majority, and it is reaching the vote on passage that can take 60.

Two bills show the difference between a majority and a supermajority, one from each party. In 2017 a Republican House passed a bill to repeal much of the Affordable Care Act, 217 to 213. It came to the Senate under special budget rules that required only a majority, and the last version put to a vote still failed, 49 to 51. In 2021 a Democratic House passed a sweeping elections bill, 220 to 210. In the Senate, the motion to take it up drew 50 votes when it needed 60.

Both bills passed the House and died in the Senate, the first because it fell short of a majority and the second because it fell short of a supermajority.

Move the slider from 50 to 60 and the bill's fate changes without a single voter changing their mind. The distance between those two numbers is where a great deal of modern legislation is lost.

**One text.** Nothing goes to the President until the two chambers have passed exactly the same words. When their versions differ, a conference committee of members from both chambers can work out a single text, which each chamber must then approve. A bill that passes both chambers in different forms and is never reconciled has not passed Congress.

**The President.** The last veto point is the oldest. Congress has overridden 112 of the 1,533 regular vetoes in American history, or about 7 percent. Because everyone knows those odds, the threat of a veto shapes a bill long before it is sent to the White House.

## A flaw, or the design?

Whether all this is a problem depends on what a legislature is for.

Alexander Hamilton defended difficulty openly. Writing in Federalist No. 73 about the President's veto, he conceded that a power to stop bad laws would sometimes stop good ones, and judged the trade worth making: "The injury which may possibly be done by defeating a few good laws, will be amply compensated by the advantage of preventing a number of bad ones." On this view, a law that can survive so many veto points has earned broad support, and laws that change slowly give people a stable set of rules to live by.

Critics answer that Hamilton was defending the veto points in the Constitution, and that Congress has since added many more. When Congress cannot act, they argue, problems go unaddressed, or the decisions shift to Presidents and courts, who are harder for voters to hold to account. Supporters of the Senate's rules reply that a 60-vote threshold forces the two parties to bargain and keeps national policy from lurching back and forth with every election.

This lesson does not settle that argument, but it does leave a puzzle. If most major bills need 60 votes in the Senate, and the majority party usually holds fewer than 60 seats, how do large and controversial laws still get passed? The next lesson explains the routes around the roadblock.
