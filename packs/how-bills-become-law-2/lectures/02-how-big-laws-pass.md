Two votes in the Senate. Listen for what they share.

August 7, 2022. The Senate passes a major climate, health and tax law. The count is 51 to 50, and a Democratic Vice President casts the deciding vote.

[beat]

July 1, 2025. The Senate passes a major tax and spending law. The count is 51 to 50, and a Republican Vice President casts the deciding vote.

[pause]

The first was the Inflation Reduction Act. The second was the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Neither had anything close to 60 supporters.

And that ought to be a puzzle. Last time, we saw that most major bills need 60 votes to get past a filibuster in the Senate. Yet both of these became law. [beat] How?

> Neither bill took the path in the textbook diagram.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to name the routes they took instead, and say what each route costs. We will take it in four steps. First, the 51-vote route. Second, the enormous bill that has to pass. Third, who writes bills like these. And last, the argument over whether Congress has adapted, or declined.

## The 51-vote route

Both of those laws traveled through a process called budget reconciliation. Congress created it in the Congressional Budget Act of 1974.

Reconciliation began as a practical idea. Each year, Congress adopts a budget plan. Reconciliation was meant as a quick way to change tax and spending laws, so that they matched the plan.

What makes it powerful is a single feature. The law limits Senate debate on a reconciliation bill to 20 hours.

[beat]

Think about what a time limit does. A debate with a time limit cannot be stretched out forever. So a filibuster is impossible. And the bill needs only a simple majority to pass.

> A reconciliation bill cannot be filibustered, so it needs only a simple majority.

Both parties have used this route for their largest priorities, as those two votes show.

But here is the turn. A route that easy would swallow every other kind of bill, if it had no limits. So the Senate fenced it in.

The fence is a rule adopted in 1985 and made permanent in 1990. It is named for Senator Robert Byrd. Under that rule, any senator may challenge a provision in a reconciliation bill as "extraneous." Extraneous means that the provision is not really about the budget.

And keeping a challenged provision takes 60 votes. [beat] That defeats the purpose of using reconciliation at all.

So here is how it works out.

> In practice, taxes and spending can travel by reconciliation. Most other policy cannot.

Let's test that. Forty-five senators plan to filibuster, and three bills are waiting. One creates a new national holiday. One is a reconciliation bill that changes income tax rates. One rewrites the rules for citizenship. [beat] Which of them could still pass the Senate with 51 votes?

[long pause]

Only the second: the reconciliation bill that changes income tax rates. Its debate is limited, so it cannot be filibustered. And the route is open only to measures about taxes, spending and the debt.

A holiday law and a citizenship law are ordinary legislation. Each would need 60 votes to end debate.

Would it help if the President had asked for one of them? No. A President's support changes nothing about Senate procedure.

## One enormous bill in place of twelve

That is the first route. The second runs through the government's own budget.

Each year, Congress is supposed to pass a dozen separate bills that fund the federal agencies. They are called appropriations bills, and they are due before the new fiscal year begins, on October 1.

Congress almost never manages it. Since the current budget system took effect, it has passed all of its spending bills on time in only four years. The most recent of those was fiscal 1997.

[pause]

So what happens when the deadline approaches and the work is unfinished? Leaders reach for one of two tools.

The first tool is a stopgap. It is a law that keeps money flowing, at roughly the current level, for a few more weeks or months. Its name is a continuing resolution, usually shortened to CR.

The second tool is a package. It rolls many spending bills into a single enormous bill, which each chamber passes in one vote. Its name is an omnibus bill.

So a continuing resolution buys time, and an omnibus bundles the work into one vote. You will hear both tools named in the news every autumn.

Now, a bill of that kind has to pass, because the alternative is a government shutdown. And that makes it valuable to anyone with an unrelated proposal that could not pass by itself.

Here is one case. The spending package of December 2022 also carried the Electoral Count Reform Act, which rewrote the rules for counting presidential electoral votes. Lawmakers call an attachment like that a rider.

> A bill that must pass can carry a measure that could not pass alone.

And the deadlines are real. In the fall of 2025, the fiscal year began with no agreement in place. Much of the federal government shut down for 43 days. [beat] It was the longest shutdown in American history.

Let's put this to work. A senator has a popular bill on drug prices, and it cannot reach 60 votes. She tries to attach it to the year-end spending package. Why might that work, when the bill alone failed? Think it through.

[long pause]

Because the package is a bill that has to pass. Senators who would filibuster the drug bill by itself may be unwilling to block the funding of the entire government in order to stop it.

The strategy has costs, though. Leaders decide what gets attached. And every addition gives some member a new reason to vote no.

## Who writes the bill?

These routes change more than the vote count. They change who writes the bill.

In the textbook, a bill is written by the committee that knows its subject. The work is done in public sessions, where any member may propose a change.

A reconciliation bill or an omnibus is usually assembled differently. Party leaders and a few committee chairs negotiate the text. They often do it in private, and often against a deadline. And the finished package reaches the floor with little room for amendment.

For the leaders, the advantages are clear. A single large package lets them trade one group's priority for another's, until enough members have a reason to vote yes. And a deadline forces a decision that might otherwise be postponed forever.

And for everyone else, the choice narrows.

> For an ordinary member, a large package is all or nothing.

A vote against one provision is a vote against everything else in the package.

Put these routes together, and the way major laws are made looks quite different from the textbook diagram. The political scientist Barbara Sinclair spent years documenting the change. She gave it a name: unorthodox lawmaking.

So hold the two paths side by side.

On the textbook path, a bill covers one subject. Committees hold hearings and rewrite the text. Members offer amendments on the floor. And the bill needs 60 votes to end debate in the Senate.

On the path big laws take now, one package covers many subjects. Party leaders negotiate the text. There are few amendments, and the deadline is near. And the package needs 51 votes through reconciliation, or it rides on a bill that has to pass.

One caution. The textbook path still exists, and most ordinary bills follow it. It is many of the largest laws of recent years that did not.

[pause]

Before we turn to the argument, a check. We have met four tools. Reconciliation. The continuing resolution. The omnibus bill. The rider. Can you still say what each one does? Take a moment.

[pause 5s]

Reconciliation lets a budget bill pass the Senate with 51 votes. A continuing resolution extends current funding for a short time. An omnibus bill combines many spending bills into one. And a rider is an unrelated measure attached to a larger bill.

## Adaptation, or decline?

Now the argument. People who study Congress disagree about what all this means. And the disagreement does not follow party lines, since both parties use every one of these tools.

One view holds that Congress has adapted sensibly. The filibuster made the textbook path nearly impassable for anything controversial. The new routes allow a government to keep running. And they allow an elected majority to enact at least some of its program.

> One view: the new routes let an elected majority enact some of its program.

The other view holds that something has been lost. A package assembled by a few leaders, and voted on under deadline pressure, gets less scrutiny than a bill that went through committee. An ordinary member may have little time to read it, and almost no chance to change it.

Voters lose something as well, on this view. It is hard to hold a representative responsible for one provision, buried in a package that funded the whole government.

> The other view: a package assembled by a few leaders gets less scrutiny.

[pause]

Now bring back Hamilton, from the last lecture. His essay can be taken in support of either side.

The essay is Federalist 73, and Hamilton wrote it in 1788. Here is one sentence of it, in two parts. He is describing how people will judge any institution that holds lawmaking back. Listen for the reason he gives at the end.

"They will consider every institution calculated to restrain the excess of law-making, and to keep things in the same state in which they happen to be at any given period, [beat] as much more likely to do good than harm."

[beat]

And then the reason: "because it is favorable to greater stability in the system of legislation."

[pause]

Here is a question you can answer only from his words. What is the good that comes from holding lawmaking back?

[long pause]

Stability. Such an institution, in his words, "is favorable to greater stability in the system of legislation." Hamilton valued stable laws. And he distrusted what he called "the excess of law-making."

Now set today's routes beside that sentence.

A law passed by 51 votes can be undone by the next 51. That is the kind of instability he feared.

Yet the roadblocks that he praised are also the reason these routes exist. A Congress that cannot use the front door will find a side one.

[pause]

So which view is right? This lecture does not settle it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Reconciliation is the 51-vote route. Senate debate is limited to 20 hours, so a reconciliation bill cannot be filibustered. But in practice, only taxes and spending can travel that way. Most other policy cannot.

[beat]

Two. Congress almost never passes its spending bills on time, so it reaches for two tools. A continuing resolution extends current funding for a short time. An omnibus bill combines many spending bills into one.

[beat]

Three. A bill that must pass can carry a measure that could not pass alone. An unrelated measure attached that way is called a rider.

[beat]

Four. These routes change who writes the bill. Party leaders negotiate the text, and for an ordinary member a large package is all or nothing. The textbook path still exists, and most ordinary bills follow it.

[beat]

Five. People who study Congress disagree about what this means. One view holds that Congress adapted sensibly. The other view holds that something has been lost: scrutiny, and a voter's hold on a representative.

[pause]

That disagreement is what this lecture leaves open. Even Hamilton can be taken both ways.

This is the last lecture of the series, so here is where the argument goes next: into the news. When a major bill is in the headlines, there are three things to ask. Which route is the bill on? How many votes does that route require? And what deadline is forcing a decision?

[pause]

So go back to those two votes. 51 to 50, with a Democratic Vice President. 51 to 50, with a Republican one. [beat] You know now which route those bills were on. And you know why neither one needed 60.
