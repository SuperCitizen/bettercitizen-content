You have probably seen the diagram of how a bill becomes a law. A bill goes in at one end. It moves from step to step. And a law comes out at the other end.

[beat]

Here is what that diagram leaves out.

In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced 16,202 bills. Over the same two years, 274 measures became law.

[pause]

That works out to fewer than two for every hundred bills introduced. And the count of new laws includes dozens that did nothing more than name a post office.

The usual diagram shows the steps correctly. What it hides is the odds. It makes the process look like a conveyor belt, when it works more like an obstacle course on which nearly every runner is eliminated.

> Lawmaking works less like a conveyor belt and more like an obstacle course.

So asking for the steps is not the most useful question. A better one has two parts. Where do bills stop? And who built the places where they stop?

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to answer both. We will take it in four steps. First, the same problem in a school, where there is no politics in it. Second, what the Constitution requires. Third, the stops that Congress built for itself. And last, the argument over whether all of this is a flaw, or the design.

## The problem on neutral ground

Start with a student. She wants her school to allow phones at lunch.

The rule can change only if four groups say yes, one after another. A student council committee. Then the full student council. Then the faculty senate. Then the principal.

And the faculty senate has a rule of its own. Any teacher may keep the discussion going, and it takes three-fifths of the teachers to end it and hold a vote.

[pause]

Now notice the shape of the fight. The student has to win four times in a row. [beat] Anyone who opposes her needs to win only once, at whichever stop is easiest.

> Whoever wants change must win every stop. An opponent must win only once.

And look at how quietly she can lose. A committee chair who never puts her proposal on the agenda has defeated it, without casting a vote. A determined minority of teachers can defeat it too, even if most of the faculty would have said yes.

Political scientists have a name for each of these stops. They call it a veto point. A veto point is a place where a proposal can be halted.

Congress has more veto points than the school does. Some of them are written into the Constitution. Others were added later, by Congress itself. [beat] Telling those two kinds apart is the work of this lecture.

## What the Constitution requires

Begin with the Constitution. Its own instructions for making a law are short, and they sit in Article One, section seven.

I am going to give you the heart of that passage, in two parts. As you listen, count who has to act before a bill becomes a law.

[beat]

"Every Bill which shall have passed the House of Representatives and the Senate, shall, before it become a Law, be presented to the President of the United States."

[pause]

And then, on what the President may do with it: "If he approve he shall sign it, but if not he shall return it, with his Objections to that House in which it shall have originated."

[pause]

Now a question, and you can answer it from those words alone. What must happen before a bill becomes a law? And which familiar steps did you not hear? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Three things must happen. The bill must pass the House. It must pass the Senate. And it must be presented to the President, who either signs it or returns it with objections, for another vote.

And what did the passage leave out? [beat] Committees. Hearings. The Rules Committee. The filibuster. It says nothing about any of them.

> The Constitution creates three veto points: the House, the Senate and the President.

Three, and no more. The two chambers must pass the same bill. And a President who objects can be overridden only by two-thirds of each chamber.

So where does everything else come from? [beat] It rests on a single clause, two sections earlier. Here is what it says.

"Each House may determine the Rules of its Proceedings."

[pause]

Each House may determine the rules. Committees come from that clause. So does the House Rules Committee. So does the Senate filibuster. And not one of them is mentioned anywhere in the Constitution.

That matters, and here is why.

> Each chamber made these rules for itself, so each chamber could change them.

## The veto points Congress built

That is what the Constitution requires. Now for the stops that Congress added, in the order a bill meets them.

First, committees. Every bill is referred to a committee, and the House alone has 20 standing committees. A committee that takes up a bill usually begins with a public hearing, where witnesses with different views testify. Then it holds a mark-up session, in which members amend the text before voting on whether to send the bill to the full chamber.

For most bills, none of that ever happens, because the chair never schedules it. [beat] Think back to the committee chair at the school, who defeated a proposal without casting a vote. This is the same move.

The House does have an escape route, called a discharge petition. If a majority of the whole House signs it, the bill goes to the floor without the committee's consent. That takes 218 members.

It rarely succeeds, partly because signing means publicly defying one's own leaders. But it can work. In November 2025, a petition collected its 218th signature, and forced a vote on a bill that House leaders had not scheduled. The bill then passed, [beat] 427 to 1.

[pause]

Second, the House floor. A bill that leaves committee still needs time on the floor, and in the House that time is rationed. The body that rations it is the Rules Committee.

For each major bill, the Rules Committee writes a special rule. A special rule sets the terms of debate: how long it lasts, and which amendments may be offered. The committee has nine members from the majority party and four from the minority. It is known as "the Speaker's Committee," because it is how the Speaker keeps control of the floor.

So a bill that the majority's leaders do not want debated does not receive a rule.

## The Senate, and the number 60

Third, the Senate floor.

The Senate allows debate to continue until senators vote to end it. Refusing to end it has a name: the filibuster.

Before 1917, the Senate had no way at all to cut off debate. That year, it adopted a rule allowing two-thirds of senators to do so. And in 1975, it lowered the bar to three-fifths of all senators, which is 60.

The vote that ends debate is called cloture. Cloture is the reason the number 60 appears in so many news stories about Congress.

Now for a near miss that trips up most people. Listen closely.

> In the Senate, a majority passes a bill. Reaching that vote can take 60.

Let me say that again, another way. Sixty is not what it takes to pass the bill. Sixty is what it takes to end the debate, so that the vote on passing can happen at all. A bar like that, set above a simple majority, is called a supermajority.

So where does the 60 come from? You might guess the Constitution, or a Supreme Court decision, or a federal law that a President signed. Think back to that single clause about rules, and take a moment.

[long pause]

It comes from a Senate rule. The Senate adopted it under its power to set its own procedures, changed it last in 1975, and could change it again. The Constitution itself requires a supermajority for only a few things, such as overriding a veto.

Two bills show the difference between a majority and a supermajority. One comes from each party.

In 2017, a Republican House passed a bill to repeal much of the Affordable Care Act. The vote was 217 to 213. The bill came to the Senate under special budget rules that required only a majority. And the last version put to a vote still failed, 49 to 51.

In 2021, a Democratic House passed a sweeping elections bill. The vote was 220 to 210. In the Senate, the motion to take it up drew 50 votes, when it needed 60.

[pause]

Both bills passed the House, and both died in the Senate. The first fell short of a majority. [beat] The second fell short of a supermajority.

The lesson page has a slider that shows this, and here is one run of it. At 50 senators in favor, a bill has enough votes to pass, with the Vice President breaking the tie, but not enough to end a filibuster. At 60, it has enough to end debate and pass.

Between those two settings, the bill's fate changes without a single voter changing their mind.

> Between 50 and 60 votes, a great deal of modern legislation is lost.

Two stops remain, and both go back to the Constitution.

One is the single text. Nothing goes to the President until the two chambers have passed exactly the same words. When their versions differ, a conference committee of members from both chambers can work out a single text, which each chamber must then approve. A bill that passes both chambers in different forms, and is never reconciled, has not passed Congress.

The other is the President. The last veto point is the oldest. American history has seen 1,533 regular vetoes. Congress has overridden 112 of them. That is about 7 percent. Because everyone knows those odds, the threat of a veto shapes a bill long before it is sent to the White House.

## A flaw, or the design?

So is all of this a problem? That depends on what a legislature is for.

Start with the case for difficulty. Alexander Hamilton defended it openly. In 1788, in Federalist 73, he was writing about the President's veto. He conceded that a power to stop bad laws would sometimes stop good ones, and he judged the trade worth making. Here is his sentence. Listen for the trade.

"The injury which may possibly be done by defeating a few good laws, [beat] will be amply compensated by the advantage of preventing a number of bad ones."

[pause]

A few good laws, defeated. A number of bad ones, prevented.

On this view, a law that can survive so many veto points has earned broad support. And laws that change slowly give people a stable set of rules to live by.

> Hamilton's trade: a few good laws defeated, a number of bad ones prevented.

Now the critics. They answer that Hamilton was defending the veto points in the Constitution, and that Congress has since added many more. When Congress cannot act, they argue, problems go unaddressed. Or the decisions shift to Presidents and courts, who are harder for voters to hold to account.

> The critics' answer: Congress has added many veto points beyond the Constitution's.

And supporters of the Senate's rules have a reply. A 60-vote threshold, they argue, forces the two parties to bargain. It keeps national policy from lurching back and forth with every election.

[pause]

Which of them is right? This lecture does not settle that argument.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Lawmaking works less like a conveyor belt and more like an obstacle course. In 2023 and 2024, fewer than two of every hundred bills became law.

[beat]

Two. A place where a proposal can be halted has a name. Can you still say it? [long pause] A veto point. And whoever wants a change must win at every one of them.

[beat]

Three. The Constitution creates three veto points: the House, the Senate and the President.

[beat]

Four. Everything else rests on one clause: "Each House may determine the Rules of its Proceedings." Committees, the Rules Committee and the filibuster are rules that each chamber made, and could change.

[beat]

Five. In the Senate, a simple majority passes a bill. Reaching that vote can take 60, and the vote that ends debate is called cloture.

[pause]

What stays open is the argument itself. Is the difficulty a flaw, or the design? Hamilton judged the trade worth making. The critics answer that Congress has since added many more veto points. Which of them you favor depends on what you think a legislature is for.

And there is a puzzle left over. Most major bills need 60 votes in the Senate. The majority party usually holds fewer than 60 seats. [beat] So how do large and controversial laws still get passed?

The next lecture explains the routes around the roadblock.

For now, think of the usual diagram once more. The next time you see it, ask the two questions it leaves out. Where do the bills stop? [beat] And who built the stops?
