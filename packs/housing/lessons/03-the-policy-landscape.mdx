# The current policy landscape

Grants Pass is a southern Oregon timber town of under 40,000 people whose city council decided, in the 2010s, to enforce its way out of visible homelessness: ordinances barring sleeping in public with so much as a blanket, enforced with $295 civil fines that escalated if unpaid, park-exclusion orders, and eventually criminal trespass charges carrying up to 30 days in jail. The town had no general homeless shelter to send people to. In June 2024 the Supreme Court used that little city to answer a question every mayor in America was asking — and *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* is only one front in a remarkable stretch of housing policymaking. Since 2018, a dozen-odd states have overridden local zoning, Congress has quietly passed the largest affordable-housing-finance expansion in decades, and the federal government has reversed its quarter-century posture on homelessness. This lesson maps the field as it stands in mid-2026.

## The preemption wave: a scoreboard

Lesson 2 ended with Fischel's homevoters — the reason city-by-city persuasion fails. Reformers drew the [[term:federalism]] conclusion: change which level of government decides. Cities are creatures of state law, so a state can simply legalize duplexes itself. Here is the scoreboard, limited to what has verifiably passed and what has verifiably happened:

| Place & law | What it did | Verified results so far |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minneapolis 2040 plan (2018) | First major U.S. city to end single-family-only zoning citywide (triplexes allowed); upzoned corridors; ended parking minimums (2021) | Housing stock +12% and rents +1% from 2017–2022, vs. +4% and +14% in the rest of Minnesota (Pew). The triplex provision itself produced about 1% of new permits — the corridor upzoning and parking repeal did the heavy lifting |
| Oregon HB 2001 (2019) | Duplexes legal in cities over 10,000; triplexes, fourplexes, townhomes, cottage clusters in cities over 25,000 and metro Portland | Local codes rewritten on schedule (2022 deadlines); production effects still maturing — Oregon paired it with the nation's first statewide rent cap (SB 608) |
| California SB 9 (2021) | Two units plus lot splits allowed on most single-family parcels | Tiny uptake: the Terner Center's first-year review found activity limited or nonexistent across the 13 largest cities — Los Angeles, the most active, permitted fewer than 40 SB 9 units in 2022. Cities blunted the law with fees, size caps, and design rules; a 2024 trial court exempted five charter cities (appeal pending), and the Legislature restated the law's reach over charter cities in SB 450 (effective 2025) |
| California ADU laws (2016–) | Successive statutes made accessory dwelling units approvable by right, statewide | The reform that worked: annual ADU permits grew twenty-fold from 2016 to about 27,000 in 2023 — more than a fifth of all homes the state permitted that year, and roughly 1 in 3 in Los Angeles |
| Washington HB 1110 (2023) | Cities 25,000–75,000 must allow 2 units per lot; cities over 75,000, 4 units — rising to 6 near transit or with affordable units | Implementation under way through local comprehensive-plan updates |
| Montana SB 323 / SB 528 (2023) | Duplexes in cities over 5,000; ADUs on single-family lots statewide — a red-state package passed with bipartisan votes | Blocked by a trial-court injunction in December 2023; Montana Supreme Court reversed it in September 2024 (*MAID v. State*), holding the challengers' claimed harms too speculative to justify an injunction |
| Austin "HOME" reforms (2023–24) | Three units allowed per lot; minimum lot size cut from 5,750 to 1,800 square feet — alongside a permitting boom of 957 apartments per 100,000 residents in 2021–23, outpacing every neighboring region | Rents in large buildings fell 7% from 2023 to 2024, the steepest drop recorded in any large U.S. metro, with older Class C buildings down about 11%; city housing stock grew 30% from 2015–2024 (Pew) |

Read the table the way a policy analyst would. First, **passing a law is not producing housing**: SB 9's trickle of projects against the ADU boom's tens of thousands a year is the cleanest design experiment in the country, and the difference is process — ADU approval is ministerial (no hearing, no discretion, capped fees), while SB 9 left cities room to smother projects in standards and left owners facing five-figure fees and owner-occupancy rules. Second, **litigation is the second battlefield**: Minneapolis spent 2022–24 fending off an environmental-review lawsuit that briefly enjoined the whole plan, until the courts and then the legislature (which exempted comprehensive plans from such review in May 2024) cleared it, and the Minnesota Supreme Court declined further review that August; Montana's reforms survived their challenge outright. Reformers won round one in court, but the playbook — sue under environmental and procedural statutes — is now standard. Third, **supply responses follow demand**: upzoning produces homes where people want to live and builders can finance them, which is why Austin's results arrived fastest — by January 2026 its median rent ($1,296) sat about 4 percent below the national figure, a sentence nobody would have written about boomtown Austin in 2021 — and rural reforms will read as quiet for years.

<Checkpoint question="A state passes a law allowing fourplexes on any residential lot — but each project requires a public design-review hearing, and cities may set their own fees. Using the SB 9 vs. ADU contrast, predict the outcome.">
  Expect SB 9–style numbers: trivially few projects. Discretionary hearings add time, risk, and a veto point for opponents; uncapped fees let hostile cities price projects out. California's ADU laws worked because they removed exactly those two levers — by-right (ministerial) approval and constrained fees. The operative question for any zoning reform is never "what does it allow?" but "who can still say no, and what can they charge?"
</Checkpoint>

## Federal levers: money, mostly through the tax code

**The 2025 LIHTC expansion.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the sprawling tax law enacted July 4, 2025, contained the largest expansion of the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit in decades, almost unnoticed in the bill's noise: a permanent 12 percent increase in each state's 9 percent credit ceiling starting in 2026, and a permanent cut in the "bond test" from 50 to 25 percent — meaning a project now needs only a quarter of its costs financed by private-activity bonds to unlock 4 percent credits, stretching states' limited bond caps across roughly twice as many projects. Industry analysts project the two changes could finance on the order of a million additional affordable rental homes over 2026–2035. Whatever one's view of LIHTC's cost-efficiency (Lesson 1), the political lesson is striking: in a polarized Congress, the developer-investor-state-agency coalition behind the credit delivered while flashier proposals died.

**Vouchers.** No comparable breakthrough: Housing Choice Vouchers remain annually appropriated, serving 2.3 million households while roughly three in four eligible households go unassisted. Closing that gap would mean roughly quadrupling the program — a recurring progressive proposal with no current legislative path; the near-term fights are over whether funding even keeps pace with rents.

**Fair-housing rules: the whiplash lever.** The Fair Housing Act of 1968 requires HUD grantees to "affirmatively further" fair housing. What that means in regulation has now flipped four times: the Obama administration's 2015 rule required jurisdictions taking HUD money to analyze and plan against segregation patterns; the first Trump administration terminated it in 2020; the Biden administration restored an interim version in 2021; and in February 2025 HUD Secretary Scott Turner announced its termination, formalized in a March 2025 interim final rule (effective that April) under which jurisdictions simply self-certify compliance. The takeaway for your mental model: conditioning federal grants on local land-use behavior is a real lever, but one that has yet to survive a change of administration.

**Mortgage policy.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac enter their eighteenth year of "temporary" conservatorship, leaving the federal government the silent architect of mortgage credit; the 2025 tax law also made the $750,000 mortgage-interest-deduction cap permanent. The homeowner side of the ledger, in other words, was made *more* durable in 2025 — another data point for the Lesson 1 observation about where federal housing money actually goes.

Why do counts concentrate where they do? Gregg Colburn and Clayton Page Aldern's *Homelessness Is a Housing Problem* (2022) runs the comparisons: city-level homelessness rates track rents and vacancy rates far better than they track local rates of poverty, mental illness, or addiction — West Virginia has more addiction and far less homelessness than coastal cities. Individual vulnerabilities decide *who* falls; housing markets decide *how many*.

**What Grants Pass held.** In *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (June 28, 2024), the Court ruled 6–3, per Justice Gorsuch, that enforcing generally applicable public-camping ordinances against people with nowhere else to sleep is not "cruel and unusual punishment," discarding the Ninth Circuit [[term:precedent]] (*Martin v. Boise*) that had barred such enforcement when shelter beds were unavailable. The ruling removed a federal constitutional barrier; it mandated nothing. Cities may now clear encampments with ordinary fines and trespass law — whether doing so without housing alternatives reduces homelessness or shuffles it is exactly the question the opinion left to politics.

**The federal pivot.** Politics answered quickly. On July 24, 2025, President Trump signed the [[term:executive-order]] "Ending Crime and Disorder on America's Streets," directing agencies to encourage civil commitment of unsheltered people with serious mental illness, to prioritize grants for jurisdictions that enforce camping and open-drug-use bans, and to end federal support for **Housing First** — the since-2000s consensus model (Tsemberis's Pathways research) that provides permanent housing without sobriety or treatment preconditions. Read the evidence on Housing First the steel-man way: randomized and longitudinal studies consistently show it outperforms treatment-first models at getting and keeping people *housed*, which is the outcome it targets; evidence that it by itself resolves addiction or psychiatric illness, or reliably saves public money, is much weaker — the gap both camps now argue from. Critics of the pivot also note a fiscal irony: conditioning aid on treatment requires treatment capacity that, like permanent supportive housing, mostly does not exist at scale.

States are running their own versions of the argument. California — home to roughly a quarter of the nation's homeless population — spent about $24 billion across five years on homelessness programs, and its 2024 state audit found the state had not consistently tracked what the money accomplished (its hotel-conversion program, Homekey, looked cost-effective at about $144,000 per unit in early rounds, versus $380,000–570,000 for new construction). Red and purple states, following Grants Pass, have multiplied camping bans. The structural fact under all of it: every pathway — Housing First, treatment-first, enforcement-first — terminates in the same shortage of units and beds.

<Checkpoint question="After Grants Pass, a city with one full shelter passes a camping ban and begins issuing fines. A resident says the policy is now “constitutionally settled and empirically settled.” What is right and wrong in that sentence?">
  Constitutionally: mostly right as to the Eighth Amendment — *Grants Pass* held such enforcement is not cruel and unusual punishment (state constitutions and other doctrines can still be litigated). Empirically: not settled at all. The ruling said cities *may* enforce, not that enforcement reduces homelessness; the evidence question — does enforcement without housing alternatives shrink the problem or relocate it? — remains open and contested, which is precisely why the policy fight moved from courtrooms to budgets.
</Checkpoint>

## What's likely next

- **More preemption, redder map.** Montana's court victory and bipartisan coalition made it the template for conservative states; expect more duplex/ADU/lot-size laws framed as property rights and deregulation, while blue states iterate on enforcement against resistant cities (California's charter-city appeal is the one to watch).
- **LIHTC implementation.** The 25 percent bond test took effect for properties placed in service after 2025; the next two years reveal whether the projected unit surge materializes or gets eaten by costs and tariff-era construction prices.
- **Voucher funding under strain.** With the program annually appropriated and rents elevated, flat funding quietly shrinks the number of families served — the least visible, highest-stakes line item in housing.
- **Tenant-protection diffusion.** Oregon- and California-style caps, just-cause statutes, and right-to-counsel programs continue spreading city by city and state by state — with New Jersey's codified fourth-round Mount Laurel obligations (2024 law; obligations began in 2025, suburban towns' challenges were dismissed in state and federal court, and the U.S. Supreme Court turned away an emergency appeal in February 2026) testing whether mandates can compel suburban affordable construction at scale.
- **Homelessness as a national political issue.** The 2025 executive order nationalized what had been a city-hall fight; expect funding conditions, encampment policy, and the fate of Housing First to feature in the 2026 and 2028 cycles.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. housing policy requires four commitments:

1. A view on **whether the binding constraint is supply, demand-side assistance, or tenant power** (the structural question from Lesson 2).
2. A view on **which level of government should decide** — and whether you hold that view consistently when your side loses a statehouse (the federalism question).
3. A view on **what to do about the people unsheltered tonight**, knowing both the Housing First evidence and its limits (the emergency question).
4. An honest account of **what evidence would change your mind** — post-Greaney, post-SB 9, nobody gets to skip this one.
