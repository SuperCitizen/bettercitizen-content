# How U.S. housing policy actually works

The median first-time homebuyer in the United States is now 40 years old. That is the oldest in the four decades the National Association of Realtors has been asking, and first-time buyers' share of the market — 21 percent in the 2025 survey — is the lowest ever recorded; through the 1980s, the typical first-timer was in their late twenties. The arithmetic behind the milestone is unforgiving: the median existing home sold for $429,300 in May 2026, and the 30-year mortgage averaged 6.48 percent in early June. A generation is spending its prime saving years renting, and politicians of every stripe have noticed. But before you can argue about *why* housing got this expensive — and Lesson 2 stages that argument properly — you need the machine itself: who actually decides whether homes get built, who finances them, and who gets help paying for them. The answers sit at three different levels of government, and the mismatch between those levels is the original sin of American housing politics.

## Three layers, one mismatch

Housing policy is a [[term:federalism]] problem wearing an economics costume. Each layer of government holds a different lever:

| Layer | What it actually controls | Signature instruments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal | Mortgage finance, tax subsidies, income-tested rental aid | Fannie/Freddie, FHA, mortgage interest deduction, Section 8, LIHTC |
| State | Enabling law that delegates land-use power downward — and, lately, preemption that claws it back | Zoning enabling acts; reform laws like Oregon HB 2001, California SB 9 |
| Local | Whether housing actually gets built | Zoning, permits, design review, parking minimums, public hearings |

Read the table bottom-up and the mismatch jumps out. The level of government that voters blame for affordability — the federal one — does not control supply. The level that controls supply — tens of thousands of cities, towns, and counties — faces no national electorate and answers instead to the people who show up at Tuesday-night planning meetings, who are disproportionately homeowners with something to protect. Washington writes checks and sets mortgage rules; city hall decides whether anything gets built to spend them on. Keep that division in view through everything that follows, because most bad housing punditry consists of demanding that one layer fix a problem another layer owns.

## Zoning: the pig in the parlor

Modern American land-use law begins in a Cleveland suburb. In the early 1920s the Village of Euclid, Ohio zoned itself into use districts, and Ambler Realty — which owned 68 acres it intended to sell for industry — sued, arguing the ordinance destroyed most of its land's value: roughly $10,000 an acre for industrial use against about $2,500 for residential, by the company's account. In *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* (1926), the Supreme Court upheld zoning as an ordinary exercise of the police power, with Justice Sutherland supplying the image that built a hundred thousand zoning codes: "A nuisance may be merely a right thing in the wrong place, like a pig in the parlor instead of the barnyard." Apartment buildings near houses, the Court suggested, could be "mere parasites" on a neighborhood. With constitutional permission secured, comprehensive zoning swept the country.

Two later cases frame what local governments may do with that power. *Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City* (1978) held that regulation which reduces a property's value is not automatically a "taking" requiring compensation — courts instead weigh economic impact, investment-backed expectations, and the regulation's character, a balancing test that in practice lets most land-use rules stand. And *Kelo v. City of New London* (2005) upheld the use of [[term:eminent-domain]] to transfer property to private developers for "economic development," a 5–4 ruling so unpopular that more than 40 states tightened their eminent-domain statutes in response. The rare counter-current runs through New Jersey: the *Mount Laurel* decisions (1975, 1983) read the state constitution to forbid exclusionary zoning and to impose on every municipality a "fair share" obligation to allow affordable housing — the doctrine behind New Jersey's 2024 statute codifying a fourth round of municipal obligations.

What does the zoning power look like in operation? A typical suburban code does several things at once, each modest-sounding, jointly decisive:

- Reserves most residential land for single-family detached houses (about 75 percent of residential land in the city of Los Angeles, by the city's own estimate; in Minneapolis since its 2018 plan reform, zero).
- Sets minimum lot sizes, setbacks, height limits, and floor-area ratios.
- Requires off-street parking, commonly one to three spaces per unit.
- Routes anything unusual through variances or rezonings — discretionary, hearing-heavy processes where neighbors object and timelines stretch into years.

The economic consequence is the central fact of this pack: **where rules make supply unresponsive, demand becomes price.** Glaeser and Gyourko (2018) document the pattern — in lightly regulated metros like Houston or Atlanta, prices hover near the cost of construction because builders answer demand with units; in tightly regulated coastal metros, prices float far above construction cost because builders legally cannot. ## The finance layer: how Washington shapes who can buy

The federal government's biggest housing role is not building homes but standing behind the loans that buy them. **Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac**, government-sponsored enterprises in federal conservatorship since their 2008 rescue, buy mortgages from lenders, bundle them into guaranteed securities, and thereby make the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage a mass-market product — an American peculiarity; most wealthy countries rely on floating rates or shorter fixed terms (Green and Wachter, 2005, trace how U.S. policy built this instrument). **FHA insurance** lets first-time and lower-income buyers in with down payments as low as 3.5 percent; **VA guarantees** do the same for veterans with nothing down.

The system's signature product has a signature side effect. Millions of households locked in mortgages near 3 percent in 2020–21; when rates jumped above 7 percent in 2023, selling meant trading a cheap loan for an expensive one. An FHFA working paper (Batzer, Coste, Doerner, and Seiler, 2024) quantified this **lock-in effect**: for every percentage point a homeowner's old rate sits below market rates, the probability of sale falls 18.1 percent — enough to have prevented an estimated 1.7 million home sales between mid-2022 and mid-2024 and, by starving the market of listings, to have pushed prices about 7 percent *higher* even as borrowing costs soared. As of mid-2026, with the 30-year averaging 6.48 percent, the thaw is slow: lock-in fades only as rates fall or life forces moves.

Then there is the tax code, the largest and least visible federal housing [[term:subsidy]]. The **mortgage interest deduction** lets itemizers deduct interest on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt — a cap from the 2017 tax law that the 2025 tax legislation (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) made permanent. The congressional Joint Committee on Taxation scores the deduction at roughly $261 billion over 2025–29, and homeownership tax expenditures as a whole — adding the capital-gains exclusion on home sales and related breaks — at about $730 billion over the same five years. Call it roughly $145 billion a year, flowing overwhelmingly to higher-income households who itemize and carry large mortgages. Hold that figure against the next section.

> **Common misconception:** "Federal housing policy means programs for the poor." By dollars, federal housing policy is mostly a program for homeowners: the tax code's homeownership subsidies (about $145 billion a year) are roughly double HUD's entire net budget (about $70 billion in fiscal 2025), which must cover vouchers, public housing, homelessness aid, and everything else.

## The renter programs: big names, capped scale

**Housing Choice Vouchers (Section 8)** are the largest rental-assistance program: about 2.3 million households — more than 5 million people. A voucher household generally pays around 30 percent of its adjusted income toward rent; the local housing authority pays the landlord the difference, up to a payment standard pegged to area fair-market rents. The catch is the program's legal character: it is *not* an [[term:entitlement-program]]. Funding is capped by annual appropriations, so only about 1 in 4 households poor enough to qualify for federal rental assistance receives any — the rest sit on waiting lists that run years long and are frequently closed to new names. Assistance for the poorest renters is, functionally, a lottery.

**LIHTC**, the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (1986), is the main engine of subsidized *construction*: the IRS gives states credit authority, state agencies award credits to developers through competitive plans, and developers sell the credits to investors to raise equity for income-restricted apartments. HUD's database counts about 3.9 million LIHTC units placed in service from 1987 through 2024 — far more than public housing ever reached. Critics fault its per-unit cost and syndication overhead; defenders answer that it is the only supply program with a durable bipartisan coalition, a point Lesson 3 revisits, because Congress quietly expanded it in 2025.

**Public housing** — government-owned, locally operated — is the [[term:new-deal]] survivor, dating to the Housing Act of 1937. Roughly 800,000 units remain — the stock has shrunk by more than a quarter-million units since the mid-1990s — housing about 1.6 million people, with a repair backlog that defines the program's politics. Since the 1998 Faircloth Amendment, HUD may not fund construction that would push a housing authority's stock above its October 1, 1999 level — a statutory ceiling on the program's growth. Smaller block grants (HOME, CDBG) round out the federal toolkit, giving states and localities flexible housing money at modest scale.

## How short is the country? Depends what you measure

"America is short X million homes" is now a stock phrase in speeches from both parties — with X ranging from under 4 million to over 7 million. The estimates are not rival answers to one question; they are answers to *different questions*, and reading them correctly is a basic civic skill.

| Estimate | Latest figure | What it actually measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freddie Mac (2024) | 3.7 million units | Gap between current supply and a "balanced" market: vacancies needed for healthy turnover plus households that never formed (was 3.8 million in its 2021 estimate) |
| Up for Growth (2025) | 3.78 million homes | "Underproduction" in 2023: housing needed for households (including suppressed household formation) minus habitable available stock |
| Zillow (2025) | 4.7 million homes | Families "doubling up" with non-relatives in 2023, net of available vacant units — a measure of latent demand |
| NLIHC *The Gap* (2026) | 7.2 million homes | Shortage of units both **affordable and available** to extremely low-income renters (incomes ≤30% of area median or the poverty line) — just 35 such homes exist per 100 ELI renter households |

The first three cluster around 4 million because they measure roughly the same thing: total stock against total demand. NLIHC's 7.2 million measures something else — not whether enough homes exist, but whether the poorest 11 million renter households can afford the ones that do. A construction boom could close the first gap while leaving most of the second: a market-rate apartment is "available" to an extremely low-income family only if rent assistance bridges the difference. That is why supply-siders and demand-siders in Lesson 2 can each brandish a "shortage" number and talk past each other.

The human indicators sit downstream of both gaps. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies counts 22.6 million cost-burdened renter households — half of all renters, paying over 30 percent of income for housing, a record. And homelessness, the crisis's sharpest edge, hit its own record: HUD's point-in-time count found 771,480 people homeless on a single night in January 2024, up 18 percent in one year, before easing 3.4 percent to 745,652 in January 2025 — the first decline since 2016. Lesson 3 returns to those numbers and the fight over what they mean.

> **Why this matters:** When a politician says "we're short 7 million homes, so we must build more," or "we built plenty and people are still homeless, so building doesn't work," each has quietly swapped one shortage definition for another. Catching that swap is half the housing debate.

## What this lesson did not settle

It did not settle *why* supply got constrained, whose interests the constraints serve, or what mix of building, subsidy, and protection would fix it. Three camps will each claim the machinery you just learned:

- **Supply-side (zoning-reform) advocates** argue the binding constraint is local regulation, and the cure is legalizing more housing where people want to live.
- **Demand-side (subsidy + assistance) advocates** argue the market will never price the poorest in, however much gets built, and the cure is closing the assistance lottery — vouchers and LIHTC at need-matching scale.
- **Tenant-protection-led advocates** argue that current tenants live in the years-long meantime, and the cure starts with rent stabilization, eviction defense, and tenant power.

Lesson 2 builds each case the way its smartest advocate would.
