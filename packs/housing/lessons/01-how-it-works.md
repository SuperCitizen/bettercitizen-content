# How U.S. housing policy actually works

Housing is mostly a state-and-local issue with significant federal financing infrastructure layered on top. The political debate often confuses these levels. Start with the structure.

## The three layers

**Federal:** sets the housing-finance infrastructure (mortgage standards, secondary market, FHA insurance) and runs the major income-tested programs (Section 8 vouchers, LIHTC tax credits, public housing).

**State:** generally minimal direct involvement. Sets enabling legislation that delegates land-use authority to localities. Some states (CA, OR, NJ) have begun to pre-empt local zoning to allow more housing types.

**Local:** runs zoning, building permits, environmental review, design review, and most of the actual decision-making about whether housing gets built. This is where supply is determined.

The housing-supply problem is mostly local. The housing-affordability programs are mostly federal. The mismatch between these levels is part of why housing policy is so politically tangled.

## How zoning shapes supply

Most U.S. residential land is zoned exclusively for single-family detached housing. The exact share varies by city — in Los Angeles, ~75% of residential land is zoned single-family; in Minneapolis (post-2018 reform), zero. The single-family-only designation prohibits duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, ADUs (in many cases), and apartment buildings.

Zoning does several things at once:
- Sets minimum lot sizes (often 5,000-20,000 sqft).
- Sets minimum setbacks from streets and lot lines.
- Sets maximum building heights and floor-area ratios.
- Restricts permitted uses (residential, commercial, industrial).
- Often requires off-street parking minimums (1-3 spaces per unit).
- Often requires public hearings for any deviation, with neighbor input.

The cumulative effect is that even where land is technically available, building anything other than the existing pattern requires either a variance (slow, expensive, often denied) or a rezoning (slower, more political, even more often denied).

The economic effect is that **prices rise rather than supply expanding** when demand grows. Glaeser & Gyourko (2018) document this: in metro areas with restrictive zoning (San Francisco, New York, Boston, Los Angeles), prices respond to demand much more than supply does; in less-restricted metros (Houston, Atlanta, Dallas), supply responds more and prices stay closer to construction costs.

## The federal housing-finance system

The federal government's largest housing role is in financing, not in building or operating housing.

**Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac** are government-sponsored enterprises that buy mortgages from primary lenders, package them into mortgage-backed securities, and guarantee timely payment. This creates liquidity in the mortgage market — the lender doesn't have to hold the mortgage to maturity. They've been in federal conservatorship since 2008.

**FHA (Federal Housing Administration)** insures mortgages with low down payments (typically 3.5%) for lower-income and first-time buyers. The premium is paid by the borrower; the insurance protects the lender if the borrower defaults.

**VA loans** are guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs for eligible veterans, with no down payment required.

**Mortgage interest deduction (MID)** — homeowners can deduct interest on up to $750K of mortgage debt (TCJA-era cap, was $1M previously). The MID is the largest housing-related federal subsidy, costing ~$30B/year in foregone revenue. It primarily benefits higher-income households (who itemize and have larger mortgages); CBO estimates that ~80% of the benefit goes to the top 20% of households.

This combined infrastructure makes 30-year fixed-rate mortgages widely available — a feature unique to the U.S. among wealthy countries (most others have variable-rate or shorter-duration norms). It's also why U.S. homeownership is heavily subsidized in ways that aren't always visible.

## The federal income-tested programs

**Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers**: The largest federal rental-assistance program. Eligible households get a voucher for ~70% of fair-market rent for their area; they pay ~30% of their income. The voucher follows the household, not the building. Currently serves ~2.3 million households. Funded through HUD; not an entitlement — supply is capped.

**LIHTC (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit)**: A tax-credit program that subsidizes private developers to build affordable rental housing. State agencies allocate credits to projects; developers sell credits to investors and use the cash to fund construction. About 2 million units built since 1986. Largest federal supply-side affordable-housing program. Critics argue it's expensive per unit; defenders argue it's the only politically viable supply-side program.

**Public housing**: Federally-owned, locally-operated rental housing. Was the dominant approach 1937-1970s; capital investment was largely defunded post-1970s. ~1 million units remain. Generally regarded as a politically depleted model that nonetheless serves a real population.

**HOME and CDBG**: Block-grant programs giving states and localities flexibility in housing-related investment. Smaller in scale than vouchers and LIHTC.

The combined federal funding for these programs is ~$60B/year — significant in absolute terms, but small relative to the ~$200B mortgage-interest tax expenditure that primarily benefits homeowners.

## The empirical record on costs and outcomes

A few claims that the data supports:

- **Housing costs have outpaced incomes in supply-constrained metros.** In San Francisco, Los Angeles, New York, Boston, Seattle, and Washington D.C., the median home price has grown 2-3× faster than median income since 2000. In less-constrained metros, the gap is smaller.

- **The U.S. is short ~3.8 million units** (NLIHC 2024 estimate of the gap between extremely-low-income renters and affordable rental units). The shortage is concentrated in expensive metros.

- **Spatial misallocation is large.** Hsieh & Moretti (2019) estimate that housing constraints in just three metros (NY, SF, LA) reduced U.S. GDP by 2-3% between 1964 and 2009 by preventing workers from moving to higher-productivity locations. The deadweight loss is substantial.

- **Homelessness has risen since 2017.** Roughly 580,000 people experienced homelessness on a given night in 2022 (HUD point-in-time count). Concentration in California, New York, and a few other states. Strongly correlated with rent levels and supply constraints, less correlated with mental illness or substance abuse than political rhetoric implies (which doesn't deny those factors matter for individuals).

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Supply-side (zoning-reform) advocates** argue the housing affordability crisis is fundamentally a supply shortage caused by local restrictions, and the solution is removing those restrictions to let the market build more housing.
- **Demand-side (subsidy + assistance) advocates** argue the housing market won't deliver affordability for low-income households even with abundant supply, and the solution is expanded vouchers, LIHTC, and direct income transfers tied to housing.
- **Tenant-protection-led advocates** argue the immediate problem for current tenants is displacement and rent gouging, that supply expansion takes years to reach the affordability margin, and the solution is rent stabilization, eviction protections, and tenant unions.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
