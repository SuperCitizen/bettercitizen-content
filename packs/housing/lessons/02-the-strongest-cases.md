# The strongest case for and against

Three positions, each presented as one its adherents would themselves endorse.

## 1. The supply-side (zoning-reform) case

The starting move is to treat housing as a market that, at its core, behaves like other markets. When demand grows and supply is constrained, prices rise. When supply can respond, prices stay closer to construction costs. The U.S. housing-affordability crisis, on this view, is what binding supply constraints look like.

The argument:

- **The price/cost gap is the smoking gun.** In Houston, building a typical single-family house costs ~$250-300K all-in (land + materials + labor + permitting). Houses sell at roughly that price plus a moderate markup. In San Francisco, building the same house would cost similar amounts (slightly more for some inputs), but it sells for $1.5M+. The gap is "regulatory tax" — what land-use restrictions add to the price beyond construction cost. Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks (2005) document this gap; subsequent work has refined the methodology but the basic finding is robust.

- **Zoning reform produces measurable supply responses.** Cities that have liberalized zoning — Minneapolis (2018, eliminated single-family-only), California (SB 9 2021, allowed lot splits), Oregon (HB 2001 2019, allowed multi-family in single-family zones) — have seen meaningful (though variable) supply responses. The objection that "supply doesn't respond to zoning reform because of construction-finance constraints" is empirically wrong at sufficient scale; it's true that any single reform takes time to translate into completed units.

- **Supply produces filtering even at the high end.** A common skeptic argument is that "luxury" housing doesn't help affordability because it's not affordable. But filtering is real: when newer, higher-end units enter the market, residents move into them from older units, freeing up middle-tier inventory, which frees up older inventory at the bottom. Mast (2019) provides the cleanest empirical estimate: every 100 new market-rate units in a metro produces ~70 vacancies in lower-income segments via filtering chains, with the effect concentrated within ~5 years.

- **The poor are housed in older units.** Almost all very-affordable rental housing in the U.S. is *old* — buildings whose construction was paid down decades ago and whose current rents reflect operating costs alone. This stock is built only by adding new market-rate units that eventually become old. Restricting new construction therefore restricts the future supply of affordable old units. The position is paradoxical only if you think about a single building; it's straightforward when you think about the housing stock as it ages over time.

- **Spatial-misallocation costs are macroeconomic.** Hsieh & Moretti (2019) estimate U.S. GDP would be 2-3% higher if housing constraints in NY/SF/LA were relaxed to peer-metro levels — workers can't move to where productivity is highest. The cost falls on workers who can't access opportunity, not just on prospective movers.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that supply expansion at scale doesn't produce filtering at the bottom of the market, that local supply constraints reflect real public goods rather than rent-seeking, or that the affordability problem is fundamentally about income distribution rather than supply. The supply-side position takes the income-distribution argument seriously and tries to combine zoning reform with rental assistance — but argues the supply piece is necessary regardless.

## 2. The demand-side (subsidy + assistance) case

The starting move is to argue the supply-side analysis, while correct in some respects, can't deliver affordability to the lowest-income population fast enough or at all. Some households need direct assistance, and supply expansion alone doesn't substitute for it.

The argument:

- **Filtering is too slow for current tenants.** The supply-side argument concedes filtering takes ~5 years to reach lower-income segments. A family facing a $300/month rent increase next year doesn't have 5 years. Voucher programs and direct subsidies provide immediate relief that supply expansion cannot match on the relevant time horizon.

- **The very-low-income market doesn't function as a market.** At the bottom of the income distribution, voucher discrimination is widespread, source-of-income discrimination is legal in most states, and transaction costs swamp the price-signal benefits supply expansion would produce. Collinson & Ganong (2018) document large landlord refusals to accept Section 8 vouchers even where doing so is technically legal. Section 8 expansion to all eligible households (currently a fraction of eligible households receive vouchers due to funding caps) would deliver more affordability than equivalent supply expansion.

- **LIHTC builds the units the market won't.** The supply-side advocate's filtering argument depends on enough market-rate units being built that older units age into affordability. But in many metros, no market-rate units are built at the affordable-to-low-income margin — the only path to those units is direct subsidy through LIHTC or vouchers. The demand-side case argues this gap requires direct intervention, not just zoning reform.

- **Income transfers have higher returns than housing-only spending.** Desmond's *Evicted* documents how housing instability cascades into job loss, child welfare effects, and poverty deepening. Programs that increase low-income household incomes (Section 8 vouchers, EITC expansion, child allowance) reduce eviction and homelessness substantially, with benefits beyond housing alone. The demand-side case argues this generalized-income approach is more efficient than housing-specific subsidies.

- **The mortgage-interest deduction is the wrong subsidy.** $30B/year flows to higher-income homeowners through the MID. Redirecting that revenue to expanded vouchers or LIHTC would deliver dramatically more affordability per dollar. The demand-side case is partly about reallocating *existing* federal housing subsidies more efficiently, not just adding new spending.

- **Source-of-income discrimination protections work where adopted.** Some states and cities prohibit landlords from refusing tenants based on source of income (i.e., voucher status). Where these protections are enforced, voucher utilization improves substantially. The demand-side case includes legal-protections-for-vouchers as a critical complement to voucher expansion itself.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that voucher programs don't actually deliver the housing they nominally fund (because of discrimination, supply constraints, or administrative friction), or that demand-side spending increases prices more than it expands access at scale. The demand-side position acknowledges these are real concerns and tries to design programs (voucher mobility, anti-discrimination laws, supply complementarity) to address them.

## 3. The tenant-protection-led case

The starting move is to argue that the supply-and-demand framework misses what happens to *current* tenants during whatever transition the other approaches require. Eviction, rent gouging, displacement — these are immediate harms that take place during the years (or decades) it takes for supply or subsidy approaches to produce results.

The argument:

- **Rent stabilization protects against displacement.** Rent control's record is genuinely mixed (Diamond, McQuade & Qian 2019 find the effects of San Francisco's expansion were ambiguous — protected tenants benefited while overall housing supply contracted). But targeted rent stabilization — limiting annual increases for existing tenants in long-occupied units, without affecting new construction — produces significant tenant security with smaller supply effects. Modern rent-stabilization (Oregon's SB 608, California's AB 1482) is designed around this distinction.

- **Eviction protections matter on their own terms.** Even setting aside cost questions, eviction has enormous downstream effects on children, employment, and health. Just-cause-eviction laws (limiting landlords to enumerated grounds for non-renewal), longer notice periods, and right-to-counsel programs have produced documented reductions in eviction rates without major supply effects. New York City's Right to Counsel program has reduced evictions substantially since adoption.

- **The market produces inequality even when it produces supply.** A supply-rich metro like Houston still has unaffordable areas, eviction problems, and homelessness. The supply-side argument treats affordability as a pure supply-and-demand outcome; the tenant-protection case argues the distribution of outcomes within a metro depends on power dynamics between landlords and tenants that supply alone doesn't fix.

- **Tenant unions and collective bargaining work.** Places with active tenant organizing (Berlin's referendum to expropriate large landlords, Spain's social-housing tenant organizing, U.S. organizations like the Rolling Jubilee) produce better tenant outcomes through collective negotiation. The case here is partly about power: supply and demand assume markets clear at competitive prices, but rental markets often function more like bilateral negotiations where the landlord has structural power. Organizing rebalances this.

- **Homelessness response requires immediate intervention.** Whether the long-run answer is supply or subsidy, the people sleeping on streets tonight need housing tonight, not a forecast about the housing stock in 2028. Housing First programs (Pathways to Housing model) have produced strong evidence of effectiveness — provide housing without preconditions, provide services. The tenant-protection case includes acute-homelessness response as a separate priority that doesn't fit neatly in supply/demand framing.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that rent stabilization's supply effects (Diamond et al.'s SF findings, classical economics arguments) outweigh tenant benefits at scale, or that eviction protections shift the cost in ways that worsen affordability for new tenants. The tenant-protection position acknowledges these are the strongest critiques and tries to design protections (vacancy decontrol, supply-side complements, time-limited stabilization) that address them.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled (or near-settled):**
- Supply-restrictive metros have higher prices (Glaeser-Gyourko-Saks; replicated extensively).
- Spatial misallocation from housing constraints is large in macroeconomic terms (Hsieh-Moretti).
- Voucher discrimination is a binding constraint on Section 8 effectiveness (Collinson-Ganong).
- Filtering happens but takes years (Mast 2019; multiple replications).

**Genuinely contested:**
- The magnitude of supply elasticity in different markets — how much new construction follows zoning reform.
- The net welfare effect of rent control (the literature is more mixed than either side admits).
- Whether the right policy mix is supply-led, demand-led, or balanced (the empirical case is mixed-method, not single-tool).
- The role of mortgage-interest subsidies vs. rental assistance (large fiscal stakes, strong distributional pattern).

A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and acknowledges what's contested. Lesson 3 turns to the policy landscape.
