# The strongest case for and against

Here is a price worth staring at. Economists Joseph Gyourko and Jacob Krimmel assembled data on actual sales of *vacant* residential land — dirt, before any house exists — and computed what they call the "zoning tax": how much regulation inflates the price of a buildable quarter-acre above what an unrestricted market would charge. In most American metros the zoning tax is near zero. In the San Francisco metro it is roughly $400,000 — for the lot alone, house not included — with six-figure zoning taxes across New York, Seattle, and Los Angeles. By the first quarter of 2026, the median single-family home in metro San Jose sold for $2,030,000, five times the national median of $404,300; eight of the ten most expensive U.S. markets were in California. Everyone in the housing debate agrees those numbers are obscene. The argument — and this lesson stages it as a proper [[term:steel-man]], each position built as its smartest adherent would build it — is about what the numbers *mean* and what to do about them.

## 1. The supply-side (zoning-reform) case

The supply-sider's starting move is spatial equilibrium, an idea owed to Sherwin Rosen (1979) and Jennifer Roback (1982). In plain English: people can move, so places must "balance their books." A city with high wages and good weather can't stay a bargain — people pile in until housing costs eat the advantage. The books can balance two ways, though. If the city lets builders respond, the inflow becomes *more residents at moderate prices*: the city grows. If it doesn't, the inflow becomes *higher prices at fixed population*: access to the city's wages gets rationed by rent, and the productivity of the place is captured by whoever already owns land there. The zoning tax is what that capture looks like in a land-sale ledger. Glaeser, Gyourko, and Saks (2005) made the same point from construction costs: in restrictive metros, homes sell far above the cost of building them, a gap that is regulation's price tag, not lumber's.

From there the case runs on three planks:

**Supply responses are real where reforms bind.** Lesson 3 walks the scoreboard — Minneapolis, Austin, Houston's by-right tradition — but the short version is that places that legalized and streamlined housing built it, and their rents behaved differently from their peers'.

**New market-rate housing helps the bottom of the market through filtering.** The skeptic's instinct — "luxury towers can't help poor renters" — misses that homes are not consumed once. Evan Mast (2023) traced the actual chains: take 686 new market-rate buildings in 12 cities, find the 52,000 people who moved in, then find who moved into *their* old homes, and so on. Building 100 new market-rate units opens the equivalent of 45 to 70 units in below-median-income neighborhoods — dozens of them in the poorest fifth — mostly within about five years. Vicki Been, Ingrid Gould Ellen, and Katherine O'Regan, reviewing this literature under the pointed title "Supply Skepticism" (2019, updated since), conclude that new supply lowers rents in the surrounding market even when the new units themselves are expensive. And over decades, filtering is where almost all unsubsidized affordable housing comes from: today's cheap apartment is a building someone built at market rate in 1968. Choke off today's market-rate construction and you have canceled 2060's affordable stock.

**The macro claim — handled with tongs.** For years the supply-side case carried a macroeconomic trump card: Chang-Tai Hsieh and Enrico Moretti's "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation" (2019), which estimated that housing constraints lowered aggregate U.S. growth by 36 percent from 1964 to 2009, and that relaxing regulation in just New York, San Francisco, and San Jose to the median city's level would lift GDP by 3.7 percent. The numbers escaped the seminar room and turned up in White House documents and a thousand op-eds. Then came replication. Brian Greaney's published comment (2026) documented coding errors — run as written, the paper's own counterfactual *lowers* output — and showed the model's results hinged on an arbitrary modeling choice; in his repaired version, the gains from liberalizing the three superstar metros are real but roughly two orders of magnitude smaller, on the order of 0.02 percent of GDP. The authors have publicly acknowledged error. The honest supply-sider's response is to stop citing the headline number and rest the case on the micro evidence — zoning taxes, price-cost gaps, filtering chains, and city-level natural experiments — which emerged from the episode intact.

> **The scholar's lens:** Why do cities choose rules that make housing scarce? William Fischel's *The Homevoter Hypothesis* (2001) supplies the political economy: for most homeowners, the house is the single largest, least diversifiable asset they will ever own, and they cannot insure it against neighborhood change. So they behave like risk-averse shareholders of the municipality, voting reliably against anything — density, shelters, apartments — that carries even small downside risk to home values. The behavior is individually rational and collectively ruinous, which is why supply-siders increasingly aim reform at the *state* level, where renters and would-be residents count too. That chess move is Lesson 3's opening topic.

What evidence would change the supply-side position? A demonstration that filtering chains break down at scale — that new building raises rather than relieves nearby rents — or that constraints mostly price in genuine public goods rather than incumbent protection. The strongest supply-siders concede the demand-side point that filtering cannot reach extremely low-income households without subsidy; their claim is that supply is necessary, not that it is sufficient.

## 2. The demand-side (subsidy + assistance) case

The demand-sider grants most of the supply economics and then asks the question it leaves open: what happens, this year, to the family at the 20th income percentile? Filtering takes half a decade to deliver its discounts. A voucher delivers affordability the month it is issued. And as Lesson 1 showed, vouchers are a lottery — about 1 in 4 eligible households served — so the single highest-yield housing policy, on this view, is closing that gap before relitigating anyone's zoning code.

The case sharpens on three findings:

**The low-income rental market is not the textbook market.** Landlords refuse vouchers at startling rates: in the Urban Institute's paired-testing study for HUD (Cunningham et al., 2018), 78 percent of contacted landlords in Fort Worth refused voucher holders outright, 76 percent in Los Angeles, 67 percent in Philadelphia — versus 31 percent in Newark and 15 percent in Washington, D.C., where source-of-income laws have teeth. Refusals ran *highest* in low-poverty neighborhoods, exactly where the program's mobility promise points. The legal fix is spreading — about two dozen states ban source-of-income discrimination, covering roughly 60 percent of voucher holders (PRRAC, 2026) — and the D.C.–Newark contrast is the demand-sider's proof that enforcement, not exhortation, moves the number. Screening practices compound the problem: income-multiple rules and credit checks fall hardest on households whose earnings are volatile, an increasingly common condition in the [[term:gig-economy]].

**Design details decide whether subsidy money reaches tenants or landlords.** Robert Collinson and Peter Ganong (2018) studied what happens when voucher generosity changes. Raise payment standards across an entire metro and landlords capture much of the increase as rent, with little gain in housing quality. Tie payment standards to ZIP-code-level rents instead — generous in expensive neighborhoods, lean in cheap ones — and voucher families relocate to measurably better neighborhoods at little extra cost. The lesson generalizes: demand subsidies in supply-constrained markets leak into prices, so the smart demand-sider is also a supply reformer, and vice versa.

**Some housing will never pencil without subsidy.** No developer can build new units that rent at levels a minimum-wage worker can pay; the math fails before the zoning hearing starts. That housing exists only through LIHTC, public housing, and vouchers. The demand-side case also notes the fiscal asymmetry from Lesson 1 with a redistributive edge: homeowner tax subsidies run about $145 billion a year while the entire voucher program serves 2.3 million households — so the money exists; it is aimed at the wrong end of the income distribution.

> **The scholar's lens:** Matthew Desmond's *Evicted* (2016) is the demand side's evidentiary anchor. Embedded in Milwaukee, Desmond and the Milwaukee Area Renters Study found that more than 1 in 8 renters experienced a forced move in 2009–2011, and that a majority of poor renting families spent over half their income on housing — a quarter of them over 70 percent. Princeton's Eviction Lab, which he founded, counts roughly 3.6 million eviction filings in a typical pre-pandemic year. Desmond's theoretical claim is the important one: eviction is not merely a symptom of poverty but a *cause* of it — wrecking jobs, schooling, credit, and mental health — so housing assistance is anti-poverty policy, not just shelter policy.

What evidence would change the demand-side position? Strong evidence that subsidies mostly inflate rents (the Collinson–Ganong leak at scale), or that expanded assistance erodes work incentives enough to swamp its benefits. Demand-siders answer the first with design (tiered standards, supply complements) and treat the second as an empirical question the eviction literature mostly resolves in their favor.

## 3. The tenant-protection-led case

The tenant advocate begins where the other two camps wave their hands: the transition. Supply works in years-to-decades; voucher expansion is an annual appropriations fight. The family facing a $300 rent hike or a no-fault eviction lives in the meantime, and policy for the meantime is what tenant protection is.

**Rent stabilization, with eyes open.** The strongest tenant advocates do not dodge the canonical study — they cite it precisely. Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade, and Franklin Qian (2019) exploited a quirk in San Francisco's 1994 rent-control expansion and found both effects at once: covered tenants were far likelier to stay in their homes (mobility fell about 20 percent), *and* affected landlords cut their supply of rental housing 15 percent — converting to condos, redeveloping — which raised citywide rents about 5.1 percent over time. Read as design guidance rather than refutation, the study says: cap increases rather than freeze rents, exempt new construction so building still pencils, and limit conversion loopholes. That is precisely the architecture of the modern statutes — Oregon's SB 608 (2019), the first statewide cap (7 percent plus inflation, tightened to a 10 percent ceiling in 2023), and California's AB 1482 (2019) (5 percent plus inflation, 10 percent max), both exempting buildings less than 15 years old. These are anti-gouging laws, not the hard rent freezes of economics-textbook lore.

**Eviction defense changes outcomes at the courthouse.** Most landlords arrive at eviction court with lawyers; most tenants, historically, arrived alone. New York City's right-to-counsel law (2017, the nation's first) put attorneys on the tenant side, and in recent city data more than four in five represented tenants facing eviction remained in their homes — though the program's reach has slipped as funding lagged, with representation rates falling from 71 percent to 42 percent between 2021 and 2024. Just-cause statutes, which require enumerated grounds for non-renewal, aim at the same target: converting tenancy from a month-to-month sufferance into a defensible legal status.

**Power, not just price.** The deepest version of this case says low-end rental markets run on asymmetry: the landlord knows the law, repeats the game, and can absorb a vacancy; the tenant often has none of the three. Desmond documented the result — rents in Milwaukee's poorest neighborhoods ran nearly as high as in middle-class ones while the underlying buildings cost far less. Organizing answers asymmetry with collectivity: tenant unions such as KC Tenants in Kansas City negotiate and legislate as a bloc, and Berlin's 2021 [[term:referendum]], in which a majority voted to expropriate large corporate landlords (non-binding, and not implemented), marks the maximal version of the impulse. Whatever one thinks of the remedies, the diagnosis — that "the market" at the bottom is a bargaining table with one chair — is the tenant movement's distinct contribution.

What evidence would change this position? Evidence that protections at scale raise rents or screening barriers for *future* tenants by more than they protect current ones (the DMQ mechanism, generalized), or that just-cause and counsel mainly delay rather than prevent displacement. The serious tenant advocate accepts the supply-siders' decades and the demand-siders' dollars — and insists both are incomplete without rights.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled, or close to it:** Restrictive regulation raises prices — the zoning tax is measurable in land sales (Gyourko–Krimmel) and price-cost gaps (Glaeser–Gyourko–Saks). New market-rate supply filters down and moderates nearby rents (Mast; Been–Ellen–O'Regan). Voucher discrimination is widespread where legal (Cunningham et al.), and voucher design governs who captures the subsidy (Collinson–Ganong). Rent control protects incumbents while shrinking rental supply (Diamond–McQuade–Qian).

**Genuinely contested:** The *macro* size of the misallocation cost, post-Greaney. How far down, and how fast, filtering reaches in the tightest markets. Whether modern capped-and-exempted stabilization avoids the classic supply damage at scale. And the central allocation question — build, subsidize, or protect first — which the evidence narrows but does not answer, because it is partly a question about whose years count.

A defensible position picks a frame, cites the studies that survive scrutiny, and names what would change its mind. Lesson 3 turns to the field where these arguments are now being run live: statehouses, Congress, and the courts.
