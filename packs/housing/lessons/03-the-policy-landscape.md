# The current policy landscape

Most housing policy continues to happen at the state and local level. The federal role is significant in financing but relatively small in direct intervention.

## State-level zoning preemption

Until ~2018, zoning was almost universally a local matter. Several states have begun to override local restrictions:

- **California**: SB 9 (2021) allows two-unit and lot-split development on most single-family parcels statewide. SB 35 (2017) streamlines approval for projects meeting affordability targets. AB 2011 (2022) allows multifamily on commercial parcels. Effects on supply have been smaller than supporters hoped — local resistance through design review and discretionary processes has limited the impact.

- **Oregon**: HB 2001 (2019) allowed duplex-through-fourplex in former single-family zones in cities over 25,000 population. SB 6080 (2023) accelerated implementation. Oregon's small-city focus has produced supply gains in mid-sized markets like Eugene.

- **Washington**: HB 1110 (2023) allows up to four units on most single-family parcels statewide, with some exceptions.

- **Minneapolis** (city): 2018 comprehensive plan allowed triplexes citywide. Often cited as the early model.

State preemption is the most-active reform area in U.S. housing policy. Whether it produces sustained supply gains depends on enforcement, parallel local resistance through other tools (design review, environmental review, parking minimums), and broader market conditions.

## Federal levers

**HUD funding conditions.** HUD can attach conditions to federal housing-funding flows (CDBG, HOME, Section 8 administrative funds) that incentivize zoning reform. The Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing rule (Obama-era, withdrawn under Trump, modified under Biden) is the most-developed example.

**Mortgage policy.** The federal-conservatorship of Fannie/Freddie gives the federal government significant indirect control over which mortgages get securitized and at what rates. Loan-limit increases, multifamily-loan support, and subsidies for affordability-targeted projects all flow through this channel.

**LIHTC expansion.** The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is the largest federal supply-side affordable-housing program. Recent proposals would expand the credit allocation and adjust the formula. The 2024 bipartisan tax bill included a small LIHTC expansion.

**Section 8 expansion.** Voucher programs are funded annually but currently serve only a fraction of eligible households. Universal voucher proposals would extend assistance to all eligible households at significant cost (~$50-100B/year depending on design).

**Tax expenditures.** The mortgage interest deduction (~$30B/year), state-and-local property tax deduction, and capital gains exclusion on home sales are all federal subsidies for homeownership. Reform proposals would scale them back to fund expanded rental assistance.

## Homelessness response

Federal policy on homelessness has consolidated around **Housing First** (Tsemberis & Eisenberg 2000) — provide permanent housing without preconditions (sobriety, treatment compliance), provide voluntary services. Empirical evidence for Housing First on housing stability and cost-savings (vs. shelter-and-services models) is strong.

Implementation challenges:
- **Encampment response**: cities vary in approach (clearance vs. tolerance, with or without housing alternatives). Recent SCOTUS ruling in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) held that anti-camping ordinances do not violate the Eighth Amendment, expanding municipal flexibility but not resolving the underlying housing question.
- **Service capacity**: Housing First requires permanent supportive housing (PSH) supply. Most metros have insufficient PSH inventory.
- **State-level homelessness funding**: California's HHAP, New York's local programs, Washington State investments have grown substantially but are still far short of what scale-out would require.

## What's likely next

- **More state-level zoning preemption.** The political coalition for it includes both progressive (housing affordability) and libertarian (property rights, removing government restrictions) voices. Expect more states to pass versions of CA SB 9 / OR HB 2001.

- **Federal LIHTC expansion + tenant assistance.** Bipartisan support for these programs is more durable than for direct supply-side intervention. Expect incremental growth.

- **Tenant-protection legislation.** Oregon's SB 608, California's AB 1482, and similar measures elsewhere — these are spreading slowly and unevenly. Expect more states to adopt rent-stabilization measures that exempt new construction (mitigating supply concerns).

- **Mortgage-interest-deduction reform.** Politically difficult but on the agenda. Reform options include capping the deduction at lower mortgage levels or converting it to a tax credit.

- **Homelessness-policy contestation.** Following Grants Pass, expect more municipal encampment-clearance activity and continued debate over whether clearance without sufficient housing alternatives is acceptable.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. housing policy requires:

1. A view on **whether the binding constraint is supply, demand, or tenant-power** (the structural question).
2. A view on **what role the federal vs. state vs. local government should play** (the federalism question).
3. A view on **what to do about acute homelessness now** (the emergency question).
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change each.
