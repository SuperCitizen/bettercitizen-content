## The first home, at 40

The median first-time homebuyer in the United States is now 40 years old. Through the 1980s, the typical first-timer was in their late twenties.

- Median first-time buyer: age 40

Before you can argue about why housing got this expensive, you need the machine itself: who decides whether homes get built, who finances them, and who gets help paying for them.

## Three layers, one mismatch

Each layer of government holds a different lever. The federal government controls mortgage finance, tax subsidies and rental aid. States write the law that delegates land-use power downward. Local governments decide whether housing actually gets built.

- Federal: finance, subsidies, rental aid
- State: delegates land-use power
- Local: zoning, permits, hearings

The level of government that voters blame for affordability, the federal one, does not control supply.

> Washington writes checks. City hall decides whether anything gets built.

## Demand becomes price

Zoning has had constitutional permission since 1926. In a case from the Village of Euclid, Ohio, the Supreme Court upheld it as an ordinary exercise of the police power.

- 1926: the Supreme Court upholds zoning

Glaeser and Gyourko document what follows. In lightly regulated metros like Houston or Atlanta, prices hover near the cost of construction, because builders answer demand with units. In tightly regulated coastal metros, prices float far above construction cost, because builders legally cannot.

- Loosely zoned: more homes, small price rise
- Tightly zoned: price spikes, few new homes

> Where rules make supply unresponsive, demand becomes price.

## Short by how many homes?

Speeches from both parties say America is short by millions of homes: under 4 million, or over 7 million. The estimates answer different questions. Freddie Mac's 3.7 million measures total stock against total demand. NLIHC's 7.2 million measures whether the poorest renters can afford the homes that exist.

- Freddie Mac: 3.7 million
- NLIHC: 7.2 million

A construction boom could close the first gap while leaving most of the second.

> The shortage estimates are answers to different questions.

## Three camps

The lesson does not settle why supply got constrained, or what would fix it.

Supply-side advocates argue the cure is legalizing more housing where people want to live.

- Supply-side: legalize more housing

Demand-side advocates argue the cure is closing the assistance lottery, because the market will never price the poorest in.

- Demand-side: close the assistance lottery

Tenant-protection-led advocates argue the cure starts with rent stabilization, eviction defense and tenant power.

- Tenant protection: stabilization, eviction defense
