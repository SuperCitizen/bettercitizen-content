## The price of dirt

The economists Joseph Gyourko and Jacob Krimmel computed how much regulation inflates the price of a buildable quarter-acre of vacant land. They call it the zoning tax. In most American metros it is near zero. In the San Francisco metro it is roughly 400,000 dollars, for the lot alone.

- Zoning tax, San Francisco metro: roughly $400,000

Everyone in the housing debate agrees a number like that is obscene. The argument is about what it means, and what to do about it.

## The supply-side case

The supply-side advocate argues that high prices in superstar metros are a legal artifact, and that the remedy is to legalize housing where people want to live. They point to Evan Mast, who traced the chains of moves behind new market-rate buildings. Building 100 new market-rate units leads 45 to 70 people to move out of below-median-income neighborhoods, almost all within about five years. They concede that supply alone never reaches the poorest households.

- Mast: 100 new units, 45 to 70 movers

> The supply-side case: high prices are a legal artifact, not a natural fact.

## The demand-side case

The demand-side advocate argues that filtering takes half a decade while a voucher works the month it is issued, and that the remedy is to close the lottery that serves about 1 in 4 eligible households. They point to a paired-testing study for HUD. Landlords refused voucher holders 78 percent of the time in Fort Worth, and 15 percent in Washington, D.C., where source-of-income laws have teeth. They concede that subsidies in supply-constrained markets leak into prices.

- Voucher refusals: Fort Worth 78%, D.C. 15%

> The demand-side case: a voucher works the month it is issued.

## The tenant-protection case

The tenant-protection-led advocate argues that both other camps legislate for the future while tenants live in the meantime, and that the remedy is capped rent increases, counsel at eviction court and organized tenant power. They point to a study of San Francisco's 1994 rent-control expansion, where covered tenants were far likelier to stay in their homes: mobility fell about 20 percent. They concede its other finding. Affected landlords cut their supply of rental housing 15 percent, which raised citywide rents about 5.1 percent.

- San Francisco: tenants stayed, rental supply fell 15%

> The tenant-protection case: tenants live in the meantime.

## What the evidence leaves open

Those findings are settled, or close to it. What is contested is the central question: build, subsidize, or protect first. The evidence narrows it but does not answer it.

- Contested: build, subsidize, or protect first
