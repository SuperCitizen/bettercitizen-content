Picture someone buying a first home. The papers are signed, and the keys change hands. How old is the buyer?

[beat]

In the United States today, the median first-time buyer is 40 years old.

That is the oldest in the four decades the National Association of Realtors has been asking. Through the 1980s, the typical first-timer was in their late twenties. And in the 2025 survey, first-time buyers were 21 percent of the market. [beat] The lowest share ever recorded.

The arithmetic behind that milestone is unforgiving. In May of 2026, the median existing home sold for about 429,000 dollars. In early June, the 30-year mortgage averaged 6.48 percent.

[pause]

A generation is spending its prime saving years renting, and politicians of every stripe have noticed.

But before anyone can argue about why housing got this expensive, they need the machine itself. Who decides whether homes get built? Who finances them? And who gets help paying for them?

The answers sit at three different levels of government. And the mismatch between those levels is the original sin of American housing politics.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to answer those three questions. And when you next hear that America is short millions of homes, you should know what to ask back.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the three layers of government, and the mismatch between them.

Second, zoning: the local power that decides what gets built.

Third, the federal money, in two halves: one for people who buy and own, and one for people who rent.

And last, the shortage numbers, and why they do not agree.

## Three layers, one mismatch

Housing policy is a federalism problem wearing an economics costume.

Put more plainly: it looks like a question about prices. Underneath, it is a question about which level of government holds which lever.

There are three levels. Take them from the top.

The federal government controls mortgage finance, tax subsidies, and rental aid that is tested by income.

The states write the enabling law that hands power over land use down to local governments. Lately, some states have been clawing that power back.

And local governments control whether housing actually gets built. Their instruments are zoning, permits, design review, parking minimums, and public hearings.

[pause]

Now take that list from the bottom up, and the mismatch jumps out.

The level of government that voters blame for affordability is the federal one. It does not control supply.

The level that does control supply is tens of thousands of cities, towns and counties. They face no national electorate. They answer to the people who show up at Tuesday-night planning meetings, and those people are disproportionately homeowners with something to protect.

> Washington writes the checks. City hall decides whether anything gets built.

Keep that division in view through everything that comes next. Most bad housing punditry consists of demanding that one layer fix a problem another layer owns.

## Zoning: the pig in the parlor

So begin at the bottom, with the lever that decides what gets built. That lever is zoning.

Modern American land-use law begins in a suburb of Cleveland. In the early 1920s, the Village of Euclid, Ohio, zoned itself into districts by use. A company called Ambler Realty owned 68 acres there, which it intended to sell for industry. It sued, arguing that the ordinance destroyed most of the land's value.

In 1926, the Supreme Court sided with the village. It upheld zoning as an ordinary exercise of the police power. And Justice Sutherland supplied the image that built a hundred thousand zoning codes. Listen for the animal.

"A nuisance may be merely a right thing in the wrong place, like a pig in the parlor instead of the barnyard."

[pause]

A pig in the parlor. Apartment buildings near houses, the Court suggested, could be "mere parasites" on a neighborhood.

With constitutional permission secured, comprehensive zoning swept the country.

So what does that power look like in operation? A typical suburban code does several things at once. Each sounds modest. Together, they are decisive.

It reserves most residential land for single-family detached houses. In the city of Los Angeles, by the city's own estimate, that is about 75 percent of residential land.

It sets minimum lot sizes and height limits, and it requires off-street parking.

And it routes anything unusual through a variance or a rezoning. Those are discretionary processes, heavy with hearings, where neighbors object and timelines stretch into years.

That last item hides the most consequential machinery. So let's walk it once: the path of a typical rezoning, and the places it can stall or die.

First, before any paper is filed, the applicant meets the planning staff, to learn what the code allows and what the politics will bear. Many projects shrink right here.

Then comes the application, with studies and filing fees that routinely reach five figures before a single hearing.

Then the staff publish a recommendation. An adverse one often ends the project.

Then a hearing before the appointed planning commission, where the neighbors testify. This is the homeowners' veto point.

Then the elected city council holds its own public hearing, and votes.

And even an approval can still draw lawsuits. A denial, by contrast, is usually the end of the road.

[pause]

Notice the shape of that path.

At every stage, someone can say no. [beat] At no stage must anyone say yes.

That is why developers price the risk of the process into what they propose. And it is why many projects are never proposed at all.

Now the economic consequence. It is the central fact of this whole series.

> Where rules make supply unresponsive, demand becomes price.

Here is what that means in practice. A 2018 study by Glaeser and Gyourko documents the pattern. In lightly regulated metros, like Houston or Atlanta, prices hover near the cost of construction, because builders answer demand with units. In tightly regulated coastal metros, prices float far above the cost of construction, because builders legally cannot.

Put another way, the same growth in demand produces homes where supply can respond, and prices where it cannot.

That is the supply-side movement's core claim about coastal metros. It is also the baseline that every rival perspective argues against. So hold on to it.

Let's test it. A booming tech metro and a flat Midwestern metro both see 50,000 new high-paying jobs announced. In which one do existing homeowners gain more wealth? And why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The tech metro, if it is the tightly zoned one.

Where supply cannot respond, new demand cannot become new homes. So it becomes higher prices for the homes that exist, and the owners already there capture the windfall. Where supply can respond, builders absorb the demand, and prices stay near the cost of construction.

And notice the politics this creates. The more restrictive the rules, the more existing owners profit from keeping them.

## Washington and the buyer

That is the local lever. Now up to Washington, and the money.

The federal government's biggest housing role is not building homes. It is standing behind the loans that buy them.

At the center are Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. They are government-sponsored enterprises, in federal conservatorship since their rescue in 2008. They buy mortgages from lenders and bundle them into guaranteed securities. That is what makes the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage a mass-market product.

Beside them sits FHA insurance, which lets first-time and lower-income buyers in with down payments as low as 3.5 percent.

Now, the system's signature product has a signature side effect.

In 2020 and 2021, millions of households locked in mortgages near 3 percent. In 2023, rates jumped above 7 percent. Selling now meant trading a cheap loan for an expensive one. [beat] So fewer owners sold.

That is the lock-in effect. In plainer words: a cheap old mortgage becomes a reason not to move.

An FHFA working paper put a number on it. From the spring of 2022 to the end of 2023, lock-in prevented an estimated 1.33 million home sales. And by starving the market of listings, it pushed prices higher, even as borrowing costs soared.

Then there is the tax code. It is the largest federal housing subsidy, and the least visible.

The mortgage interest deduction lets people who itemize deduct the interest on up to 750,000 dollars of mortgage debt. Add the capital-gains exclusion on home sales, and the related breaks, and the homeownership subsidies in the tax code come to roughly 145 billion dollars a year.

That money flows overwhelmingly to higher-income households, who itemize and carry large mortgages.

Roughly 145 billion dollars a year. Hold that figure. You will need it in a few minutes.

## Washington and the renter

Now the other half of the federal money: the renter programs. There are three, with big names and capped scale.

The first is the Housing Choice Voucher, known as Section 8. It is the largest rental-assistance program, and it serves about 2.3 million households.

Here is how it works. A voucher household generally pays around 30 percent of its adjusted income toward rent. The local housing authority pays the landlord the difference, up to a ceiling called the payment standard.

The catch is the program's legal character. It is not an entitlement. Qualifying for help does not mean receiving it, because the funding is capped by annual appropriations, and not by need.

So only about 1 in 4 of the households poor enough to qualify for federal rental assistance receives any. The rest sit on waiting lists that run for years, and that are frequently closed to new names.

> About one in four eligible households gets federal rental help. The rest wait.

For the poorest renters, assistance is, functionally, a lottery.

Now reach back. A few minutes ago, I asked you to hold a figure: what the tax code's homeownership subsidies come to in a year. Can you still call it up?

[long pause]

Roughly 145 billion dollars.

Set that beside HUD's entire net budget, which was about 70 billion dollars in fiscal 2025. That budget must cover vouchers, public housing, homelessness aid, and everything else. The subsidies for homeowners are roughly double it.

Many people assume that federal housing policy means programs for the poor. The dollars say otherwise.

> By dollars, federal housing policy is mostly a program for homeowners.

The second renter program is the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, or LIHTC, created in 1986. It is the main engine of subsidized construction.

State agencies award the credits to developers, through competitive plans. The developers then sell the credits to investors, to raise the equity for income-restricted apartments. HUD's database counts about 3.9 million units placed in service that way, far more than public housing ever reached.

Critics fault its cost per unit. Defenders answer that it is the only supply program with a durable bipartisan coalition. We will come back to that in the third lecture, because Congress quietly expanded it in 2025.

The third program is public housing: owned by government, operated locally, and dating to the Housing Act of 1937. Roughly 800,000 units remain. And a 1998 law, the Faircloth Amendment, put a statutory ceiling on the program's growth.

Let's test those three. A developer wants equity to build income-restricted apartments. A single mother wants help paying rent on a private-market unit she found near her job. Which program serves each? And which of the two might wait years for an answer? Think it through.

[long pause]

The developer wants the tax credit: credits sold to investors finance construction. The mother wants a voucher: aid that follows the tenant to a private unit.

And she is the one who waits. Vouchers are capped by appropriations, not by need. The tax credit is rationed too, because developers compete for credits. But that queue is measured in funding rounds. [beat] Not in years of a family's life.

## How short is the country?

One last stop: the shortage numbers.

That America is short some millions of homes is now a stock phrase, in speeches from both parties. The number ranges from under 4 million to over 7 million.

It is tempting to hear those as rival answers to one question. They are not.

> The shortage estimates are answers to different questions.

Three of the estimates cluster around 4 million. They come from Freddie Mac, from Up for Growth, and from Zillow. And they cluster because they measure roughly the same thing: the total stock of homes, against the total demand for them.

The fourth comes from the National Low Income Housing Coalition, and it is 7.2 million. It measures something else. Not whether enough homes exist. [beat] Whether the poorest renters can afford the ones that do.

By that count, for every 100 renter households with extremely low incomes, just 35 homes are both affordable and available.

Here is why the difference matters. A construction boom could close the first gap, while leaving most of the second. A market-rate apartment is available to an extremely low-income family only if rent assistance bridges the difference.

Put another way: one number asks whether the country has enough homes. The other asks whether its poorest renters can pay for them.

Now put the two measures to work. A politician says: "We're short 7 million homes, so we must build more." What has quietly gone wrong in that sentence? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The speaker has swapped one definition of shortage for another. That figure of 7 million does not measure whether enough homes exist. It measures whether the poorest renters can afford the ones that do. And a construction boom could leave most of that gap in place.

The swap runs the other way too. "We built plenty and people are still homeless, so building doesn't work." That speaker has changed definitions as well.

Catching that swap is half the housing debate.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Three layers of government, and a mismatch. Washington writes the checks. City hall decides whether anything gets built.

[beat]

Two. Zoning has had the Supreme Court's permission since 1926. In a rezoning, every stage is a place where someone can say no. And where rules make supply unresponsive, demand becomes price.

[beat]

Three. Washington stands behind the mortgage, through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and it subsidizes owning through the tax code. By dollars, federal housing policy is mostly a program for homeowners.

[beat]

Four. The renter programs are capped. Vouchers help pay the rent, the tax credit builds apartments, and public housing has a legal ceiling. About one in four eligible households gets federal rental help. The rest wait.

[beat]

And five. The shortage estimates are answers to different questions. The figures near 4 million set the stock of homes against demand. The figure of 7.2 million asks what the poorest renters can afford.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: why supply got constrained, whose interests the constraints serve, and what mix of building, subsidy and protection would fix it.

Three camps will each claim the machinery you have just heard. For now, each gets the same two sentences: what it argues is wrong, and what it argues is the cure.

Supply-side advocates, the zoning reformers, argue that the binding constraint is local regulation. The cure, they argue, is legalizing more housing where people want to live.

> The supply-side case: the binding constraint is local regulation.

Demand-side advocates, the camp of subsidy and assistance, argue that the market will never price the poorest in, however much gets built. The cure, they argue, is closing the assistance lottery, with vouchers and the tax credit at a scale that matches need.

> The demand-side case: the market will never price the poorest in.

Tenant-protection-led advocates argue that current tenants live in the years-long meantime. The cure, they argue, starts with rent stabilization, eviction defense, and tenant power.

> The tenant-protection case: current tenants live in the years-long meantime.

You may already lean toward one of the three. So take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

Keep your answer. The next lecture builds each case the way its smartest advocate would.

For now, go back to that first-time buyer, 40 years old, with the keys in hand. [beat] You can now say who decides whether homes get built, who finances them, and who gets help paying for them. Why it took until 40 is the argument still to come.
