Picture a timber town in southern Oregon. Under 40,000 people. Its name is Grants Pass.

In the 2010s, its city council decided to enforce its way out of visible homelessness. It passed ordinances that barred sleeping in public with so much as a blanket. The penalty was a civil fine of 295 dollars, which escalated if unpaid. Then came orders excluding people from the parks, and eventually criminal trespass charges, carrying up to 30 days in jail.

The town had no general homeless shelter to send people to.

[pause]

In June of 2024, the Supreme Court used that little city to answer a question every mayor in America was asking. We will come to its answer.

But Grants Pass is only one front. Since 2018, a dozen or so states have overridden local zoning. Congress has quietly passed the largest expansion of affordable-housing finance in decades. And the federal government has reversed its quarter-century posture on homelessness.

Last time, we heard three cases argued. This time, we map the field where those arguments are being run, as it stands in the middle of 2026. By the end, you should be able to say what the states have changed, what Washington has changed, and what the Court did and did not decide.

Three fronts, and then a look ahead. First, the statehouses, and a scoreboard. Second, Washington, and the money. Third, homelessness: the count, the ruling, and the pivot. And last, what comes next.

## The statehouses: a scoreboard

Last time, we met the homevoter: the reason that persuading one city at a time fails. Reformers drew a conclusion from that. Change which level of government decides.

Cities are creatures of state law. So a state can simply legalize duplexes itself. That move is called preemption.

Here is the scoreboard, limited to what has verifiably passed, and what has verifiably happened.

Start with Minneapolis. In 2018, it became the first major American city to end single-family-only zoning citywide. It also upzoned its corridors, and later ended parking minimums. Between 2017 and 2022, its housing stock grew 12 percent. Its rents rose 1 percent. [beat] In the rest of Minnesota, rents rose 14.

One caution on that result. The triplex provision itself produced about 1 percent of new permits. The corridors and the parking repeal did the heavy lifting.

Then Austin. It allowed three units per lot and cut its minimum lot size, alongside a permitting boom. From 2023 to 2024, rents in its large buildings fell 7 percent. That was the steepest drop recorded in any large American metro.

And then California, which ran two experiments at once.

The first is a law called SB 9, from 2021. It allowed two units, plus lot splits, on most single-family parcels. By the end of 2023, only 266 projects had been permitted or completed in the whole state.

The second is a series of laws on accessory dwelling units, or ADUs. They made those units approvable by right, statewide. In 2016, California saw 1,336 ADU permits. In 2023, nearly 27,000. [beat] More than 21 percent of all the homes the state permitted that year.

One state, two laws: a trickle from the first, and a boom from the second. The difference is process.

ADU approval is ministerial. That means no hearing, no discretion, and capped fees. SB 9 left cities room to smother projects in standards, and it left owners facing five-figure fees.

> Passing a law is not producing housing.

That is the first way to read the scoreboard. There are two more.

Litigation is the second battlefield. Montana, a red state, passed duplex and ADU laws in 2023, with bipartisan votes. A trial court blocked them, and in 2024 the Montana Supreme Court reversed that. Minneapolis spent two years fending off an environmental-review lawsuit that briefly enjoined its whole plan. Reformers won round one in court. But the playbook, suing under environmental and procedural statutes, is now standard.

And supply responses follow demand. Upzoning produces homes where people want to live, and where builders can finance them. That is why Austin's results arrived fastest, and why rural reforms will seem quiet for years.

Let's test the first reading. A state passes a law allowing fourplexes on any residential lot. But each project requires a public design-review hearing, and cities may set their own fees. Predict the outcome. Take a moment.

[long pause]

Expect numbers like SB 9's: trivially few projects. A discretionary hearing adds time, risk, and a veto point for opponents. Uncapped fees let a hostile city price projects out. California's ADU laws worked because they removed exactly those two levers.

So the operative question is never what a reform allows.

> Ask of a zoning reform: who can still say no? What can they charge?

## Washington: the money

Now to Washington, where the lever is money, and most of it moves through the tax code.

Start with the biggest change. On July 4, 2025, a sprawling tax law was enacted: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Inside it, almost unnoticed in the noise, was the largest expansion of the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit in decades.

It made two changes, both permanent. First, each state's ceiling on what are called 9 percent credits rises by 12 percent, starting in 2026.

Second, it cut something called the bond test. To unlock the other kind of credit, the 4 percent credit, a project used to need half of its costs financed by private-activity bonds. Now it needs a quarter. That stretches each state's limited bond cap across roughly twice as many projects.

Industry analysts project that the two changes could finance on the order of a million additional affordable rental homes, between 2026 and 2035.

A quick test. In 2027, a developer's affordable project will have 30 percent of its costs financed by those bonds. Does it qualify for the 4 percent credits?

[long pause]

Yes. The test fell from 50 percent to 25, so 30 clears it.

And notice the political lesson. In a polarized Congress, the coalition behind the credit, of developers, investors and state agencies, delivered. Flashier proposals died.

[pause]

Vouchers saw no comparable breakthrough. They remain annually appropriated, and they serve 2.3 million households, while roughly three in four eligible households go unassisted. Closing that gap would mean roughly quadrupling the program. That is a recurring progressive proposal, and it has no current legislative path. The near-term fights are over whether funding even keeps pace with rents.

Then there is a lever that keeps flipping. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 requires those who receive HUD grants to "affirmatively further" fair housing. What that means in regulation has now flipped four times.

A 2015 rule, under the Obama administration, required jurisdictions taking HUD money to analyze patterns of segregation and plan against them. The first Trump administration terminated it. The Biden administration restored an interim version. And in 2025, HUD terminated that, under a rule by which jurisdictions simply self-certify compliance.

So conditioning federal grants on local land-use behavior is a real lever. But it has yet to survive a change of administration.

[pause]

Last, mortgages. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are entering the eighteenth year of a conservatorship that was labeled temporary. And the 2025 tax law made the cap on the mortgage interest deduction permanent.

> In 2025, the homeowner side of the federal ledger was made more durable.

## Homelessness: the count, the ruling, the pivot

The third front. We take the count first, then the Court, then the politics.

HUD counts the people who are homeless on a single night each January. In January 2024, that count surged 18 percent. It reached a record: more than 770,000 people. The drivers were rents, the end of pandemic-era protections, and in some cities, migrant arrivals.

In January 2025, the count fell by 3.4 percent. It was the first decline since 2016. [beat] One good year is not yet a trend.

Why do the counts concentrate where they do? Gregg Colburn and Clayton Page Aldern ran the comparisons, in a book called Homelessness Is a Housing Problem. Across cities, rates of homelessness track rents and vacancy rates far better than they track local rates of poverty, mental illness, or addiction. West Virginia has more addiction, and far less homelessness, than the coastal cities.

The book's finding, in two sentences:

> Individual vulnerabilities decide who falls. Housing markets decide how many.

Now back to Grants Pass, and what the Court held.

On June 28, 2024, the Court ruled six to three, in an opinion by Justice Gorsuch. Enforcing generally applicable public-camping ordinances, against people with nowhere else to sleep, is not "cruel and unusual punishment." That discarded a precedent of the Ninth Circuit, which had barred such enforcement when shelter beds were unavailable.

Be exact about what the ruling did.

> Grants Pass removed a federal constitutional barrier. It mandated nothing.

Cities may now clear encampments with ordinary fines and trespass law. Whether doing so without housing alternatives reduces homelessness, or shuffles it, is exactly the question the opinion left to politics.

Let's test that. After Grants Pass, a city with one full shelter passes a camping ban, and begins issuing fines. A resident says the policy is now "constitutionally settled and empirically settled." What is right in that sentence? And what is wrong? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Constitutionally, the resident is mostly right, as to the Eighth Amendment. The Court held that such enforcement is not cruel and unusual punishment. State constitutions and other doctrines can still be litigated.

Empirically, nothing is settled at all. The ruling said that cities may enforce. It did not say that enforcement reduces homelessness. Does enforcement without housing alternatives shrink the problem, or relocate it? That question remains open and contested, which is why the fight moved from courtrooms to budgets.

[pause]

Politics answered quickly, with a federal pivot. On July 24, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order, titled "Ending Crime and Disorder on America's Streets."

The order directs agencies to do three things. To encourage civil commitment of unsheltered people with serious mental illness. To prioritize grants for jurisdictions that enforce bans on camping and open drug use. And to end federal support for Housing First.

Housing First had been the consensus model since the 2000s. It provides permanent housing, without preconditions of sobriety or treatment.

So what does the evidence on it say? Take it the steel-man way, at full strength in both directions.

Randomized and longitudinal studies consistently show that Housing First outperforms treatment-first models at getting and keeping people housed. That is the outcome it targets.

The evidence that it resolves addiction or psychiatric illness by itself, or that it reliably saves public money, is much weaker.

> Housing First outperforms at keeping people housed. Beyond that, the evidence is much weaker.

That gap is the ground both camps now argue from.

Critics of the pivot also note a fiscal irony. Conditioning aid on treatment requires treatment capacity. And that capacity, like permanent supportive housing, mostly does not exist at scale.

The states are running their own versions of the argument. California is home to roughly a quarter of the nation's homeless population. Across five years, it spent about 24 billion dollars on homelessness programs. And its state audit, in 2024, found that the state had not consistently tracked what the money accomplished. Red and purple states, after Grants Pass, have multiplied camping bans.

[pause]

Under all of it sits one structural fact. Housing First, treatment first, enforcement first.

> Every pathway ends in the same shortage of units and beds.

You may already lean toward one of those three pathways. So take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## What comes next

Now the look ahead. Each of last time's three cases has something to watch, and so does homelessness.

For the supply-side case, watch for more preemption, on a redder map. Montana's court victory and bipartisan coalition made it the template for conservative states. Expect more laws on duplexes, ADUs and lot sizes, framed as property rights and deregulation. Blue states, meanwhile, iterate on enforcement against resistant cities.

For the demand-side case, watch two things. One is whether the tax credit's projected surge of units materializes, or gets eaten by costs. The other is voucher funding under strain. With rents elevated, flat funding quietly shrinks the number of families served. It is the least visible, highest-stakes line item in housing.

For the tenant-protection case, watch for diffusion. Rent caps in the style of Oregon and California, just-cause statutes, and right-to-counsel programs continue spreading, city by city and state by state. Alongside them, New Jersey's Mount Laurel obligations, which bind its suburban towns, are testing whether mandates can compel affordable construction at scale.

And on homelessness, the executive order nationalized what had been a city-hall fight. Expect funding conditions, encampment policy, and the fate of Housing First to feature in the election cycles of 2026 and 2028.

## What to keep

One reach back, before we gather up. Early on, I gave you two questions to ask of any zoning reform. Can you still say them?

Who can still say no? And what can they charge?

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. States are overriding local zoning. Minneapolis and Austin built, and rents rose 1 percent in the first and fell in the second. California's SB 9 produced a trickle. Passing a law is not producing housing.

[beat]

Two. In Washington, the money moves mostly through the tax code. In 2025, Congress expanded the housing tax credit, and vouchers saw no comparable breakthrough. The homeowner side of the federal ledger was made more durable.

[beat]

Three. Individual vulnerabilities decide who falls. Housing markets decide how many.

[beat]

Four. Grants Pass removed a federal constitutional barrier. It mandated nothing. Whether enforcement reduces homelessness is still an open question.

[beat]

And five. Washington has moved to end its support for Housing First. Housing First outperforms at keeping people housed. Beyond that, the evidence is much weaker. And every pathway ends in the same shortage of units and beds.

[pause]

This is the last lecture of the series, so there is no next one. The argument goes to you. And a defensible position on American housing policy requires four commitments.

A view on whether the binding constraint is supply, demand-side assistance, or tenant power.

A view on which level of government should decide. And whether you hold that view consistently, when your side loses a statehouse.

A view on what to do about the people who are unsheltered tonight, knowing both the Housing First evidence and its limits.

And an honest account of what evidence would change your mind. [beat] Nobody gets to skip that one.

When you are back at a screen, you can declare a position of your own, and run a steel-man drill against the perspective you find least persuasive.

For now, go back to Grants Pass. A town of under 40,000 people, with no general shelter, and a fine of 295 dollars. [beat] The Court said what that town may do. Whether it works, the Court left to politics. And that argument is now yours.
