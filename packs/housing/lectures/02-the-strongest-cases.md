Picture a vacant lot. A quarter of an acre of residential land, ready to build on. No walls, no foundation. Just dirt.

How much of that lot's price is the land? [beat] And how much is the law?

Two economists, Joseph Gyourko and Jacob Krimmel, found a way to measure it. They assembled data on actual sales of vacant residential land. And they computed what they call the zoning tax: how much regulation inflates the price of a buildable quarter-acre, above what an unrestricted market would charge.

In most American metros, the zoning tax is near zero.

In the San Francisco metro, it is roughly 400,000 dollars. [beat] For the lot alone. House not included.

[pause]

Everyone in the housing debate agrees that a number like that is obscene.

> The camps agree on the numbers. They argue about what the numbers mean.

And about what to do.

Last time, we covered the machine. This time, the argument, staged as three cases. Each is built the way its smartest advocate would build it. By the end, you should be able to state all three in their own terms, and to say what the evidence settles among them, and what it cannot.

We will take it in four steps. First, the supply-side case. Second, the demand-side case. Third, the tenant-protection-led case. Each gets the same treatment: where it starts, the three arguments that carry it, and what evidence would change it.

And last, the scorecard: what is settled, and what is contested.

## The supply-side case

Start with the supply-side advocate, the zoning reformer.

This case starts from an idea called spatial equilibrium, owed to Sherwin Rosen and Jennifer Roback. In plain English: people can move, so places must balance their books. A city with high wages and good weather cannot stay a bargain. People pile in, until housing costs eat the advantage.

The books can balance in two ways. If the city lets builders respond, the inflow becomes more residents at moderate prices, and the city grows. If it does not, the inflow becomes higher prices at a fixed population. Access to the city's wages gets rationed by rent, and the gain is captured by whoever already owns land there.

The zoning tax, on this view, is what that capture looks like in a ledger.

> The supply-side case: high prices in superstar metros are a legal artifact.

A legal artifact. Not a natural fact.

Three arguments carry it.

First, supply responds where reforms bind. Places that legalized and streamlined housing built it, and their rents behaved differently from their peers'. The next lecture walks that scoreboard.

Second, filtering. The skeptic says that luxury towers cannot help poor renters. This case answers that homes are not consumed once, and it points to a study by Evan Mast. He took 686 new market-rate buildings, found the people who moved in, then found who moved into their old homes, and so on down the chain.

His finding: building 100 new market-rate units leads 45 to 70 people to move out of below-median-income neighborhoods. Each move opens a home behind it, and almost all of this happens within about five years.

Over decades, this case argues, filtering is where almost all unsubsidized affordable housing comes from. Today's cheap apartment is a building someone built at market rate in 1968.

Third, a claim about the whole economy, and this one is handled with tongs. For years this case carried a trump card: a paper by Hsieh and Moretti. It estimated that relaxing regulation in just three metros would lift GDP by 3.7 percent.

Then came replication. In 2026, a published comment by Brian Greaney documented coding errors. In his repaired version the gain is real, but roughly two orders of magnitude smaller: on the order of 0.02 percent. The authors have publicly acknowledged error.

[pause]

So the honest supply-sider stops citing the headline number, and rests the case on evidence at smaller scale: the zoning tax, and the filtering chains. That evidence emerged from the episode intact.

And why would a city choose rules that make housing scarce? This case points to William Fischel, and his homevoter hypothesis. For most homeowners, the house is the largest asset they will ever own, and they cannot insure it against neighborhood change. So they vote, reliably, against anything that puts its value at even a small risk. That is why supply-siders increasingly aim reform at the state level, where renters and would-be residents count too.

[pause]

What would change this position? A demonstration that filtering chains break down at scale, so that new building raises nearby rents and does not relieve them. Or evidence that the restrictions mostly price in genuine public goods, and not the protection of incumbents.

And its strongest advocates concede that filtering cannot reach extremely low-income households without subsidy. Their claim is that supply is necessary. [beat] Not that it is sufficient.

Let's test this case. A council member looks at a proposed tower, where every unit rents for 3,000 dollars a month, and says that approving it does nothing for working families.

Using Mast's evidence on chains of moves, what is the strongest factual rebuttal? And what part of the concern survives it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The rebuttal: the tower's tenants come from somewhere, and each move frees an older, cheaper unit behind it. Per 100 new units, 45 to 70 people move out of below-median-income neighborhoods.

The surviving concern: the chain takes years, and it rarely reaches extremely low-income households. Their gap is closed by assistance, not by construction alone.

Both can be true. And that is why the camps' best versions overlap more than their rallies suggest.

## The demand-side case

Now the demand-side advocate, of the subsidy-and-assistance camp. This case is built the same way.

It starts by granting most of the supply economics. Then it asks the question that economics leaves open. What happens, this year, to the family at the 20th income percentile?

Filtering takes half a decade to deliver its discounts. A voucher delivers affordability the month it is issued. And vouchers, as we saw last time, are a lottery: about 1 in 4 eligible households is served. So on this view, the highest-yield housing policy is closing that gap, before relitigating anyone's zoning code.

> The demand-side case: a voucher works the month it is issued.

Three arguments carry it.

First, the low-income rental market is not the textbook market. This case points to a study by the Urban Institute, for HUD, of how landlords respond to voucher holders. In Fort Worth, 78 percent of the landlords contacted refused them outright. In Newark, and in Washington, D.C., laws against source-of-income discrimination have teeth, and refusals ran far lower: in Washington, 15 percent. This case takes that contrast as proof that enforcement, not exhortation, moves the number.

And refusals ran highest in low-poverty neighborhoods, exactly where the program's promise of mobility points.

Second, design decides whether subsidy money reaches tenants or landlords. Here this case points to Robert Collinson and Peter Ganong. They studied what happens when the payment standard, the ceiling on what a voucher pays, is made more generous.

Raise it across an entire metro, and landlords capture much of the increase as rent, with little gain in housing quality. Tie it to rents in each ZIP code, generous in expensive neighborhoods and lean in cheap ones, and something else happens. Voucher families relocate to measurably better neighborhoods, at little extra cost.

The wider point: where supply is constrained, demand subsidies leak into prices. So the smart demand-sider is also a supply reformer. And vice versa.

Third, some housing will never pencil without subsidy. No developer can build new units that rent at a level a minimum-wage worker can pay. The math fails before the zoning hearing starts. That housing exists only through the tax credit, public housing, and vouchers.

And the money exists, this case argues: homeowner tax subsidies run about 145 billion dollars a year. On this view, it is aimed at the wrong end of the income distribution.

Behind those three stands the evidence this case leans on most. It points to Matthew Desmond, and his book Evicted. In Milwaukee, he found that more than 1 in 8 renters experienced a forced move, across the years 2009 to 2011. A majority of poor renting families spent over half their income on housing. And he argues that eviction is not merely a symptom of poverty. It is a cause of it, wrecking jobs, schooling, credit and mental health. So housing assistance, on his argument, is anti-poverty policy, and not just shelter policy.

[pause]

What would change this position? Strong evidence that subsidies mostly inflate rents, at scale. Or evidence that expanded assistance erodes work incentives enough to swamp its benefits.

Its advocates answer the first with design. And they treat the second as an empirical question, one that the eviction research mostly resolves in their favor.

Let's test this case. A city wants its voucher families in higher-opportunity neighborhoods. So it raises voucher payment standards by 20 percent, citywide. What is the likely result? And what design would work better? Think it through.

[long pause]

The likely result: landlords across the city pocket much of the increase as higher rents, with little improvement in where voucher families actually live.

The better design: payment standards tiered by ZIP code, with higher ceilings only in expensive, high-opportunity neighborhoods. [beat] Same dollars. Different geometry.

## The tenant-protection-led case

And the tenant-protection-led advocate. This case is built the same way.

It starts where, on its view, the other two camps wave their hands: the transition. Supply works over years, even decades. Voucher expansion is an annual appropriations fight. The family facing a 300-dollar rent hike, or a no-fault eviction, lives in the meantime. And policy for the meantime is what tenant protection is.

> The tenant-protection case: others legislate for the future. Tenants live in the meantime.

Three arguments carry it.

First, rent stabilization, with eyes open. The strongest tenant advocates do not dodge the canonical study. They cite it precisely. Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade and Franklin Qian studied San Francisco's expansion of rent control in 1994, and they found both effects at once.

Covered tenants were far likelier to stay in their homes: their mobility fell by about 20 percent. And affected landlords cut their supply of rental housing by 15 percent, converting to condos and redeveloping. Over time, that raised rents citywide by about 5.1 percent.

This case takes that as design guidance, not as refutation. Cap increases, and do not freeze rents. Exempt new construction, so that building still pencils. Limit the conversion loopholes. That is the architecture of the modern statutes in Oregon, which passed the first statewide cap, and in California. Both exempt buildings less than 15 years old. On this view, they are anti-gouging laws, and not the hard rent freezes of textbook lore.

Second, eviction defense changes outcomes at the courthouse. Most landlords arrive at eviction court with lawyers. Most tenants, historically, arrived alone. New York City's right-to-counsel law, the nation's first, put attorneys on the tenant side. In recent city data, more than four in five represented tenants facing eviction remained in their homes, though the program's reach has slipped as funding lagged.

Just-cause statutes aim at the same target. They require listed grounds for refusing to renew a lease. The aim is to turn a tenancy from a month-to-month sufferance into a defensible legal status.

Third, power, not just price. At the low end, this case argues, rental markets run on asymmetry. The landlord knows the law, repeats the game, and can absorb a vacancy. The tenant often has none of the three.

This case, too, points to Desmond. He documented rents in Milwaukee's poorest neighborhoods running nearly as high as in middle-class ones, while the buildings cost far less. Its answer is organizing: tenant unions, such as KC Tenants in Kansas City, that negotiate and legislate as a bloc. The market at the bottom, on this view, is a bargaining table with one chair.

[pause]

What would change this position? Evidence that protections at scale raise rents or screening barriers for future tenants, by more than they protect current ones. Or evidence that just-cause rules and counsel mainly delay displacement, and do not prevent it.

And its serious advocates accept the supply-siders' decades and the demand-siders' dollars. They insist that both are incomplete without rights.

Let's test this case. Two proposals. One is a hard cap that holds all rents flat, in all buildings, forever. The other is a cap of inflation plus 7 percent, which exempts buildings under 15 years old. Using the San Francisco study, why might a tenant advocate prefer the second? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because that study showed how landlords escaped rent control: they removed units from the rental market. The hard cap maximizes that incentive to exit, and it tells builders never to build rentals again.

The second proposal targets gouging, and leaves normal increases and new construction untouched. It protects incumbents at a much smaller cost to future tenants. [beat] It trades away some protection to remain durable.

## Settled, and contested

Now the scorecard. First, one reach back. At the very start, I gave you a vacant quarter-acre in the San Francisco metro. How much did regulation add to its price? And what was that amount called?

[long pause]

Roughly 400,000 dollars. The zoning tax. And that finding heads the list of what is settled.

Settled, or close to it. Four findings.

Restrictive regulation raises prices. The zoning tax is measurable in land sales.

New market-rate supply filters down, and moderates nearby rents.

Voucher discrimination is widespread where it is legal, and voucher design governs who captures the subsidy.

And rent control protects incumbents, while shrinking rental supply.

[pause]

Genuinely contested. Four questions.

The size of the cost to the whole economy, after Greaney's correction.

How far down, and how fast, filtering reaches in the tightest markets.

Whether modern stabilization, capped and with exemptions, avoids the classic supply damage at scale.

And the central question of allocation.

> Build, subsidize, or protect first? The evidence narrows that question. It does not answer it.

It does not, because that is partly a question about whose years count.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The camps agree on the numbers. They argue about what the numbers mean. In the San Francisco metro, the zoning tax on a quarter-acre lot is roughly 400,000 dollars.

[beat]

Two. The supply-side case: high prices in superstar metros are a legal artifact. It points to the zoning tax, and to the chains of moves that a new building sets off. It would change on evidence that new building raises nearby rents.

[beat]

Three. The demand-side case: a voucher works the month it is issued. It points to landlords refusing vouchers where the law lets them, and to design, which decides whether subsidy reaches tenants or landlords. It would change on evidence that even well-designed assistance mostly inflates rents at scale.

[beat]

Four. The tenant-protection case: others legislate for the future. Tenants live in the meantime. It points to the San Francisco study, taken as design guidance, and to counsel at eviction court. It would change on evidence that modern protections cost future tenants more than they protect current ones.

[beat]

And five. Four findings are settled, or close to it. Regulation raises prices. New supply filters down. Voucher design governs who captures the subsidy. And rent control protects incumbents while shrinking supply. But build, subsidize, or protect first? The evidence narrows that question. It does not answer it.

[pause]

So this lecture gives no verdict among the three. A defensible position picks a frame, cites the studies that survive scrutiny, and names what would change its mind.

The next lecture turns to the field where these arguments are now being run live: statehouses, Congress, and the courts.

For now, go back to that quarter-acre of dirt. Roughly 400,000 dollars of law in its price, and a number that every camp accepts. [beat] What divides them is, in part, a question about whose years count.
