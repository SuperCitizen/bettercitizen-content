Ohio, in the three years from March 2017 to March 2020. A utility called FirstEnergy, with its affiliates, routed roughly 60 million dollars to a nonprofit called Generation Now.

The nonprofit spent it. It elected allies of the Ohio House Speaker, Larry Householder. It defended them. And then it crushed a ballot referendum.

All of that served one bill: House Bill 6, a bailout of two nuclear plants, funded by ratepayers. The price was 1.3 billion dollars.

[pause]

Ohio voters saw the ads. What they could not see, anywhere, was who paid for them.

Why not? Because Generation Now was organized under section 501(c)(4) of the tax code, and an organization of that kind owes the public no donor list.

Federal prosecutors called it likely the largest bribery scheme in Ohio history. In 2023, Householder was sentenced to 20 years for racketeering.

Now here is the detail to sit with.

> The bribery was the crime. The secrecy was not.

Every dollar of anonymity in that scheme used legal architecture, available to anyone. [beat] That architecture is our subject.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to follow a dollar from a donor's check to a campaign ad, and say exactly where the donor's name falls away.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the term itself: what dark money means, and what it does not.

Second, the vehicles: four kinds of organization, and the one rule that sorts them.

Third, the pipeline, hop by hop.

And last, the measure of it: how much money, and how much law.

## The term is narrower than the outrage

The phrase "dark money" gets thrown at everything, from corporate PACs to lobbying. So we start with a strict definition, because in campaign-finance scholarship the term means something specific.

> Dark money is election spending whose original source is concealed from the public.

Take it a piece at a time. Spending intended to influence elections. [beat] An original source. [beat] Concealed from the public.

The classic vehicles are two kinds of tax-exempt group. One is the 501(c)(4), which the tax code calls a social welfare organization. The other is the 501(c)(6), a trade association. Both may engage in electoral activity, within limits we will come to, and neither is required to disclose its donors.

Now three near-misses, because a definition is easiest to hold by what it leaves out.

First. Dark money is not just outside money. A super PAC is outside money, and it is not dark by default, because a super PAC must itemize its contributors to the Federal Election Commission, the FEC.

Second. Dark money is not just big money. A 5 dollar donation to a social welfare organization is dark. A 50 million dollar check to a super PAC, from a named billionaire, is not.

[beat]

The size of the check is not the test. The visibility of the source is.

And third, the line blurs, in one direction. Suppose a nonprofit that names no donors gives to a super PAC, or an opaque shell LLC does. The super PAC discloses a name, as it must. [beat] But the name is a dead end.

Researchers call that gray money. Hold on to the term, because gray money is now the dominant pattern.

## Four vehicles, one rule

So who are the players? Here is the first surprise: the tax code defines them, not election law.

Four kinds of organization do most of the work, and their differences are the whole game. For each one, ask the same question. Must it name its donors to the public?

Start with the charity, the 501(c)(3). Donations to it are tax-deductible. But a rule from 1954, the Johnson Amendment, bars charities from intervening in candidate campaigns at all, and that includes churches. So the charity is not the electoral vehicle of choice.

Next, the 527 political organization, the category that includes parties and PACs. Electioneering is its purpose, and a 527 must name its contributors.

Congress imposed that duty in 2000, after a wave of anonymous spending by 527 groups on what were called issue ads. That law worked. It also taught political operatives a durable lesson.

> The disclosure obligation follows the tax classification.

Put another way: the duty to name donors attaches to the kind of entity, not to the kind of activity. Reclassify the same activity as social welfare, and the obligation evaporates.

Which brings us back to the social welfare organization, and to its cousin, the trade association. Donations to them are not deductible. They may electioneer, on one condition: it cannot be the group's primary activity. And they owe the public no donor list.

[pause]

Everything hangs on that word, primary. So where does it come from?

The statute says these organizations must operate "exclusively for the promotion of social welfare." Exclusively. Then a Treasury regulation, in 1959, softened that word to "primarily engaged." And since then, neither Congress nor the IRS has ever defined what primarily means.

Practitioners operate on a folk rule: keep electoral activity under 50 percent of spending, and the exemption is safe. But that number appears in no statute and no regulation.

The vagueness is not an oversight that nobody noticed. It is a vacuum that proved impossible to fill.

In 2013, the IRS proposed a rule to define political activity for these groups. It drew more than 150,000 public comments, a record for an IRS rulemaking. Reformers thought it too weak. Conservative groups saw it as a censorship tool.

The agency retreated in May 2014. And since fiscal year 2016, Congress has attached riders to its appropriations bills that forbid the IRS from finalizing any such rule.

So the folk rule governs, because nothing else does.

One more piece, and it is recent. Under Treasury regulations finalized in 2020, these groups no longer report donor names even to the IRS. Only amounts.

Before 2020, at least one government agency knew who the donors were. Now, in the ordinary course, none does.

Let's test what we have. A donor network wants to spend 40 million dollars influencing Senate races, without any public donor trail. It can organize its spending vehicle as a 527, or as a 501(c)(4).

Which does it choose? And what constraint does that choice impose? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It chooses the social welfare organization. A 527 must publicly disclose its contributors, so it cannot deliver anonymity. The social welfare organization owes the public no donor list.

But there is a price. Electoral work cannot be that organization's primary activity. Under the folk rule, it must keep its reported political spending under roughly half its budget, and document enough social welfare activity to cover the rest.

The disclosure obligation follows the tax classification. [beat] And so does the constraint.

## Where disclosure dies

Those are the vehicles. Now assemble the machine.

The classic dark-money structure is a relay. Each hop is individually legal, and each hop is individually reported. And the original source disappears at the first handoff.

Walk it with me, and watch what the public record shows at each hop.

Hop one: the original donor. An individual, a corporation or a union writes the check. It goes to a nonprofit, not to any committee that reports to the FEC. What does the public see at this hop? [beat] Nothing.

Hop two: the nonprofit, a social welfare organization. It owes the public no donor list, and it reports amounts, not names, even to the IRS. From here the money can hop among several such nonprofits, or through a trade association, before it moves on. Each added hop is another dead end.

Hop three: the super PAC. It itemizes its contributors to the FEC, exactly as the law requires. But the line on its report gives only the name of a nonprofit, and a figure: 10 million dollars. A truthful dead end. That is the gray money.

And last, the political ad. It ends with the words "Paid for by Americans for a Better Tomorrow."

[pause]

Notice what the relay accomplishes. No hop breaks a law. No report contains a falsehood.

> Every filing in the chain is accurate. The original source is gone.

There is a variant, and it produced the founding scandal of the genre. The donor forms a shell LLC, which can give straight to a super PAC and then dissolve.

In March 2011, a Delaware entity called W Spann LLC was formed. In April, it gave 1 million dollars to Restore Our Future, the super PAC supporting Mitt Romney. In July, it dissolved.

[beat]

A corporate lifespan of about four months. Exactly long enough to write one check.

Only after watchdog complaints did a former Bain Capital executive, Edward Conard, come forward and ask the super PAC to name him in its filings. His million became visible because he volunteered. The structure that hid it remains available.

Now put the machine to work. You pull a super PAC's FEC report, and on it you find a contribution of 7 million dollars. It comes from a social welfare organization called Heartland Prosperity Alliance.

What do you now know about the original source of that money? And earlier I gave you a name for a disclosed donor that leads nowhere. Can you call it back? Take a moment.

[long pause]

You know the legal identity of the intermediary. About the original source, you know nothing.

The nonprofit's public filing will show its total revenue and spending, a year or more late. It will not show donor names.

So the trail ends there, with three exceptions. A donor comes forward. A leak or a lawsuit pries the records open. Or a state law, like Arizona's Proposition 211, forces the tracing.

And the name for that dead end? [beat] Gray money.

## How much money is dark

So how much money moves this way? Measuring concealed spending is measuring a shadow, so respect the method. Two very different numbers are in circulation.

The first number is the spending that dark-money groups themselves report to the FEC. By that measure, dark money peaked in 2012, at about 309 million dollars. Then it nearly vanished. By 2022, the figure was under 25 million.

Here is a question, then. A friend sees those two figures and concludes that the dark-money era is ending. You know how the pipeline works. What is wrong with the inference? Think it through.

[long pause]

That first number measures only direct spending that the groups report themselves. And the dominant strategy shifted. Rather than buying ads in their own names, these groups now mostly transfer money to super PACs. They also buy online ads, and television issue ads, that fall outside the FEC's reporting categories.

The second number tracks those flows. The Brennan Center and OpenSecrets assemble it from FEC reports, television ad tracking, and platform ad libraries. It tells the opposite story.

In 2024, on that fuller estimate, dark money set a record: roughly 1.9 billion dollars. That is up from about 1 billion in 2020.

And of the 2024 total, about 1.3 billion was money transferred to super PACs.

[pause]

The reported number fell because the concealment improved. It did not fall because the activity declined.

Even the larger figure is a floor. The Brennan analysis is explicit that it "necessarily, and perhaps substantially, underestimates the true scale," since some categories of undisclosed spending cannot be tracked at all.

So keep one habit from this section. Always ask what a campaign-finance statistic actually counts.

One more verified fact, and it should discipline anyone's partisan instincts. In 2024, dark money predominantly benefited Democrats. Roughly 1.2 billion dollars was aligned with Democratic causes. About 664 million was aligned with Republican ones.

Both coalitions built the machine. Both run it.

## Where the law stands

That is the money. Now the law, which rests on three load-bearing facts. They point in different directions.

Fact one. Disclosure of election spending is constitutionally secure, in principle.

You may have heard that Citizens United created dark money. Not quite. That 2010 decision freed corporations, nonprofit corporations included, to spend their treasury funds on express electoral advocacy. That made the social welfare form newly useful for election work. But the disclosure gap itself comes from the tax code, and from FEC regulations written decades earlier.

And the Court's majority in Citizens United endorsed disclosure, eight to one, with only Justice Thomas dissenting on that portion. Here is the reason it gave: "this transparency enables the electorate to make informed decisions and give proper weight to different speakers and messages."

[pause]

Informed decisions, and proper weight. Eight justices endorsed that premise. The gap between the premise and the regulatory reality is the central irony of the system since 2010.

Fact two. The regulations under that secure authority are full of holes. Some were patched recently. Most were not.

Take one that was not. For broadcast ads that name a candidate in the weeks before an election, FEC rules require naming only the donors who gave 1,000 dollars or more "for the purpose of furthering" such ads. Almost no donor ever states that purpose, which is why groups routinely report zero donors.

Fact three. A countervailing doctrine is rising. In 2021, in Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, the Supreme Court struck down a California rule, six to three. The rule was a blanket demand that charities hand their major-donor lists to the state attorney general.

Bonta concerned charity regulation, not election law, and the majority took pains not to disturb the precedents on electoral disclosure. But its logic is the strongest doctrinal resource the donor-privacy side has had since 1958, and both camps know it. What happened in 1958 is where the next lecture begins.

Put the three facts together, and one thing follows.

> The dark-money gap is regulatory and statutory, not constitutional.

In plainer words: Congress could mandate disclosure of these electoral donors tomorrow, without offending current doctrine. A bill called the DISCLOSE Act has proposed exactly that since 2010. Whether Congress should, and how Bonta might reshape the doctrine, is where honest people divide.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Dark money is election spending whose original source is concealed from the public. It is not all outside money, and it is not all big money. A super PAC itemizes its donors. The classic dark vehicles, the social welfare organization and the trade association, do not.

[beat]

Two. The disclosure obligation follows the tax classification. A charity may not electioneer at all. A 527 must name its contributors. A 501(c)(4) may electioneer, if that is not its primary activity, and it owes the public no donor list. And neither Congress nor the IRS has ever defined primarily.

[beat]

Three. The pipeline: donor, to nonprofit, to super PAC, to ad. Disclosure dies at the first hop. Every filing in the chain is accurate, and the original source is gone.

[beat]

Four. The numbers. What the groups report themselves fell to under 25 million dollars by 2022. The fuller estimate reached about 1.9 billion in 2024, and even that is a floor. The reported number fell because the concealment improved.

[beat]

And five. The law. The dark-money gap is regulatory and statutory, not constitutional. Disclosure is secure in principle, porous in the regulations, and newly contested under Bonta.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It was the plumbing, not the verdict. Knowing how the pipeline works tells you nothing, taken alone, about whether the system is a scandal or a shield.

Three kinds of people study the same machine, and each one sees something different in it.

The disclosure-first reformer looks at Generation Now.

> To the reformer, Generation Now is the inevitable product of structural secrecy.

The donor-privacy advocate looks at Alabama in the NAACP era, and at modern doxxing.

> To the privacy advocate, anonymous association is constitutionally sacred.

And the structural-reform centrist looks at both of them.

> To the centrist, reformer and privacy advocate are both right, about different donors.

The next lecture makes each of those three cases at full strength. Before it does, try one yourself. Choose the view you find least convincing, and build the best case for it that you can. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

Keep your version. The next lecture sets it beside the one its smartest advocate would make. Its whole subject is a collision: transparency's settled constitutional pedigree, against the new momentum of associational privacy.

For now, go back to Ohio. Sixty million dollars. A bailout. A sentence of twenty years. [beat] The bribery was the crime. The secrecy was not. And you can now say, hop by hop, how that secrecy was built.
