The spring of 1956. Alabama's attorney general, John Patterson, goes to court to expel the NAACP from his state. And as part of the suit, he demands the organization's complete membership list.

Everyone understood what the list was for. An NAACP member, named in 1950s Alabama, faced firing, foreclosure, and worse. The organization had watched it happen.

So the NAACP produced every record demanded. [beat] Except the names. It took a contempt fine of 100,000 dollars, and it fought to the Supreme Court.

In 1958, in NAACP v. Alabama, a unanimous Court held that the Constitution protected the secrecy of the list. Justice Harlan wrote for the Court. Listen for one word: indispensable.

Privacy in group association, he wrote, "may in many circumstances be indispensable to preservation of freedom of association, particularly where a group espouses dissident beliefs."

[pause]

Indispensable to freedom of association.

Sixty-eight years later, one thing is still true.

> Every argument about dark money is, underneath, an argument about NAACP v. Alabama.

The argument is about a donor to a politically active nonprofit, in a polarized America. One side sees the heir of an NAACP member in Patterson's Alabama. The other sees something else entirely.

Last time, the plumbing. This time, the argument. By the end, you should be able to state each of three positions the way its smartest advocate would, and to say what is settled and what is still contested.

Four stops. First, the case law, which both sides share. Then three positions, each at full strength: the disclosure-first reformer, the donor-privacy advocate, and the structural-reform centrist.

Each one gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, the evidence it points to, the objection it has to answer, and what would change its holder's mind.

## One Court, two lines of cases

What makes this debate unusually honest is one fact.

> Both sides argue from the same precedents.

Lay those precedents out, and they fall into two lines.

The first line is about elections. In 1976, in Buckley v. Valeo, the Court upheld the federal rules that require disclosure of campaign contributions and spending. And in 2010, in Citizens United, it upheld disclosure rules for electoral ads, eight to one.

The second line is about association. It starts in 1958, with NAACP v. Alabama: a membership list, demanded by a state government, and a Court that said no, nine to nothing. It reaches 2021, and a case called Bonta. California's attorney general had demanded the major-donor lists of charities. The Court struck that demand down on its face, six to three.

Bonta also set a test for compelled disclosure of donors: exacting scrutiny, with a requirement of narrow tailoring. In plainer words, a blanket demand will not do.

[pause]

And there is a bridge. When Buckley upheld campaign disclosure, it left an exemption, for any group that could show a "reasonable probability" of "threats, harassment, or reprisals." In 1982, in Brown v. Socialist Workers, the Court granted that exemption to a harassed minor party.

So here is the pattern. The Court has upheld every electoral disclosure regime it has squarely reviewed. It has struck down compelled exposure of members and donors outside the electoral context. And it has kept a harassment exemption alive between them.

The whole modern fight is over which line one person belongs to: the electoral donor to a social welfare nonprofit.

Reformers say this is Buckley and Citizens United territory. It is money spent to elect candidates.

Privacy advocates say a donor to a multi-purpose advocacy nonprofit is an associate, not a campaign contributor. This is NAACP and Bonta territory.

> Campaign contributor, or associate: the fight is over which one the nonprofit's donor is.

Neither side is making the frame up.

Let's test the two lines. Here are two demands for names.

In the first, a state tells every nonprofit that solicits donations there to file its full donor list with the consumer-protection bureau. The stated purpose is to deter charity fraud.

In the second, a federal bill covers groups that spend over 10,000 dollars on election ads. It requires them to name the donors, above that same amount, who funded the ads.

Which precedents govern which demand? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first is Bonta, on its facts. It is a blanket dragnet to a regulator, with no tie to elections. A demand like that now faces exacting scrutiny, and California's version failed on its face.

The second belongs to the Buckley and Citizens United line. It is targeted disclosure of electoral spending, the kind the Court has repeatedly upheld. And the harassment exemption stays available to any group that can show a reasonable probability of reprisals.

So the constitutional fate of a disclosure rule turns on which line it falls into. That is why each side fights so hard over the framing.

## The disclosure-first reformer

Begin with the disclosure-first reformer. What the reformer is protecting is the voter's information: the power to see who is behind a message, and to weigh it.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Citizens United was a bargain. The majority deregulated independent spending on an explicit premise: that "prompt disclosure" would let citizens "see whether elected officials are 'in the pocket' of so-called moneyed interests."

Eight justices signed that. [beat] And the nonprofit pipeline, the machine from the last lecture, voids the premise while keeping the deregulation.

So closing the pipeline is not a constitutional revolution. It is collecting on the Court's own promissory note.

> The reformer's case: Citizens United was a bargain, and only one side was delivered.

Now the evidence the reformer points to. Three things.

First, the informational interest is doing real work. Voters use the source of a message the way they use a party label. An ad from "Citizens for a Stronger Tomorrow" is processed differently once you know the funder is a pharmaceutical trade association.

So concealment, the reformer argues, is not a side effect. It is the point of the structure. The industry buys the credibility of a name it could never run under.

[beat]

Second, the chilling effect, measured, is small. The standard privacy objection is that disclosure deters giving. The best empirical test is a 2016 study by Wood and Spencer, which used the variation in state disclosure rules.

It found the deterrence negligible: on the order of less than one lost donor per candidate, when contributions become more publicly visible. Donors, the reformer argues, mostly do not behave like dissidents in Patterson's Alabama, because mostly they are not.

[beat]

Third, the networks are real. Jane Mayer's book Dark Money documented a small circle of donors building an integrated, deliberately opaque apparatus around a long-term policy program. One need not share her politics, the reformer argues, because later data confirmed the structure across the spectrum.

The reformer's name for these networks is shadow parties. Democracies regulate the money of parties in the open. Shadow parties currently answer to no one.

[pause]

Then the objection the reformer has to answer, which is Bonta. The answer: Bonta is a case about a charity regulator's filing-cabinet dragnet, not about elections. Chief Justice Roberts's opinion left the electoral-disclosure cases untouched. And the Buckley exemption already protects genuinely endangered groups, case by case.

And what would change the reformer's mind? Rigorous evidence that retaliation driven by disclosure operates at scale, on ordinary donors. Not anecdotes. Records like the one in Brown, multiplying across the ideological spectrum.

## The donor-privacy advocate

Now the donor-privacy advocate, built the same way. What this advocate is protecting is private association: the freedom to join a cause without being exposed for it.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. It begins where this lecture began, with the membership list as a weapon. And the tradition is older than campaign-finance law. It includes the Federalist Papers, published under the pseudonym Publius. It includes the Court's recognition, in 1995, that "anonymity is a shield from the tyranny of the majority."

So compelled donor disclosure, on this view, is not transparency. [beat] It is the state building a retaliation database, and calling it sunlight.

> The privacy advocate's case: compelled disclosure builds a retaliation database and calls it sunlight.

Now the evidence the privacy advocate points to. Three things.

First, Bonta moved the doctrine, on purpose. The Court struck California's regime on its face, treating the chilling of association as a harm to the whole system.

And the disclosure in Bonta ran only to a government office, not to the public. The Court found a chill anyway. If confidential disclosure to a regulator chills association, the advocate argues, publication on a searchable federal website chills it more.

[beat]

Second, the threat model has modernized. The 1958 weapon was the White Citizens' Council. The 2026 weapon is the screenshot.

Doxxing, employer pressure campaigns, and boycott by database operate at internet speed, against donors on both sides: to an LGBT-rights group in one county, to a traditionalist religious charity in the next.

And Wood and Spencer measured donors who stopped giving. The deeper worry, the advocate argues, is the donors who never start, whom no study of contribution records can observe.

[beat]

Third, "loophole" is a framing, not a fact. Congress wrote donor disclosure into law for 527 groups in 2000. In every Congress since 2010, it has declined to do the same for social welfare nonprofits. And in 2020, the Treasury deliberately stopped collecting those groups' donor names, for which it had no tax-administration use.

A policy reaffirmed that many times is a choice.

[pause]

Then the objection the privacy advocate has to answer, which is Justice Scalia. He put the opposing tradition at its sharpest in a 2010 case called Doe v. Reed. "Requiring people to stand up in public for their political acts fosters civic courage," he wrote, "without which democracy is doomed."

The answer: Scalia was writing about citizens signing referendum petitions, and so exercising legislative power. That is not the same as giving to an association that, among many activities, runs some ads. Civic courage is for civic acts. Association is allowed to be quiet.

And what would change the privacy advocate's mind? Evidence that retaliation against ordinary donors is rare, and can be remedied: the Wood and Spencer line, extended and replicated. Plus a disclosure design tightly limited to unambiguous electioneering, by major funders.

## The structural-reform centrist

Last, the structural-reform centrist, built the same way. What the centrist is protecting is both things at once: transparency for elections, and privacy for association.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The first two camps are arguing about the wrong unit. The question is not "disclosure: yes or no?" [beat] The question is which donors, to which activity, disclosed to whom.

And the case law, on a careful reading, already sketches the settlement.

> The centrist's case: disclose the electioneering, not the association.

Now the evidence the centrist points to. Three things.

First, a design principle. Tie disclosure to spending on electoral communications above a threshold, and to the large donors who fund it, not to membership in an organization. The DISCLOSE Act framework draws its lines at 10,000 dollars.

The principle comes from a 2010 paper by Richard Briffault. The informational interest is overwhelmingly about big money, so disclosing small donors delivers privacy costs with little informational payoff. In the centrist's phrase: raise the floors, publish the whales.

[beat]

Second, the safety valve that already exists. Earlier I described an exemption that Buckley created and Brown honored. Who qualifies for it? See if you can call it back.

[long pause]

Any group showing a reasonable probability of threats, harassment, or reprisals.

The centrist would make that exemption workable: a standing FEC process, with a flexible standard of evidence. Then the genuinely endangered get the NAACP's protection, and anonymity does not become the default for a utility buying a bailout.

[beat]

Third, the tax classification. Much of the problem, the centrist argues, is not disclosure law at all. It is the undefined "primarily engaged" standard from the last lecture.

A bright-line rule would attack the laundering at the level of the vehicle. Electoral activity above a fixed share of spending would force reclassification as a 527, which must disclose.

[pause]

Then the objection the centrist has to answer, which is Congress. That rule requires an IRS rulemaking, and Congress has barred it by rider since 2016. The answer: that is a political fact, not a design flaw.

And what would change the centrist's mind? Evidence that the middle is unbuildable. That exemption processes get weaponized, or rubber-stamped. That thresholds just relocate the money, as the migration out of 527 groups did after 2000. Or that neither coalition will trade.

## One bill, three answers

Three positions. Now run one proposal past all of them.

A bill covers any group that spends 50,000 dollars or more on electoral ads. It requires public disclosure of every donor to such a group over 10,000 dollars. And it adds an FEC exemption process, modeled on Brown v. Socialist Workers.

Which camp objects to what, and on which precedent? Work it through.

[pause 5s]

The privacy advocate objects that, after Bonta, even this targeted regime must survive exacting scrutiny, with narrow tailoring. And an exemption by application makes endangered donors out themselves to a government office first. That was the core harm in NAACP v. Alabama.

The reformer mostly cheers, but objects from the other flank. The exemption could become a loophole. And the money could migrate to "issue ad" formats the bill does not cover, as it did after 2000.

The centrist argues that both objections prove the design right. It splits electoral transparency from associational privacy, at the seam the Court itself drew.

Whether an exemption regime can be both protective and impossible to game is a contested question, not a settled one.

[pause]

Now the harder exercise. Choose the position you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Both sides argue from the same precedents. Electoral disclosure has been upheld every time the Court squarely reviewed it. Compelled exposure of members and donors, outside elections, has been struck down. And a harassment exemption bridges the two.

[beat]

Two. Campaign contributor, or associate: the fight is over which one the nonprofit's donor is.

[beat]

Three. The reformer's case: Citizens United was a bargain, and only one side was delivered. Its evidence: voters use the source of a message, the measured chill is small, and the networks are real.

[beat]

Four. The privacy advocate's case: compelled disclosure builds a retaliation database and calls it sunlight. Its evidence: Bonta moved the doctrine, the threat has modernized, and Congress keeps choosing this policy.

[beat]

And five. The centrist's case: disclose the electioneering, not the association. Its evidence: a threshold for big money, an exemption that already exists, and a bright line in the tax code.

[pause]

Now sort what is settled from what is not.

Settled: these nonprofits owe the public no donor list, and since 2020 the IRS does not collect the names either. Settled: the pipeline is real and heavily used, at about 1.9 billion dollars in 2024 on the fuller count. Settled: electoral disclosure is constitutional under Citizens United, and blanket donor dragnets outside elections are not, under Bonta. And a harassment exemption exists, and has been honored.

Here is what this lecture has not settled, because nobody has.

It is contested whether Bonta's test will migrate into the review of election law. Lower courts have divided over how far it reaches.

It is contested whether the chill on ordinary donors is negligible, as Wood and Spencer found, or systematically underestimated, because of the donors who never start.

And it is contested whether concealed money changes policy outcomes, or merely funds fights that would happen anyway.

[pause]

Underneath all of it sits the case we started with. Both camps claim NAACP v. Alabama. The reformer says it protects the harassed, through an exemption. The privacy advocate says it protects everyone, through a default. The open question is which reading better fits a world where you cannot know in advance which causes will become dangerous to support.

The Court has not answered which First Amendment tradition should govern this donor. A defensible position picks a frame, defends it against the strongest opposing case, not the weakest, and names the evidence that would change it.

The next lecture turns from the argument to the policy landscape: what reformers, regulators and the states are actually doing about it, as of mid-2026.

For now, go back to Alabama, and to the one thing the NAACP would not hand over. [beat] The names. Whether today's donor is the heir of those members is the question the Court has left open. You can now argue it three ways.
