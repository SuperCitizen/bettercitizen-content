September 22, 2022. The United States Senate is voting on whether to debate a bill called the DISCLOSE Act. The bill would require politically active nonprofits to name their large donors.

The tally is 49 to 49. Every Democrat present votes yes. Every Republican present votes no.

[beat]

To go ahead, the Senate needs sixty votes. That is called the cloture rule. And under the cloture rule, a tie is a burial.

[pause]

There was nothing new about the funeral. The same bill, in its first incarnation, passed the House in June 2010. Then it died in the Senate, twice that year. The second time, that September, it fell one vote short of cloture: 59 to 39.

From that first defeat to the present Congress: sixteen years. Eight Congresses. And a dark-money total that grew to roughly 1.9 billion dollars in the 2024 cycle.

> The DISCLOSE Act: introduced in every Congress, enacted never.

Last time, three positions on disclosure. This time, the ground they are fighting over, as of mid-2026. By the end, you should be able to explain why the landscape refuses to move at the federal level, and where it is moving anyway.

Four stops. First, Congress: one bill, sixteen years. Second, the regulators who can't. Third, the states, where the action has gone. And last, what to watch from here.

## One bill, sixteen years

Start with what the bill does. It has been the flagship of campaign-finance reformers since the spring after Citizens United, and its core mechanism has been stable across every version.

An organization that spends above a threshold on campaign-related disbursements must report that spending to the FEC within 24 hours. In the current Senate version, the threshold is 10,000 dollars per cycle. The organization must also disclose its major donors. And transfer-tracing rules follow the money that hops between affiliated groups before it lands.

In plainer words: spend real money on campaigns, and you say quickly who funded you, however many hands the money passed through.

Later versions added more. Provisions on foreign money. And disclaimers that identify the top funders on the face of the ad.

Now the history. In 2010, the bill passed the House and failed in the Senate. It was reintroduced in every Congress after that. In 2021 and 2022, it rode inside a larger bill, the Freedom to Vote Act. In September 2022 came the tie. And in 2026 it is pending again, as the DISCLOSE Act of 2026. It will not clear sixty votes in this Senate. [beat] It returns anyway.

There is a pattern in that history.

> Every floor defeat came from the 60-vote threshold, not from losing a majority.

That teaches a structural lesson, and it reaches well beyond this bill. Disclosure polls well across parties. It commands majorities. And it still cannot pass, because under current Senate rules the number that matters is not 50. It is 60.

There is a second reason. The parties' institutional positions have hardened, even where voters' views have not.

Republican leadership has framed the bill, since 2010, as donor intimidation dressed as transparency. That is Mitch McConnell's long-standing position, sharpened after Bonta. Democratic leadership has made the bill a messaging staple, precisely because it fails.

[pause]

A reformer, a privacy advocate and a cynic can each find their priors confirmed in that. So keep the three positions from the last lecture in view as we go.

Let's test the pattern. The bill won 59 Senate votes in 2010, and 49 in 2022. Yet its advocates say public support has not collapsed.

What explains a bill losing ten Senate votes while its polling holds steady? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Party sorting, not a change of opinion. In 2010, a handful of Republican senators were gettable, and the Democratic caucus was larger. By 2022, disclosure had become an issue of institutional identity. Democrats for, Republicans against, regardless of opinion at the district level.

So the lesson is about the Senate. Where sixty votes are needed, an idea's popularity matters less than one other thing: whether it cuts across the party divide, or reinforces it.

## The regulators who can't

If Congress is gridlocked, why not the agencies? Because each of the relevant regulators is, in its own way, structurally disabled. And the last two years have made that vivid. Three stories.

Story one. The IRS is forbidden to act.

Two lectures ago, we followed this story to 2016. Whether a group qualifies as a social welfare nonprofit turns on two words: primarily engaged. That standard has gone undefined since 1959.

In 2013, the IRS proposed a rule, and it drew a record: more than 150,000 comments. In 2014, the agency retreated. Then came an appropriations rider, renewed ever since, that bars the IRS from spending any funds to clarify the standard.

The story now has a remarkable coda. On September 30, 2025, in a case called Freedom Path v. IRS, a federal district court held that the agency's two governing standards are unconstitutionally vague. "Primarily engaged" is one of the two.

So think about where that leaves the agency. A court has declared the standard too vague to satisfy due process. Why can't the IRS just write a clearer rule? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not because of the court. The ruling declared the existing standard unconstitutionally vague, but it did not bar a fix. Congress did, by forbidding the IRS from spending money on one.

The binding constraint is an appropriations rider. Process, not doctrine.

So the legal position, as of mid-2026, is not far from Kafka. [beat] Too vague to enforce. Forbidden to clarify.

Whatever your camp, that is the strongest single exhibit for one claim: that the regime for these nonprofits is not a considered policy equilibrium, but an accident nobody is allowed to fix.

Story two. The Johnson Amendment is wobbling, carefully.

The Johnson Amendment is the 1954 ban on campaign intervention by charities. It is the wall that keeps deductible charity money out of elections.

In July 2025, in a case called National Religious Broadcasters v. Long, the IRS signed a proposed consent decree. In it, the agency took a position. The Amendment, "properly interpreted," does not reach a house of worship speaking to its own congregation about electoral politics, through its customary channels.

On March 31, 2026, the court dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction. So the settlement was never entered, and the Amendment stands unmodified on the books.

But the tax agency has shown its hand on enforcement. And within days, the Treasury announced that formal guidance for religious organizations is coming.

Watch this line. If charities gain electoral room, the dark-money map redraws itself around anonymous money that is also deductible. That would dwarf the story of the social welfare nonprofits.

Story three. The FEC deadlocks by design.

The commission has six members. No more than three may come from one party. And it takes four votes to act.

[beat]

That arithmetic invites ties of three to three. And on politically sensitive enforcement, the invitation is accepted.

A parting report by Commissioner Ann Ravel, in 2017, documented the trend. In 2006, about 3 percent of substantive votes ended in deadlock. By 2016, roughly 30 percent did.

Here is the consequence. The courts threw out a narrowing FEC regulation, and restored a broader duty to disclose the donors behind independent expenditures. And still, groups file reports listing no donors. Watchdogs complain. The complaints die in ties.

> A disclosure obligation without an enforcer is a suggestion.

Now step back from the three stories. A reform proposal that begins "the FEC should" or "the IRS should" is an incomplete sentence, until it explains how the agency gets the capacity to act.

The binding constraints in this lecture have been three pieces of process, not policy. You have met each one. Can you name all three?

[long pause]

A Senate rule: sixty votes. An appropriations rider on the IRS. And a commission structure that needs four votes out of six.

That is a recurring lesson in civics.

> When an outcome outlasts majority support, look for the veto point, not the conspiracy.

## The states are moving

Federal paralysis has shifted the action to the states. They regulate their own elections. And increasingly, they write the disclosure rules that Congress won't. Three experiments matter most.

California, under a law from 2017. Ballot-measure ads and independent-expenditure ads must name their top three funders on the ad itself.

[beat]

Montana, under a law from 2015. Dark-money groups that spend in state races must register, and report their spending and the donors behind it. The Ninth Circuit upheld that law in 2019.

[beat]

And Arizona. In 2022, by citizen initiative, voters passed Proposition 211, the Voters' Right to Know Act. It won about 72 percent of the vote.

Arizona's is the one to study, because it attacks the pass-through pipeline at its joint.

The usual question is who gave to the spender. And the usual answer is a nonprofit. Proposition 211 requires something else: tracing through every intermediary, to the person whose money it actually was.

> Arizona's Proposition 211 traces the money to its original source.

Here is how it works. The law covers anyone who spends 50,000 dollars or more on campaign media in a statewide race. In a local race, the trigger is 25,000. Those spenders must disclose every donor of 5,000 dollars or more, traced back to the original source.

That is precisely the disclosure the federal regime cannot produce. And it passed with about 72 percent support, in a swing state, which complicates any tidy partisan story about what voters want.

It is also the test case for whether Bonta reaches electoral disclosure.

Challengers argue that original-source tracing burdens associational privacy under the First Amendment, exactly as the dragnet in Bonta did.

Defenders argue that Bonta itself preserved the line of cases on electoral disclosure.

Montana's experience cuts in the defenders' favor, because its law survived review in federal court. But that was before Bonta.

[pause]

Where does Arizona's law stand, as of mid-2026? It is in force, and it has survived challenges so far. But in September 2025, the Arizona Supreme Court allowed a legislative challenge to proceed, and the merits are still pending. A definitive ruling, whenever it lands, will be the most important dark-money decision since Bonta itself.

One quieter lesson, about which states act. A deep-blue state, California. A red-leaning state, Montana, whose hostility to moneyed capture dates to the Copper Kings. And a purple state, Arizona, by direct democracy.

In federal politics, dark-money regulation is coded by party. Closer to the ground, it is not reliably coded that way.

Let's put Arizona's law to work. A national social welfare nonprofit runs 400,000 dollars of ads in a Phoenix mayoral race. The money comes from twelve donors, who each gave the group more than 5,000 dollars for the campaign.

What does Proposition 211 require that federal law would not? And which Supreme Court case will the group's lawyers cite in response? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Federal law would require, at most, that the organization be named as the spender. Its donors stay dark.

Proposition 211 goes further. The spending exceeds the local threshold of 25,000 dollars. So the law requires disclosure of those twelve donors, traced to the original source, through any nonprofits in between.

And the group's lawyers will cite Bonta: compelled donor disclosure must survive exacting scrutiny, with narrow tailoring. The state will answer from the Buckley and Citizens United line on electoral disclosure.

That collision is the open constitutional question of the next few years.

## What to watch

So where does this go? Four tripwires. Any one of them would reshape the landscape.

One. A ruling on the merits of Proposition 211. It would be the first square test, after Bonta, of original-source disclosure in elections. If the law survives, expect copycat initiatives. If it falls, Bonta has effectively migrated into election law.

[beat]

Two. The aftermath of Freedom Path. A holding that the standard is vague, colliding with a rider that forbids clarifying it, forces someone's hand. An appeal. A repeal of the rider. Or a regime where the IRS cannot police "primarily engaged" at all, which means functionally unlimited electioneering by these nonprofits.

[beat]

Three. The enforcement of the Johnson Amendment. The settlement failed on jurisdiction, but the IRS's stated interpretation survives as a signal, and Treasury guidance is on the way. Watch what the guidance actually permits. And watch for electoral money testing the charity wall.

[beat]

Four. The perennial return of the DISCLOSE Act. It will not clear sixty votes in this Senate. The serious question is whether any future majority treats the filibuster as the price of the status quo. Historically, disclosure has been the campaign-finance reform that majorities support and veto points kill.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The DISCLOSE Act: introduced in every Congress, enacted never. Every floor defeat came from the 60-vote threshold, not from losing a majority.

[beat]

Two. The regulators can't. A rider bars the IRS from clarifying a standard that a court has called unconstitutionally vague. The FEC needs four votes out of six, and deadlocks. A disclosure obligation without an enforcer is a suggestion.

[beat]

Three. The states are moving: California, Montana, and Arizona. Arizona's Proposition 211 traces the money to its original source. It passed with about 72 percent, and it is the test of how far Bonta reaches.

[beat]

And four. When an outcome outlasts majority support, look for the veto point, not the conspiracy. Here there are three: a Senate rule, a rider, and a commission's structure.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not a prediction. And it is not a verdict on whether the stalemate is a tragedy or a safeguard. Each of the three camps has an answer, and the facts you have just heard feed all three.

The disclosure-first reformer looks at the DISCLOSE Act.

> The reformer reads sixteen years of failure as proof the system protects its funders.

The donor-privacy advocate looks at Bonta, at the rider, and at the episode over the Johnson Amendment.

> The privacy advocate reads Bonta and the rider as institutions properly wary of compelled exposure.

The structural-reform centrist looks at Arizona.

> The centrist reads Arizona's 72 percent as a public wanting targeted, original-source disclosure.

Disclosure of large donors, the centrist adds, which neither national coalition will legislate.
