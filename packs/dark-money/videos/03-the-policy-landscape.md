## Forty-nine to forty-nine

On September 22, 2022, the Senate voted on whether to debate the DISCLOSE Act, a bill that would require politically active nonprofits to name their large donors. The tally was 49 to 49. Under the sixty-vote cloture rule, a tie is a burial.

- DISCLOSE Act, 2022: 49–49

The first version had passed the House in June 2010, then died in the Senate that September, one vote short at 59 to 39.

## A majority is not enough

Every floor defeat came from failing the Senate's 60-vote threshold, not from losing a majority. Disclosure polls well across parties, and the parties' institutional positions have hardened all the same.

- The threshold is 60, not 50

> When an outcome persists against majority support, look for the veto point.

## The regulators who can't

Why not the agencies? The tax rule says a 501(c)(4) nonprofit must be "primarily engaged" in social welfare, and nobody has defined "primarily". In 2025 a federal district court held that standard unconstitutionally vague. Since fiscal year 2016, Congress has barred the IRS from spending any funds to clarify it.

- IRS: too vague to enforce, forbidden to clarify

[check q2]

The Federal Election Commission has six commissioners, no more than three per party, and needs four votes to act. That arithmetic invites three-to-three ties.

- FEC: six seats, four votes to act

> A disclosure obligation without an enforcer is a suggestion.

## Where it is moving anyway

The action has shifted to the states. In 2022 Arizona voters passed Proposition 211 with about 72 percent of the vote. It requires large spenders on campaign media to disclose their major donors, traced through intermediaries to the original source: the person whose money it actually was.

- Arizona Prop 211: about 72%
- Traced to the original source

Challengers argue from the Supreme Court's Bonta decision that this burdens associational privacy. Defenders answer that Bonta preserved the electoral-disclosure cases. The merits litigation is pending.

## Tragedy or safeguard

The lesson gives no verdict on the stalemate, and its facts feed three camps. A disclosure-first reformer reads sixteen years of failure as a system protecting its funders. A donor-privacy advocate reads the same record as institutions properly wary of compelled exposure. A structural centrist reads Arizona's 72 percent as a public that wants targeted disclosure neither national coalition will legislate.

- Reformer: the system protects its funders
- Privacy advocate: institutions properly wary
- Centrist: the public wants targeted disclosure
