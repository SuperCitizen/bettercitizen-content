## The list Alabama wanted

In 1956, Alabama's attorney general went to court to expel the NAACP from his state, and demanded its complete membership list. A member named in 1950s Alabama faced firing, foreclosure, and worse. The NAACP produced every record except the names, and took a 100,000 dollar contempt fine. In 1958 a unanimous Supreme Court held that the Constitution protected the secrecy of the list.

- NAACP v. Alabama, 1958: 9–0

Is a donor to a politically active nonprofit today the heir of that NAACP member, or something else entirely?

## One Court, two lines of cases

Both sides argue from the same precedents. The Court has upheld every electoral disclosure regime it has squarely reviewed, including in Citizens United, 8 to 1. It has struck down compelled exposure of members and donors outside elections. In Bonta, in 2021, it struck down, 6 to 3, California's blanket demand for charities' major-donor lists.

- Electoral disclosure: upheld
- Blanket donor lists, outside elections: struck down

> The fight is over which line the nonprofit's election donor belongs to.

## The disclosure-first reformer

The reformer argues that Citizens United was a bargain: spending was deregulated on the premise of prompt disclosure, and the nonprofit pipeline voids the premise. The reformer points to a 2016 study that found disclosure deterred less than one donor per candidate.

> The reformer's case: disclosure was the Court's own promise.

## The donor-privacy advocate

The privacy advocate argues that a donor list is a weapon, and that compelled disclosure builds a retaliation database. The advocate points to Bonta, where the Court found a chill even though the lists went only to a government office, and to donors who never start giving, whom no study can observe.

> The privacy advocate's case: association is allowed to be quiet.

## The structural-reform centrist

The centrist argues that both are debating the wrong unit. The question is which donors, to which activity, disclosed to whom. The centrist points to a safety valve the Court already made in Buckley: an exemption for any group showing a reasonable probability of threats, harassment, or reprisals. It was granted to a harassed minor party in Brown v. Socialist Workers.

> The centrist's case: disclose the electioneering, not the association.

## What the Court has not answered

Which First Amendment tradition should govern this donor is the live question, and the Court has not answered it.

- Still open: which tradition governs
