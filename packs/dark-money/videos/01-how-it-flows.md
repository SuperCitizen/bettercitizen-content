## The ads Ohio could not trace

Between 2017 and 2020, the Ohio utility FirstEnergy and its affiliates routed roughly 60 million dollars to a nonprofit called Generation Now. The nonprofit spent it on politics, in service of a bailout of two nuclear plants. Ohio voters saw the ads. They could not see who paid for them.

[beat]

Federal prosecutors called it likely the largest bribery scheme in Ohio history. The nonprofit owed the public no donor list.

> The bribery was the crime. The secrecy was legal.

## What the term means

Dark money is spending intended to influence elections whose original source is concealed from the public. The classic vehicle is a 501(c)(4), a tax-exempt "social welfare" organization. It may do electoral work, so long as that is not its primary activity, and it owes the public no donor list.

- Election spending, source concealed
- 501(c)(4): no public donor list

A super PAC is different. It must itemize its contributors to the Federal Election Commission, so it is not dark by default.

- Super PAC: donors itemized to the FEC

[check q1]

## Where disclosure dies

Now follow one check. A donor gives to a 501(c)(4), and the public sees nothing. The nonprofit gives to a super PAC. The super PAC reports its contributor exactly as the law requires, and the name on the line is the nonprofit's.

- Donor to nonprofit: nothing public
- Nonprofit to super PAC: a dead end

Researchers call that gray money. No hop breaks a law, and no report contains a falsehood.

> Every filing in the chain is accurate. The original source is gone.

## Measuring a shadow

How much is there? Two numbers are in circulation. What nondisclosing groups themselves reported to the FEC peaked at about 309 million dollars in 2012, and fell to under 25 million by 2022. A fuller estimate, which counts transfers to super PACs and issue ads, reached about 1.9 billion dollars in 2024, a record.

- Self-reported: $309 million, then under $25 million
- Fuller estimate, 2024: about $1.9 billion

The reported number fell because the strategy changed. The groups now mostly transfer money to super PACs.

> Always ask what a campaign-finance statistic actually counts.
