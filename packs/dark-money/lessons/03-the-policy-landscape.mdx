# The current policy landscape

On September 22, 2022, the United States Senate voted on whether to debate the DISCLOSE Act — the bill that would require politically active nonprofits to name their large donors. The tally was 49–49: every Democrat present voted yes, every Republican present voted no, and under the sixty-vote cloture rule a tie is a burial. There was nothing new about the funeral. The same bill, in its first incarnation, had passed the House 219–206 in June 2010 and then died in the Senate twice that year — the second time, that September, one vote short of cloture at 59–39. From that first defeat to the present Congress lie sixteen years, eight Congresses, and a [[term:dark-money]] total that grew to roughly $1.9 billion in the 2024 cycle. This lesson maps the policy landscape as of mid-2026 — which, more than anything, means explaining why the landscape refuses to move at the federal level, and where it is moving anyway.

## The DISCLOSE Act: sixteen years of the same idea

The DISCLOSE Act (the acronym strains for "Democracy Is Strengthened by Casting Light On Spending in Elections") has been [[term:campaign-finance]] reformers' flagship since the spring after *Citizens United*. Its core mechanism has been stable across every version: organizations spending above a threshold on campaign-related disbursements — the current Senate version, S. 3991 (the DISCLOSE Act of 2026), uses $10,000 per cycle — must report the spending to the FEC within 24 hours and disclose their major donors, with transfer-tracing rules designed to follow money that hops between affiliated groups before it lands. Later versions added foreign-money provisions (extending the foreign-national spending ban to digital ads and ballot-measure campaigns) and disclaimers identifying top funders on the face of ads.

Every floor defeat in that sequence came from failing the Senate's 60-vote cloture threshold, not from losing a majority — and that pattern teaches a structural lesson that transcends this bill. Disclosure polls well across parties, commands majorities, and still cannot pass — because under current Senate rules the relevant threshold is not 50 but 60, and because the parties' *institutional* positions have hardened even where voters' views have not. Republican leadership has framed the bill since 2010 as donor intimidation dressed as transparency (Mitch McConnell's long-standing position, sharpened after *Bonta*); Democratic leadership has made it a messaging staple precisely because it fails. A reformer, a privacy advocate, and a cynic can all find their priors confirmed in that sentence — which is why it is worth keeping the three perspectives from Lesson 2 in view throughout this one.

<Checkpoint question="The DISCLOSE Act won 59 Senate votes in 2010 and 49 in 2022 — yet its advocates say public support hasn't collapsed. What explains a bill losing ten Senate votes while its polling holds steady?">
  Party sorting, not opinion change. In 2010 a handful of Republican senators were gettable and the Democratic caucus was larger; by 2022 the vote was a pure party-line 49–49, because disclosure had become an institutional-identity issue — Democrats for, Republicans against — regardless of district-level opinion. The lesson: in a 60-vote Senate, an idea's popularity matters less than whether it cross-cuts or reinforces the party divide.
</Checkpoint>

## The regulators who can't

If Congress is gridlocked, why not the agencies? Because each of the three relevant regulators is, in its own way, structurally disabled — and the last two years have made that vivid.

**The IRS is forbidden to act.** Lesson 1 told the story to 2016: the undefined "primarily engaged" standard, the 2013 proposed rule on "candidate-related political activity" that drew a record 150,000-plus comments, the 2014 retreat, and the appropriations rider — renewed ever since — that bars the IRS from spending any funds to clarify 501(c)(4) status. The story now has a remarkable coda. In *Freedom Path, Inc. v. IRS* (D.D.C., September 30, 2025), a federal district court held that the IRS's two governing standards — "primarily engaged," with its drifting 49-percent-or-something interpretations, and the multi-factor facts-and-circumstances test of Revenue Ruling 2004-6 — are unconstitutionally *vague*. So as of mid-2026 the legal position is close to Kafka: a court has declared the standard too vague to satisfy due process, while Congress forbids the agency from writing a clearer one. Whatever your camp, this is the strongest single exhibit for the claim that the (c)(4) regime is not a considered policy equilibrium but an accident nobody is allowed to fix.

**The Johnson Amendment is wobbling — carefully.** The 1954 ban on 501(c)(3) campaign intervention is the wall that keeps *deductible* charity money out of elections. In July 2025, in *National Religious Broadcasters v. Long*, the IRS signed a proposed consent decree taking the position that the Amendment, "properly interpreted," does not reach a house of worship speaking to its own congregation about electoral politics through customary channels. On March 31, 2026, the court dismissed the case without prejudice for lack of jurisdiction — the Anti-Injunction Act bars such suits "in respect to" taxes — so the settlement was never entered and the Amendment stands unmodified on the books. But the tax agency has shown its hand on enforcement, and Treasury announced within days that formal guidance for religious organizations is coming. Watch this line: if (c)(3)s gain electoral room, the dark-money map redraws itself around *deductible* anonymous money, which would dwarf the (c)(4) story.

**The FEC deadlocks by design.** Six commissioners, no more than three per party, four votes to act: the arithmetic invites 3–3 ties, and on politically sensitive enforcement the invitation is accepted. Commissioner Ann Ravel's parting report, *Dysfunction and Deadlock* (2017), documented substantive-vote deadlocks rising from about 3 percent in 2006 to roughly 30 percent by 2016. The post-*CREW* world shows the consequence: even after the courts vacated the FEC's narrowing regulation and restored a broader statutory duty for independent-expenditure donor disclosure (Lesson 1), groups file reports listing no donors, watchdogs complain, and the complaints die in ties. A disclosure obligation without an enforcer is a suggestion.

> **Why this matters:** Reform proposals that begin "the FEC should…" or "the IRS should…" are incomplete sentences until they explain *how the agency gets the capacity to act*. The binding constraints here are a Senate rule, an appropriations rider, and a commission structure — process, not policy. This is a recurring civics lesson: when an outcome persists for sixteen years against majority support, look for the veto point, not the conspiracy.

## The states as laboratories — and the laboratories are open

Federal paralysis has shifted the action to the states, which regulate their own elections and, increasingly, write the disclosure rules Congress won't. Three experiments matter most:

| State | Law | Mechanism | Status as of mid-2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| California | DISCLOSE Act, AB 249 (2017) | Ballot-measure and independent-expenditure ads must name their top three funders on the ad itself, with follow-the-money rules | In force |
| Montana | DISCLOSE Act, SB 289 (2015) | Dark-money groups spending in state races must register and report spending and attributable donors | In force; upheld by the Ninth Circuit in *NAGR v. Mangan* (2019), cert. denied (2020) |
| Arizona | Proposition 211, "Voters' Right to Know Act" (2022, passed with about 72% of the vote) | Spenders of $50,000+ statewide ($25,000 local) on campaign media must disclose donors of $5,000+, traced through intermediaries to the **original source** | In force; survived challenges so far; Arizona Supreme Court allowed a legislative challenge to proceed (Sept. 2025), merits litigation pending |

Arizona's Proposition 211 is the one to study, because it attacks the pass-through pipeline at its joint: rather than asking *who gave to the spender* (answer: a nonprofit), it requires tracing to the original source — the person whose money it actually was. That is precisely the disclosure the federal regime cannot produce, and it passed not through a legislature but by citizen initiative, with about 72 percent support in a swing state, which complicates any tidy partisan story about what voters want. It is also, predictably, the test case for whether *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* reaches electoral disclosure: challengers argue original-source tracing burdens associational privacy under the [[term:first-amendment]] exactly as the Schedule B dragnet did; defenders answer that *Bonta* itself preserved the electoral-disclosure line of cases. Montana's experience cuts in the defenders' favor — its 2015 law survived federal-court review — but that was before *Bonta*. A definitive merits ruling on Prop 211, whenever it lands, will be the most important dark-money decision since *Bonta* itself.

The state story also has a quieter lesson about *which* states act. These laws passed in a deep-blue state (California), a red-leaning state with a populist anti-corporate streak older than the [[term:super-pac]] (Montana, whose hostility to moneyed capture dates to the Copper Kings), and a purple state by direct democracy (Arizona). Dark-money regulation is politically coded *federal-partisan*, but it is not reliably coded that way closer to the ground.

<Checkpoint question="A national 501(c)(4) runs $400,000 of ads in a Phoenix mayoral race, funded by twelve donors who each gave it more than $5,000 for the campaign. What does Arizona's Prop 211 require that federal law would not — and which Supreme Court case will its lawyers cite in response?">
  Federal law would require, at most, that the *organization* be named as the spender — its donors stay dark. Prop 211, because the spending exceeds the $25,000 local threshold, requires disclosure of the $5,000+ donors *traced to the original source*, through any intermediary nonprofits. The group's lawyers will cite *AFPF v. Bonta* (2021): compelled donor disclosure must survive exacting scrutiny with narrow tailoring. The state will answer from the *Buckley*/*Citizens United* electoral-disclosure line — and that collision is the live constitutional question of the next few years.
</Checkpoint>

## What to watch from here

Four tripwires, each of which would reshape the landscape:

1. **A merits ruling on Proposition 211** — the first square post-*Bonta* test of original-source electoral disclosure. If it survives, expect copycat initiatives; if it falls, *Bonta* has effectively migrated into election law.
2. **The *Freedom Path* aftermath.** A vagueness holding against the (c)(4) standard, colliding with the appropriations rider, forces someone's hand: an appeal, a rider repeal, or a regime where the IRS cannot police "primarily engaged" at all — functionally unlimited (c)(4) electioneering.
3. **The Johnson Amendment's enforcement posture.** The *NRB* settlement failed on jurisdiction in March 2026, but the IRS's stated interpretation survives as a signal, and Treasury has announced that formal guidance for religious organizations is on the way. Watch what the guidance actually permits — and watch for electoral money testing the (c)(3) wall.
4. **The DISCLOSE Act's perennial return.** S. 3991 will not clear sixty votes in this Senate. The serious question is whether any future majority treats the filibuster as the price of the status quo — disclosure being, historically, the campaign-finance reform that majorities support and veto points kill.

## What this lesson is not

It is not a prediction, and it is not a verdict on whether the stalemate is tragedy or safeguard — Lesson 2's three camps each have an answer, and this lesson's facts feed all three. A disclosure-first reformer reads sixteen years of DISCLOSE failure as proof the system protects its funders; a donor-privacy advocate reads *Bonta*, the rider, and the *NRB* episode as institutions properly wary of compelled exposure; a structural centrist sees in Arizona's 72 percent a public that wants precisely the targeted, large-donor, original-source disclosure neither national coalition will legislate.
