# The current policy landscape

## Federal proposals

**DISCLOSE Act (multiple versions)**: Requires organizations spending above a threshold on electoral activity to disclose top donors. Has not advanced past Senate cloture in any version since 2010.

**IRS rule-making on 501(c)(4) primary purpose**: Could clarify "primary" definition. Treasury has not undertaken significant action since the IRS targeting controversy of 2013 (which produced political pressure to avoid c4 enforcement).

**FEC coordination enforcement**: Tighter rules on coordination between 501(c)(4)s and Super PACs would constrain the donor-routing pipeline. The FEC has been deadlocked for over a decade.

**Freedom to Vote Act (S. 2747)**: Comprehensive voting-rights and campaign-finance bill includes DISCLOSE provisions. Has not cleared Senate cloture.

## State-level activity

Some states have stricter disclosure than federal law:
- **Montana**: requires disclosure of major donors funding political ads.
- **California**: requires disclosure of donors funding ballot-initiative advocacy.
- **Several states**: have donor-disclosure rules for state-level electoral activity.

State-level activity is often litigated. AFP v. Bonta (originally about California's required donor disclosure for charities) is the most-prominent recent case.

## What's likely next

- **Continued legislative efforts.** DISCLOSE provisions will be reintroduced and may pass when Senate dynamics align.
- **IRS guidance.** Treasury may eventually issue clearer "primary purpose" guidance, though political resistance is strong.
- **Litigation around 501(c)(4) "primary" definition.** Cases challenging IRS classification decisions will continue.
- **State-level activity.** Continued state-by-state divergence with periodic federal-court litigation.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on dark-money policy requires:

1. A view on **whether the disclosure-vs.-privacy trade-off is being struck correctly**.
2. A view on **what reforms (if any) would help** — and at what cost.
3. A view on **the constitutional limits** of available reforms.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
