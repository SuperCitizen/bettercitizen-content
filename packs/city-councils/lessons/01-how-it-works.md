# How U.S. city councils actually work

Most Americans live in cities or towns governed by a city council, but the precise structure varies enormously. The U.S. has roughly 19,000 incorporated municipalities, and they operate under several distinct governmental forms.

## Two main forms of city government

**Strong-mayor / mayor-council**: the mayor is a directly-elected executive with significant powers — appointing department heads, vetoing council legislation, drafting the budget. The council functions more like a legislative branch. Examples: New York, Los Angeles, Houston, Chicago, Philadelphia.

**Council-manager**: the council is the dominant body. It hires a professional city manager who serves as chief executive (similar to a corporate CEO). The mayor is often a council member with mostly ceremonial duties (sometimes elected separately, sometimes selected by the council). Examples: Phoenix, San Antonio, Dallas, San Jose, Charlotte.

Roughly 60% of U.S. cities use council-manager; ~30% use strong-mayor; the rest use commission systems or town-meeting forms (small New England towns).

The choice matters significantly:
- Strong-mayor cities have more visible executive politics, more frequent partisan election dynamics.
- Council-manager cities tend toward technocratic professional administration with less visible political conflict.

## How councils are elected

Council members may be elected:

- **At-large**: all voters citywide vote for all council seats. Tends to favor citywide-recognizable candidates and may underrepresent minority neighborhoods. Common in council-manager cities.
- **By district (ward)**: each council member represents a specific geographic district. Provides more direct neighborhood representation but can produce parochial coalitions. Common in strong-mayor cities.
- **Hybrid**: some seats at-large, others by district. Tries to combine benefits of both.

Council size varies enormously: from 5 members (small towns) to 51 (New York City Council).

## What councils actually do

Most city councils have authority over:

- **Local laws ("ordinances")** — zoning, noise rules, parking, business licensing, sanitation, public health.
- **City budget** — approve the annual budget, typically presented by the mayor or city manager.
- **Property taxes** — set rates within state-allowed limits.
- **Capital projects** — approve major infrastructure (roads, parks, public buildings).
- **Department oversight** — confirm appointments, hold hearings on department performance.
- **Planning and zoning** — approve master plans, variances, rezoning requests.

What councils don't typically control:
- Schools (separate elected boards in most states; covered in the school-boards pack).
- State law and regulations (preempt local action in many areas).
- Federal-level matters.
- Most criminal-justice policy (state-controlled in most cases).

## The local legislative process

The basic shape:

1. **Proposal.** A council member, the mayor, or a city department drafts a proposed ordinance or resolution.
2. **Committee review.** The council often has committees (Finance, Public Safety, Land Use, etc.) that review proposals first.
3. **First reading + public comment.** The council holds a public meeting where the proposal is read for the first time, and members of the public can comment.
4. **Discussion and amendment.** Council members debate and may amend the proposal.
5. **Final vote.** Sometimes a second reading at a separate meeting; sometimes immediate.
6. **Mayoral action (in strong-mayor cities).** The mayor can sign or veto.

Most council meetings are public. Most councils maintain agendas online in advance. Public comment is generally allowed at each meeting (with time limits, often 1-3 minutes per speaker).

## State preemption

A defining feature of U.S. local government: cities are creatures of the state. Their authority is delegated by the state legislature and can be expanded or restricted at any time. State preemption is the practice of state law overriding local regulation. Recent examples:

- **Minimum wage**: many states preempt local minimum-wage ordinances.
- **Plastic bag bans**: several states preempt local plastic-bag rules.
- **Anti-discrimination ordinances**: some states preempt local LGBTQ-protection laws.
- **Gun regulation**: nearly all states preempt local firearm regulation.
- **Rent control**: many states preempt local rent regulation.

This is one of the most consequential dimensions of U.S. local politics — the question of how much policy authority cities actually have.

## What's at stake at the city-council level

Despite the constraints of state preemption, city councils control:
- Land use and zoning (the housing pack examines this in detail).
- Police budgets and oversight.
- Parks, libraries, and public spaces.
- Local business regulation.
- Public-health responses (especially evident during COVID).
- Climate adaptation and resilience planning.
- Local transportation (transit subsidies, bike infrastructure, parking).
- Affordable-housing programs (within state rules).

City councils make decisions that immediately affect residents' daily lives. Yet voter participation in city-council elections is dramatically lower than in federal or even state elections — often 10-25% in non-presidential election cycles. The result: city councils are often elected by a small subset of residents, frequently the most-engaged or longest-resident.

This is the structural opportunity for civic engagement: small voter bases mean individual votes matter much more, and individual public-comment participation matters much more, than at higher levels of government.
