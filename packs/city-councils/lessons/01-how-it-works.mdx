# How U.S. city councils actually work

In January 2025, Portland, Oregon did something quietly historic: it stopped being the last major American city run by a *commission* — the form where each elected commissioner personally runs a slice of the bureaucracy, fire here, parks there — and rebooted itself with a 12-member council elected from four districts, a citywide mayor, and a professional city administrator. Voters had approved the overhaul in 2022 by about 57 percent, retiring a structure Portland had kept for more than a century after nearly every other big city abandoned it. Here is the detail to sit with: nothing about that choice was dictated by the U.S. Constitution, the State of Oregon could have blocked or rewritten it, and the city next door runs on an entirely different operating system. The United States has 19,491 municipal governments (plus 16,214 towns and townships), and unlike the federal government — one constitutional design, endlessly litigated — city government is a menu. Which item your city picked determines who actually holds power there, and most residents have never been told there was a menu at all.

## Creatures of the state

Before the menu, the kitchen. Cities appear nowhere in the U.S. Constitution. Their legal status was fixed in the nineteenth century by an argument between two state judges. Iowa's John F. Dillon held (in an 1868 railroad case, then in the era's dominant treatise) that municipalities are mere "creatures of the state," possessing only the powers the legislature expressly grants — construe any doubt *against* the city. Michigan's Thomas Cooley answered in 1871 that local self-government was an inherent right the state must respect. Dillon won. The U.S. Supreme Court ratified his view in *Hunter v. City of Pittsburgh* (1907), holding that states may create, reshape, or abolish their municipalities essentially at will, and "Dillon's Rule" remains the default grammar of American local government.

The states themselves softened the rule's harshest edge through **home rule**: most state constitutions now let cities (often above a population threshold) adopt charters and legislate on local affairs without itemized permission. But home rule is a delegation, not sovereignty — what the state grants, the state can preempt, as the final section of this lesson shows. Hold both ideas at once: your city council is the government closest to your daily life, *and* it serves at the pleasure of a legislature that may sit hundreds of miles away.

## The menu of forms

Two forms dominate, with two minor survivors. The labels sound bureaucratic; the stakes are about where executive power lives.

**Mayor-council ("strong mayor").** Voters elect both a council (the legislature) and a mayor who is a genuine chief executive: hiring and firing department heads, drafting the budget, vetoing ordinances. This is the form of most very large, older cities — New York (with its 51-member council), Chicago (50 aldermen), Houston, Philadelphia — and its politics look like politics: visible executive ambition, mayoral coalitions, veto fights. (A "weak mayor" variant scatters executive power across the council and elected boards; many small towns work this way.)

**Council-manager.** Voters elect a council that sets policy and hires a professional **city manager** as chief executive — a CEO answerable to the council-as-board, with the mayor often a councilmember wearing a ceremonial sash. This was the Progressive Era's signature municipal invention, designed explicitly to take administration away from the [[term:patronage-system]] of machine politics — the world of [[term:tammany-hall]] — and give it to credentialed professionals. Staunton, Virginia hired the first city manager in 1908; today the council-manager form governs Phoenix, San Antonio, Dallas, San Jose, Fort Worth, and Charlotte, and it is the most common form among sizable cities: the National League of Cities and ICMA report it grew from 48 percent of surveyed municipalities in 1996 to 55 percent by 2006, and ICMA's 2018 survey found council-manager (40 percent) edging mayor-council (38 percent) across all responding municipalities, with the remainder split among commission, town meeting, and representative town meeting.

**Commission.** Born in Galveston, Texas after the catastrophic 1900 hurricane: commissioners elected citywide each *run a department* — legislature and executive fused. It spread early, then collapsed under its own design (no one coordinates; every commissioner defends a fiefdom). Under 1 percent of cities use it now, and with Portland's 2025 exit it has no big-city exemplar left.

**Town meeting.** In New England, about 5 percent of municipalities still legislate by assembly of the eligible voters themselves — direct democracy surviving in institutional amber, usually with elected "selectboards" handling business between meetings.

The form shapes the politics. Mayor-council cities produce visible, personality-driven executive politics and clearer electoral accountability for outcomes; council-manager cities trade that for administrative professionalism and lower-temperature politics, at the cost of an executive no voter can fire. Political scientists since Banfield and Wilson's *City Politics* (1963) have read the council-manager map as a residue of the Progressive reform coalition — strongest in the newer cities of the Sunbelt and West, weakest in the old machine cities of the Northeast and Midwest, which kept their strong mayors.

<Checkpoint question="Residents of a council-manager city are furious about how snow removal was handled and want to “vote the bum out.” Who is the bum — and what does the answer reveal about the form's basic trade-off?">
  The official actually running snow removal is the **city manager**, whom nobody elected and nobody can vote out; only the council can fire the manager. Voters' recourse is to replace council members until the council replaces the manager. That indirection is the design: professional administration insulated from electoral pressure — which is either the cure for patronage politics or an accountability gap, depending on the year you ask.
</Checkpoint>

## Who elects the council — and why the method is a fight

Council members are elected three ways: **at-large** (every voter votes for every seat), **by district/ward** (the city is mapped into single-member districts), or **hybrid**. The choice looks procedural and is anything but. At-large systems — another Progressive reform, meant to make members serve "the whole city" — systematically advantage candidates with citywide name recognition and money, and they can let a bare citywide majority win every seat, shutting geographically concentrated minority communities out entirely. That arithmetic made at-large elections a central target of voting-rights litigation. California's Voting Rights Act of 2002 lowered the proof needed to challenge at-large systems, and the result has been one of the era's quietest electoral transformations: before the CVRA, 36 of California's 483 cities elected councils by district; by the Rose Institute's count two decades later, 229 did — nearly half the state — most switching under threat of suit. District systems carry their own pathology, the parochial logrolling politics of "my ward's project for yours," which is why many cities split the difference with hybrids.

Two more structural choices quietly shape who governs. Most council elections are formally **nonpartisan** (no party labels on the ballot — one more Progressive legacy). And many are **off-cycle**, held on dates with nothing federal on the ballot. Sarah Anzia's *Timing and Turnout* (2014) shows off-cycle timing is not a neutral scheduling quirk: turnout collapses, but *organized* groups — municipal unions, homeowner associations, developers — turn out and mobilize regardless, so their votes weigh more. Portland State University's "Who Votes for Mayor?" project found turnout below 15 percent of eligible voters in ten of America's 30 largest cities' most recent mayoral elections, with Dallas, Fort Worth, and Las Vegas in single digits — and voters over 65 participating at several times the rate of voters under 35. Whoever your council is, it was chosen by a small, old, organized electorate.

<Checkpoint question="A city of 100,000 is 30% Latino, concentrated in three neighborhoods. Under its at-large system, no Latino candidate has ever won one of the seven council seats. A switch to seven single-member districts would likely change that — why, mechanically?">
  At-large, the citywide majority (70%) can outvote the minority for *all seven* seats — 30% of every contest is 0% of the seats. Districting converts geographic concentration into representation: the three neighborhoods anchor two or three districts where Latino voters are the majority and can elect their preferred candidates. This is precisely the mechanism the California Voting Rights Act weaponized, moving the state from 36 to 229 district-elected cities — and the reason civil-rights plaintiffs target at-large systems while defenders of at-large argue it prevents ward parochialism.
</Checkpoint>

## What councils do, and how an ordinance becomes law

A council is a legislature, and its products are **ordinances** (binding local laws) and **resolutions** (formal statements). The portfolio: zoning and land use, the city budget and property-tax rate (within state caps), business licensing, parks and libraries, police budgets and oversight, water and sanitation, streets and parking, capital projects and the bonds that finance them. The pipeline will look familiar from civics, miniaturized — and as in Congress, most of the mortality is upstream of the floor:

Every state has an open-meetings law requiring posted agendas and public deliberation, and most councils now post agendas, video archives, and roll-call votes online — which means the entire pipeline above is watchable, and Lesson 2 is about where in it a resident's effort actually lands.

The scholar's lens explains why these small-bore powers generate ferocious politics. Charles Tiebout's famous 1956 model pictures metropolitan residents "voting with their feet" — choosing among the region's many municipalities the bundle of taxes and services they prefer, which disciplines cities the way markets discipline firms. William Fischel's *The Homevoter Hypothesis* (2001) supplies the motive force: for most homeowners, the house is the dominant asset in their portfolio, un-diversifiable and immovable, so anything a council does that might touch property values — a zoning variance, a homeless shelter, an apartment building — triggers defense of the family balance sheet. Tiebout explains why there are 19,491 municipalities; Fischel explains who shows up at 10 p.m. to fight the fourplex.

## Preemption: the leash, demonstrated

Dillon's Rule stopped being an abstraction the moment cities started legislating progressively in conservative states (and occasionally vice versa). The Economic Policy Institute counts 25 states that now preempt local minimum-wage ordinances — Alabama's 2016 nullification of Birmingham's $10.10 minimum wage, passed days after the city acted, is the textbook case. States have preempted local plastic-bag bans, rent regulation, firearm rules, and anti-discrimination ordinances. Texas escalated the genre in 2023 with HB 2127 — opponents call it the "Death Star" law — which preempts local regulation across *entire fields* (labor, agriculture, finance, natural resources) unless the state explicitly authorizes it. Houston, San Antonio, and El Paso sued; a Travis County judge declared the law unconstitutional in August 2023; it took effect anyway pending appeal, and in July 2025 a state appeals court reversed the trial judge and upheld it — by late 2025 private plaintiffs were suing Dallas over more than 80 separate ordinances. As of mid-2026, the leash is short and getting shorter, and the live constitutional fights in [[term:federalism]]'s bottom tier are about exactly how short.

> **Why this matters:** When you ask "why doesn't my city just fix this?", the honest answer is often that the state took the tool away — or never granted it. Diagnosing whether an issue is *council-solvable* is the first skill of local engagement, and Lesson 2 builds on it.

## What this lesson did not settle

The mechanics are now yours: forms, election methods, the ordinance pipeline, the state leash. What remains genuinely contested is normative, and this pack flags it rather than resolves it: whether professional management beats elected executive accountability; whether at-large or district elections produce better representation (the answer differs by what you mean by "represent"); whether moving elections on-cycle is a democratic repair or, as Anzia documents its opponents arguing, a way to drown local issues in national noise; and where preemption stops being coherent statewide policy and becomes the strangling of self-government. Lesson 2 turns from how the institution works to how you work the institution — including the [[term:recall]], the [[term:ballot-initiative]], and the unglamorous craft of the three-minute public comment.
