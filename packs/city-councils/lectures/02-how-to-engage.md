It is two minutes to seven, on a Tuesday evening, at city hall.

A clerk hands you a paper card. You print your name, and the agenda item you want to address. When your turn comes, you get a lectern, a microphone, and a countdown timer. Three minutes, in most cities. Sometimes two. Sometimes one, when the room is full. The light goes yellow at thirty seconds.

[beat]

Behind the dais sit the people who will vote tonight: on the budget, on the rezoning, on the police contract. Those are sums and stakes that most state legislators never touch so directly. And eleven other speakers have signed up. So the public's entire spoken input on your issue will run about half an hour.

[pause]

Here is the strange double truth of local engagement.

> No level of American government is easier to reach. Almost nobody reaches for it.

Last time, we took the machine apart: the forms of city government, the elections, the ordinances, the state's leash. This lecture is about working it. Which door to knock on. When. And with what in hand.

We will take it in four steps.

First, finding the right door.

Second, the meeting itself, and your three minutes in it.

Third, your odds, and the seats that sit above the lectern.

And last, the heavy machinery, and what to expect, honestly.

## The right door

Start with three look-ups, all on your city's website.

One: who represents you. In a city that elects by district, an address lookup returns your member. In an at-large city, every member answers to you, on paper. In practice, you want the one whose committee covers your issue, or who has made that issue a signature.

Two: which committee owns the issue. Most councils route their work through standing committees: Finance, Public Safety, Land Use, Transportation. And the committee hearing, not the vote of the full council, is where text gets written and minds are open.

Three: where the agenda lives. State open-meetings laws require agendas to be posted in advance. California's Brown Act, among the strictest of its kind, requires 72 hours for a regular meeting. And with the agenda comes a packet: staff reports, fiscal notes, draft ordinances. That packet is the city government's diary. It is published before every meeting, and opened by almost no one.

[pause]

Now one distinction that will save you months. Is your problem a service request, or a policy request?

A service request is operational: a pothole, a missed trash pickup, a broken streetlight, a noise complaint. For those, the fastest route is the department itself, usually through a three-one-one line or portal.

Why not the council? Because of what a council is.

> Councils legislate. They do not dispatch crews.

In council-manager cities, the form we met last time, the charter often bars council members from directing staff at all.

The council is the right door when the rule is the problem: the speed limit, the zoning category, the budget line, the ordinance that doesn't exist yet. That is a policy request.

> A service request goes to the department. A policy request goes to the council.

Let's test it. Two neighbors are angry about the same street. One wants the potholes in the pavement fixed. The other wants the street redesigned, so that trucks stop using it as a cut-through.

Who should contact whom? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The pothole is a service request. It goes straight to public works, through three-one-one, and copying a council member adds little.

The redesign is a policy request. It needs a budget line, possibly an ordinance, and a place in the transportation committee's queue. So it runs through the council member, and through the committee that owns streets.

Matching the request to the channel is the first competence of local engagement. Mix them up, and you waste the only resource you control: [beat] your own persistence.

## The meeting and the three minutes

You have the right door. Now walk through it, into the meeting.

A council meeting is mostly the ritual ratification of work done upstream. Items arrive with a staff report and a committee recommendation. Many are bundled onto a consent calendar: a set of routine items, passed in a single vote, unless a member asks to pull one out for discussion. In many cities, a public speaker can ask too.

Watch one meeting online before you ever attend, and you learn the rhythm: presentations, consent, public hearings on noticed items, general comment.

You also learn the structural fact underneath. By the night of the final vote, positions are usually set.

> The meeting is where decisions are recorded. They are made earlier.

Made where? In committee hearings, in staff drafting, and in briefings before the meeting. So the working rule is to show up one stage earlier than instinct suggests.

[pause]

When you do speak, the craft is real, and it is learnable. Council members report what actually registers with them, and it comes down to three habits.

Be specific. Not "I'm against this." Instead: "I oppose Item 14, the Oak Street rezoning, for two reasons." And rather than demanding instant action, ask for a specific next step: a study, an amendment, a continuance.

Be personal. Not abstract statistics, and not a prepared essay that runs to the end of the timer. One point, one story, under the limit: what this does to your block, your commute, your rent.

And be steady. Rather than attacking the members' motives, acknowledge the trade-off, then argue your side of it. And rather than appearing once, furious, appear three meetings running, calm.

[beat]

Specific. Personal. Steady.

Organizers who do this for a living add two things.

First, coordinate. Five speakers covering five distinct angles beat fifteen speakers repeating one sentence. Most councils handle ceded or pooled time poorly, so the division of labor has to happen before the sign-up cards.

Second, put it in writing anyway. A two-paragraph email to each member, before the vote, enters the record. It reaches the members who were absent, and it gives staff language they can lift into a memo. Council offices do read mail from identifiable residents. A form-letter campaign gets tallied. [beat] An original letter gets answered.

One caution, about a common misconception. People say that public comment is where the council learns what the public thinks. Mostly, it isn't. Members know the room is unrepresentative: older, angrier, and more organized than the city.

So what does comment actually do? It creates a public record. It signals that someone is watching an issue. And occasionally, it hands a sympathetic member the story or the argument they needed.

That is worth three minutes. But keep it in proportion.

> Public comment is the opening move, not the strategy.

Let's see what has stuck. Near the start, I drew a line between two kinds of request. Bring that line back, because here is a harder case.

You want a stop sign at the corner on your street. What do you actually do first?

[long pause]

It sounds like a rule change, so it sounds like a job for the council. It isn't. Not at first.

In most cities, traffic-control devices follow engineering standards, and a traffic study by the department. So a stop sign starts its life as a service request. You file it with the transportation or public-works department, usually through three-one-one. And you copy your council member's office, so that it is on their radar.

The member becomes the lever only if the study stalls, or if the standard itself is the obstacle. Then the stop sign graduates to a policy request, through the committee that owns streets. Public comment, and the heavier machinery, come later. [beat] If at all.

## Your odds, and the rungs above the lectern

Now the honest question. Does any of this work? Two books frame what to expect.

The first is by Eric Oliver, from 2012: Local Elections and the Politics of Small-Scale Democracy. Oliver describes most American local politics as managerial democracy. The contests are low in information, and low in conflict. Incumbents win on custodial performance. Candidates are known through civic networks, not through media. And the electorate is small and unrepresentative, dominated by what he calls stakeholders: people with concentrated interests in land and services.

That is unflattering. It is also the opportunity. In a system where so few take part, organized persistence is disproportionately powerful.

The second book is Sarah Anzia's Timing and Turnout, from 2014. It makes the same point from the calendar. Off-cycle elections, the kind we met last time, shrink the electorate to the organized. Which is exactly why groups with payrolls on the line never miss one.

You cannot match a municipal union's stamina alone. But a neighborhood association can. A merchants' group can. A tenants' coalition can.

[pause]

A third scholar supplies the vertical axis. In 1969, Sherry Arnstein published a classic called "A Ladder of Citizen Participation." Picture that ladder. On a low rung, you are informed. Higher up, you are consulted. At the top is delegated power.

Where does public comment sit? On the consultation rung. And Arnstein filed consultation under a blunt word. [beat] Tokenism.

But cities offer real rungs above the lectern: planning commissions, zoning boards of appeal, budget advisory committees, police oversight bodies. These are appointed seats, and frequently too few people apply for them. On them, residents cast votes that bind, or that formally precede the council's own.

If you have expertise and patience, an application to a commission is worth fifty public comments.

[pause]

Now put all three scholars to work. Your group wants the city's rules on accessory dwelling units loosened. You can muster ten people, once.

Where is the highest leverage? Ten speakers at the final council vote, or something else? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

Something else. Comment at the final vote is consultation, after positions have set. That is Arnstein's tokenism rung, at the least decisive moment.

The higher-leverage plan has three parts. Get the issue onto the planning commission's agenda, and testify there, where the text is drafted. Put one or two of your members on the commission's public-review committees. And have the rest write, individually, to the members of the land-use committee, before the staff report is finalized.

> Small-scale democracy rewards being organized, early and persistent, not loud and late.

## The heavy machinery

Sometimes the council itself is the obstacle. For that, many cities give voters direct tools. Those cities are concentrated in the West, a Progressive-Era legacy. And there are three tools.

The ballot initiative. Residents draft an ordinance, or a charter amendment, and force a public vote on it by gathering signatures.

The referendum runs the other direction. It suspends an ordinance the council has just passed, and puts the survival of that ordinance to the voters. The signature deadline is usually short, measured in weeks.

And the recall. It removes an officeholder in the middle of a term.

[beat]

The initiative writes a law. The referendum challenges one. The recall removes an officeholder.

All three are governed by state law and by the city charter: signature thresholds, deadlines, single-subject rules. And none is available everywhere. Direct democracy is itself an item on the menu, one your state and your charter may or may not have ordered.

Treat these tools as the heavy machinery they are. Signature drives take organization and money. Opponents litigate the ballot titles. And a tool built to check machine politics is now deployed by well-funded interests as often as against them. That is Anzia's logic again, at petition scale.

Still, the mere credible threat of a referendum changes the council's math. That is why organized groups keep the manual on the shelf.

[pause]

And the deepest tool is the seat itself. Council races are frequently uncontested, or decided by a few hundred votes. Your city clerk publishes the filing windows and the signature requirements. People who began at the lectern now sit on dais after dais, across the country.

## What to expect, honestly

Set your clock by the institution's. An ordinance takes months, from idea to second reading. A budget request missed in spring waits a year. A commission appointment may take two application cycles.

And you are competing with professionals: land-use counsel for developers, union representatives, chamber lobbyists. They treat persistence as a job description.

Some defeats are structural. If the state has preempted the field, no amount of local organizing can pass the ordinance. That is the leash from last time, and the honest move is to redirect your energy to the legislature.

And one engagement at a time, the realistic win is incremental: an amendment, a study, a continuance, a relationship with a staffer who now returns your email.

## The whole path

Let's gather it up, in the order the institution actually processes it. Five steps to keep.

One. Diagnose. A service request goes to the department. A policy request goes to the council. And if the state has preempted the field, neither door opens.

[beat]

Two. Look up three things: your member, the committee that owns the issue, and the agenda packet.

[beat]

Three. The committee stage. Testify there, and send original written comments, while the text is still being drafted. The meeting is where decisions are recorded. They are made earlier.

[beat]

Four. The council meeting. Three minutes at the lectern, specific, personal and steady, plus a letter for the record. You are building the record, and signaling that the issue matters. Public comment is the opening move, not the strategy.

[beat]

And five. Escalate. Apply for a commission seat. Organize a coalition for the long haul, because small-scale democracy rewards being organized, early and persistent. Reach for the initiative, the referendum or the recall, where charter and state law allow. Or file for the seat itself.

[pause]

Now notice where the lectern sits. It is the step everyone pictures when they hear the words "civic engagement." It comes after the stages where outcomes are actually shaped. And it comes before the rungs where residents hold real power.

> On the engagement path, the lectern sits fourth of five.

What does a first month look like? Four small things. Looking up your member, and the committee that owns your issue. Subscribing to the agenda mailing list. Watching one meeting. And sending one specific, original email about one item. When you are back at a screen, the Coach in this app can help you draft it.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not a promise that showing up changes outcomes. The evidence is narrower than that. Organized, early, persistent showing up changes outcomes. The three minutes of the unorganized public mostly build the record.

And a deeper question stays open, one the last lecture flagged. This is a system highly responsive to those who take part, and empty of everyone else. Is it best repaired by moving local elections onto the federal calendar, or by redesigning participation itself? That is unresolved.

[beat]

There is a third repair. You, personally, raising the participation rate by one.

So go back to city hall. Two minutes to seven, on a Tuesday evening. The clerk is holding out a paper card. [beat] You know now that the card is the fourth step, not the first. And you know where the first three are.
