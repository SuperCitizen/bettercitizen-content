In January 2025, the city of Portland, Oregon, changed its government. Not the people in office. The design itself.

[beat]

For more than a century, Portland had been run by a commission. In that design, each elected commissioner personally runs a slice of the bureaucracy. Fire here. Parks there. Nearly every other big city had abandoned it. Portland was the last major American city still using it.

Then, in 2022, about 57 percent of Portland's voters approved an overhaul. The city rebooted itself with a council of 12 members, elected from four districts, a citywide mayor, and a professional city administrator.

[pause]

Here is the detail to sit with. Nothing about that choice was dictated by the United States Constitution. The State of Oregon could have blocked it, or rewritten it. And the city next door runs on an entirely different operating system.

The United States has 19,491 municipal governments, before you even count the towns and townships. The federal government has one constitutional design, endlessly litigated. City government is something else.

> City government is a menu.

Which item your city picked determines who actually holds power there. And most residents have never been told there was a menu at all.

[pause]

So let's open it. By the end, you will be able to say who really runs a city, and who chose its council. You will also know why a city sometimes cannot fix a problem that everyone can see.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the kitchen: where a city gets its power.

Second, the menu itself: the forms a city government can take.

Third, the council: how its members are elected, and by whom.

And last, the work: how a local law gets made, and how a state can unmake it.

## Creatures of the state

Before the menu, the kitchen. Where does a city's power come from?

Not from the Constitution. Cities appear nowhere in it. Their legal status was fixed in the nineteenth century, by an argument between two state judges.

The first was John F. Dillon, of Iowa. In a railroad case in 1868, Dillon held that cities are mere "creatures of the state." A city possesses only the powers its legislature expressly grants, and any doubt is settled against the city.

The second was Thomas Cooley, of Michigan. In 1871 he answered that local self-government was an inherent right, one the state must respect.

[beat]

Dillon won.

In 1907, in a case called Hunter v. City of Pittsburgh, the Supreme Court ratified his view. States, it held, may create, reshape, or abolish their municipalities, almost at will. [pause] And Dillon's Rule remains the default grammar of American local government.

> Under Dillon's Rule, a city has only the powers its state grants.

Put another way, a city does not begin with power and give some of it up. It begins with none, and holds what it is handed.

The states themselves softened the rule's harshest edge, with something called home rule. Under home rule, most state constitutions now let cities adopt their own charters, and legislate on local affairs, without asking permission item by item.

But notice what home rule is. It is a delegation. It is not sovereignty. The state hands the power down, and the state can reach back in and override the city. That overriding has a name: preemption.

> What the state grants, the state can preempt.

So hold two ideas at once. Your city council is the government closest to your daily life. And it serves at the pleasure of a legislature that may sit hundreds of miles away.

## The menu

That is the kitchen. Now the menu.

Two forms of city government dominate, and two minor ones survive. The labels sound bureaucratic. The stakes are not. Each form is a different answer to one question: where does executive power live?

Start with the form called mayor-council. You may know it as the strong mayor. Voters elect a council, which is the legislature, and they elect a mayor, who is a genuine chief executive. That mayor hires and fires department heads, drafts the budget, and vetoes ordinances.

This is the form of most very large, older cities: New York, Chicago, Houston, Philadelphia.

[beat]

The second form is called council-manager. Here the council sets policy, and then hires a professional to be the chief executive. That professional is the city manager. Think of a company. The council is the board, and the manager is the CEO, answerable to that board. And the mayor? Often a council member wearing a ceremonial sash.

The council-manager form was the signature municipal invention of the Progressive Era. It was designed, explicitly, to take administration away from the patronage system of machine politics, the world of Tammany Hall, and to give it to credentialed professionals. Today it governs Phoenix, San Antonio, Dallas and San Jose, and among sizable cities it is the most common form of all.

Then the two survivors.

The commission was born in Galveston, Texas, after the catastrophic hurricane of 1900. Commissioners are elected citywide, and each one runs a department: legislature and executive, fused. The form spread early, then collapsed under its own design. No one coordinates, and every commissioner defends a fiefdom. Under 1 percent of cities use it now.

The other survivor is the town meeting. In New England, about 5 percent of municipalities still legislate by an assembly of the voters themselves.

[pause]

Four forms. But keep your eye on the two that dominate, and on the one difference between them.

> Voters elect a strong mayor. The council hires a city manager.

In one city, voters choose the executive directly. In the other, the council buys executive capacity on the professional market.

And the form shapes the politics. A mayor-council city gets visible politics, driven by personality, and clearer accountability at the ballot box for how things turn out. A council-manager city trades that for administrative professionalism, and politics at a lower temperature. The cost is an executive no voter can fire.

Let's test that. Here is a scene.

In a council-manager city, residents are furious about how snow removal was handled. They want to "vote the bum out."

Who is the bum? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The official actually running snow removal is the city manager. Nobody elected the manager, and nobody can vote the manager out. [beat] Only the council can fire the manager.

So the voters' recourse is indirect. They replace council members, until the council replaces the manager.

That indirection is the design: professional administration, insulated from electoral pressure. Is that the cure for patronage politics, or a gap in accountability? [beat] It depends on the year you ask.

## Who elects the council

That is who runs a city. Now, who picks the council? There are three ways to elect one.

At-large: every voter in the city votes for every seat. By district, or ward: the city is mapped into districts, and each district elects a single member. Or a hybrid of the two.

That choice looks procedural. It is anything but.

The at-large system was another Progressive reform, meant to make each member serve "the whole city." In practice, it systematically favors candidates with citywide name recognition, and money. And it can do something more. It can let a bare citywide majority win every seat, and shut out entirely a minority community concentrated in a few neighborhoods.

Let's put numbers on that. Picture a city of 100,000 people. It is 30 percent Latino, and those residents are concentrated in three neighborhoods. The council has seven seats, all elected at-large. No Latino candidate has ever won one.

Now suppose the city switches to seven districts, one member each. That would likely change the result. Why? Work out the mechanics.

[pause 5s]

Here is the mechanism. At-large, the same citywide majority, 70 percent, votes in all seven contests, so it can outvote the minority seven times running. [beat] Thirty percent of every contest is zero percent of the seats.

Districts change the arithmetic. Those three neighborhoods now anchor two or three districts where Latino voters are the majority, and there they can elect the candidates they prefer.

> Districts convert geographic concentration into representation.

That arithmetic made at-large elections a central target of voting-rights lawsuits. And it is no thought experiment. In 2002, California's Voting Rights Act lowered the proof needed to challenge an at-large system. Before that law, 36 California cities elected their councils by district. Two decades later, by the count of the Rose Institute, [beat] 229 did. That is nearly half the state, and most switched under threat of a suit.

Districts carry a pathology of their own: the parochial trading of "my ward's project for yours." Defenders of at-large elections argue that their system prevents exactly that. And it is why many cities split the difference, with a hybrid.

[pause]

Two more choices quietly shape who governs.

First, most council elections are formally nonpartisan, with no party labels on the ballot.

Second, many council elections are off-cycle. They are held on dates with nothing federal on the ballot.

That sounds like a neutral scheduling quirk. It is not. In a 2014 book called Timing and Turnout, Sarah Anzia shows what off-cycle timing does. Turnout collapses. But organized groups turn out and mobilize regardless: municipal unions, homeowner associations, developers. So their votes weigh more.

How far does turnout collapse? A project at Portland State University studied who votes for mayor in America's 30 largest cities. In ten of them, the most recent mayoral election drew fewer than 15 percent of eligible voters. And voters over 65 took part at several times the rate of voters under 35.

> Whoever your council is, it was chosen by a small, old, organized electorate.

## What a council does

We know who runs the city, and who chose the council. Now, what does a council actually do?

A council is a legislature, and it produces two things. An ordinance is a binding local law. A resolution is a formal statement. Hold on to that pair. I will ask for it back.

The portfolio is the stuff of daily life. Zoning and land use. The city budget, and the property-tax rate. Parks and libraries, police budgets and oversight, water, streets and parking.

How does an ordinance become law? The path will sound familiar from Congress, in miniature. Let's walk it in order.

First, a proposal. A council member, the mayor, or city staff drafts an ordinance and gets it onto the docket.

Then, committee review. A standing committee, such as Finance, Public Safety, or Land Use, holds hearings, takes a staff report, and amends the text. And here is the quiet danger. An item can sit in committee indefinitely. Nothing forces a vote.

Next, a first reading. The full council takes up the proposal at a public meeting, with comment from citizens.

Then debate and amendment on the floor.

Then a second reading, and the final vote. Many city charters require passage at a second, later meeting before an ordinance is adopted.

Only then, executive action. In a mayor-council city, the mayor signs or vetoes, and the council can usually override a veto by a supermajority. In a council-manager city, the ordinance takes effect as the charter provides.

[pause]

Six stages. And as in Congress, the losses come early. Most ordinances that die, die before they reach the full council.

Now, that pair I asked you to hold. Tonight, your council wants to do two things. It wants to declare the city's support for a state ballot measure. And it wants to lower the default speed limit on residential streets.

One of those needs an ordinance. One needs a resolution. Which is which?

[long pause]

The declaration takes a resolution. The speed limit takes an ordinance.

Here is the reason. An ordinance is binding local law, and anything that regulates conduct needs one. A speed limit regulates conduct. A resolution is a formal statement of the council's position, with no regulatory force, and a declaration of support is exactly that.

> An ordinance binds. A resolution only states a position.

And even the speed limit has a catch. Often the council can change it only within limits the state vehicle code allows. [beat] Dillon's Rule again.

[pause]

These powers look small. So why do they generate such ferocious politics?

William Fischel, in a 2001 book called The Homevoter Hypothesis, supplies the motive. For most homeowners, the house is the dominant asset they own. It cannot be diversified, and it cannot be moved. So anything a council does that might touch property values sets off a defense of the family balance sheet: a zoning variance, a homeless shelter, an apartment building.

Fischel explains who shows up at ten o'clock at night to fight the fourplex.

## The leash

One stop is left: what the state can undo.

Early on, I gave you a rule named for an Iowa judge. What did it say about a city's powers? See if you can still say it.

[long pause]

Under Dillon's Rule, a city has only the powers its state grants. And what the state grants, the state can preempt.

That stopped being an abstraction the moment cities began legislating progressively in conservative states, and occasionally the other way around.

Take the minimum wage. The city of Birmingham, Alabama, set a minimum wage of 10 dollars and 10 cents. Days later, in 2016, the State of Alabama nullified it. [beat] That is the textbook case, and it is not alone. The Economic Policy Institute now counts 25 states that preempt local minimum-wage ordinances.

Nor does it end with wages. States have preempted local bans on plastic bags, local rent regulation, local firearm rules, and local anti-discrimination ordinances.

Then, in 2023, Texas escalated. It passed a law that opponents call the "Death Star" law. That law does not preempt one ordinance, or one subject. It preempts local regulation across entire fields, such as labor, agriculture and finance, unless the state explicitly authorizes it.

Houston, San Antonio and El Paso sued, and a trial judge declared the law unconstitutional. It took effect anyway, pending appeal. Then, in July 2025, a state appeals court upheld it.

[pause]

As of mid-2026, the leash is short, and getting shorter. And the open constitutional fights are about exactly how short.

Why does this matter to you? Because sooner or later you will ask: why doesn't my city just fix this? And the honest answer is often that the state took the tool away. [beat] Or never granted it.

> The first skill of local engagement: asking whether the council can solve the problem.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A city is a creature of its state. Under Dillon's Rule, a city has only the powers its state grants. Home rule is a delegation, not sovereignty.

[beat]

Two. City government is a menu, and the question on it is where executive power lives. Voters elect a strong mayor. The council hires a city manager.

[beat]

Three. How a council is elected decides who can win a seat: at-large, by district, or a hybrid. Districts convert geographic concentration into representation. And with nonpartisan, off-cycle elections, your council was chosen by a small, old, organized electorate.

[beat]

Four. An ordinance binds. A resolution only states a position. An ordinance travels from proposal, to committee, to two readings, to executive action, and most that die, die early.

[beat]

And five. What the state grants, the state can preempt. So before you ask why your city will not fix something, ask whether the council can solve it at all.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. The mechanics are yours now. What remains contested is a matter of values.

Does professional management beat an elected executive whom voters can hold to account? Do at-large or district elections produce better representation? That answer differs with what you mean by "represent."

Moving local elections onto the federal calendar may be a democratic repair. Or it may be, as Anzia documents its opponents arguing, a way to drown local issues in national noise.

And at some point, preemption stops being coherent statewide policy and becomes the strangling of self-government. Where that point lies is not settled either.

The next lecture turns from how the institution works to how you work the institution. It takes up the recall, the ballot initiative, and the unglamorous craft of the three-minute public comment.

For now, go back to Portland. For more than a century, the city kept a form that nearly everyone else had dropped. Then its voters picked up the menu, and ordered again. [beat] Your city ordered from the same menu. And now you know there was one.
