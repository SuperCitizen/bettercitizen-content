Two rulings, from the same court, in the same year.

[beat]

February 1969. The Supreme Court rules for a group of Iowa students, who had been suspended for wearing black armbands to protest the war in Vietnam.

June 1969. The Court rules for a Ku Klux Klan leader in Ohio, who had been convicted for what he said at a rally.

[pause]

Teenagers opposing a war, and a leader of the Klan. They had nothing in common [beat] except the sentence of the Constitution that protected them.

Did the Court endorse either message? It did not, and it did not need to. The First Amendment does not ask whether speech is admirable.

> The First Amendment asks only whether the government may punish the speech.

That is the key to reading the whole Bill of Rights. Each of its ten amendments is addressed to the government, and each one says what the government may not do.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to say who the Bill of Rights protects you from, and who it does not. We will take it in four steps.

First, where the list came from, and why the Constitution's leading defenders did not want it. Second, what the ten amendments cover. Third, a limit on the list's reach that surprises most people the first time they meet it. And last, the edges: the places where a right ends.

## A promise extracted

Start in 1787. The Constitution that left Philadelphia that year contained no bill of rights, and its leading defenders thought that was correct.

Alexander Hamilton made their case in the Federalist essays. Here is his question, from Federalist 84, in 1788. As you hear it, listen for what he takes for granted.

"For why declare that things shall not be done which there is no power to do?"

[pause]

No power to do. And Hamilton had an example ready.

"Why, for instance, should it be said that the liberty of the press shall not be restrained, [beat] when no power is given by which restrictions may be imposed?"

[pause]

Now a question, and the answer is in Hamilton's own words. What is he taking for granted about the new federal government? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Twice, he says that there is no power. So he is taking for granted that the government will stay inside its listed powers. On that assumption, a right needs no protection where no power threatens it.

That was the core of his argument. The new government would hold only the powers the Constitution listed, and nothing on that list allowed it to censor a newspaper or interfere with anyone's religion. A bill of rights would therefore be pointless.

And he went further. [beat] He called a bill of rights dangerous. Forbidding the government to do something, he reasoned, might be taken to imply that it otherwise could.

[pause]

Many Americans were not convinced. Among them was George Mason of Virginia, who had refused to sign the Constitution because it lacked a declaration of rights. His objections circulated widely.

These opponents made three points. The listed powers were broad. Congress could pass any law "necessary and proper" for carrying them out. And a government able to tax and to raise armies would find a way to reach the press, [beat] if it wanted one.

In plainer words, the listed powers were elastic, and officials tend to read their own authority generously. So the opponents wanted the limits in writing.

> A written limit gives a citizen, and a court, specific words to enforce.

Notice what the two sides shared. Both agreed that liberty should be protected. They disagreed about whether structure alone would do it.

And here is how it came out.

[beat]

> The opponents lost the vote on ratification, but they won this argument.

Ratification means approval, and the Constitution was approved. But several states approved it only on the understanding that amendments would follow.

Then James Madison changed course. Madison had shared Hamilton's view, but he had come to see three things.

He saw how much voters cared about these protections. He saw how a written list could teach citizens what their rights were. And he saw how adopting a modest set of amendments might head off opponents who wanted to rewrite the Constitution more drastically.

He introduced his list in the First Congress, on June 8, 1789. The House approved seventeen amendments. The Senate reduced them to twelve. [beat] The states ratified ten. And those ten took effect on December 15, 1791.

## What the ten amendments cover

That is where the list came from. Now, what is on it? The ten amendments are easier to remember in four groups, so let's take the groups in order.

The first group is belief and expression. It holds a single amendment, the First, which protects religion, speech, the press, assembly and petition.

[beat]

The second group is arms, homes and searches. The Second Amendment protects keeping and bearing arms. The Third says no soldiers may be quartered in homes, which means housed there. And the Fourth forbids unreasonable searches and seizures.

[beat]

The third group is fair process, and it runs from the Fifth Amendment to the Eighth. Here you find due process. You find the Fifth's protection against forced self-incrimination, which means being made to speak against yourself. You find the Sixth's promise of a lawyer and a jury in a criminal trial. And you find the Eighth's ban on cruel and unusual punishments.

[beat]

The fourth group is what is left over. The Ninth says the people keep rights the list does not name. The Tenth says that powers not given to the federal government stay with the states, or with the people.

[pause]

Belief and expression. Arms, homes and searches. Fair process. What is left over.

Now notice where the weight of that list falls. Half of the ten amendments concern one subject: [beat] how the government may investigate, try and punish a person. That was no accident.

The men who wrote the list remembered what they had accused the British Crown of doing. The Declaration of Independence had complained of "Quartering large bodies of armed troops among us." And it had complained of "depriving us in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury."

[pause]

Set those two complaints beside the list. Take the first one, quartering troops. Which amendment answers it?

[long pause]

The Third: no soldiers quartered in homes. And the complaint about trial by jury is answered by the jury clauses of the Sixth and Seventh Amendments. The answers match the complaints almost point for point.

> Much of the Bill of Rights reads as a list of things already done.

Done by a government, to the very people who wrote the list.

## Who is bound

Now for the limit that surprises people. The First Amendment opens with five words that are easy to hurry past. [beat] "Congress shall make no law."

[pause]

Those five words say who is bound. First Amendment rights, like the rest of the Bill of Rights, are protections against the government.

> The Bill of Rights protects you against the government, and against no one else.

So who counts as the government? A public school does, and so do a city police department and a state university. All of them are bound.

A private employer is not bound. Neither is a private school, or a company that runs a social media platform.

Here is what that means in practice. When a platform removes a post, or an employer fires someone over a comment, the First Amendment is not violated, because no government has acted. Other laws may apply to those situations. [beat] This one does not.

It follows that the same act can be lawful or unlawful, depending on who performs it. A state university that punishes a student for a political opinion has a constitutional problem. A private college that does the same thing [beat] does not.

Let's test that on four situations. In which one has the First Amendment been violated?

One. A social media company deletes a user's post about an election.

Two. A private employer fires a worker for a comment made online.

Three. A city fines a resident for a yard sign criticizing the mayor.

Four. A private school expels a student for wearing a protest shirt.

Take a moment.

[long pause]

The third. [beat] Only the city is the government. The company, the employer and the private school may be acting unfairly, and other laws may limit what they can do. But the First Amendment restrains government action alone.

Now change one word in the fourth situation. Make the school a public one, and that case would raise a First Amendment question too.

## Rights have edges

Now the last step: the edges. A right that is addressed to the government is still not unlimited. Much of constitutional law consists of tests for where a right ends.

The two cases from 1969 each supplied one.

Take the Klan case first. It is called Brandenburg v. Ohio. The Court held that a state may not punish speech that merely advocates force or lawbreaking.

A state may punish such speech only when two things are true, and here the Court's own words matter. The speech must be "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action." [beat] And it must be "likely to incite or produce such action."

[pause]

To incite is to stir people into acting, and imminent means about to happen. So the test has two parts. The speech is aimed at lawbreaking that is about to happen, and the speech is likely to bring it about.

> Under Brandenburg, ugly ideas are protected. Incitement to immediate violence is not.

Now the armband case, Tinker v. Des Moines. The Court held that students keep their rights "at the schoolhouse gate." But it also recognized that a school is not a public park.

So school officials may restrict student speech that would substantially disrupt the work of the school. They may not restrict it merely because they dislike the message, or fear that someone might take offense at it.

> Under Tinker, a school may restrict speech that would substantially disrupt its work.

Now put the two tests side by side, and think back to how this lecture began. One test asks whether speech is directed to imminent lawless action, and likely to produce it. The other asks whether speech would substantially disrupt the work of a school. [beat] What does neither test ask?

[long pause]

Neither test asks which viewpoint is being expressed.

That is how one year could hold both rulings. The rule that protected a Klan leader in June is the same rule that protected teenagers opposing a war in February. [beat] It is the only kind of rule that could protect both.

> Neither speech test depends on the viewpoint being expressed.

Speech is not the only right with edges. Other amendments carry their limits in their own words. The Fourth does not forbid all searches, only "unreasonable" ones. And deciding what is reasonable, in a world of cars, phones and cameras, is work the text hands to judges, case by case.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Bill of Rights is addressed to the government. Each of its ten amendments says what the government may not do.

[beat]

Two. The list was a promise. Hamilton asked why declare that things shall not be done which there is no power to do. His opponents lost the vote on ratification, but they won this argument, and ten amendments took effect in 1791.

[beat]

Three. The ten amendments fall into four groups. Belief and expression. Arms, homes and searches. Fair process. What is left over. And half of the ten concern how the government may investigate, try and punish a person.

[beat]

Four. The Bill of Rights protects you against the government, and against no one else. So the same act can be lawful or unlawful, depending on who performs it.

[beat]

And five. Rights have edges, and courts mark them with tests. Under Brandenburg, ugly ideas are protected, and incitement to immediate violence is not. Under Tinker, a school may restrict speech that would substantially disrupt its work. Neither test depends on the viewpoint being expressed.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Where do the edges belong? That is argued in every generation. Courts are still working out how tests written for pamphlets and armbands apply to speech posted online from a student's bedroom. On that question, reasonable judges disagree.

[pause]

And a larger gap remains. In 1791, the Bill of Rights restrained only the federal government. A state legislature, a city police force and a public school were all beyond its reach. [beat] Which means that neither of the 1969 cases could have been brought when the amendments were new.

How did that change? That is the subject of the next lecture.

For now, go back to 1969. February, and then June. Teenagers in armbands, and a leader of the Klan. The sentence that protected them both did not ask whether their speech was admirable. [beat] It asked only whether the government may punish it.
