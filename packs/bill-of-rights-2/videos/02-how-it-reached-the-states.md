## The wharf in Baltimore

John Barron owned one of the most profitable wharves in Baltimore. Then the city rebuilt its streets, and every heavy rain washed sand and earth down to the waterfront, until the water at his wharf was too shallow for large ships.

Barron sued the city. He relied on the Fifth Amendment's promise that private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation.

- John Barron against Baltimore

## The Court's answer, 1833

In 1833 the Supreme Court ruled against him, without ever deciding whether his property had been taken. Chief Justice John Marshall held that the Bill of Rights did not apply to Baltimore, to Maryland or to any other state.

- 1833: Barron v. Baltimore

For decades afterward, a state could restrict speech, search a home without a warrant or try a defendant without a lawyer. It answered only to its own constitution.

> In 1833 the Bill of Rights restrained the federal government alone.

## No State shall

The change began with the Civil War. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, was written with the conduct of the states in mind. The First Amendment begins "Congress shall make no law." This one begins "No State shall."

- 1868: the Fourteenth Amendment

It forbids a state to take life, liberty or property without "due process of law." Over many years the Court concluded that certain rights are so fundamental that no state can deny them without denying due process. It identified those rights one case at a time. The method is called selective incorporation.

- Selective incorporation: one right at a time
- The route: the Due Process Clause

> The Bill of Rights reached the states one right at a time.

## One doctrine, different beneficiaries

In 1963, in Gideon v. Wainwright, a unanimous Court held that Florida had to provide a lawyer to a poor man charged with a felony. The decision was welcomed by advocates for the rights of defendants.

In 2010, in McDonald v. City of Chicago, the Court held by five votes to four that the right to keep and bear arms binds states and cities. The decision was welcomed by advocates for gun rights.

- Gideon, 1963: a lawyer
- McDonald, 2010: keeping and bearing arms

The audiences were very different. The legal reasoning is the same.

> Once a right is judged fundamental, it binds every level of government.

## Who decides?

Someone has to decide which rights are fundamental. Under the method the Court chose, that someone is the Court itself. Whether nine judges should hold that power is an argument that has not ended.

- Which rights are fundamental? The Court decides
