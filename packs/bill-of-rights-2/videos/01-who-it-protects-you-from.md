## Two rulings in 1969

In February 1969 the Supreme Court ruled for a group of Iowa students, suspended for wearing black armbands to protest the war in Vietnam. In June of the same year it ruled for a Ku Klux Klan leader in Ohio, convicted for what he said at a rally.

- February 1969: the armband students
- June 1969: a Klan leader

The students and the Klan leader had nothing in common except the sentence of the Constitution that protected them.

## The question it asks

The Court did not endorse either message, and it did not need to. The First Amendment does not ask whether speech is admirable. It asks only whether the government may punish it.

That is the key to the whole Bill of Rights. Each of its ten amendments is addressed to the government, and says what the government may not do.

> Each of the ten amendments says what the government may not do.

## Who is bound

The First Amendment opens with five words that are easy to pass over: "Congress shall make no law."

A public school, a city police department and a state university are all parts of the government, and all of them are bound. A private employer, a private school and a company that runs a social media platform are not.

- Bound: public school, police, state university
- Not bound: private employer, private platform

When a platform removes a post, or an employer fires someone over a comment, the First Amendment is not violated, because no government has acted. Other laws may apply. This one does not.

> No government action, no First Amendment violation.

## Rights have edges

A right addressed to the government is still not unlimited. Each case from 1969 supplied a test.

In the Klan case, Brandenburg v. Ohio, the Court held that a state may not punish speech that merely advocates force or lawbreaking. It may punish such speech only when it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action, and is likely to produce it.

- Brandenburg: incitement to imminent lawless action

[check q3]

In the armband case, Tinker v. Des Moines, the Court held that school officials may restrict student speech that would substantially disrupt the work of the school. They may not restrict it merely because they dislike the message.

- Tinker: substantial disruption, not dislike

> Neither test depends on the viewpoint being expressed.

[check q4]

## Still argued

Where the edges belong is argued in every generation. Courts are still working out how these tests apply to speech posted online, and reasonable judges disagree.

- The edges are still disputed
