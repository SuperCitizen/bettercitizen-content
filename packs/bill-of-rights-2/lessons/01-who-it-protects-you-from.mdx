# Who does the Bill of Rights protect you from?

In February 1969 the Supreme Court ruled for a group of Iowa students who had been suspended for wearing black armbands to protest the war in Vietnam. In June of the same year it ruled for a Ku Klux Klan leader in Ohio who had been convicted for what he said at a rally.

The students and the Klan leader had nothing in common except the sentence of the Constitution that protected them. The Court did not endorse either message, and it did not need to, because the First Amendment does not ask whether speech is admirable. It asks only whether the government may punish it.

That is the key to reading the whole Bill of Rights, since each of its ten amendments is addressed to the government and says what the government may not do. This lesson looks at where the list came from, what it covers, and a limit on its reach that surprises most people who meet it for the first time.

## A promise extracted

The Constitution that left Philadelphia in 1787 contained no bill of rights, and its leading defenders thought that was correct. Alexander Hamilton made the case in the Federalist essays.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Federalist No. 84 (1788)">

"For why declare that things shall not be done which there is no power to do? Why, for instance, should it be said that the liberty of the press shall not be restrained, when no power is given by which restrictions may be imposed?"

</Callout>

Hamilton's argument was that the new government would hold only the powers the Constitution listed, and that nothing on that list allowed it to censor a newspaper or interfere with anyone's religion. A bill of rights would therefore be pointless, and he went further and called it dangerous, since forbidding the government to do something might be taken to imply that it otherwise could.

Many Americans were not convinced, among them George Mason of Virginia, who had refused to sign the Constitution because it lacked a declaration of rights and whose objections circulated widely. The opponents pointed out that the listed powers were broad, that Congress could pass any law "necessary and proper" for carrying them out, and that a government able to tax and to raise armies would find a way to reach the press if it wanted one.

The opponents lost the vote on ratification but won this argument, because several states approved the Constitution only on the understanding that amendments would follow. James Madison, who had shared Hamilton's view, changed course. He had come to see how much voters cared about these protections, how a written list could teach citizens what their rights were, and how adopting a modest set of amendments might head off opponents who wanted to rewrite the Constitution more drastically. He introduced his list in the First Congress on June 8, 1789. The House approved seventeen amendments, the Senate reduced them to twelve, and the states ratified ten, which took effect on December 15, 1791.

<Checkpoint question="Hamilton argues that a bill of rights is unnecessary. What does his argument assume about the federal government, and what would his opponents say is wrong with that assumption?">

Hamilton assumes that the government will stay inside its listed powers, so that a right needs no protection where no power threatens it. His opponents answered that the listed powers were broad and elastic, and that officials tend to read their own authority generously. A written limit gives a citizen, and a court, specific words to enforce. Both sides agreed that liberty should be protected and disagreed about whether structure alone would do it.

</Checkpoint>

The weight of the list falls on the third group, and that was no accident. The men who wrote it remembered what they had accused the British Crown of doing. The Declaration of Independence had complained of "Quartering large bodies of armed troops among us" and of "depriving us in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury." The Third Amendment and the jury clauses of the Sixth and Seventh answer those complaints almost point for point, so that much of the Bill of Rights reads as a list of things a government had already done to its authors.

## The near miss: who is bound

The First Amendment opens with five words that are easy to read past: "Congress shall make no law." [[term:first-amendment]] rights, like the rest of the Bill of Rights, are protections against the government and against no one else.

A public school, a city police department and a state university are all parts of the government, and all of them are bound, while a private employer, a private school and a company that runs a social media platform are not. When a platform removes a post or an employer fires someone over a comment, the First Amendment is not violated, because no government has acted. Other laws may apply to those situations, but this one does not. The same act can therefore be lawful or unlawful depending on who performs it: a state university that punishes a student for a political opinion has a constitutional problem, while a private college that does the same thing does not.

## Rights have edges

A right that is addressed to the government is still not unlimited, and much of constitutional law consists of tests for where a right ends.

The two cases from 1969 each supplied one. In the Klan case, *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, the Court held that a state may not punish speech that merely advocates force or lawbreaking. It may punish such speech only when it is "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action." Ugly ideas are protected under that test, and incitement to immediate violence is not.

In the armband case, *Tinker v. Des Moines*, the Court held that students keep their rights "at the schoolhouse gate," and it also recognized that a school is not a public park. School officials may restrict student speech that would substantially disrupt the work of the school. They may not restrict it merely because they dislike the message or fear that someone might take offense at it.

Other amendments carry their limits in their own words. The Fourth does not forbid all searches, only "unreasonable" ones, and it allows a warrant to issue on "probable cause." Deciding what is reasonable in a world of cars, phones and cameras is work the text hands to judges, case by case.

Notice that neither of the speech tests depends on the viewpoint being expressed. The rule that protected a Klan leader in June is the same rule that protected teenagers opposing a war in February, which is the only kind of rule that could protect both.

## What this lesson does not settle

Where the edges belong is argued in every generation, and courts are still working out how tests written for pamphlets and armbands apply to speech posted online from a student's bedroom, a question on which reasonable judges disagree.

A larger gap remains as well, because in 1791 the Bill of Rights restrained only the federal government. A state legislature, a city police force and a public school were all beyond its reach, which means that neither of the 1969 cases could have been brought when the amendments were new.
