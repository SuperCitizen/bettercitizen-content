# The Data

Two numbers from the same election. In June 2021, New York City ran the largest ranked-choice election in American history, and 95 percent of voters told Edison Research exit pollsters the ballot was simple to complete. In that same election, about 140,000 ballots — roughly 15 percent — ranked neither of the two finalists for mayor and sat inactive in the final round. Advocates quote the first number. Opponents quote the second. Both are real, and a book that shows you only one of them is not a book of evidence; it's a brochure. So here is the full ledger as the research actually stands — what's strong, what's modest, and what cuts against me — because the case for [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] is strong enough to survive its own footnotes.

## Turnout: modest where it's direct, strong where it's structural

Start with the claim you've probably heard in its inflated form: "RCV increases turnout." The careful version is less exciting. David Kimball and Joseph Anthony, comparing RCV cities to demographically similar plurality cities, found no statistically significant change in general-election [[term:voter-turnout]]. That's the null result, and it deserves to be stated plainly.

Now the findings layered on top of it. Eamon McGinn, exploiting the staggered adoption of RCV in Minnesota's Twin Cities metro, estimated a 9.6 percentage-point turnout increase in mayoral elections there, largest in higher-poverty precincts. Courtney Juelich and Joseph Coll, matching individual voter records across seven RCV and fourteen non-RCV local elections, found no difference for the general public — but young voters were about 9 points more likely to vote in RCV cities. And in 2024, Dowling, Tolbert, Micatka and Donovan published the mechanism: voters in RCV cities report significantly more campaign contact and more in-person candidate outreach, because a candidate who needs your second choice has a reason to knock on your door even when you're wearing a rival's button. My honest summary: direct turnout effects are modest and context-dependent — positive in some designs, absent in others, concentrated among the young and the lightly mobilized.

The structural effect is a different story, and it is the one I'll defend without hedging. Where RCV replaces a separate [[term:runoff-election]], the turnout gain is mechanical and large. [[org:fairvote|FairVote]] examined every regularly scheduled federal primary runoff from 1994 to 2022 — 276 of them — and turnout fell between the first and second round in 266, or 96 percent, with a *median* decline of 40 percent. A December runoff is an election held for the most motivated tenth of the electorate, at full taxpayer price. An instant runoff asks everyone's backup preference while everyone is still in the room. You don't need a regression to see which method counts more voters; you need subtraction.

<Checkpoint question="A campaign flyer says 'Studies show RCV boosts turnout.' A skeptic's op-ed says 'Studies show RCV does nothing for turnout.' Based on this section, what would an honest one-sentence summary say?">
  Both are cherry-picking the same literature. Direct effects on general-election turnout are modest and mixed — null overall in matched-city comparisons (Kimball & Anthony), positive in specific settings (about 9.6 points in Twin Cities mayoral races; about 9 points for young voters) — while the large, undisputed gain comes when RCV replaces low-turnout runoffs, which shed a median 40 percent of voters between rounds. The defensible claim is structural, not magical.
</Checkpoint>

## Civility: real, measurable — and bounded

The civility claim is the one voters volunteer first, so political scientists went looking. Todd Donovan, Caroline Tolbert and Kellen Gracey surveyed voters in RCV cities and in matched plurality cities holding local elections, and found that voters in RCV cities were significantly more satisfied with the conduct of campaigns and less likely to perceive them as negative. Candidates themselves reported subtle differences — more appeals for second-choice support, more cross-endorsements.

Then, in 2023, Donovan and Tolbert revisited the question with new data, including New York's 2021 mayoral primary, and published a paper whose subtitle should be framed on every advocate's wall: "Does Evidence Fit the Normative Narrative?" Their answer: only partly. Voters do perceive less negativity; about a third report candidates explicitly asking for second- or third-place rankings; but the positive-tone effects did not extend to frontrunners — the candidates with the most at stake campaign about as hard either way. The incentive is real (I showed you Murkowski and Peltola ranking each other in the Alaska chapter), but it is a thumb on the scale, not a personality transplant for American politics. That is what I will claim, because that is what the data support.

## Representation: the pipeline opens

Here the numbers are striking, with one caveat I'll flag myself. RepresentWomen's tracking of a decade of municipal RCV elections (2010–2019) finds women winning 48 percent of them; as of its 2020 report, women held 46 percent of RCV-elected mayoralties, against roughly a quarter of comparable mayoralties nationally. New York's first ranked-choice council election in 2021 produced the first majority-women city council in the city's history. And a 2025 study in the *American Journal of Political Science* by Jonathan Colner found the mechanism upstream of the results: RCV increases *candidate entry*, particularly among women and candidates of color, because a crowded field no longer means mutually assured destruction by vote-splitting — a finding consistent with round-by-round data showing candidates of color gaining, not losing, as ballots transfer.

The caveat: cities that adopt RCV are not random cities. They are disproportionately places already trending toward diverse candidate pools, and the observational studies can't fully untangle that. The entry-effect research is the strongest causal evidence we have; the raw win percentages are suggestive, not proof. I'd rather tell you that than have an opponent tell you I didn't.

## The costs, measured honestly

Two costs show up consistently in the data, and you met both in the last chapter. First, ballot errors: Pettigrew and Radley's analysis puts the rejection rate for ranked contests at 0.53 percent, about ten times the 0.04 percent of pick-one contests on the same ballots, with the gap concentrated in lower-income, lower-education precincts. The mitigations are real but partial: Cormack's 2026 follow-up found New York's error rates falling with each RCV cycle, and 89.3 percent of the city's 2021 Democratic primary voters successfully ranked multiple candidates in at least one race. In all four of those elections, the winner finished with less than half of *total* votes cast — 43.4 to 45.7 percent — which is the factual core of the "faux majority" charge, and I won't dispute the arithmetic. What I dispute is the baseline. Under [[term:plurality]] rules those same winners would have won with the same pluralities and *no* losing voter would have had any say between the finalists; under a traditional runoff, a median 40 percent of voters simply don't return. Exhaustion is the visible version of a loss every system imposes — RCV is the only one that prints it in the tabulation report where critics can quote it. And the design lever works: where rankings are uncapped or capped at five instead of three, exhaustion falls; Alaska's August 2022 special-election count carried 94 percent of ballots into the final round.

## The scoreboard

| Claim | Best evidence | My verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Replaces low-turnout runoffs with one high-turnout decision | 96% of 276 federal runoffs lost turnout; median −40% (FairVote) | Strong |
| Majority-backed winners among continuing ballots | Every IRV tabulation by construction; exhaustion 9.6–27.1% under 3-rank caps (Burnett & Kogan) | Strong, with a real asterisk |
| More candidate entry; more women and candidates of color running and winning | Colner (2025, *AJPS*); RepresentWomen 2010–2019 data | Promising, partly observational |
| Less negative campaigning | Donovan/Tolbert/Gracey (2016); Donovan & Tolbert (2023): "only partly fits" | Modest and real, not transformative |
| Direct turnout boost | Kimball & Anthony null; McGinn +9.6 pts (mayoral); Juelich & Coll +9 pts (youth only) | Mixed, context-dependent |
| Voters can handle it | 95% "simple" (Edison/Common Cause NY); 89.3% ranked multiple (NYC CFB); errors 0.53% vs. 0.04% and falling with experience (Pettigrew & Radley; Cormack) | Yes — with an equity obligation |

Read the table the way a skeptic would and it still says this: every cost of ranked-choice voting is measurable, bounded, and shrinkable by design choices, while the costs of the status quo — spoiled elections, December runoffs nobody attends, fields cleared by fear of vote-splitting — are structural and permanent. What this chapter does not settle is whether these American findings hold at scale and over decades. For that, we leave the country: Australia has been ranking ballots since 1918, and Ireland's voters have twice refused to give them up. That evidence is next.
