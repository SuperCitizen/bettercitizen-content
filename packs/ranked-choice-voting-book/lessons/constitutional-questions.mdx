# Constitutional Questions

On November 13, 2018, Congressman Bruce Poliquin led Maine's Second District race on first choices, 46.3 percent to 45.6 — with no majority, and the state's brand-new ranked-choice count about to run. So he went to federal court and asked a judge to stop it: counting the voters' backup preferences, his lawyers argued, would violate the United States Constitution. The judge — Lance Walker, appointed by President Trump and on the bench barely a month — declined to halt the count, let the transfers run, and a month later rejected every one of Poliquin's constitutional claims on the merits. Jared Golden took his seat in Congress.

Opponents of ranked choice voting have made the constitutional argument in Minnesota, California, Maine, and Alaska, in state court and federal court, before elections and after them. Here is the scorecard you should carry around: under the U.S. Constitution, the challenges have lost every time. The one place ranked choice voting has genuinely lost on the law is a different place than most people think — and it teaches reformers something important. Both halves of that story are this chapter.

## What the Constitution actually says about how we count

Start with the text, because the text is almost the whole answer. The Constitution tells us *who* runs congressional elections — the Elections Clause of Article I, Section 4 gives states the power over "Times, Places and Manner," subject to congressional override — and says nothing about *how a winner must be calculated*. No clause requires plurality winners. The word "plurality" does not appear. States have run majority-requirement elections since the founding era, and Georgia ran a full statewide runoff for a U.S. Senate seat as recently as December 2022; if the Constitution silently mandated plurality rules, all of that would be unconstitutional, and nobody has ever seriously believed it is. This is [[term:federalism]] doing what it was built to do: states experimenting with the machinery of their own democracy.

That history is why Poliquin's lead argument — that Article I's provision for election "by the People" implies a plurality count — went nowhere in *Baber v. Dunlap*. The court entered judgment for the state on every claim: Article I, [[term:due-process|due process]], the First Amendment, the Voting Rights Act. Poliquin dropped the suit before New Year's. And when four Maine voters returned in 2020 to challenge ranked ballots in the presidential race, Judge Walker was more direct. His "limited charge," he wrote in *Hagopian v. Dunlap*, was "to determine only whether the RCV Act is contrary to the text of the United States Constitution. It is not."

<Checkpoint question="A friend says: 'Surely the Founders assumed the candidate with the most votes wins — so plurality is baked into the Constitution.' What's the strongest two-sentence rebuttal, using only things courts have actually relied on?">
  The Constitution assigns states the manner of elections and never prescribes a counting rule — and the founding generation itself ran elections requiring majorities, so plurality can't be a hidden constitutional command. Georgia still elects senators by majority-or-runoff; if "most votes in one round" were constitutionally required, that system would have been struck down long ago.
</Checkpoint>

## One person, one vote — the myth that won't die

The most persistent legal attack sounds intuitive: if my ballot gets counted for my second choice after my first is eliminated, didn't I just vote twice? The doctrine being invoked is real — *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) grounded "one person, one vote" in the [[term:equal-protection|Equal Protection Clause]] — but it's about districts of unequal population, where one person's vote literally weighs more than another's. Ranked-choice tabulation has no such asymmetry, and you can verify it by just watching the count: in every round, every active ballot counts exactly once, for exactly one candidate. A ranking isn't extra votes; it's standing instructions for your *one* vote.

The leading federal case is *Dudum v. Arntz* (2011), where San Francisco voters attacked the city's instant runoff — interestingly, from the opposite direction, arguing that the city's three-ranking limit disenfranchised voters whose ballots exhausted. Judge Marsha Berzon's opinion for the Ninth Circuit upheld the system: ballots are not counted unequally, exhausted ballots reflect the voter's own choice to rank no further, and the burden on voting rights is minimal. The court rejected the equal-protection theory and a Voting Rights Act claim besides. Two years earlier, the Minnesota Supreme Court had reached the same destination in *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis* (2009), rejecting right-to-vote, association, and equal-protection challenges under both the federal and state constitutions before Minneapolis had counted a single ranked ballot.

## Where RCV actually lost — and why it wasn't the U.S. Constitution

Now the loss, because honest advocacy reports its losses. In 2017, Maine's Supreme Judicial Court issued a unanimous advisory opinion that the ranked-choice law Mainers had just passed conflicted with the *Maine* Constitution — which provides that the governor and state legislators are elected "by a plurality of all votes returned." A nineteenth-century clause, written to end the chaos of majority-requirement elections that repeatedly failed to produce winners, now froze single-round counting into place for state general elections. A majority, the justices noted, is always a plurality — but the multi-round tabulation that *finds* that majority is not the single count the clause contemplates. The result is the odd compromise Maine lives with today: ranked ballots in primaries and in all federal elections, but old-style plurality counts for governor and the Legislature in November.

And the ceiling has held. In April 2026, the same court unanimously advised that LD 1666, a bill to extend ranked choice voting to those state general elections, would be unconstitutional for the same reason — a ranked ballot's "series of instructions," the justices wrote, is inconsistent with the constitutional concept of a single vote. Until Mainers amend their constitution, that's the law.

Read the loss precisely, though. No court — Maine's included — has ever held that ranked choice voting violates anyone's *rights*. The Maine opinions are about a specific textual clause in one state constitution; Alaska's Supreme Court, facing its own constitution in *Kohlhaas v. State* (2022), upheld top-four ranked-choice elections outright. The practical lesson for reformers is about drafting, not doctrine: read your state constitution *first*, and if it contains an election-method clause, the honest path is a constitutional amendment, not a statute and a prayer.

<Checkpoint question="Your state's constitution says state senators are elected 'by the greatest number of votes.' A reform group asks whether an RCV statute is enough, or whether they need a constitutional amendment. What do you tell them — and which precedent makes you cautious?">
  Be cautious: Maine's plurality clause sounds equally innocuous, and its high court read it — unanimously, twice, in 2017 and again in 2026 — as freezing single-round counting into place for the offices it covers, even though an RCV winner ultimately *has* the greatest number of votes in the final round. A court could read your clause either way, so the durable route is an amendment, or applying RCV first to offices the clause doesn't reach (Maine kept primaries and federal races). The federal Constitution is no obstacle either way; your state's text is the question.
</Checkpoint>

## The fights that remain

So what's actually still contested, legally, as of mid-2026? Three things worth your attention.

First, statutory bans. Nineteen states now prohibit ranked choice voting by statute — Ohio became the nineteenth in March 2026. A ban is not a constitutional holding; it is a policy choice by a legislature, repealable by a legislature. The lawyers lost the courtroom argument, and the argument moved to statehouses, which is itself a kind of concession about where the law stands.

Second, the Voting Rights Act. Courts have rejected VRA challenges to ranked choice voting so far — *Dudum* did — and the more interesting live question runs the other direction: election lawyers have proposed ranked systems *as remedies* in vote-dilution cases. The Congressional Research Service's survey of this litigation (LSB10837) is the sober reference if you want the full map, including what Congress could do under its Elections Clause power — which cuts both ways, toward mandating ranked ballots for federal races or toward banning them.

Third, procedural warfare. The most recent high-profile RCV lawsuit wasn't about counting at all: in Washington, D.C., the local Democratic Party and its chairman sued to keep Initiative 83 — ranked choice voting plus semi-open primaries — from reaching voters, on ballot-procedure grounds. The courts let the vote proceed, and in November 2024 about 73 percent of D.C. voters approved it, carrying every ward. When a party establishment's best remaining legal theory is about petition timing rather than the Constitution, you've learned where the merits stand.

## Settled and unsettled

Settled: ranked choice voting is constitutional under existing federal doctrine — one person, one vote included — and every federal court to reach the merits has said so. Settled, too: a state constitution can still say no, as Maine's does for its state offices, which is a drafting problem reformers must respect. Unsettled: how Voting Rights Act doctrine and ranked ballots will interact as more jurisdictions adopt them, and whether Congress ever uses its Elections Clause power in either direction.

The deepest thing this chapter should leave you with is the asymmetry. The status-quo side's strongest honest argument was never that ranked choice voting is *unlawful* — it's that it is *unwise*, a case they're entitled to make and voters are entitled to weigh. The Constitution, it turns out, is neutral on counting methods. Which means the fight over ranked choice voting is exactly what it should be: political. The next chapter is about how to win it, state by state.
