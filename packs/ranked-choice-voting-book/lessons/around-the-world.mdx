# Around the World

On December 14, 1918, in the sheep country of western Victoria, voters in the Australian district of Corangamite did something no national electorate had done before: they ranked their candidates. James Scullin — a future Labor prime minister — led the first count with about 42 percent. Under the American way of counting, he would have won right there. But Australia had just rewritten its election law, and as the trailing candidates were eliminated and their ballots flowed to second choices, a farmer named William Gibson overtook him and won with about 56 percent of the final count. The majority, not merely the largest faction, prevailed. That was more than a century ago. So when an op-ed tells you [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] is an untested experiment, I want you to hear the claim for what it is: a confession that the author never looked past the water's edge.

## Australia: a century of proof

Here is the part of the story American conservatives should love: ranked ballots came to Australia not from progressive reformers but from a conservative government protecting itself from the spoiler effect. In 1918 the brand-new Country Party began running candidates against the governing Nationalists in rural seats, splitting the anti-Labor vote. That October, in a by-election for the Western Australian seat of Swan, Labor took the seat with barely a third of the vote while two candidates of the right divided the rest. Billy Hughes's Nationalist government answered within weeks: the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918 introduced preferential voting — what Americans now call [[term:instant-runoff]] voting — so that allied parties could compete against each other without handing the seat to their common opponent. The first test was Corangamite that December. The spoiler problem, solved by statute, by the right, before the end of World War I.

And it has run ever since. Every Australian House of Representatives election for over a hundred years has been conducted with ranked ballots — through depression, war, and dozens of changes of government. Australians rank *every* candidate on the ballot, by law. Layer on [[issue:compulsory-voting]], adopted in 1924, and you get something no American election has ever had: majority winners chosen with near-universal participation. Roughly nine in ten enrolled Australians cast a ballot, election after election — a [[term:voter-turnout]] figure American officials would describe as a typo. In 1948 Australia doubled down, switching its Senate to the single transferable vote (STV) — the proportional, multi-winner cousin of instant runoff — first used at the 1949 election. One country, both ranked systems, running side by side for three-quarters of a century.

Let me be straight about what Australia does and does not prove. It does not prove that ranked ballots dissolve two-party politics: the House remains dominated by two big blocs, because single-winner instant runoff is a majoritarian system, not proportional representation. And Australia's requirement to rank every candidate pushes some incomplete ballots into the "informal" (rejected) pile — a real cost, and one most American designs avoid by allowing partial rankings. What a century of Australian elections *does* prove is the thing opponents claim to doubt: ordinary voters, at national scale, on paper ballots, can rank candidates — and did so for decades before the computer existed.

  Vote-splitting on the right: the Country Party and the Nationalists were dividing the anti-Labor vote, letting Labor win seats with a third of the vote. Ranked ballots let allied parties compete without electing their common opponent. The mechanism is partisan-neutral — it protects whichever side happens to have more candidates than coordination, which is why it has been adopted, at different times, by parties across the spectrum.

## Ireland: the voters defended it — twice

Ireland has elected its parliament by STV since independence, and in 1937 wrote it into the constitution itself: Article 16.2.5° requires that members of the Dáil be elected "on the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote." Irish constituencies elect three to five members each, so seats in the Dáil track vote shares closely — one of the most proportional outcomes any legislature achieves. And Ireland still counts those ranked paper ballots *by hand*, in public count centres, over days, with the eliminations and transfers broadcast like sport. So much for "the machines can't handle it."

But here is my favorite part of the Irish story, and the part I quote at every town hall. Twice, a governing party asked Irish voters to abandon STV for first-past-the-post — the [[term:plurality]] system the United States uses. Twice, the voters said no. In 1959, Éamon de Valera's government put the switch to a referendum; voters rejected it by roughly 52 to 48 — on the very same day they elected de Valera president. They liked the man; they refused to give up the ballot. The government tried again in 1968 and lost by roughly 61 to 39 — a wider margin the second time. Think about what that means. The only national electorates ever asked, after decades of actually using ranked ballots, whether they would like to stop... declined, emphatically. Familiarity with ranking does not breed contempt. It breeds attachment.

## The quiet spread

Once you start looking, ranked ballots turn up everywhere the British constitutional tradition touched — and beyond. Malta has used STV since 1921. Northern Ireland elects its Assembly and its local councils by STV. Scotland passed the Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004 and has elected every council by STV since 2007. New Zealand lets its councils opt into STV for local elections, and a growing set — including the capital, Wellington — has done so. India's constitution prescribes the single transferable vote for electing its president, cast by members of parliament and the state legislatures. Papua New Guinea moved its national elections to limited preferential voting in the 2000s, asking voters to rank at least three candidates.

Read that timeline again and notice its shape. The adoption dates span a hundred years; the adopters span conservative governments, constitutional framers, and devolved parliaments; and the most recent entries — the *newcomers* to this club — are American states. We are not inventing something exotic. We are catching up to Australia's grandparents.

## What the world's experience settles — and what it can't

The international record settles three things. First, **voters can do this**: a century of national elections, conducted on paper, counted by hand where machines never entered the picture. Second, **it is durable**: the Irish kept it through two referendums, and no Australian government of either party has seriously moved to strip preferential voting from the House. Third, **it is ideologically homeless**: adopted by Australian conservatives dodging spoilers, Irish framers seeking proportionality, and a Labour–Liberal Democrat coalition in Scotland — which is exactly what you would expect of a counting rule rather than a partisan program.

Now, the honest caveats — because my opponents are not wrong to raise them. A status-quo defender will point out that every country in this chapter is a parliamentary democracy, that Australia pairs its ranked ballots with compulsory voting, and that transferring an institution across constitutional systems is never automatic. Fair. An alternative-reform advocate will point out something subtler: most of the world's ranked-ballot success stories are *STV in multi-member districts* — proportional representation — while most American RCV proposals are single-winner instant runoff. If Ireland's consensus politics is what attracts you, a single-winner ballot in a gerrymandered district will not deliver it. Also fair, and I will come back to multi-winner RCV later in this book. But notice what neither objection touches: the claim that ranking itself is unproven, confusing, or uncountable. That claim does not survive contact with a single Australian election night.

  The Irish referendums. The only two times a national electorate with *decades* of ranked-ballot experience was asked to give the system up — 1959 and 1968 — it refused, and by a wider margin the second time. That is revealed preference from experienced users deciding their own future elections, not a survey of people imagining a system they have never touched.

## What this chapter did not settle

I have not shown that what works in Canberra or Dublin transfers cleanly to a system with separation of powers, partisan primaries, fifty separate election bureaucracies, and voluntary voting — that argument has to be made on American evidence, and the chapters on Maine, Alaska, and the cities make it. Nor have I resolved the live family quarrel between single-winner RCV and proportional STV; the world's data honestly favors the latter for proportionality, and reformers should say so out loud. What this chapter does settle is narrower and, for our debate, decisive: the ranked ballot is proven technology, run at national scale since 1918. "Untested" is off the table. The question is not whether it can work. The question — the rest of this book — is how we get it here.
