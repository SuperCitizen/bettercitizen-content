# Your Move

Seven hundred forty-three votes. That was the entire margin, after a recount, by which Alaskans kept their election system in November 2024 — 160,973 to 160,230. The 2020 margin that created the system in the first place was 3,781. Now divide those numbers by what a single committed volunteer does in a campaign season — the hundreds of doors, the shifts at the farmers market, the letters to the editor, the twenty friends who actually vote because you asked. Margins like Alaska's are not abstractions that happen *to* people. They are made, vote by vote, by people who decided the outcome was partly their job.

You've read the book. You know what the pick-one ballot discards, what the ranked ballot fixes, what the evidence supports, where the courts stand, how the movement wins and loses. The only chapter left is the one I can't write for you. But I can hand you the map, the allies, and the script.

## First, know your terrain

Your move depends entirely on where you're standing, so start with three questions.

**Does your state have the initiative?** Twenty-six states offer a statewide initiative or referendum process — check yours on Ballotpedia. If you're in one, you live where Maine 2016 and Alaska 2020 were possible, and where the price of admission is signatures and organization rather than legislators' permission.

**Has your state banned it?** As of mid-2026, nineteen have. If you're in one of them, your fight is different but not hopeless — a ban is a statute, and statutes get repealed by the same majorities that passed them. The constitutional chapter matters here: nothing about a ban is permanent law; it's a policy choice wearing law's clothing.

**Can your city move alone?** This is the question most people never ask. Much of the movement's entire history — San Francisco, Minneapolis, New York, D.C., Greenbelt, Skokie — is city charters, not state law. Whether your city can act depends on your state's home-rule rules: some cities can amend their charters by local vote, others need state enabling legislation first. Your city clerk's office and the Ranked Choice Voting Resource Center can answer that question for your zip code in an afternoon.

<Checkpoint question="Your state: no citizen initiative, no RCV ban, and cities with broad home-rule charter powers. Where's your leverage — and why is it the same play San Francisco ran in 2002?">
  The city. With no initiative process, statewide adoption needs the legislature, and legislatures move only after local proof exists — so the highest-leverage act is building that proof where you live: a charter amendment campaign in a home-rule city. That's precisely the San Francisco 2002 play, which turned one city's working example into a region's, then a movement's. No ban means the laboratory is open; the absence of an initiative just means the experiment starts local instead of statewide.
</Checkpoint>

## Join people who are already working

None of this requires founding anything. The infrastructure exists, it's real, I've verified it, and every group below would put you to work this month.

[[org:fairvote|FairVote]] has been the movement's research and advocacy hub since 1992 — it began life as Citizens for Proportional Representation, became the Center for Voting and Democracy, and has spent three decades building the evidence base this book leans on. Start there for the data and the national picture. **Rank the Vote** — yes, it shares this book's name; the name is good — is a network of volunteer-driven state groups: if your state has one, that's your local door. [[org:representus|RepresentUs]] runs cross-partisan campaigns for anti-corruption and election reforms, including ranked choice voting, and is built for people who want a campaign to plug into. [[org:unite-america|Unite America]] funds and publishes the "primary problem" research you saw two chapters ago. **Veterans for All Voters** — founded and led by military veterans, renamed from Veterans for Political Innovation in 2023 — organizes specifically among veterans and military families for open primaries and majority-winner elections, and its credibility in red and purple states is exactly what 2024 showed the movement lacks. The **Ranked Choice Voting Resource Center** does the unglamorous load-bearing work: technical assistance for election officials and campaigns on ballot design, tabulation, and voter education. And in many states, the [[org:league-of-women-voters|League of Women Voters]]' state chapters have studied and endorsed ranked ballots — check whether yours is one of them.

What would they actually have you do? Signature shifts, if your state or city has a live measure. Testimony at the statehouse when a ban or a local-option bill gets its hearing — committee rooms are emptier than you think, and a constituent who shows up is rarer than a lobbyist. Tabling and demo elections — ranking pizza toppings converts more skeptics than any white paper. Poll-watching and education during a first rollout, which is where reforms are actually won or lost. Money, if you have it and time you don't.

## Make the case the way it wins

The 2024 post-mortems are a gift; use them in your own conversations.

Lead with outcomes, not machinery. "Majority winners." "No more spoilers." "A runoff without a second election." The mechanism — rounds, transfers, eliminations — comes second, after someone asks how. Campaigns that led with mechanism spent 2024 explaining; campaigns that led with outcomes spent it persuading.

Concede what must be conceded. You've watched me do it all book: the center squeeze is real, the money evidence is thin, civility gains are bounded, statewide voters said no five times in 2024. Overclaiming is how advocates manufacture their own backlash; the credibility you bank by conceding is what makes your affirmative claims land.

And answer the partisanship charge with the record, not with indignation.

## Run

I'll keep the dare from the original draft of this chapter, because I meant it: consider running. School board, library board, city council. The whole argument of this book is that pick-one ballots rig the entry decision — that ordinary people with broad appeal but no machine behind them are filtered out before voters ever meet them. Where ranked ballots exist, that filter weakens; candidacies multiplied in the cities that adopted them. If you live where the reform has won, the system is now built for someone like you. And if you run where it hasn't won, run the way the reform teaches anyway — asking to be people's second choice if you can't be their first. Candidates who campaign that way build the constituency for the rules that reward it.

This year hands you specific assignments, wherever you live. Alaska votes on repeal — round three — on November 3, 2026; if you know Alaskans, they will decide this with a margin made of conversations. Washington, D.C. is running its first ranked elections, and a smooth rollout there is the next national proof point. Nineteen statehouses hold bans that will someday face repeal bills, and yours may be one of them. The FairVote tracker — 49 jurisdictions, nearly 14 million voters, and counting — will tell you exactly where the line currently runs through your state.

Here is the last thing I'll say, and it's the thesis of the whole book wearing work clothes. The people who benefit from the broken system will never volunteer to fix it — not because they're villains, but because no one audits the rules that made them winners. Every reform in this book — every city, both states, all of it — happened because people with no permission and no office decided the rules were their business. Seven hundred forty-three votes. The system is listening whether you speak or not; silence is also a ballot, and it always gets counted for the status quo.

Your move.
