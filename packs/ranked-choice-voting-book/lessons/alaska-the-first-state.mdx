# Alaska: The First State

Don Young held Alaska's only seat in the U.S. House for forty-nine years — he had been the state's congressman since before most living Alaskans were born. When he died in March 2022, forty-eight candidates filed to replace him: a former governor turned vice-presidential nominee, a Republican scion of the state's most famous Democratic family, a Yup'ik former state legislator who had managed salmon runs on the Kuskokwim, and a North Pole city councilman legally named Santa Claus, who finished sixth. Under the old rules, party primaries would have carved that field up before most voters ever saw it. Under Alaska's brand-new rules — the rules this chapter is about — all forty-eight ran on a single ballot, every voter chose among all of them, and the four with the most votes advanced. American politics had never run an election like it.

## How it happened

In November 2020, Alaskans passed Ballot Measure 2 by [[term:ballot-initiative]]: 174,032 votes to 170,251, a margin of 3,781 votes. I won't pretend that's a landslide. Reform never wins in landslides, because it asks voters to bet on something they've never seen.

Measure 2 was three reforms in one package. It abolished party primaries in favor of a single [[issue:nonpartisan-primaries|nonpartisan primary]] in which every candidate for an office appears on one ballot and the top four finishers advance, regardless of party. It adopted [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] for the general election. And it required disclosure of the true sources of certain "dark money" flowing into Alaska campaigns. The pieces are not separable, and this matters: the top-four primary moves the real decision out of low-turnout partisan primaries and into November, and ranked choice is what makes a four-way November race workable — without it, four candidates on a general ballot is a spoiler machine.

The coalition behind it was deliberately cross-partisan — independents (more Alaskans register as nonpartisan or undeclared than as Republicans and Democrats combined), moderate Republicans, and reform-minded Democrats, with significant money from national democracy-reform groups, a fact opponents have never tired of mentioning. Opponents also sued. In *Kohlhaas v. State* (2022), the Alaska Supreme Court rejected the challenge, holding that nothing in the state constitution forbade the election system Alaskans had chosen.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose a state adopts ranked-choice voting for the general election but keeps its closed party primaries. Walk the incentives: what does the November ballot look like, and how much work is left for RCV to do?">
  Not much. Each party still sends one nominee, chosen by its small primary electorate, so November is still mostly a two-candidate race — RCV rarely changes the outcome, and the pressure toward the extremes happens upstream, in the primary, untouched. That's why Measure 2 bundled the top-four primary with RCV: the primary reform moves the real choice to November, and RCV is what keeps a four-way November from becoming a spoiler contest.
</Checkpoint>

## Trial by fire: the 2022 special election

Don Young's death meant the new system's maiden voyage would be improvised — a statewide by-mail special primary on June 11, 2022, with those forty-eight candidates. The top four: Sarah Palin, Nick Begich III, independent Al Gross, and Mary Peltola. Gross withdrew days later, and election officials kept the August ballot at three. The election came on August 16; two weeks later, with the absentee ballots in, officials ran the first ranked-choice count in Alaska's history — an [[term:instant-runoff]], with the whole country watching.

Here is the official tabulation, in full:

| Count | Peltola (D) | Palin (R) | Begich (R) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First choices | 75,799 (40.19%) | 58,973 (31.27%) | 53,810 (28.53%) |
| After Begich's elimination | 91,266 (51.48%) | 86,026 (48.52%) | — |

No candidate had a majority of first choices, so Begich, in last place, was eliminated, and his ballots moved to whichever candidate each of his voters had ranked next.

Look at what the transfers actually show. Begich's voters broke toward Palin nearly two to one — exactly what you'd expect from a fellow Republican's supporters. The system did not divert Republican votes; it delivered them. They simply weren't enough. In the final head-to-head count against Palin, Peltola had more votes, and she became the first Alaska Native ever elected to Congress.

Then came the screaming. Senator Tom Cotton called the result "a scam to rig elections." But walk through what the critics were actually claiming: that a candidate whom nearly seven in ten voters declined to rank first, and who then *lost a two-candidate final count*, was somehow entitled to the seat. The old plurality rules might have handed it to her anyway. The new rules asked the one question that should decide an election — of the candidates remaining, whom do more voters prefer? — and Palin lost it.

## The critique I have to answer

Honest advocacy means conceding the strongest point against you, so here it is. Two mathematicians, Adam Graham-Squire and David McCune, analyzed the special-election ballots and found that Begich would have beaten Peltola head-to-head *and* Palin head-to-head. He was what voting theorists call the Condorcet winner — the consensus candidate — and the instant-runoff count eliminated him first, because consensus candidates can be short on first-choice votes. This is the "center squeeze," and it is real. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise.

What I don't concede is that this indicts the reform, for two reasons. First, the old system would never have found Begich either: a 2022 closed Republican primary, with Donald Trump's endorsement behind Palin, almost certainly nominates Palin — and the [[term:plurality]] count that hid this information for a century would have given us no way to know a consensus candidate had been passed over. We only know Begich was the Condorcet winner *because* ranked ballots exist. Second, the failure was bounded and self-correcting: in the November rematch eleven weeks later — same three candidates plus a Libertarian, far higher turnout — Peltola took 48.8% of first choices and won the final count 54.96% to 45.04%. Given a second look and more information, Alaskans didn't reverse the "fluke." They ratified it, emphatically.

## What changed

The race I keep coming back to isn't Peltola's. It's Lisa Murkowski's. In February 2021 she had voted to convict Trump at his second impeachment trial; her own state party censured her for it, and Trump endorsed her challenger, Kelly Tshibaka. Murkowski is precisely the kind of incumbent the old system existed to execute.

<Checkpoint question="Under the old rules — a closed Republican primary, then a pick-one general — where does Murkowski's 2022 reelection bid most likely end, and what specifically about the new rules changes that?">
  It ends in the [[term:primary-election|primary]], where a small, censure-minded partisan electorate gets the only vote that matters. The top-four primary made it impossible to eliminate her with partisan votes alone, and ranked choice let Democrats and independents support her in November without abandoning their own first choice. The new system doesn't guarantee moderates win; it guarantees the whole electorate decides whether it wants one.
</Checkpoint>

And the whole electorate did decide: Murkowski trailed in no round, edging Tshibaka 43.4% to 42.6% on first choices and winning the final count 53.7% to 46.3%. On the very same ballots, Alaskans reelected their conservative Republican governor outright — Mike Dunleavy cleared 50% of first choices, no transfers needed — while sending Democrat Peltola back to the House. Voters, not the method, picked the winners.

The tone changed too, in ways I'll state carefully. The incentive is structural: when your opponent's supporters hold your fate in their second choices, scorching the earth has a price. Murkowski and Peltola — a Republican and a Democrat — publicly said they would rank each other on their own ballots, something close to unimaginable in any other state's Senate-plus-House cycle. And the November tabulation shows the price of running the other way: after a fall of Palin–Begich fratricide, more than a fifth of Begich's final-round ballots ranked neither remaining candidate and simply exhausted. Candidates who want to win under these rules learn to ask for second choices, which means learning to be rankable. Whether that durably lowers the temperature of Alaska politics is a claim I'll defend with data in a later chapter; what Alaska 2022 shows is the mechanism working as designed.

## They came back — and they're back again

Nobody surrenders a duopoly gracefully. In 2024, opponents put repeal on the ballot, and it failed by 743 votes out of more than 321,000 cast — 160,973 to 160,230, confirmed by a recount. The closest ballot-measure result in Alaska history. Twice now, Alaskans have voted for this system, both times by about a point. A point is not a mandate anyone can coast on. It is, however, two consecutive verdicts from the only jury that counts, delivered after the system had actually been used — the second time with full knowledge of everything the critics had said about it.

On that same 2024 ballot, by the way, the system's opponents won under it. Nick Begich — eliminated first in the 2022 special — came back and beat Peltola for the House seat, 51.3 percent to 48.7 in the final ranked-choice round. Whatever this system is, it is not a machine for electing Democrats.

Which brings us to the fight happening as I write. On December 31, 2025, Lieutenant Governor Nancy Dahlstrom certified a *third* ranked-choice ballot measure: repeal organizers submitted 42,837 verified signatures against a requirement of 34,098, putting repeal back before voters in November 2026. Governor Mike Dunleavy signed the petition. So did Congressman Begich — who holds his seat, remember, because ranked ballots gathered him a final-round majority in 2024. A coalition called Protect Alaska's Elections, backed by unions and Alaska Native organizations, is organizing the defense. I won't predict the outcome; after 3,781 votes in 2020 and 743 in 2024, anyone who predicts Alaska ballot measures is guessing. I'll say only what the record supports: the system's opponents have now asked Alaskans the same question three times in six years, and the answer, so far, has been a stubborn, narrow, twice-repeated *keep it*. If you live in Alaska, the third answer is yours to give.

## What Alaska settles — and what it doesn't

Alaska settles the feasibility question. A state with one of the most independent electorates in the country ran a forty-eight-candidate primary, counted ranked ballots under a national microscope, elected Republicans and Democrats on the same ballots, survived a Supreme Court challenge and one repeal campaign — and now faces a second, in November 2026, with the verdict not yet in. "It can't work here" is no longer an argument anyone can make with a straight face; "it won't be allowed to keep working" remains a live threat, and pretending otherwise would insult both you and the volunteers knocking doors in Anchorage right now.

What it doesn't settle: whether one state's experience generalizes, whether the center squeeze will recur often enough to matter, and whether the civility effects are durable or a honeymoon. Those are live questions, and I'll take them up with data rather than anecdotes when we get to the research chapter. First, though, credit where it's owed. Alaska was the first state to run its entire general election, governor on down, on ranked ballots — but it wasn't the first state to adopt them. That distinction belongs to Maine, which got there earlier, the hard way, and nearly lost it twice. That story is next.
