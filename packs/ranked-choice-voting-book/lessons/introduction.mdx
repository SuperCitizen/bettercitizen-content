# Introduction: Your Vote Is Broken

You walk into a voting booth. You pick one person. You leave.

That's it. That's the entirety of your democratic expression — a single mark in a world that is anything but binary. You may have spent weeks weighing three candidates, admiring one, tolerating another, dreading the third. The ballot doesn't care. It asks exactly one question — *pick one* — and it discards everything else you know.

And if you voted for the person who came in third? Your voice didn't just lose. It vanished, as if you never showed up at all.

This is how nearly every American election works. And it's insane.

## The question your ballot never asks

In November 2000, the presidency of the United States came down to 537 votes — George W. Bush's certified margin over Al Gore in Florida, out of nearly six million cast. On those same Florida ballots, 97,488 people voted for Ralph Nader. Were most of them Gore supporters denied a backup choice? Secret Bush sympathizers? Voters who would have stayed home in a two-man race? Here is the embarrassing answer: nobody knows, and nobody *can* know. The ballot never asked. A pick-one election decided that closely produced a constitutional crisis, a Supreme Court case, a generation of recrimination — and zero information about what its own voters actually preferred. The next chapter dissects that pathology in full; for now just notice that the most consequential American election in living memory could not answer the simplest question about itself.

The fix this book argues for is almost embarrassingly small. Same candidates, same booths, same Tuesday in November. One change: instead of picking one candidate, you rank them — first choice, second choice, third, as far down as you care to go. If your favorite can't win, your ballot counts for your next choice instead of going in the trash. That's [[term:ranked-choice-voting]], and the version used for single offices — the [[term:instant-runoff]] — does with one trip to the polls what a runoff election does with two.

Ranked choice voting fixes the pick-one pathology. Not with revolution, not with a constitutional amendment, not with some untested theory cooked up in a think tank. It fixes it with arithmetic, with common sense, and with a track record now running from Canberra to Cambridge, Massachusetts, from Maine's mill towns to every village in Alaska.

<Checkpoint question="A friend tells you: 'I like the third-party candidate best, but I'm voting for the major-party one — I can't waste my vote.' Under pick-one rules, what exactly is the system asking your friend to do, and what information dies in that booth?">
  It's asking them to vote *against* their judgment — to record a first choice they don't actually hold, because recording their honest one could help elect the candidate they like least. And the lie compounds: the official tally now shows one more enthusiastic major-party voter and one fewer third-party supporter, so the next cycle's candidates, donors, and journalists all read demand that doesn't exist and miss demand that does. The strategy is rational. The ballot made it so. That's the book's first claim: blame the system, not the voter.
</Checkpoint>

## Where the fight stands

This book would have been a speculative pamphlet twenty years ago. It isn't one now, because the experiment has been run. Maine's voters adopted ranked choice voting by citizen initiative in 2016 — the first state in American history — and have defended it at the ballot box since. Alaska followed in 2020 and ran its entire 2022 general election, governor on down, on ranked ballots. New York City counted more than a million of them in its June 2025 mayoral primary without breaking a sweat. By [[org:fairvote]]'s count, as of early 2026, 49 American jurisdictions — home to nearly 14 million voters across 22 states and the District of Columbia — use ranked choice voting or have adopted it for upcoming elections.

And I'll tell you the other half, because you should hear it from me first. The backlash is just as real. Nineteen states, most recently Ohio in March 2026, have passed laws *banning* ranked choice voting before most of their cities could try it. Alaska's opponents, having lost a repeal vote by 743 votes in 2024, qualified another repeal for the November 2026 ballot — that fight is live as you read this. Maine's highest court advised in April 2026 that the state constitution still blocks ranked ballots from general elections for governor. Anyone who tells you this reform is inevitable is selling something. Anyone who tells you it's dying should have to explain why the places that actually use it keep voting to keep it.

## How to read this book

The chapters ahead move from problem to proof to playbook. First, the diagnosis: what pick-one [[term:plurality]] voting actually measures, and the spoiler-and-extremism machine it builds. Then the mechanics of the fix, which you'll run yourself, round by round. Then the evidence — Alaska, Maine, two decades of American cities, a century abroad — followed by the opposition's best arguments, the academic data, and finally what it takes to bring ranked choice voting to your state.

One promise before we start. I am an advocate; this book has a thesis, and the platform you're reading it on rightly flags it as contested. So here is the deal I'll keep with you in every chapter: real numbers from official sources, named opponents quoted at their strongest, and a concession wherever the other side has a point — because they sometimes do, and you'll trust nothing else I say if I pretend otherwise. When Alaska's 2022 count passed over the candidate who would have beaten either rival head-to-head, I'll show you the math. When a city repeals the reform, I'll tell you why.

What this introduction did not settle: everything. It asserted; the rest of the book shows receipts. We start where the damage is clearest — with the arithmetic of the one-vote ballot, and the strange American habit of electing leaders most voters voted against.
