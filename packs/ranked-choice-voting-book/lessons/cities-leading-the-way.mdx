# Cities Leading the Way

On a Tuesday in June 2021, nearly a million New Yorkers did something the pundit class had spent two years insisting ordinary voters couldn't handle: they ranked. Thirteen candidates for mayor, a ballot that let you list up to five in order, and the largest test [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] had ever faced in America. The voters were fine. The voters were better than fine. It was a city agency that face-planted — and even that, as you'll see, proved the reform's point rather than the critics'.

States get the headlines. Maine and Alaska are the chapters everyone quotes back to me. But the states didn't invent this movement — they inherited it. For two decades, American cities have been the laboratories where ranked choice voting was tried, stress-tested, occasionally repealed, and steadily refined. If you want to know whether RCV works, don't ask a think tank. Ask a city clerk.

## New York City: the stress test

In 2019, New York City voters approved Ballot Question 1, adopting RCV for primary and special elections. Note the margin: **73.5 percent**. That is not a squeaker engineered by activists on a sleepy ballot line — almost three out of four voters in the nation's largest city said yes.

The June 2021 Democratic mayoral primary was the first full-scale test, and it was everything a stress test should be: thirteen candidates, more than 942,000 ballots, a genuinely unsettled race. [[person:eric-adams]] led the first count and won in the eighth round of tabulation, beating Kathryn Garcia by 7,197 votes — 50.4 percent to 49.6. And here is the number the skeptics never mention: in that final round, roughly 85 percent of ballots were still alive, still expressing a preference between the last two candidates. Under the old system, everyone who picked the third- through thirteenth-place candidates would simply have been discarded in round one.

Now, the famous fiasco. A week after the primary, the city's Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that was visibly wrong — staff had failed to clear test ballots from a pre-election trial run before hitting the button. They retracted it within hours and re-ran the count clean. Critics declared RCV too complicated to administer. But notice what actually broke: not the ranking, not the algorithm, not the voters. An agency with a decades-long reputation for dysfunction made a data-handling error it could have made in any election under any system. The certified count was orderly and final.

The voters themselves delivered the verdict. An Edison Research exit poll commissioned by Common Cause New York found that **95 percent** of primary voters found their ballot simple to complete, and **77 percent** wanted to keep RCV for future local elections.

<Checkpoint question="Critics pointed at the June 2021 tabulation mess as proof that RCV is too complicated to count. Based on what actually happened, what failed — and why does the distinction matter?">
  The Board of Elections failed to remove **test ballots** from its system before publishing a preliminary tally — a human data-handling error, retracted within hours, that could have corrupted a plurality count just as easily. The ranking algorithm, the voters, and the certified re-count all worked exactly as designed. The distinction matters because the case against RCV rests on the claim that the *method* is fragile; New York showed the method was sound and an *agency* was sloppy.
</Checkpoint>

## The Twin Cities: proof of durability

A single election can be a fluke. Fifteen years cannot. Minneapolis voters approved RCV in 2006 and first used it in November 2009 — so early that no certified counting equipment existed yet, and election staff hand-counted the ballots over fifteen days using a precinct-level procedure they had to invent themselves (it's still called the "Minneapolis Method"). St. Paul adopted RCV by referendum in 2009 and first used it in 2011.

Since then, the Twin Cities have run ranked-choice municipal elections over and over — Minneapolis in 2013, 2017, 2021, and 2023, with every one of the twenty-two offices on its municipal ballot now elected this way. No meltdown, no mass confusion, no exodus of bewildered voters. The most boring fact in this book may be the most important one: in the places that have used RCV the longest, it has become unremarkable. Durability is the evidence opponents can't argue with, which is why they rarely bring Minnesota up.

## The Bay Area: where the majority got its teeth back

San Francisco voters adopted RCV by [[term:ballot-initiative]] in March 2002 and first used it in November 2004, electing seven seats on the Board of Supervisors. It has been the city's normal ever since — mayor, district attorney, sheriff, the lot.

But the election that taught the country what RCV actually *does* happened across the bay. Oakland adopted RCV in 2006 and first used it in 2010, in an open race for mayor. Former state senate leader Don Perata led the first count with about 34 percent of first choices; councilmember Jean Quan sat at 24. Under [[term:plurality]] rules, Perata wins and the story ends — a candidate two-thirds of the city didn't pick takes city hall. Instead, as trailing candidates were eliminated and their voters' backup rankings transferred, Quan consolidated the broader coalition and won the final round 51 to 49, becoming Oakland's first Asian American woman mayor.

Perata's supporters cried foul, and you can understand the instinct: their guy "came in first." But he came in first the way a 34 percent candidate always does — by being the largest fragment, not the majority's choice. RCV didn't take anything from him. It simply asked the question plurality voting never asks: *whom do most voters actually prefer?* Oakland answered.

<Checkpoint question="Perata led Oakland's 2010 first count 34–24, yet Quan's win is the system working, not failing. What's the argument — as the reform advocate makes it?">
  Thirty-four percent is a **plurality fragment**, not a majority. When eliminated candidates' supporters expressed their backup preferences, most of them preferred Quan — meaning a Perata win would have installed the candidate a majority of voters ranked *below* his rival. RCV surfaces the majority coalition that a single-choice count leaves invisible. (The status-quo defender's rebuttal — that first preferences should carry special weight — is taken up in "The Opposition.")
</Checkpoint>

## The growing list — and the honest part

Look at how many different roads now lead to the same place. Salt Lake City got RCV from a **Republican state legislature**: Utah's H.B. 35 (2018) created an opt-in municipal pilot, expanded in 2021, and that fall Salt Lake City and some twenty other Utah cities ran ranked-choice elections. Santa Fe's voters approved RCV in 2008 with 65 percent — then watched their own city government stall for a decade until the New Mexico Supreme Court cleared the way in January 2018; in the March 2018 mayoral debut, more than 96 percent of voters ranked at least four of the five candidates. Arlington, Virginia ran the first ranked-choice election in Virginia's history in its June 2023 county board primary, under a 2020 state enabling law — and its board voted that December to keep using it.

Now the honest part, because advocates who hide the ledger's other column deserve the skepticism they get. A few places have walked away. Pierce County, Washington adopted RCV in 2006 and repealed it decisively in 2009 after two rocky elections. Aspen, Colorado used it once and dropped it in 2010. And Burlington — the case opponents cite most — repealed RCV in 2010, 52 to 48, after a mayoral election whose winner trailed in first choices.

Here's why I still write "almost none are going back" with a straight face: Burlington itself came back. In March 2021, eleven years after the repeal, its voters reinstated RCV for city council races by 64 percent. The most famous repeal in the movement's history reversed itself, by a bigger margin than the repeal ever had. Meanwhile [[org:fairvote]]'s running tally of jurisdictions using RCV has done nothing but grow — and the adoptions keep coming through every available door: citizen initiatives, charter commissions, city councils, state pilot programs, even court orders enforcing votes that city halls tried to ignore.

## What this chapter settles — and what it doesn't

The municipal record settles three things. RCV runs at scale: New York counted close to a million ranked ballots in a single primary and certified an orderly result. It endures: the Twin Cities have run it routinely for more than a decade and a half. And it does what it promises: Oakland's 2010 election replaced a plurality fragment with a majority coalition, exactly as designed.

What it does not settle, and I won't pretend otherwise: whether success in nonpartisan city races transfers cleanly to high-stakes partisan statewide elections — that's why the Maine and Alaska chapters exist — and whether the repeal stories are noise or signal. Status-quo defenders read Burlington 2010 and Pierce County as voters rejecting a confusing import; alternative-reform advocates argue cities should be piloting different systems entirely. Both deserve a full hearing, and the next chapter, "The Opposition," gives them one.
