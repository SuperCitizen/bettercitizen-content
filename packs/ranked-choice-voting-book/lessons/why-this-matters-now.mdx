# Why This Matters Now

On March 17, 2026, Governor Mike DeWine signed Ohio Senate Bill 63 without a statement. The law doesn't just decline to adopt ranked choice voting — it forbids Ohio's cities from trying it, and any city that does forfeits its state local-government funding. Ohio was the nineteenth state to enact a ban, and the second in three months.

Sit with the strangeness of that. Nobody passes nineteen laws against a thing that's failing on its own. You don't statutorily prohibit, with financial penalties, an idea you expect voters to reject anyway. The ban wave is the political system's own assessment of this reform's trajectory — and it's why I wrote this chapter, the one that answers *why now* instead of *why at all*.

## The stress readings

Every era thinks itself uniquely divided, so let's use instruments instead of vibes. Pew Research Center has tracked [[term:partisan]] antipathy for decades, and the 2022 readings were the worst in the series: 72 percent of Republicans said Democrats were more *immoral* than other Americans, and 63 percent of Democrats said the same of Republicans — up from 47 and 35 percent just six years earlier. Note the word. Not wrong. Not misguided. *Immoral.* That is not a measurement of disagreement; it's a measurement of contempt.

And here is the second reading, the one that makes the first actionable: Americans aren't loyal partisans gleefully waging this war. In Pew's October 2025 survey, 31 percent said *both* parties are too extreme, and 26 percent said *neither* party governs honestly and ethically. A political system producing record contempt and record alienation simultaneously is not expressing what its citizens want. Something in the machine is manufacturing this.

## The disappearing general election

I've argued throughout this book that the machine's worst gear is the [[term:primary-election|primary]]–plurality pipeline. This is where I show you its current dimensions. Before you read them, commit to a guess.

If you guessed high on the first and low on the second, you've been paying attention; most people get the *direction* right and the magnitude wrong. Unite America's analysis of the 2024 cycle found 87 percent of House races were effectively over after the primary — decided in elections where about 7 percent of Americans voted. In 169 districts, the decisive primary ballot had *one candidate on it*. The Cook Political Report's index, meanwhile, counts competitive districts (partisan lean within five points) at 82 — exactly half the 164 of 1999. And Cook's own analysis undercuts the comfortable villain: most of that decline came from voters sorting themselves, not from [[term:gerrymandering|gerrymandered]] lines, which means redistricting reform alone cannot restore the November election. For most Americans, in most races, the general election is a formality and the real election is a tiny, unrepresentative [[term:voter-turnout|turnout]] event they don't vote in. The machine manufacturing contempt has an address.

<Checkpoint question="A reformer says: 'Fix gerrymandering and competition returns.' Using the Cook analysis, explain why that fix — alone — disappoints.">
  Cook's own decomposition found the overwhelming share of the swing-seat collapse (about 81% of it) came from *realignment* — voters geographically sorting into like-minded places — and only about 19% from redrawn lines. Independent commissions can stop mapmakers from making it worse, but they can't unsort the country: a fairly drawn map of a deep-red or deep-blue region still yields safe seats, whose only real election is the primary. That's why reforms that change *who wins safe seats and how* — primary and ballot reform — attack a layer redistricting can't reach.
</Checkpoint>

## The status quo is armed

Now back to the bans, because the timeline of mid-2026 deserves to be seen whole.

Nineteen states have now prohibited ranked choice voting — seventeen of them under unified Republican control when they did it, per Ballotpedia, though Kansas and Kentucky enacted theirs under divided government. Indiana opened 2026 with a ban in February; Ohio followed in March with the funding penalty attached. Utah's decade-long municipal pilot — bipartisan, locally beloved in use, credited by the Sightline Institute's review with cutting participating cities' election costs by roughly 40 percent — was allowed to expire on January 1, 2026, participation having withered from 23 cities to 12 to 4 as the national party climate turned. A former Utah Republican Party chair, Stan Lockhart, gave the honest epitaph: once Donald Trump blamed ranked choice voting for Sarah Palin's 2022 loss, "that was the end of ranked choice voting in Utah." In April 2026, Trump himself called for Alaskans to repeal their system at this November's election — round three. And the same month, Maine's high court reaffirmed that its constitution's plurality clause walls off any expansion to state general elections.

I want to be fair about what the ban wave is *not*. It is not evidence that ranked choice voting fails voters — Utah's cities and Alaska's electorate kept choosing it when asked. The honest description is that one party's national apparatus has classified this reform as a threat, and the other party's establishment is no friend either; remember that it was D.C.'s *Democratic* Party that sued to keep Initiative 83 from its voters, and both parties fought the 2024 measures. The duopoly disagrees about nearly everything except the rules that make it a duopoly.

## Windows open. Windows close.

American electoral reform doesn't trickle; it comes in floods, when public disgust and organized reformers and a usable mechanism line up. The Progressive Era stacked the secret ballot, the direct primary, the citizen initiative (Oregon, 1902), and the Seventeenth Amendment's direct election of senators (1913) into roughly one generation. Watergate produced the 1974 campaign-finance overhaul within two years of the break-in. Between floods, the channel silts up: the same proposals that passed in bunches become "unrealistic" for decades.

I believe — and I label this as argument, not data — that we are inside such a window now, opened by exactly the disgust Pew keeps measuring, and that the ban wave is what the closing of a window looks like in real time. Adoption is slow by design: initiative campaigns need signatures, majorities, sometimes two majorities (Nevada's double-passage rule erased its 2022 win in 2024), then implementation. A ban needs one legislative session. That asymmetry means the *capacity to experiment* — the city pilots and local options every past win grew from — can be stripped faster than it can be used. Nineteen states' worth of laboratories are now closed. The reform can still win the country, but it cannot win it at leisure.

## What this chapter settles — and what it doesn't

The measurements are settled: the antipathy series, the swing-seat count, the primary arithmetic, the nineteen bans — those are facts with sources, and any account of American politics that ignores them is fantasy. What's genuinely contested is the inference I draw: that this is a closing window demanding action on *this* reform now, rather than patience or a different reform altogether. You've now read the strongest versions of all three positions, which is more than either party's messaging will ever give you.

What I ask you to take forward is only this: the status quo is not neutral ground that will wait politely while we deliberate. It is organized, funded, and legislating. Whatever you decide about ranked choice voting, decide it *as a participant* — because the deciding is happening either way. What participation concretely looks like is the final chapter.
