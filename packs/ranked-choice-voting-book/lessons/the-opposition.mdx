# The Opposition

On March 17, 2026, Governor Mike DeWine signed Ohio Senate Bill 63 without a public statement, and Ohio became the nineteenth state to ban [[term:ranked-choice-voting]]. The law doesn't just decline to adopt ranked ballots for state races — no Ohio city had asked for that. It forbids Ohio's own municipalities from using them, and it adds a financial garrote: any charter city that tries loses its share of the state's Local Government Fund. Cincinnati and Lakewood had been exploring RCV. They are no longer allowed to. A reform is being banned, in state after state, faster than it is being adopted — and if you want to change that, you'd better understand exactly who is doing the banning, and why the smartest of them think they're right.

## Who actually opposes this

Three groups, and only one of them is the cartoon you may be expecting.

The first is the organized, institutional opposition. The Heritage Foundation fired the opening shot in an August 2019 issue brief by Hans von Spakovsky calling RCV "a scheme to disconnect elections from issues and allow candidates with marginal support from voters to win elections." The Honest Elections Project, led by Jason Snead, has made repealing and banning RCV a headline priority, publishing model legislation and annual "Safeguarding Our Elections" reports that celebrate each new ban and declare that "the 2024 election proved that there is no public appetite for RCV." The Foundation for Government Accountability runs parallel campaigns. This is a coordinated [[term:think-tank]]-and-statehouse operation, and it has been strikingly effective: seventeen of the nineteen ban states had Republican trifectas when they acted.

The second group is party establishments — and here I mean *both* parties. It was the D.C. Democratic Party that sued, twice, to keep ranked choice voting off Washington's ballot in 2024. It was six New York City Council members, all members of the council's Black, Latino and Asian Caucus, who sued in December 2020 to delay the city's voter-approved rollout, arguing the city had not prepared minority and senior voters. (Their own caucus split — other Black and Latino council members and community leaders publicly backed the system.) And it was Senator Tom Cotton who called Alaska's first ranked-choice count "a scam to rig elections." Parties that have mastered one set of rules do not thank you for changing the rules. That is not a left-right instinct; it is an incumbent instinct.

The third group deserves the most respect: sincere critics who share my goals and doubt my remedy. Lee Drutman — a political scientist who wrote one of the most influential pro-reform books of the last decade — publicly updated his views in 2023, arguing that single-winner RCV delivers, at best, modest moderation, and that reformers should spend their scarce energy on proportional representation instead. Some progressives worry, as Council Member I. Daneek Miller did in New York, that ranking dilutes the influence of cohesive minority voting blocs. These critics are not defending a duopoly. They are asking whether I've picked the right tool — a question I take up across this book, and the alternative-reform case deserves your attention before you settle your own view.

But the bans pick up establishment votes from both parties (Ohio's bill was co-sponsored by Columbus Democrat Bill DeMora, and Kansas and Kentucky enacted bans under divided government), because party organizations of every stripe are threatened by rules that loosen their gatekeeping. ## Their best arguments, at full strength

I am going to give you the opposition's three strongest arguments the way their smartest advocates make them, because if my case can't survive contact with the real thing, it isn't worth your time.

**One: ranked ballots cause more voter errors, and the errors aren't evenly distributed.** This is no longer speculation; it's measured. Stephen Pettigrew and Dylan Radley analyzed millions of ballots and found that votes in ranked-choice races are rejected at about ten times the rate of votes in single-choice races on the same ballots — 0.53 percent versus 0.04 percent — and that nearly one in twenty RCV voters mismarks their ballot in at least one way (an overvote, a skipped ranking, a duplicated ranking). Worse, the mismarks concentrate in lower-income precincts and precincts with fewer college degrees. The strongest version of this argument is an equity claim: the cognitive tax of ranking falls heaviest on exactly the voters reformers claim to champion.

**Two: "majority winner" is sometimes an accounting fiction.** Craig Burnett and Vladimir Kogan examined more than 600,000 ballot images from four early RCV elections and documented ballot "exhaustion" — ballots that rank only eliminated candidates and so carry no preference into the final round — at rates from 9.6 percent to 27.1 percent. In all four elections, the winner's "majority" was a majority of final-round ballots but *less than half of all votes cast*: 45.1 percent in Oakland, 45.6 percent in Pierce County, 43.4 percent in San Francisco, 45.7 percent in San Leandro. In New York's 2021 mayoral primary, roughly 140,000 ballots — about 15 percent — ranked neither finalist and were inactive at the end. Heritage calls the result a "faux majority," and on the raw arithmetic, the phrase has bite.

**Three: it's energy spent on the wrong reform.** Drutman's critique, from the left flank of the reform world: the political-science evidence for single-winner RCV's moderating effects is thin, the two-party doom loop is a *proportional-representation* problem, and every dollar and volunteer hour spent on RCV is a dollar not spent on multi-member districts that would actually fix it.

## My answers — argument by argument

**On errors: ten times a very small number is still a very small number, and it shrinks.** A 0.53 percent rejection rate means 99.5 percent of ranked votes count, against 99.96 percent under pick-one rules — a real difference I won't wave away, but one that should be weighed against what ranked ballots buy. And the rate is not fixed: Lindsey Cormack's 2026 follow-up study of New York found error rates falling as voters gained experience with the system, and New York's own data show the system was not beyond its voters — 89.3 percent of 2021 Democratic primary voters ranked multiple candidates in at least one race, and 95 percent told Edison Research exit pollsters the ballot was simple to complete. The equity concern is the serious one, and the answer is the same as it was for every prior ballot innovation, from the Australian ballot to vote-by-mail: design and education money, which is why I treat voter-education budgets as non-negotiable in the implementation chapter. What the error data cannot support is the conclusion the ban states drew from it. You do not protect a voter who might mismark a ranked ballot by *forbidding her city* from letting her rank at all.

**On exhausted ballots: compare against the real alternative, not against perfection.** Under [[term:plurality]] rules, every ballot cast for a losing candidate is "exhausted" the instant the polls close — it conveys nothing about the voter's preference between the finalists, and nobody calls that disenfranchisement. Under a traditional [[term:runoff-election]], the voters themselves are exhausted: in 96 percent of federal primary runoffs from 1994 to 2022, turnout *fell* between rounds, with a median decline of 40 percent. Against those baselines, an instant runoff that carries 73 to 90 percent of ballots into the final round is not a scandal; it is an improvement. And exhaustion is partly a design choice, not a fixed cost: the elections Burnett and Kogan studied capped voters at three rankings — San Francisco's 27.1 percent came from a sixteen-candidate race with only three slots — which is exactly why newer systems allow five rankings (New York), or as many as there are candidates (Maine). Some voters will still decline to rank rivals they reject, and that is their right; an abstention freely chosen is not a vote stolen.

**On "wrong reform": I'll take the ally's critique seriously — in its own chapter.** Drutman is right that single-winner RCV is not proportional representation and right that the moderation evidence is modest (I'll show you exactly how modest in the next chapter, with the studies on the table). Where he's wrong, I think, is in treating the two as rivals: multi-winner RCV *is* a proportional system — it's how Portland, Oregon now elects its council — and every city that learns to rank is a city where proportional ranking stops sounding exotic. A reform that can actually pass, and that builds the infrastructure for the bigger one, is not a detour.

## What the bans themselves tell you

Look at the architecture of the bans, because it reveals what their authors actually fear. Almost none of these nineteen states was about to adopt RCV statewide. The bans are *preemption* laws: they strip cities and counties of the power to choose their own local election method — the same cities conservatives otherwise praise as laboratories of democracy. Ohio reached for the budget weapon. Missouri went further: its legislature placed a constitutional ban on the November 2024 ballot, bundled it with a prohibition on noncitizen voting that duplicated what Missouri law already required, and titled the package so the citizenship language led. It passed with 68.4 percent — and a carve-out grandfathering St. Louis's existing nonpartisan system, which suggests even the drafters knew an actual repeal of a working local system wouldn't sell. When one side of a debate works to make sure voters never get to run the experiment, it has told you its own estimate of how the experiment would come out. Alaska's voters were asked, twice, with the experience fresh — and twice declined to go back.

None of this settles whether the critics' empirical claims hold up across the full record; it settles who the critics are and what their best case is. Errors are real but small and shrinking; exhaustion is real but better than the alternatives' hidden versions of the same thing; the ally's critique is about sequencing, not substance.
