# How to Pass RCV in Your State

On Election Day 2014, while the rest of America was voting, volunteers in Maine stood outside polling places collecting signatures — about 40,000 in a single day — for a reform that wouldn't reach the ballot for two more years. They eventually filed 75,369 signatures, qualified their initiative, and in November 2016 Maine voted 52 to 48 to become the first state in the country to adopt ranked-choice voting. Then the legislature tried to delay-and-repeal it, and those same volunteers ran a people's veto campaign and won *again*, 54 to 46, in June 2018. I tell you this up front because the single most important thing to know about passing [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] is that it is not an argument you win once. It is a campaign you win three times: adoption, implementation, and defense.

## First, know which door your state has

There are two ways in, and your state constitution has already decided which one you get.

About half the states — 26, plus D.C. — give citizens some form of direct democracy: the [[term:ballot-initiative]], the popular [[term:referendum]], or both. Twenty-one allow citizen-initiated statutes; eighteen allow citizen-initiated constitutional amendments. If you live in one of these states, you can take the question over the heads of legislators whose own careers were built on the current rules. It is no accident that the only two states using RCV statewide — Maine and Alaska — both got there by initiative.

Everywhere else, the path runs through the legislature, and it is slower but not hopeless — it just arrives in smaller packages. Hawaii's legislature adopted RCV in 2022 for federal special elections and county-council vacancies (Act 47), the kind of low-stakes beachhead that lets election officials build competence before anyone's governorship rides on it. Colorado's HB21-1071 created a state-supported local option, which Boulder used to rank its first mayoral ballots in 2023. Virginia's legislature opened a local option for county boards and city councils, which Arlington has used since 2023. The legislative route means accepting the politician's eternal question — "how does this affect me?" — and designing first steps where the honest answer is "barely."

<Checkpoint question="Your state has no initiative process, and the majority party chairs both elections committees. Applying this section, what does a realistic first bill look like — and what does it deliberately leave out?">
  It looks like Hawaii's Act 47 or Colorado's HB21-1071: RCV for special elections, military and overseas runoff ballots, or an opt-in for cities — venues where no sitting legislator's seat is at stake. It deliberately leaves out statewide and legislative offices, because a bill that threatens the careers of the people voting on it dies in committee. The beachhead builds administrative competence and a local track record, which is the raw material every later, bigger campaign runs on.

Walk that flow and you'll notice what it implies: the ballot question is the *middle* of the campaign, not the end. The 2016 Maine vote took two years of signature-gathering and education before it and two more years of legal and political defense after it — including an advisory opinion from Maine's supreme court that forced the law back to primaries and federal races only, and the people's veto that saved even that.

## What 2024 taught us — concede it, then learn from it

I will not spin this. November 2024 was the worst night the statewide RCV movement has had. Adoption measures lost everywhere they appeared: Colorado's Proposition 131 took 46 percent, Idaho's Proposition 1 took 30 percent, Nevada's Question 3 — which had *passed* its first vote in 2022 — failed its required second vote with 47 percent, Oregon's Measure 117 took 42 percent, and Arizona's Proposition 140 failed with 41. Missouri voters approved a legislature-written constitutional ban the same night. Only D.C., where Initiative 83 won 72.9 percent, and Alaska, which beat repeal by 743 votes, kept the map from being a shutout.

Three lessons, learned the expensive way. First, bundling cuts both ways: Alaska's 2020 package worked, but the 2024 measures mostly asked voters to swallow open primaries *and* new general-election rules *and* unfamiliar ballots in one gulp — Idaho's drubbing suggests the dose matters. Second, the opposition showed up funded and organized this cycle, with model legislation and national messaging that had matured since 2020 — the bans you read about in "The Opposition" are the same network playing offense. Third, status-quo bias is the house edge: a confused voter votes no. The movement's response has been visible in 2025 and 2026 — back to cities, back to legislatures, back to defending Alaska — and slower, which is not the same as stopped. Reform movements in America have always advanced in waves; women's suffrage lost dozens of state referenda before it stopped losing.

## Implementation is the campaign after the campaign

Here is the part most advocates skip and I refuse to: a yes vote buys you an engineering project. Three line items decide whether your reform survives its first contact with reality.

**Equipment.** Modern tabulators from the major vendors can run ranked elections, but "can" means certified software, tested configurations, and counties that actually license it — readiness varies county by county, which is why the Bipartisan Policy Center's *Reform Meets Reality* report and the RCV Resource Center's state readiness assessments exist. Put equipment audits in the bill text, with funding.

**Education.** New York's $15 million campaign before June 2021 — ads, multilingual outreach, community partnerships — is the benchmark, and it bought a 95 percent "the ballot was simple" verdict in a city of eight million. The error-rate disparities you saw in "The Data" are the cost of skimping. Budget education like turnout depends on it, because for the most vulnerable voters it does.

**A sunset is a death sentence — negotiate it out.** Utah is the cautionary tale. Its 2018 pilot (HB 35) let cities opt in; 23 did in 2021, and voters in those cities ranked happily. But pilots expire: participation fell to 12 cities by 2023 as clerks and councils anticipated the end, extension bills stalled in Senate committees, and on January 1, 2026, the program sunset and every participating city reverted to plurality elections, regardless of what its own voters preferred. Nobody repealed Utah's RCV. It was simply allowed to lapse — which, for the opposition, is victory at zero cost. If your legislature offers you a pilot with a sunset clause, understand that you are being offered a reform with a pre-installed self-destruct, and the burden of proof at renewal will sit on you forever.

What this chapter settles: there is a playbook, it has receipts, and its failure modes — under-organized signature drives, over-stuffed measures, unfunded education, sunset clauses — are now as well documented as its wins. What it doesn't settle is where the map actually stands after all this winning and losing, ban by ban and debut by debut. That inventory — including what happens in Alaska this November and what just happened in Washington, D.C. — is the next chapter.
