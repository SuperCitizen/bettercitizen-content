# The Path to Fifty States

On the night of November 5, 2024, the [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] movement lost five statewide ballot measures in five states in about six hours. If this book were a sales brochure, this chapter would skip that night. It isn't, and the path to fifty states runs directly through an honest autopsy of it — because the same night also contained, in plain sight, the evidence for how this reform actually spreads.

## Two doors into a state

There are exactly two ways a state adopts a new election system: its people impose it, or its legislature passes it. The first door is the [[term:ballot-initiative]], and it only exists in some buildings — 26 states provide for a statewide initiative or [[term:referendum]] process. Every statewide ranked-choice victory so far has come through that door: Maine in 2016, Alaska in 2020, both by citizen initiative, both over the objection of the political class. That's not a coincidence. Asking legislators to change the rules that elected them is asking the house to vote against the house; citizens with petitions don't need permission.

The second door — the legislature — opens rarely and usually only partway: a pilot program here, a local option there, ranked ballots for a narrow class of voters. But partway matters, as we'll see, and in roughly half the country it's the only door there is. Strategy in a non-initiative state and strategy in an initiative state are different sports.

And some doors are now barred from the inside: as of mid-2026, nineteen states have banned ranked choice voting by statute. The map of the possible, then, has three colors — initiative states, legislature-only states, and ban states, where the first task is repeal. Anyone who tells you there's one national playbook hasn't looked at the map.

## The beachhead model

Here's the history the movement is built on, with dates, because the dates are the argument.

San Francisco passed Proposition A in March 2002, 55 to 45, and ran the country's first big-city instant-runoff election in November 2004. Nothing melted. Neighboring Bay Area cities followed. Minneapolis voters adopted ranked ballots and first used them in 2009 — after the Minnesota Supreme Court waved the system through. Maine, where Portland had already been ranking mayoral ballots, became the first state in 2016. New York City — the largest electorate of any American city — adopted it in 2019 with 73.5 percent of the vote. Alaska went furthest in 2020, ranking its entire general election. At every step, the unanswerable argument was the beachhead next door: *they did it, it works, go watch.*

The model's logic is simple: cities are where voters can *meet* the reform before being asked to marry it. People who have used ranked ballots stop finding them scary — which, as the 2024 results are about to show, is the whole ballgame.

<Checkpoint question="Why would a movement with national ambitions spend twenty years on city ordinances instead of going straight for statewide ballot measures? Name the specific strategic asset a city win creates that a white paper can't.">
  A working example adjacent to the next electorate. Statewide campaigns are won or lost on whether voters believe the thing works in practice, and the only unanswerable proof is a neighboring jurisdiction running it without drama — San Francisco for the Bay Area, Portland for Maine, Takoma Park for D.C. A city win also builds the other assets campaigns need: trained volunteers, election officials with experience, and local politicians who won under the system and will say so.

Now the autopsy. Five statewide measures to adopt ranked choice voting (most bundled with open primaries) lost in November 2024, and the margins deserve to be stated plainly rather than mumbled.

Idaho's Proposition 1 lost 30.4 to 69.6. Arizona's Proposition 140 lost with 41.3 percent. Oregon's Measure 117 — the only one that was ranked choice voting alone, unbundled — took 42.3 percent. Nevada's Question 3, which had *passed* 53–47 in 2022, failed its constitutionally required second vote with 46.9 percent. Colorado's Proposition 131 reached 46.5. Montana's two-part package split the difference and, in doing so, ran the movement's most informative natural experiment: the open-primary half (CI-126) drew 48.9 percent while the majority-winner half (CI-127) drew 39.6 — same electorate, same night, nine points apart.

What did the post-mortems conclude? Three things, consistently. First, bundling hurt: most of these measures asked voters to swallow open primaries *and* ranked generals in one gulp, and Montana's split result is as clean a measurement of the bundling penalty as we will ever get. Second, complexity messaging worked: opponents didn't have to win an argument that the reform was bad, only plant doubt that it was confusing — and "if you're confused, vote no" is the oldest play in ballot-measure politics. Third, the duopoly closed ranks: as [[org:unite-america|Unite America]]'s Nick Troiano put it afterward, the initiatives were "largely swept up in a highly polarized climate," with *both* parties and "their aligned special interests fighting tooth and nail against these initiatives." The Sightline Institute's review added a strategic conclusion I accept: Alaska's 2020 win tempted the movement to skip the city-by-city step in states with no beachheads, and 2024 was the bill for that shortcut.

But read the same night's fine print. Washington, D.C. adopted ranked choice voting with about 73 percent. Bloomington, Minnesota voted to *keep* its system. Alaska's repeal failed. And inside Oregon's loss, Measure 117 *won* in Benton and Multnomah counties — the two places already using ranked ballots. The pattern across every 2024 contest is almost embarrassingly legible: electorates that had used the reform voted for it; electorates being asked to adopt it sight-unseen voted no.

## The federal lever

There is a third door I haven't mentioned, because it's locked — but worth describing, since the key exists. Congress has Elections Clause power over the manner of congressional elections, and in December 2025, Senators Peter Welch and Andy Kim and Representatives Jamie Raskin and Don Beyer reintroduced the Ranked Choice Voting Act, which would require ranked ballots in congressional primaries and generals beginning in 2028. I'll be straight with you: that bill is not passing this Congress. Its function, for now, is standard-setting — putting the destination on the federal map and giving members from RCV states something to co-sponsor.

The quieter federal story is already running, and it's my favorite fact to hand skeptics: six states — Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina — *already send ranked ballots* to their military and overseas voters, so a soldier abroad can vote in a federal runoff without waiting on a second envelope that may never arrive in time. Six deep-red states, using ranked ballots routinely, for decades' worth of election cycles, without controversy — because there, the reform solves an administrative problem everyone can see. Hawaii fills certain vacancies by ranked ballot for the same practical reason. When ranked choice voting is a tool rather than a tribal symbol, Americans use it without flinching.

## What "fifty states" actually means

So here is the path as I see it from mid-2026, stripped of prophecy. Defend the beachheads: Alaska votes on repeal *again* this November, and holding it matters more than any new acquisition. Implement well where we've won: D.C.'s rollout this year is the next national demonstration. Rebuild the city ladder in the states that skipped it — the Greenbelts and Skokies are not consolation prizes; they are the supply chain of future statewide wins. Unbundle where bundling kills, and draft around state constitutions instead of into them. Keep the federal standard alive for the day the count of RCV states makes it thinkable.

I won't insult you with a tidy three-phase timetable ending in inevitability. Movements that mistake their roadmap for reality get 2024 — and I know the history well enough not to claim familiarity guarantees loyalty; Burlington, Vermont repealed its instant runoff in 2010 before re-adopting it a decade later. What I'll claim instead is narrower and grounded in the most recent evidence: in the 2024 cycle, every American electorate already living with ranked ballots that was asked about them — Alaska, Bloomington, the Oregon counties — voted to keep or expand them. A reform that wins wherever it's known and loses mainly where it's a stranger has a marketing problem, not a product problem. The question is speed, and speed is a function of the work. Why the work is urgent *now* — why this window, this decade — is the next chapter.
