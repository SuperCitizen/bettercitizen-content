# Primaries and the Extremism Machine

Before you read a word of my argument, commit to three guesses. The numbers below come from [[org:unite-america|Unite America]]'s analysis of the 2022 midterms — the most recent complete dataset of its kind — and they describe the elections that actually decide who sits in the U.S. House.

If you guessed low on the first number or high on the second, you now understand American politics better than most newspaper columnists. In 2022, 359 of 435 House seats — 83 percent — were safe enough for one party that the November election was a formality; the real choice happened in a [[term:primary-election]], and just 8 percent of the voting-age population voted in the primaries that settled them. Of those 359 seats, 129 were decided by primaries with literally one candidate on the ballot. That is the machine this chapter is about. The general election is the show; the primary is the decision — and almost nobody is in the room where it happens.

## How the machine works

Two forces built it. The first is the vanishing competitive district: between [[term:gerrymandering]] and the way Americans have sorted themselves geographically — Democrats packing into cities, Republicans spreading across rural counties — the number of districts that could plausibly go either way in November has collapsed. The second is the turnout cliff: primaries are held on odd dates, in odd months, for contests most voters never hear about, and participation routinely runs at a fraction of November's.

Put them together and you get a transfer of power that no one ever voted to enact: in a safe district, the only election with suspense is the primary, so the only electorate the representative answers to is the primary electorate. Win that, and November is a coronation. Which means the threat that disciplines an incumbent's every vote isn't "will my district fire me?" It's "will my primary fire me?" — being "primaried," a word American politics had to invent. The behavioral effect doesn't require many incumbents to actually lose primaries (few do). It requires every incumbent to *watch* the handful who did. Ask Liz Cheney, who lost her 2022 Wyoming primary by 37 points after the most prominent break with her party's leader in a generation — every Republican in Congress absorbed that lesson at machine speed, which was precisely the point.

<Checkpoint question="A representative from a district that is 70–30 for her party faces a vote she knows is popular with her district overall but loathed by her party's most engaged activists. Walk the machine: what does the math tell her to do?">
  Vote with the activists. Her November electorate is irrelevant — a 70–30 district cannot fire her in a general election. Her primary electorate, a small, intensely engaged, ideologically motivated slice, absolutely can. The rational move is to obey the slice, not the district — and the chilling part is that this isn't corruption or cowardice. It's arithmetic. Change her math and you change her vote; that's the entire theory of primary reform.
</Checkpoint>

## The honest complication: what the political science actually says

Now the part an advocate is supposed to skip. The cleanest academic test of the obvious fix — "open the primaries and moderates will win" — came back negative. Eric McGhee, Seth Masket, Boris Shor, Steven Rogers, and Nolan McCarty, in a 2014 *American Journal of Political Science* study of state legislators across decades, found that the openness of a primary system has little if any effect on the ideology of the politicians it produces. Merely letting independents into a partisan primary doesn't reliably moderate anyone: the same activists dominate, candidates still court the same organized factions, and the party's brand still screens who runs. And the caricature of primary voters as foaming ideologues is itself shaky — they look more like the party's most *loyal* voters than its most extreme ones.

So why am I still writing this chapter? Because the machine's problem was never primarily the *voters* in the primary — it's the primary's *monopoly on consequence*. The defect isn't who shows up in May; it's that May is final in 83 percent of districts. McGhee and colleagues studied variations on the question "who may vote in the party's gate?" The Alaska model abolishes the gate's finality altogether: every candidate on one ballot, the top four advance regardless of party, and the binding decision moves to November, where everyone votes and [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] makes a four-way race work without spoilers. That's not "opening" the primary — it's demoting it from verdict to scrimmage. The null result on open primaries is, read carefully, the *argument* for the bigger surgery: half-measures don't change the math, so change the math.

The proof of concept arrived in 2022, and you met her two chapters ago. Lisa Murkowski had cast the most primary-able vote imaginable — to convict her own party's president at an impeachment trial — and her party censured her for it. Under the old closed-primary rules, that's a career obituary. Under top-four, she advanced from the all-comers primary, and in November — the only round that now binds — she won the final count 53.7 to 46.3, with a coalition no closed primary would ever have let her assemble. On the very same ballots, Alaskans reelected a conservative Republican governor outright. The system didn't manufacture moderates; it let the *whole* electorate, instead of one party's most engaged tenth, decide whether it wanted this one.

## Final five, final four — and what happened in Nevada

The fullest blueprint comes from an unlikely pair: Katherine Gehl, a former food-company CEO, and Michael Porter, the Harvard Business School strategy scholar, whose 2020 book *The Politics Industry* (Harvard Business Review Press) analyzes the two parties as a textbook duopoly — competing not to solve public problems but to deepen customer lock-in, with the primary as the industry's most effective customer-discipline tool. Their remedy, Final Five Voting, is Alaska's architecture with one more lane: a single all-candidate primary sending five finalists to an instant-runoff general. Alaska runs the top-four version. Nevada tried for five — and taught the movement a lesson in humility: its Question 3 passed with 53 percent in 2022, then failed the constitutionally required second vote in 2024 with 47 percent, after the opposition had two full years to organize against a stationary target. The machine, it turns out, also gets a vote on whether it is dismantled.

And the runoff states deserve a paragraph, because they are the machine's most expensive accessory. Several states resolve crowded primaries with a second, later primary — a runoff — which sounds majoritarian until you watch the median 40 percent of first-round voters fail to return. A device meant to legitimize nominees instead hands them to an even smaller, even more activated electorate, at full taxpayer cost. Instant-runoff ballots solve precisely this, in one trip, which is why some runoff states already mail ranked ballots to their soldiers overseas.

## What this chapter settles — and doesn't

It settles the diagnosis: in 83 percent of House districts the binding election is a primary that 8 percent of adults attend, and rational politicians serve the electorate that can fire them, with [[term:incumbency]] preserved by obedience to the slice rather than the district. It settles, too, that the honest version of the cure is structural — moving finality from the primary to a full-electorate November count — not the feel-good version the null results already buried. What it does not settle is whether Alaska's experience generalizes beyond Alaska, or how much moderation the bigger surgery buys; that evidence is accumulating one cycle at a time, and I gave you my best reading of it in "The Data." There is also a force this chapter has treated as scenery when it is actually a character: the institutions that decide what you hear about all of this — who frame a fifteen-day count as chaos and a censured senator's survival as a fluke. The press and this reform have a complicated relationship, and it gets the next chapter.
