## The night of five losses

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

On the night of November 5, 2024, the ranked-choice voting movement lost five statewide ballot measures in five states in about six hours. If this book were a sales brochure, this chapter would skip that night.

> The path to fifty states runs through an honest autopsy of that night.

## The beachhead model

Here is the history the movement is built on. San Francisco passed ranked ballots in March 2002. Minneapolis first used them in 2009. Maine became the first state in 2016. New York City adopted them in 2019 with 73.5 percent of the vote. Alaska went furthest in 2020.

- San Francisco: 2002
- Maine: 2016
- New York City: 73.5% in 2019

Cities are where voters can meet the reform before being asked to marry it.

## The accounting

The margins deserve to be stated plainly. Idaho's measure took 30.4 percent. Arizona's took 41.3, Oregon's 42.3, Colorado's 46.5, and Nevada's 46.9.

- Idaho: 30.4%
- Nevada: 46.9%

The post-mortems concluded three things. Bundling ranked ballots with open primaries hurt. Opponents only had to plant doubt that the reform was confusing. And both parties fought the measures. One review added a conclusion I accept: the movement skipped the city-by-city step, and 2024 was the bill.

- Bundling, complexity, both parties opposed

## The same night's fine print

Washington, D.C., adopted ranked choice voting with about 73 percent. Bloomington, Minnesota, voted to keep its system. Alaska's repeal failed. And inside Oregon's loss, the measure won in the two counties already using ranked ballots.

> Electorates that had used it voted yes. Electorates asked sight-unseen voted no.

I say that is a marketing problem, not a product problem. I do not claim familiarity guarantees loyalty: Burlington, Vermont, repealed its instant runoff in 2010, before re-adopting it a decade later.

- Burlington: repealed 2010, later re-adopted

The lesson charts every 2024 result, reads Montana's split vote as a natural experiment, and describes the two doors into a state and a third, federal one that is locked.
