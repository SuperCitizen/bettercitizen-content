## Two capitals, one reform

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. It gives the map as of mid-2026.

In June, voters in Washington, D.C., rank candidates for mayor for the first time, under an initiative that 72.9 percent of them approved. Three months earlier, in Columbus, the governor signed the law making Ohio the nineteenth state to ban ranked-choice voting.

> Both are true at once: a first ranked election, and a nineteenth ban.

## Where it is used

FairVote counts 49 jurisdictions that use ranked-choice voting or have adopted it, reaching nearly 14 million voters across 22 states and D.C. Jurisdictions, not states. Only Maine and Alaska use it statewide. Most of the 49 are cities and counties.

- 49 jurisdictions, not states
- Nearly 14 million voters
- Statewide: Maine and Alaska

Six states use it for one purpose only. They mail ranked ballots to military and overseas voters, so a runoff does not need a second envelope. Several states that ban ranked-choice voting for everyone else still rank their soldiers' ballots.

- 6 states: military and overseas ballots

## Where it is banned

Nineteen states prohibit ranked-choice voting, almost all by forbidding their own cities from adopting it. Seventeen of the nineteen bans came from states under unified Republican control. Kansas and Kentucky enacted theirs under divided government, and Ohio's passed with bipartisan votes.

- 19 state bans, 2022 to 2026

> In both maps, the action is states deciding for cities.

I call that the laboratory being padlocked. That is my reading, not a count.

## What 2026 decides

Two votes move the map. D.C. is the debut, and only half of its initiative is running: the Council funded the ranked ballots and left the semi-open primaries unfunded. Alaska is the other. On November 3, 2026, Alaskans vote on repeal for a third time. They kept the system in 2024 by 743 votes. I won't predict it.

- D.C.: half funded
- Alaska: third vote, November 3, 2026
