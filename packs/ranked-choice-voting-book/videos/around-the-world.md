## Corangamite, 1918

On December 14, 1918, voters in the Australian district of Corangamite ranked their candidates. No national electorate had done that before. James Scullin led the first count with about 42 percent. Then the trailing candidates were eliminated, and a farmer named William Gibson overtook him. Gibson won with about 56 percent of the final count.

- First count: Scullin, about 42%
- Final count: Gibson, about 56%

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. My claim: the ranked ballot is proven technology.

## A conservative fix

Ranked ballots came to Australia from a conservative government protecting itself from the spoiler effect. In 1918 the new Country Party began running against the governing Nationalists, splitting the anti-Labor vote. The government answered within weeks, with the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918.

- Australia, 1918: a fix for vote-splitting

Every Australian House election for over a hundred years has been conducted with ranked ballots.

> Australia has ranked its House ballots for over a hundred years.

## The voters who refused to stop

Ireland has elected its parliament by ranked ballot since independence. Twice, a governing party asked Irish voters to switch to first-past-the-post, the system the United States uses. In 1959 voters rejected the switch by roughly 52 to 48. In 1968 they rejected it by roughly 61 to 39.

- 1959: no, roughly 52 to 48
- 1968: no, roughly 61 to 39

> Asked twice to give up ranked ballots, Irish voters said no.

## What my opponents are right to raise

A status-quo defender will point out that every country in this chapter is a parliamentary democracy, and that Australia pairs its ranked ballots with compulsory voting. Fair.

- Status quo: parliamentary systems, compulsory voting

An alternative-reform advocate will point out that most of these success stories elect several members per district, which is proportional representation. Most American proposals elect one winner. Also fair.

- Alternative reform: abroad, mostly multi-member districts

Australia's House remains dominated by two big blocs. And I have not shown that what works in Canberra or Dublin transfers cleanly to American conditions.
