## A Tuesday in June

On a Tuesday in June 2021, nearly a million New Yorkers ranked their choices for mayor. Thirteen candidates, up to five rankings each. It was the largest test ranked choice voting had ever faced in America.

- New York, 2021: 13 candidates, 5 rankings

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. My claim: for two decades, cities have been this reform's laboratories.

## What it does: Oakland

Oakland first used ranked choice voting in 2010, in an open race for mayor. Don Perata led the first count with about 34 percent of first choices. Jean Quan sat at 24. Under plurality rules, Perata wins and the story ends.

- First count: Perata about 34%, Quan 24%

Instead, trailing candidates were eliminated, and their voters' backup rankings transferred. Quan won the final round, 51 to 49.

- Final round: Quan 51, Perata 49

> Thirty-four percent is the largest fragment, not the majority's choice.

Perata's supporters cried foul. You can understand the instinct: their candidate came in first.

## Scale, and a stumble

In New York, a week after that 2021 primary, the Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that was visibly wrong. Staff had failed to clear test ballots from a trial run. They retracted it within hours and re-ran the count clean.

- The error: test ballots left in

Critics declared ranked choice voting too complicated to administer. I answer that an agency made a data-handling error it could have made under any system.

Then the city ran it again, bigger. The June 2025 mayoral primary drew over a million Democratic ballots, and the tabulation was released on schedule.

- 2025: over a million ballots, on schedule

## The other column of the ledger

A few places have walked away. Pierce County, Washington repealed it in 2009. Aspen, Colorado dropped it in 2010. Burlington, Vermont repealed it in 2010, 52 to 48. And nineteen states have banned it outright.

- Repeals: Pierce County, Aspen, Burlington
- 19 states have banned it

Burlington itself came back. In March 2021 its voters reinstated it for city council races, by 64 percent.

> Burlington repealed it in 2010 and brought it back in 2021.

Are the repeals noise or signal? Status-quo defenders read them as voters rejecting a confusing import. Alternative-reform advocates argue cities should be piloting different systems entirely. I say the voters who use it keep it. That is not settled here.
