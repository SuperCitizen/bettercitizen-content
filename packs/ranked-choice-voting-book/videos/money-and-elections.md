## An election almost nobody attended

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

On October 1, 2013, New York City held a runoff for public advocate. No candidate had passed the 40 percent the law required, so the city ran the top two again. It cost an estimated 13 million dollars. About 6.5 percent of eligible voters showed up.

- $13 million
- 6.5% turnout

> Thirteen million dollars, to ask what the first ballot could have asked.

## The runoff tax

Wherever the law demands a majority winner, somebody pays for the second round. An instant runoff uses the same majority logic in one trip to the polls. Where runoffs exist, I call that bookkeeping, not advocacy.

The other side of the ledger: a place that never held runoffs does not pocket those savings, and switching has real one-time costs. If a county clerk says the transition is not free, she is right.

- No runoffs, no savings

## The spoiler-fear subsidy

The harder argument is the one I care about. Under pick-one rules, a donation to a third candidate can fire backward: it may split your own side's vote and elect the candidate you fear most. So serious money flows into two channels. When a ballot can transfer, funding an outsider cannot backfire that way.

- Pick-one: funding an outsider can backfire

This is a structural argument. It is not a claim that elections get cheaper, or that dark money dries up.

> Not one dollar fewer may be spent. Who can compete for the dollars changes.

## What the evidence shows

The empirical record is thin, and I will say so plainly. On tone, surveys found voters in ranked-choice cities less likely to call campaigns negative. A 2023 follow-up found the effect real but bounded: the most viable candidates did not report gentler campaigns. And one study found Maine's 2018 congressional campaign was more negative than comparable districts.

- Civility: real but bounded
- Maine 2018: more negative

On money itself, there is no body of evidence that ranked choice voting changes much of anything yet. What the record does show is an entry effect: more people run.

- Money totals: no evidence yet
- Entry: more candidates run
