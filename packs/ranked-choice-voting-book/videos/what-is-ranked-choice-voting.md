## One sentence

The entire reform fits in one sentence: instead of picking one candidate, you rank them. First choice, second choice, third, as many or as few as you want.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. This chapter is the owner's manual, fine print included.

- Rank as many as you want

## One vote, one candidate

A ranked ballot is not a points system. Your second choice does not get half a vote. At every moment of the count, your ballot is one vote for one candidate: the highest-ranked one still in the race.

> One ballot, one vote: for your highest-ranked candidate still in the race.

So ranking a backup cannot hurt your favorite. The count never looks at your second choice until your first choice has been eliminated.

- Second choice: read only after elimination

## The count

Count everyone's first choices. If a candidate has a majority of the ballots in play, that is the winner. If nobody does, the candidate in last place is eliminated. Each of those ballots moves, at full strength, to whoever that voter ranked next. Count again, and repeat.

- Last place is eliminated
- Those ballots move to the voter's next choice

The lesson runs a four-candidate race of 1,000 ballots. Rivera led the first round with 380 votes, and lost. Fifty ballots ranked only candidates who were eliminated. Those ballots exhausted: they had nothing more to say. Okafor won with 520, a majority of the 950 ballots still active.

- Exhausted: no ranked candidate remains
- Okafor: 520 of 950 active ballots

> The winner needs a majority of the ballots still in play.

[check q2]

## What ranking does not promise

Now the fine print, stated by an advocate on purpose. Ranked choice voting does not guarantee that the consensus candidate wins. A candidate who would beat every rival head-to-head can still be eliminated early, for lack of first choices. It happened in Alaska in August 2022.

- A head-to-head favorite can be eliminated

Exhausted ballots are real. And a majority of continuing ballots is not always a majority of everyone who showed up.

- Majority of active ballots, not of all cast

The lesson lets you step through that race round by round. It also has the method's history, back to Australia in 1918, and the multi-winner form used for city councils.
