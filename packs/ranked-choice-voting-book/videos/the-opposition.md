## The nineteenth state

On March 17, 2026, Ohio became the nineteenth state to ban ranked choice voting. The law forbids Ohio's own cities from using it. Ranked ballots are being banned, in state after state, faster than they are being adopted.

- Ohio, March 2026: the 19th ban

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. Here I give the opposition's three strongest arguments the way their smartest advocates make them.

## One: errors

Stephen Pettigrew and Dylan Radley analyzed millions of ballots. Votes in ranked races were rejected at about ten times the rate of votes in single-choice races on the same ballots: 0.53 percent against 0.04. And the mismarks concentrate in lower-income precincts.

- Rejected: 0.53% ranked, 0.04% pick-one

> The equity claim: ranking's burden falls heaviest on the voters reformers champion.

## Two: the majority

Craig Burnett and Vladimir Kogan studied four early ranked elections. From 9.6 to 27.1 percent of ballots ranked only eliminated candidates, and carried no preference into the final round. In all four, the winner had less than half of all votes cast.

- Exhausted ballots: 9.6% to 27.1%

> Heritage calls it a "faux majority." On the raw arithmetic, the phrase has bite.

## Three: the wrong reform

Lee Drutman is a political scientist who wrote an influential pro-reform book. He argues that the evidence for moderation is thin, and that reformers should spend their energy on proportional representation instead.

- Drutman: proportional representation instead

## My answers

On errors: 99.5 percent of ranked votes count, against 99.96 percent under pick-one rules. That is a real difference, and I will not wave it away. One follow-up study of New York found error rates falling with experience.

- 99.5% counted, against 99.96%

On exhausted ballots: compare the real alternative. Under plurality, every ballot cast for a losing candidate says nothing about the finalists.

On the wrong reform: Drutman is right that the moderation evidence is modest. I think he is wrong to treat the two reforms as rivals.

> My answer: errors are real but small, and exhaustion beats the alternatives.

None of this settles whether the critics' empirical claims hold up across the full record. The lesson names who opposes this reform, in both parties, and shows what the bans themselves do.
