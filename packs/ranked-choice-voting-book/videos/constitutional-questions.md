## The congressman who sued

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

On November 13, 2018, Congressman Bruce Poliquin led Maine's Second District on first choices, 46.3 percent to 45.6, with no majority. The state's new ranked-choice count was about to run. He asked a federal judge to stop it, arguing that counting backup preferences would violate the United States Constitution.

- Maine, 2018: Poliquin sues

## What the text says

The Constitution gives the states power over the "Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections, subject to Congress. It says nothing about how a winner must be calculated. The word "plurality" does not appear. Georgia ran a statewide runoff for a Senate seat as recently as December 2022.

> No clause of the Constitution requires plurality winners.

The judge, Lance Walker, declined to halt the count, and a month later rejected every one of Poliquin's constitutional claims.

## One person, one vote

The most persistent attack sounds intuitive. If my ballot counts for my second choice after my first is eliminated, did I vote twice? Watch the count. In every round, every active ballot counts exactly once, for exactly one candidate. In 2011 the Ninth Circuit upheld San Francisco's system in Dudum versus Arntz: ballots are not counted unequally.

- Each round: one ballot, one candidate

> A ranking is not extra votes. It is standing instructions for one vote.

## The one real loss

Honest advocacy reports its losses. In 2017, Maine's Supreme Judicial Court advised, unanimously, that ranked-choice voting conflicted with the Maine Constitution, which elects the governor and legislators "by a plurality of all votes returned." In April 2026 the same court said so again. So Maine ranks its primaries and federal elections, and not its November elections for governor and the Legislature.

- Maine's plurality clause: 2017, 2026

> No court has held that ranked choice voting violates anyone's rights.

## What is still contested

Nineteen states now ban ranked choice voting by statute. A ban is a policy choice, not a constitutional holding. The other side's strongest honest argument was never that the method is unlawful. It is that it is unwise, a case they are entitled to make and voters are entitled to weigh.

- Not unlawful. Unwise? That is political.
