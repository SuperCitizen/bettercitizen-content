## One mark

You walk into a voting booth. You pick one person. You leave.

[beat]

You may have spent weeks weighing three candidates: admiring one, tolerating another, dreading the third. The ballot does not care.

> A pick-one ballot asks one question and discards everything else you know.

This is the introduction to Rank the Vote, a book by Chris Borgia. It is an argument for a reform, and the "I" in it is the author.

## The question the ballot never asked

In November 2000 the presidency came down to 537 votes. That was George W. Bush's certified margin over Al Gore in Florida, out of nearly six million cast.

- Bush's margin in Florida: 537 votes

On those same ballots, 97,488 people voted for Ralph Nader. Whom would they have chosen between the other two?

- Nader's Florida vote: 97,488

[pause]

> Nobody knows, and nobody can know. The ballot never asked.

## The fix I argue for

The fix this book argues for is small. Same candidates, same booths, same Tuesday in November. One change: you rank the candidates. First choice, second choice, third, as far down as you care to go.

- Rank them: 1st, 2nd, 3rd

> If your favorite cannot win, your ballot counts for your next choice.

That is ranked choice voting.

## Both halves

The experiment has been run. Maine's voters adopted ranked choice voting in 2016, and Alaska followed in 2020. By FairVote's count, as of early 2026, 49 American jurisdictions use it or have adopted it. They are home to nearly 14 million voters.

- 49 jurisdictions, nearly 14 million voters

And you should hear the other half from me first. The backlash is just as real. Nineteen states have passed laws banning it, most recently Ohio in March 2026. Alaska's opponents lost a repeal vote by 743 votes in 2024. They have qualified another for the November 2026 ballot.

- 19 states have banned it
- Alaska votes on repeal again, November 2026

Anyone who tells you this reform is inevitable is selling something.

## The deal

I am an advocate, and this book has a thesis. So here is my promise for every chapter: real numbers from official sources, named opponents quoted at their strongest, and a concession wherever the other side has a point.

- Opponents quoted at their strongest

This introduction asserts. It settles nothing. The lesson sets the two ballots side by side and maps the chapters ahead, from the diagnosis to the evidence to the opposition's best arguments.
