## The sermon

Every election cycle you hear the same sermon: do not waste your vote on a third party. Under pick-one rules, that is correct advice. Supporting your favorite candidate can help elect your least favorite.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. My claim here: the fault belongs to the ballot, not to the voters.

> When honesty is a strategic blunder, the system is wrong, not the voter.

## The largest fragment wins

Pick-one voting goes by the name plurality. It does not find the candidate most voters want. There is no majority to clear. Whoever holds the largest single fragment wins.

- Plurality: the largest fragment wins

In 2010, Paul LePage won the governorship of Maine with 37.6 percent of the vote. More than three of every five Mainers had marked someone else.

- Maine 2010: governor with 37.6%

> A divided majority loses to a unified minority.

## What the ballot cannot say

In 2000, Florida certified George W. Bush's margin over Al Gore: 537 votes. On those same ballots, 97,488 Floridians voted for Ralph Nader.

- Margin: 537 votes
- Nader: 97,488 votes

Did Nader cost Gore the presidency? The honest answer is that it cannot be proven. Exit polls suggested Nader's voters leaned toward Gore, but a lean is not a count, and some would have stayed home.

> The pick-one ballot collected no second choices.

And the fear does the work even when no election flips. Voters who see a third candidate losing abandon her in advance, and donors go with them. Maurice Duverger called that the psychological effect.

- Duverger: supporters defect in advance

## Not physics, and not the last word

Duverger's law is a strong tendency, not physics. Canada and the United Kingdom use plurality and keep stubborn regional third parties.

And others answer me. A status-quo defender says pick-one voting is simple, fast to count, familiar and decisive. An alternative-reform advocate says ranked ballots are the wrong fix, and that approval or score systems cure the same disease more cleanly. Each gets a chapter later in this book.

- Status quo: simple, fast, familiar, decisive
- Alternative reform: approval or score voting

This chapter settles the diagnosis, not the cure. The lesson has the full Florida arithmetic, Ross Perot's 1992 run, and both of Duverger's effects, with a case to test yourself on.
