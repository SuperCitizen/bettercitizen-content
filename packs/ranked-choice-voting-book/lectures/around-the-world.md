December 14, 1918. Sheep country, in western Victoria. The Australian district of Corangamite is holding an election, and its voters are doing something new. They are ranking their candidates.

James Scullin, a future Labor prime minister, leads the first count, with about 42 percent. Under the American way of counting, he would have won right there.

[beat]

But Australia had just rewritten its election law. The trailing candidates were eliminated. Their ballots flowed to second choices. And a farmer named William Gibson overtook him, and won with about 56 percent of the final count.

The majority prevailed, and not merely the largest faction.

[pause]

That was more than a century ago. So when an op-ed tells you ranked choice voting is an untested experiment, I want you to hear the claim for what it is. It is a confession that the author never looked past the water's edge.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter counted up the American research, and left one thing open: whether those findings hold at scale, and over decades. For that, we leave the country.

We will take it in four steps. First, Australia. Second, Ireland. Third, how far ranked ballots have quietly spread. And last, what the world's experience settles, and what it can't.

## Australia: a century of use

Here is the part of the story American conservatives should love. Ranked ballots did not come to Australia from progressive reformers. They came from a conservative government, protecting itself from the spoiler effect.

In 1918, the brand-new Country Party began running candidates against the governing Nationalists, in rural seats. That split the anti-Labor vote. In October, a special election in the Western Australian seat of Swan showed what that meant. Labor took the seat with barely a third of the vote, while two candidates of the right divided the rest.

The Nationalist government of Billy Hughes answered within weeks. The Commonwealth Electoral Act of 1918 introduced what Australians call preferential voting. Americans now call it instant-runoff voting. Allied parties could compete against each other, without handing the seat to their common opponent.

The first test was Corangamite, that December.

> Australia's ranked ballot came from conservatives solving a spoiler problem.

[pause]

And it has run ever since. Every election for the Australian House of Representatives, for over a hundred years, has used ranked ballots. Through depression, through war, through dozens of changes of government.

Two more facts about the Australian design. Australians rank every candidate on the ballot, by law. And since 1924, voting has been compulsory. Roughly nine in ten enrolled Australians cast a ballot, election after election. American officials would describe that turnout figure as a typo.

Then, in 1948, Australia doubled down. It switched its Senate to the single transferable vote, known as STV. That is the proportional, multi-winner cousin of instant runoff. One country has run both ranked systems, side by side, for three-quarters of a century.

[pause]

Let me be straight about what Australia does and does not prove.

It does not prove that ranked ballots dissolve two-party politics. The House remains dominated by two big blocs. Single-winner instant runoff is a majoritarian system. It is not proportional representation.

And the rule that you must rank every candidate has a real cost. It pushes some incomplete ballots into the rejected pile, which Australians call informal. Most American designs avoid that cost, by allowing partial rankings.

What a century of Australian elections does prove is the thing opponents claim to doubt. Ordinary voters, at national scale, on paper ballots, can rank candidates. They did it for decades before the computer existed.

Let's test that history. Australia adopted ranked ballots in 1918, under a conservative government. What problem was it solving? And what does that tell you about the claim that ranked choice voting inherently favors one party? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The problem was vote-splitting on the right. Two parties were dividing the anti-Labor vote, and Labor was winning seats with a third of it.

So the mechanism is neutral between parties. It protects whichever side happens to have more candidates than coordination. That is why parties across the spectrum have adopted it, at different times.

## Ireland: the voters defended it, twice

Now Ireland. Ireland has elected its parliament by STV since independence. In 1937, it wrote the method into the constitution itself. The text requires that members of the Dáil, its parliament, be elected "on the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote."

Irish constituencies elect three to five members each. So seats in the Dáil track vote shares closely. It is one of the most proportional outcomes any legislature achieves.

And Ireland still counts those ranked paper ballots by hand. In public count centres. Over days. With the eliminations and transfers broadcast like sport.

So much for "the machines can't handle it."

[pause]

But here is my favorite part of the Irish story, the part I quote at every town hall.

Twice, a governing party asked Irish voters to abandon STV. The replacement on offer was first-past-the-post, the plurality system the United States uses. Twice, the voters said no.

In 1959, the government of Éamon de Valera put the switch to a referendum. Voters rejected it, by roughly 52 to 48. And on the very same day, they elected de Valera president. They liked the man. They refused to give up the ballot.

The government tried again in 1968. It lost by roughly 61 to 39. A wider margin, the second time.

[pause]

Think about what that means. The only national electorates ever asked, after decades of using ranked ballots, whether they would like to stop, declined. Emphatically.

> Twice, Irish voters were asked to give up ranked ballots. Twice, they refused.

Familiarity with ranking does not breed contempt. It breeds attachment.

Now a harder question. Australia's House and Ireland's Dáil both use ranked ballots. Yet Australia stays two-bloc, while Ireland's parliament is highly proportional. What drives the difference? Think it through.

[long pause]

The number of winners per district. Australia elects one member from each district. Ireland elects three to five, so seats can track vote shares.

The ballot looks nearly identical in both. But single-winner instant runoff finds each district's majority winner. STV, in multi-member districts, is proportional: a party with 30 percent of the vote can win roughly 30 percent of the seats. The counting method and the ranking rules are real differences too. They don't change that divide.

> One winner per district is majoritarian. Three to five winners is proportional.

Hold on to that distinction. It comes back before the end.

## The quiet spread

Once you start looking, ranked ballots turn up everywhere the British constitutional tradition touched, and beyond.

Malta has used STV since 1921. Northern Ireland elects its Assembly and its local councils by STV. Scotland has elected every council that way since 2007.

New Zealand lets its councils opt into STV for local elections, and a growing set has done so, including the capital, Wellington. India's constitution prescribes the single transferable vote for electing its president. And Papua New Guinea moved its national elections to a ranked system in the 2000s, asking voters to rank at least three candidates.

[pause]

Lay those dates on a timeline, and notice its shape. The adoptions span a hundred years. The adopters span conservative governments, constitutional framers and devolved parliaments.

And the most recent entries, the newcomers to this club, are American states. Maine adopted ranked choice voting statewide, by citizen initiative, in 2016. Alaska followed in 2020.

We are not inventing something exotic. We are catching up to Australia's grandparents.

## What the world settles, and what it can't

In my reading, the international record settles three things.

First, voters can do this. A century of national elections, conducted on paper, and counted by hand where machines never entered the picture.

Second, it is durable. The Irish kept it through two referendums. And no Australian government, of either party, has seriously moved to strip preferential voting from the House.

Third, it is ideologically homeless. It was adopted by Australian conservatives dodging spoilers. By Irish framers seeking proportionality. And by a coalition of Labour and the Liberal Democrats in Scotland. That is what you would expect of a counting rule, and not of a partisan program.

> Voters can do it. It lasts. And no party owns it.

[pause]

Now the honest caveats, because my opponents are not wrong to raise them. There are two, and each gets its turn.

A status-quo defender will point out that every country in this chapter is a parliamentary democracy. Australia pairs its ranked ballots with compulsory voting. And transferring an institution across constitutional systems is never automatic.

> The status-quo defender's point: transfer across constitutional systems is never automatic.

That is fair.

An alternative-reform advocate will point out something subtler. Most of the world's ranked-ballot success stories are STV, in multi-member districts. That is proportional representation. Most American proposals are single-winner instant runoff. So if Ireland's consensus politics is what attracts you, a single-winner ballot in a gerrymandered district will not deliver it.

> The alternative-reform advocate's point: most of the world's successes are proportional STV.

That is also fair, and I come back to multi-winner ranked choice voting later in this book.

One of those two objections probably convinces you less than the other. Take that one. What is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case. Then notice what neither objection touches: the claim that ranking itself is unproven, confusing, or uncountable. I don't think that claim survives contact with a single Australian election night.

One last test, and it reaches back. An opponent says: "Voters will try ranking once, hate it, and demand repeal." Which piece of evidence from this chapter answers that most directly? And why is it stronger than a poll?

[long pause]

The Irish referendums. In 1959 and in 1968, a national electorate with decades of experience was asked to give the system up. It refused, and by a wider margin the second time.

That is stronger than a poll, because it is not a survey of people imagining a system they have never touched. It is experienced users, deciding their own future elections.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Australia's ranked ballot came from conservatives solving a spoiler problem, in 1918. Every House election since has used it.

[beat]

Two. Twice, Irish voters were asked to give up ranked ballots. Twice, they refused.

[beat]

Three. The same ballot runs two different systems. One winner per district is majoritarian. Three to five winners is proportional.

[beat]

Four. What the record settles, as I read it: voters can do it, it lasts, and no party owns it. And the two fair objections: transfer across constitutional systems is never automatic, and most of the world's successes are proportional STV.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled.

I have not shown that what works in Canberra or Dublin transfers cleanly to the United States. This is a system with separation of powers, partisan primaries, fifty separate election bureaucracies, and voluntary voting. That argument has to be made on American evidence, and the chapters on Maine, Alaska and the cities make it.

Nor have I resolved the family quarrel between single-winner ranked choice voting and proportional STV. The world's data honestly favors STV for proportionality. Reformers should say so out loud.

What this chapter does settle is narrower. The ranked ballot is proven technology, run at national scale since 1918. "Untested" is off the table.

[pause]

So go back to Corangamite, in December 1918, and the farmer who came from behind on second choices.

The question is not whether it can work.

[beat]

The question, for the rest of this book, is how we get it here. The next chapter starts on that: how to pass ranked choice voting in your state.
