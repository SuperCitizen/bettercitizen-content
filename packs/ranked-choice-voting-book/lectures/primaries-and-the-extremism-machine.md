Before I make any argument, three guesses. They are about the midterm elections of 2022, and about who really elects the U.S. House.

First guess. What share of House seats were so safe for one party that the primary effectively decided the winner?

[beat]

Second. What share of America's voting-age population cast a ballot in those deciding primaries?

[beat]

And third. In what share of those decided seats was the deciding primary uncontested, with one name on the ballot? Settle on your three numbers.

[pause 5s]

Here are the answers. They come from an analysis by a group called Unite America, the most recent complete dataset of its kind.

The first is 83 percent. That is 359 seats where November was a formality, and the real choice happened in a primary.

The second is 8 percent. Eight percent of the voting-age population voted in the primaries that settled those seats.

And the third is 36 percent. In 129 of those seats, the primary that decided everything had one candidate on the ballot.

[pause]

If you guessed low on the first or high on the second, you now understand American politics better than most newspaper columnists. And you have the subject of this chapter.

> The general election is the show. The primary is the decision.

And almost nobody is in the room where it happens.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked-choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was the map of the states, and it ended by asking why the fight has hardened as it has. My answer is that the deepest argument is about primaries.

We will take it in four steps. First, how the machine works. Second, a research finding that an advocate is supposed to skip. Third, the design I think answers it, and one senator who tested it. And last, the fullest blueprint, and a defeat.

## How the machine works

Two forces built the machine.

The first is the vanishing competitive district. Part of it is gerrymandering. Part of it is the way Americans have sorted themselves, with Democrats packing into cities and Republicans spreading across rural counties. Between the two, the number of districts that could plausibly go to one party or the other in November has collapsed.

The second force is the turnout cliff. Primaries are held on odd dates, in odd months, for contests most voters never hear about. Participation routinely runs at a fraction of November's.

Now put the two together. In a safe district, the only election with suspense is the primary. So the only electorate the representative answers to is the primary electorate. Win that, and November is a coronation.

Nobody ever voted to enact that transfer of power. But it changes the question an incumbent asks before every vote. The question is no longer "will my district fire me?"

[beat]

It is "will my primary fire me?" American politics had to invent a word for it: being primaried.

And notice, the effect does not require many incumbents to lose primaries. Few do. It requires every incumbent to watch the handful who did.

Ask Liz Cheney. She made the most prominent break with her party's leader in a generation. In 2022 she lost her Wyoming primary, by 37 points. Every Republican in Congress absorbed that lesson, which was precisely the point.

[pause]

Let's walk the machine with a case. A representative sits in a district that is 70 to 30 for her party. She faces a vote that is popular with her district overall, and loathed by her party's most engaged activists. What does the math tell her to do? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Vote with the activists. Her November electorate cannot fire her, not in a district like that. Her primary electorate can. It is a small, intensely engaged slice, and the rational move is to obey the slice, not the district.

The chilling part is that this is not corruption, and it is not cowardice. It is arithmetic.

> Change her math, and you change her vote.

That sentence is the entire theory of primary reform.

## The honest complication

Now the part an advocate is supposed to skip.

There is an obvious fix. Open the primaries, let independents in, and moderates will win. That fix got its cleanest academic test in 2014, and the test came back negative.

The political scientist Eric McGhee and four colleagues studied state legislators across decades. Here is what they found.

> The finding: opening a primary has little if any effect on legislators' ideology.

In plainer words, letting independents into a party's primary does not reliably moderate anyone. The same activists dominate. Candidates still court the same organized factions. And the party's brand still screens who runs.

There is a second blow to the usual story. The caricature of primary voters as foaming ideologues is itself shaky. They look more like their party's most loyal voters than its most extreme ones.

[pause]

So why am I still writing this chapter?

Because I do not think the machine's problem was ever mainly the voters in the primary. I think it is the primary's monopoly on consequence. The defect is not who shows up in May. It is that May is final, in 83 percent of districts.

> My reading: the defect is the primary's monopoly on consequence.

McGhee and his colleagues studied one question, in many variations: who may vote at the party's gate? The Alaska model does something else. It abolishes the gate's finality.

Here is how. Every candidate runs on one primary ballot. The top four advance, whatever their party. And the binding decision moves to November, where everyone votes. There, ranked-choice voting makes a four-way race work without spoilers.

That is not opening the primary. It is demoting the primary, from a verdict to a scrimmage.

So I take the null result, read with care, as the argument for bigger surgery. Half-measures do not change the math. So change the math.

That is my reading, and it is not the only one. A skeptic could take the same study to show that primary structure is irrelevant, and that reformers should abandon election design. I think that overreads it, because the study never tested a system like Alaska's. But take the reading you find less convincing, mine or the skeptic's, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## One senator, one test

A design is a theory. Here is the case I call the proof of concept. It arrived in 2022, and it is one case.

Lisa Murkowski, a senator from Alaska, had cast the most primary-able vote imaginable. She voted to convict her own party's president at an impeachment trial. Her party censured her for it.

Under the old rules, with a closed primary, that is a career obituary.

Under top-four, she advanced from the primary that was open to all comers. And in November, the only round that now binds, she won the final count, 53.7 to 46.3. She won it with a coalition no closed primary would ever have let her assemble.

[beat]

Now the detail that keeps this honest. On the very same ballots, Alaskans reelected a conservative Republican governor, outright.

So the system did not manufacture moderates. It let the whole electorate decide whether it wanted this one, where before the deciding voice was one party's most engaged tenth.

> Top-four did not manufacture a moderate. It let the whole electorate decide.

Set that beside the study. The study tested participation rules: an open gate against a closed gate, on the same party primary. It did not test a system that strips the primary of finality, and hands the binding choice to the full November electorate. So an honest advocate cites the null result, and then aims the reform at the primary's monopoly on consequence.

## The blueprint, and Nevada

That is the design in Alaska. The fullest blueprint comes from an unlikely pair.

Katherine Gehl is a former chief executive of a food company. Michael Porter is a strategy scholar at Harvard Business School. In 2020 they published a book called The Politics Industry.

It analyzes the two parties as a textbook duopoly. On their account, the parties compete to deepen customer lock-in, not to solve public problems. And the primary is the industry's most effective tool for disciplining its customers.

Their remedy is called Final Five Voting. It is Alaska's architecture with one more lane. A single primary, with every candidate on it, sends five finalists to a ranked general election. Alaska runs the version with four.

Nevada tried for five, and taught the movement a lesson in humility. Its measure passed in 2022, with 53 percent. But Nevada's constitution required a second vote. In 2024 the measure failed that vote, with 47 percent. By then the opposition had spent two full years organizing against a target that was standing still.

The machine, it turns out, also gets a vote on whether it is dismantled.

[pause]

One more piece, because it is the machine's most expensive accessory. Several states settle a crowded primary with a second, later primary, called a runoff. That sounds like majority rule. Then you watch who comes back. At the median, 40 percent of the first round's voters fail to return.

So a device meant to legitimize nominees hands them to an even smaller, even more activated electorate. And taxpayers pay for it in full.

A ranked ballot solves exactly this, in one trip. That is why some runoff states already mail ranked ballots to their soldiers overseas.

[pause]

One question before we gather up. I opened with three guesses. Can you still give me the first two numbers, and say what each one counts?

[long pause]

Eighty-three percent of House seats were effectively decided in a primary. And 8 percent of the voting-age population voted in those primaries.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. In 2022, 83 percent of House seats were effectively decided in primaries, and 8 percent of the voting-age population voted in them. The general election is the show. The primary is the decision.

[beat]

Two. The machine has two parts: the vanishing competitive district, and the turnout cliff. In a safe seat, the question is "will my primary fire me?" Change her math, and you change her vote.

[beat]

Three. The honest complication. The finding: opening a primary has little if any effect on legislators' ideology. My reading: the defect is the primary's monopoly on consequence.

[beat]

Four. The design. One primary with every candidate, four finalists or five, and a ranked general election that binds. In Alaska, top-four did not manufacture a moderate. It let the whole electorate decide.

[beat]

And five. Nevada. A yes in 2022, a no in 2024. The machine also gets a vote.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, as I see it. The diagnosis: the binding election in most House districts is a primary that few adults attend, and rational politicians serve the electorate that can fire them. And the honest version of the cure is structural. It moves finality from the primary to a November count of the full electorate.

Here is what it does not settle. Does Alaska's experience generalize beyond Alaska? And how much moderation does the bigger surgery buy? That evidence is accumulating, one cycle at a time.

There is also a force I have treated as scenery, when it is a character. It is the institutions that decide what you hear about all of this. They are the ones who frame a count of fifteen days as chaos, and a censured senator's survival as a fluke. The press and this reform have a complicated relationship, and it gets the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to the room where the decision happens. In most districts, it is a primary.

[beat]

And almost nobody is in it.
