June 2026. Washington, D.C., is a week away from its first ranked-choice primary, and the local NBC station runs a story. Here is the headline. "Ranked choice voting will delay DC primary results."

[beat]

Now go past the headline, into the article, and you learn the actual schedule. The Board of Elections will keep receiving mail ballots through June 26, as long as they were postmarked by election day. So the final tabulation runs on or after the 26th.

That ten-day wait is a mail-ballot receipt deadline. It is a law that has nothing to do with ranking, and it would hold the certified result open in a tight pick-one race too. The ranked count itself, once the ballots are in hand, takes a computer minutes.

But the headline has already taught a million readers what to blame. [beat] And it taught them wrong.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about primaries, and it ended on a force it had treated as scenery: the institutions that decide what you hear about all of this. This chapter is about them.

And since it is a chapter about framing, I'll concede mine up front. I'm an advocate, and the press's job is not to make my reform look good. My complaint is narrower than that.

> Election coverage keeps reporting the calendar as if it were the algorithm.

We will take it in four steps. First, the wait, and what really causes it. Second, New York in 2021, the disaster every opponent cites. Third, the part of the problem that is real. And last, what better looks like, because better already exists.

## The scoreboard breaks

Election-night journalism is built around a scoreboard. Precincts report, the bar chart fills, and somebody calls the race by 11 p.m.

Ranked elections break that scoreboard in two ways. One is real but trivial. The other is fake but vivid.

The real one first. Under ranked choice voting, you cannot run the elimination rounds until you have almost all the ballots. The reason is the order of the count: whoever finishes last in round one determines everything downstream. So a jurisdiction waits for its absentee deadline, and then it tabulates.

Take Alaska, in August 2022, and its famous fifteen days of silence. State law gives overseas absentee ballots fifteen days to arrive. The Division of Elections had announced all along that it would run the count on August 31. And it did, on a public livestream, in minutes. Certification followed on September 2.

As advocates pointed out at the time, a tight plurality race could not have been called before those ballots arrived either. Alaska's presidential result in 2020 wasn't called for days, for the same reason.

So the difference is purely theatrical. A pick-one race trickles in, and the pundits narrate the trickle. A ranked race holds its suspense for one scheduled reveal. Same calendar. [beat] Radically different coverage.

Stories about "no winner for two weeks" ran nationwide anyway. Most of them mentioned the absentee deadline somewhere around paragraph nine. And that is how the most durable misinformation about ranked ballots gets born.

> The misinformation is not lies. It is true facts, filed under the wrong cause.

Now the fake one. It is the headline reflex that treats the reveal itself as suspicious.

In Alaska, Sarah Palin led the first-choice votes in early returns, and Mary Peltola won the final count. In hostile coverage, the gap between election-night impressions and the actual result became evidence of trickery. It was the difference between an incomplete count and a complete one. Every American election has always had that difference.

Let's test this. A wire story reports: "Because of ranked choice voting, Alaskans waited 15 days to learn their new representative." What is the most precise correction? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The fifteen days came from the window the law gives overseas absentee ballots to arrive. The ranked tabulation ran in minutes once the ballots were in, on a date announced in advance. A tight pick-one race would have waited on the same mail.

And the error matters. It takes a cost of mail-ballot deadlines, which voters broadly support, and assigns it to the voting method.

## New York, 2021

That is the calendar. Now the catastrophe.

On June 29, 2021, New York City's Board of Elections published a preliminary ranked-choice tally for the Democratic mayoral primary. It showed Eric Adams's lead over Kathryn Garcia collapsing. Hours later, the board retracted it. It admitted that it had left about 135,000 test ballots inside the count, from a systems check run before the election.

For a news cycle, "ranked-choice voting" and "botched count" fused into a single story. Adams himself suggested the system couldn't be trusted.

Here is the honest anatomy. The error was human and institutional. The board is a patronage agency, with a bipartisan record of dysfunction that runs back decades, generations before New York ranked a ballot. It ran test data through its tabulator, and it forgot to clear it.

The corrected count, with a full paper trail that can be audited by hand, produced an orderly result. In the final round, Adams had about 403,000 votes. Garcia had about 395,000.

Nothing about ranking caused the blunder. Just the year before, in an ordinary pick-one election, the board's print vendor had sent defective absentee-ballot envelopes to nearly a hundred thousand Brooklyn voters.

> New York's error was human and institutional. Nothing about ranking caused it.

[pause]

But I won't hide behind that, because the rollout was a choice. A brand-new tabulation pipeline. Run by the city's least-trusted agency. With preliminary results released before the absentee ballots were integrated.

Every step was legal. Every step was explained in advance. And every step was a gift to whoever wanted the headline "RCV chaos."

Voters, for what it's worth, told the count apart from the ballot. In Edison Research's exit poll, 95 percent said the ranked ballot was simple. And 75 percent wanted to keep it. The city has ranked calmly ever since.

So the lesson I take from New York is not a comfortable one for my side.

> Reforms don't get to choose the institutions that implement them.

A voting method will be judged by the agency that runs it. Administration is the reform's reputational ceiling.

Now put yourself at an editor's desk on that June 29. The board has just retracted its tally, and two reporters pitch you headlines.

The first: "Ranked-choice voting throws NYC mayor's race into chaos." The second: "BOE includes 135,000 test ballots in count, retracts results."

What does each headline teach its readers? And which one is accurate? Think it through.

[long pause]

The first assigns the failure to the voting method. Readers learn that ranked choice voting breaks elections, and the facts don't support that claim. The second assigns it to the agency and the specific act. Readers learn that an error occurred, that it could be audited, and that it was caught. Which is what happened.

Both headlines are clickable. Only one survives contact with the correction.

And there is a simple test for any sentence written on election night.

> Would this sentence still be true if the error had happened in a pick-one race?

"BOE includes test ballots" passes. "RCV causes chaos" fails.

## The part that is real

So far I have defended the count. Here is the turn, because some of the friction is structural, and I'd rather explain it than deny it.

Horse-race journalism runs on two instruments. One is the poll before the election. The other is the call on election night. Ranking complicates both.

A pollster can't just ask, "whom do you support?" She needs each respondent's full ranking. Then she has to simulate the elimination rounds, on a sample a fraction the size of the electorate, and the error compounds at every round. FiveThirtyEight devoted a segment to exactly this puzzle.

The call gets harder too. A lead in the first round becomes a trap for the unwary analyst, because the first round is not the result.

> Under ranked counting, polls and election-night calls do get harder.

None of this makes a ranked election unknowable. It makes the cheap instruments cheaper than the truth. Journalism has faced that problem before, and it solved it with better instruments.

## What better looks like

And better exists, in production, today.

San Francisco has run ranked elections for two decades. On election night it posts preliminary results, round by round, right next to the pick-one races. Then, before certification, it publishes the complete, anonymized ballot file. Any journalist, professor, or critic can re-run the entire count themselves.

FairVote has called it the gold standard, and for a reason. It converts the count from a black box into a dataset.

> San Francisco posts the rounds on election night, then publishes every ballot.

There is more. Free tools, like one called RCVis, turn the rounds into graphics a viewer absorbs in seconds. And the Journalist's Resource, at Harvard's Shorenstein Center, publishes a working explainer with tips for reporters. Lead with when results arrive, and why. Report first choices as first choices, never as "the result." Explain transfers with the actual numbers.

Washington's debut shows the learning curve in action. Alongside that "delay" headline, the city's local outlets ran patient explainers, walking voters through ranking five candidates. And the Board of Elections published its tabulation calendar weeks out.

So the problem is not that journalists can't cover this. The scoreboard habit dies slowly. And the people working to ban ranked ballots quote every lazy headline as evidence.

The fix list is short and unglamorous. Three groups, with one job each.

Election officials. Announce the tabulation calendar early. Run preliminary rounds on election night, where the law allows. And publish the ballot file.

[beat]

Journalists. Report the calendar as the calendar, and the rounds as arithmetic. Retire "no winner yet," and tell people when we'll know, and why.

[beat]

And advocates, my own side. Stop promising instant results. "Instant" describes the runoff, not the mail.

[pause]

One question before we gather up. I opened with a headline from Washington, and a ten-day wait. What caused that wait?

A mail-ballot receipt deadline. Not the ranking. The calendar, not the algorithm.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Election coverage keeps reporting the calendar as if it were the algorithm. The wait comes from ballot deadlines. The ranked count takes minutes.

[beat]

Two. New York's error was human and institutional. Nothing about ranking caused it. But reforms don't get to choose the institutions that implement them, and that rollout was a gift to the headline writers.

[beat]

Three. Under ranked counting, polls and election-night calls do get harder. That makes the cheap instruments cheaper than the truth. It does not make the election unknowable.

[beat]

And four. Better exists. San Francisco posts the rounds on election night, then publishes every ballot. And for any sentence about a count, ask: would this still be true if the error had happened in a pick-one race?

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. It is the harder, cultural question. The economics of political journalism still reward the scoreboard and the call. Will the press adopt those better models before the next viral "chaos" cycle? Or must every new jurisdiction's debut survive its own round of miscoverage first? I don't know.

By now I have shown you the machine, the map, the evidence, and the megaphone. What I haven't shown you is the money: who funds these campaigns, on both sides, and what that buys. That ledger is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to that headline in Washington. Ten days of waiting, and a million readers taught what to blame.

The count took minutes. The calendar took the ten days.
