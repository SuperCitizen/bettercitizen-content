November 13, 2018. In Maine's Second District, Congressman Bruce Poliquin leads on first choices, 46.3 percent to 45.6.

He has no majority. And the state's brand-new ranked-choice count is about to run.

[beat]

So he goes to federal court, and asks a judge to stop it. Counting the voters' backup preferences, his lawyers argue, would violate the United States Constitution.

The judge is Lance Walker. He was appointed by President Trump, and he has been on the bench barely a month. He declines to halt the count, and lets the transfers run. A month later, he rejects every one of Poliquin's constitutional claims on the merits.

Jared Golden took the seat in Congress.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about money, and it ended on an argument the opposition almost never makes anymore: that ranked choice voting is unconstitutional. This chapter is about what happened when they made it.

They made it in Minnesota, in California, in Maine, and in Alaska. In state court and in federal court. Before elections, and after them. And here is the scorecard to carry around.

> Under the U.S. Constitution, the challenges to ranked choice voting have lost every time.

The one place ranked choice voting has genuinely lost on the law is a different place than most people think. Both halves of that story are this chapter.

We will take it in four steps. First, what the Constitution says about counting. Second, the attack called one person, one vote. Third, the real loss, in Maine. And last, the fights that remain.

## What the Constitution says about counting

Start with the text, because the text is almost the whole answer.

The Constitution tells us who runs congressional elections. The Elections Clause, in Article One, gives the states power over the "Times, Places and Manner," and lets Congress override them. It says nothing about how a winner must be calculated.

No clause requires plurality winners. The word "plurality" does not appear.

> The Constitution says who runs elections. It does not say how to count them.

And the history runs the same way. States have run elections that required a majority since the founding era. Georgia ran a full statewide runoff for a U.S. Senate seat as recently as December 2022. If the Constitution silently mandated plurality rules, all of that would be unconstitutional. Nobody has ever seriously believed it is.

That is federalism doing what it was built to do: states experimenting with the machinery of their own democracy.

And that history is why Poliquin's lead argument went nowhere. Article One provides for election "by the People." His lawyers argued that the phrase implies a plurality count. The case was called Baber versus Dunlap, and the court entered judgment for the state on every claim: Article One, due process, the First Amendment, the Voting Rights Act. Poliquin dropped the suit before New Year's.

In 2020, four Maine voters came back, to challenge ranked ballots in the presidential race. That case was Hagopian versus Dunlap, and Judge Walker was more direct. Listen for the last three words. His "limited charge," he wrote, was "to determine only whether the RCV Act is contrary to the text of the United States Constitution. It is not."

It is not.

[pause]

Let's test this. A friend says: "Surely the Founders assumed the candidate with the most votes wins, so plurality is baked into the Constitution." What is the strongest short rebuttal, using only what courts have relied on? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Two sentences will do it. The Constitution assigns the manner of elections to the states, and it never prescribes a counting rule. And the founding generation itself ran elections that required majorities, so plurality can't be a hidden constitutional command.

Georgia still elects senators by majority, or runoff. If "most votes in one round" were constitutionally required, that system would have been struck down long ago.

## One person, one vote

That is the text. Now the most persistent attack, and it sounds intuitive. If my ballot gets counted for my second choice after my first is eliminated, didn't I just vote twice?

The doctrine being invoked is real. A case called Reynolds versus Sims, in 1964, grounded "one person, one vote" in the Equal Protection Clause. But that doctrine is about districts of unequal population, where one person's vote weighs more than another's. Ranked-choice tabulation has no such asymmetry, and you can verify that by watching the count.

> In every round, every active ballot counts exactly once, for exactly one candidate.

Put another way, a ranking isn't extra votes. It is standing instructions for your one vote.

The leading federal case is Dudum versus Arntz, from 2011. San Francisco voters attacked the city's instant runoff, and from the opposite direction. The city let voters rank only three candidates. They argued that the limit disenfranchised voters whose ballots ran out of rankings.

Judge Marsha Berzon wrote the opinion for the Ninth Circuit, and it upheld the system. Three holdings. Ballots are not counted unequally. An exhausted ballot reflects the voter's own choice to rank no further. And the burden on voting rights is minimal. The court rejected the equal-protection theory, and a Voting Rights Act claim besides.

Two years earlier, the Minnesota Supreme Court had reached the same destination. In a case called Minnesota Voters Alliance versus City of Minneapolis, it rejected challenges based on the right to vote, on association, and on equal protection. It did so under both the federal and the state constitutions, before Minneapolis had counted a single ranked ballot.

Now a test of the mechanics. One voter ranks five candidates. Her neighbor ranks only one. Under instant-runoff rules, who has more voting power?

[long pause]

Neither. In any round, each ballot counts once, for one continuing candidate. Rankings are contingency instructions for a single vote, not additional votes.

If the neighbor's one choice is eliminated, her ballot exhausts. Courts treat that as her own choice, not as the system diluting anyone. That is the mechanics question both of those cases turned on.

## Where ranked choice voting lost

Now the loss, because honest advocacy reports its losses.

In 2017, Maine's Supreme Judicial Court issued a unanimous advisory opinion. It said the ranked-choice law Mainers had just passed conflicted with the Maine Constitution. That constitution provides that the governor and state legislators are elected "by a plurality of all votes returned."

It is a nineteenth-century clause. It was written to end the chaos of elections that required a majority, and repeatedly failed to produce winners. Now it froze single-round counting into place for state general elections.

A majority, the justices noted, is always a plurality. But the count of many rounds that finds that majority is not the single count the clause contemplates.

The result is the odd compromise Maine lives with today. Ranked ballots in primaries, and in all federal elections. Old-style plurality counts for governor and the Legislature in November.

And the ceiling has held. In April 2026, the same court considered a bill to extend ranked choice voting to those state general elections. It advised, unanimously, that the bill would be unconstitutional, for the same reason. A ranked ballot's "series of instructions," the justices wrote, is inconsistent with the constitutional concept of a single vote.

Until Mainers amend their constitution, that is the law.

[pause]

But take the loss precisely. No court, Maine's included, has ever held that ranked choice voting violates anyone's rights. The Maine opinions are about a specific clause, in one state's constitution.

> The one real loss came from a state constitution's plurality clause.

Alaska's Supreme Court faced its own constitution in 2022, in a case called Kohlhaas versus State. It upheld top-four, ranked-choice elections outright.

So the practical lesson for reformers is about drafting, not doctrine. Read your state constitution first. If it contains a clause on the method of election, the honest path is a constitutional amendment, not a statute and a prayer.

Let's put that to work. Your state's constitution says state senators are elected "by the greatest number of votes." A reform group asks you: is a ranked-choice statute enough, or do we need an amendment? What do you tell them? Think it through.

[long pause]

Tell them to be cautious. Maine's plurality clause sounds just as harmless, and its high court took it, unanimously and twice, as freezing single-round counting into place for the offices it covers. That was so even though a ranked-choice winner does have the greatest number of votes in the final round.

A court could take your clause either way. So the durable route is an amendment. Or apply ranked choice voting first to offices the clause doesn't reach, as Maine did with primaries and federal races.

The federal Constitution is no obstacle either way. Your state's text is the question.

## The fights that remain

So what is still contested, legally, as of mid-2026? Three things.

First, statutory bans. Nineteen states now prohibit ranked choice voting by statute. Ohio became the nineteenth in March 2026. A ban is not a constitutional holding. It is a policy choice by a legislature, and a legislature can repeal it. As I see it, the lawyers lost the courtroom argument, and the argument moved to statehouses. I take that as a kind of concession about where the law stands.

[beat]

Second, the Voting Rights Act. Courts have rejected challenges to ranked choice voting under that Act, so far. The more interesting open question runs the other direction. Election lawyers have proposed ranked systems as remedies, in vote-dilution cases.

The Congressional Research Service has a survey of this litigation, and it is the sober reference if you want the full map. It covers what Congress could do under its Elections Clause power. And that power cuts both ways: toward mandating ranked ballots for federal races, or toward banning them.

[beat]

Third, procedural warfare. The most recent high-profile lawsuit wasn't about counting at all. In Washington, D.C., the local Democratic Party and its chairman sued to keep Initiative 83 from reaching voters. That initiative was ranked choice voting, plus semi-open primaries. The grounds were ballot procedure.

The courts let the vote proceed. In November 2024, about 73 percent of D.C. voters approved it, and it carried every ward.

Here is my reading of that. When a party establishment's best remaining legal theory is about petition timing, and not about the Constitution, you have learned where the merits stand.

[pause]

One question before we gather up. Early on, I said the text of the Constitution is almost the whole answer. What does that text settle about elections, and what does it leave unsaid?

[long pause]

It settles who runs them: the states, subject to Congress. It leaves unsaid how a winner must be calculated. The Constitution says who runs elections. It does not say how to count them.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The Constitution says who runs elections. It does not say how to count them. The word "plurality" does not appear.

[beat]

Two. Under the U.S. Constitution, the challenges to ranked choice voting have lost every time. In Maine, twice. In San Francisco. In Minneapolis.

[beat]

Three. One person, one vote. In every round, every active ballot counts exactly once, for exactly one candidate. A ranking is standing instructions for one vote.

[beat]

And four. The one real loss came from a state constitution's plurality clause. Maine's high court has said so twice, in 2017 and in 2026. So read your state constitution first.

[pause]

Here is how I would sort it. Settled: ranked choice voting is constitutional under existing federal doctrine, one person, one vote included. Every federal court to reach the merits has said so. Settled, too: a state constitution can still say no, as Maine's does for its state offices. That is a drafting problem, and reformers must respect it.

Unsettled: how Voting Rights Act doctrine and ranked ballots will interact, as more jurisdictions adopt them. And whether Congress ever uses its Elections Clause power, in either direction.

[pause]

The deepest thing I want to leave you with is an asymmetry. The strongest honest argument on the status-quo side was never that ranked choice voting is unlawful. It is that ranked choice voting is unwise. That is a case they are entitled to make, and voters are entitled to weigh.

So weigh it. Wise, or unwise. Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

The Constitution, it turns out, is neutral on counting methods. Which means the fight over ranked choice voting is exactly what it should be: political. The next chapter is about how to win it, state by state.
