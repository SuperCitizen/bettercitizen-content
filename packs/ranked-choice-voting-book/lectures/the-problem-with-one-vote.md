November 26, 2000. The State of Florida certifies the most consequential number in modern American politics.

[beat]

537.

That was George W. Bush's final margin over Al Gore, out of nearly six million votes cast. And with it came the presidency.

On those same ballots sat another number. Nobody certified it, and everybody noticed it. 97,488 Floridians had voted for Ralph Nader.

[pause]

Set the two side by side. The margin of the American presidency was one half of one percent of one candidate's protest vote.

And the ballots themselves, the official record of the electorate's will, could say nothing about what those Nader voters would have wanted instead. [beat] Nothing.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. The book is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is its author. The introduction only asserted. This chapter begins the case, with my diagnosis.

That silence in Florida is this chapter's subject. Not Bush. Not Gore. Not Nader. [beat] The ballot.

We will take it in four steps. First, what our voting system actually finds. Second, the spoiler effect, and why the standard version of it overreaches. Third, a cage built by a counting rule. And last, what all of this does to an honest voter, and what my opponents say in reply.

## The largest fragment wins

Our standard voting system goes by several names. Plurality voting. First-past-the-post. Pick-one.

Here is its dirty secret. It doesn't find the candidate most voters want. It finds the candidate who survives the most fractured opposition.

There is no majority threshold to clear. Despite the horse-racing name, there is no post to get past. Whoever holds the largest single fragment wins, even if that fragment is a sliver, and everyone outside it is appalled.

This is not a hypothetical. In 2010, Paul LePage won the governorship of Maine with 37.6 percent of the vote.

He finished ahead of an independent, Eliot Cutler. A Democrat, Libby Mitchell, took 18.8 percent, and two more candidates split the rest.

More than three of every five Mainers marked someone other than the winner.

[pause]

Four years later, LePage was reelected with 48.2 percent, in another three-way race. That was a stronger showing. It was still short of a majority.

Maine will get its own chapter, because what its voters did about this is the founding story of the modern reform movement. For now, hold on to the mechanism.

> A divided majority loses to a unified minority.

Every time. By design.

Put another way, plurality does not ask whom most voters want. It rewards the candidate whose opposition is the most divided.

## The spoiler effect

That is what the system finds. Now, what it does to a third candidate.

Every election cycle, you hear the same sermon. "Don't waste your vote on a third party."

Notice that this isn't cynicism. Under pick-one rules, it is correct advice. When supporting your favorite candidate can elect your least favorite, abandoning your favorite is the rational move.

Something is wrong with a system in which honesty is a strategic blunder. And the wrongness belongs to the system, not the voters.

But I want to make this case carefully, because the standard version of it overreaches.

Go back to Florida. A margin of 537 votes. And next to it, a third-party vote 181 times its size.

Did Nader cost Gore the presidency?

[long pause]

Here is the honest answer. It cannot be proven. And that is the deepest indictment available.

Exit polls and later studies suggested that Nader's voters leaned toward Gore. But a lean is not a count, and some of them would have stayed home. The pick-one ballot collected no second choices. So the one election that most needed the information never gathered it.

A well-designed instrument tells you what its users meant. [beat] Ours shrugged.

> The pick-one ballot collects no second choices.

The same goes for the biggest third-party run of the modern era. In 1992, Ross Perot won 18.9 percent of the national popular vote. That was nearly twenty million votes. It earned him exactly zero electoral votes. Bill Clinton won the presidency with 43 percent.

Republicans spent years insisting that Perot had spoiled it for George H. W. Bush. The polling evidence says Perot's voters split roughly evenly.

But notice what happened next, because this is the spoiler effect's real damage. Both parties absorbed a lesson: third candidates are mortal threats. And they built the modern machinery of exclusion. Ballot-access lawsuits. Debate-stage thresholds. The ritual drumbeat about the wasted vote.

The spoiler effect doesn't need to actually flip elections to do its work.

> The fear of the spoiler polices the field, cycle after cycle.

Let's test that. A city's race for mayor polls at 40, 35 and 25. A conservative leads. A progressive runs second. A second progressive trails, at 25 percent. Pundits call her a spoiler, and demand that she drop out.

Under pick-one rules, what exactly makes her a spoiler? And what would we need to know to be sure she is one? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

She is a spoiler only if her voters' second choices lean toward the other progressive. Then she is splitting a coalition that would otherwise beat the conservative.

And that is the trap. Pick-one ballots never record second choices, so nobody can know whether she is spoiling anything. She will be blamed either way, and pressured out either way.

The system can't distinguish a spoiler from a contender, so it treats every third candidate as one.

> The defect is informational before it is mathematical.

## Duverger's cage

Now step back from single elections. Why does America have exactly two viable parties, when Americans, by every poll, are not two kinds of people?

[beat]

A French political scientist gave the canonical answer in the 1950s. His name was Maurice Duverger, and his book was called Political Parties.

Duverger observed that plurality voting, in single-winner districts, presses relentlessly toward two-party politics. It does so through two distinct effects.

The first is the mechanical effect. It works at the count. Take a party with, say, 19 percent of the vote, spread across the country. It wins almost nothing. You have already met the example: Perot, in 1992. Nearly twenty million votes went in, and zero electoral votes came out.

The second is the psychological effect. It works before the count. Voters who see that result coming defect in advance. They abandon their true preference for a viable lesser evil. And donors, journalists and talented candidates defect with them.

> The mechanical effect kills third parties. The psychological effect ensures they're never born.

Political scientists promote almost nothing to the status of a law. They made an exception here. In 1982, William Riker formally christened it "Duverger's law."

Riker also catalogued its caveats. Canada and the United Kingdom are plurality countries with stubborn regional third parties, and they keep the law honest. It is a strong tendency, not physics.

But America is its purest case. Every district, every statehouse, every governorship. One vote, one round, largest fragment wins.

We did not choose two-party politics in any deliberative sense. [beat] We built a cage, and then mistook the cage for the climate.

Try the two effects on a case. A talented independent considers running for Senate. Polls show that 22 percent of voters would consider her. But she can't raise money, because donors say she can't win. And she can't poll higher, because voters say the same.

Which of Duverger's effects is at work? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The psychological effect. The mechanical effect operates at the counting stage, and nothing here has been counted yet.

Her supporters privately prefer her, and they are defecting in anticipation, before a single vote is cast. Under pick-one rules, a vote for a likely loser is a vote withheld from the lesser evil. This is the effect that prevents third candidacies from ever maturing.

## Voting against

Add it up, and you reach this chapter's bluntest claim. Plurality voting forces millions of Americans to falsify their preferences at the ballot box, every election.

They don't vote for someone. They vote against someone, through the nearest viable instrument.

The ballot then launders the strategy into data. The official returns show support the voters don't feel, and erase support they do. Mandates get claimed on the strength of votes that were really vetoes. And parties learn that they own their voters' support no matter what, because it has nowhere else to go.

[pause]

Before the replies, pull something back. Early in this chapter I gave you a mechanism, about a divided majority. Can you still say it?

[long pause]

A divided majority loses to a unified minority. That was Maine in 2010: a governor elected with 37.6 percent.

The vote cast against someone grows from the same root. The ballot holds one mark, so the count never learns whom those voters actually prefer.

Now the replies. There are two, and each gets a full chapter later in this book.

A status-quo defender will answer, fairly, that pick-one voting is simple, fast to count, familiar, and decisive. And that coordination on two broad coalitions has its own virtues.

> The status-quo defender's answer: pick-one is simple, fast, familiar, and decisive.

An alternative-reform advocate will answer that ranked ballots are the wrong fix. On that view, approval voting or score voting cures the same disease more cleanly.

> The alternative-reform advocate's answer: approval or score voting cures the disease more cleanly.

And I will answer both the same way. What neither can defend is the thing this chapter set out to show. A ballot that collects one mark, in a field of three or more, cannot answer the question elections exist to ask: whom these voters actually prefer.

> My answer: a one-mark ballot cannot say whom voters actually prefer.

Florida in 2000 couldn't answer it. Maine in 2010 couldn't answer it. The instrument is blind exactly where the stakes are highest.

[pause]

Three positions, then. Mine, the status-quo defender's, and the alternative-reform advocate's. Take the one you find least convincing. What is the strongest case you could make for it?

Keep that case. You will need it for the chapters that belong to my opponents.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Plurality doesn't find the candidate most voters want. It finds the largest fragment. A divided majority loses to a unified minority.

[beat]

Two. The pick-one ballot collects no second choices. So whether Nader cost Gore the presidency cannot be proven. The defect is informational before it is mathematical.

[beat]

Three. The spoiler effect doesn't need to flip elections. The fear of the spoiler polices the field, cycle after cycle.

[beat]

Four. Duverger's two effects. The mechanical effect kills third parties. The psychological effect ensures they're never born. Together they are a strong tendency, not physics.

[beat]

And five. Strategic voting belongs to the system, not the voters. Against that stand two replies. The status-quo defender's: pick-one is simple, fast, familiar, and decisive. And the alternative-reform advocate's: approval or score voting cures the disease more cleanly.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. Whether ranking fixes any of it. At what cost. With what new failure modes.

Those are real questions with real answers, and some of those answers are uncomfortable for my side. That's next: the next chapter takes up ranking itself.

What I count as settled is the diagnosis. You have also heard two people who would answer me, and their chapters are still to come.

[pause]

For now, go back to Florida. A margin of 537. And 97,488 ballots that could say nothing more.

[beat]

Your vote isn't broken because you use it badly. It's broken because it was never built to carry what you mean.
