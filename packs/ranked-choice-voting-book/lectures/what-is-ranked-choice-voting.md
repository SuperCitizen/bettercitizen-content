Strip away the policy fights. Strip away the lawsuits, and the cable-news segments. What is left fits in one sentence.

[beat]

> Instead of picking one candidate, you rank them.

First choice, second choice, third. As many or as few as you want.

That's it. That's the machine. Everything else in this book is what happens when you turn it on.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. The book is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is its author. The last chapter was my diagnosis of the pick-one ballot. This chapter is the owner's manual for the fix I am arguing for.

By the end, you will have followed a count, round by round. And you will know the honest fine print, including the parts my opponents are right to bring up.

We will take it in four steps. First, the ballot. Second, the count, with one small election you can follow by ear. Third, how old this machinery is, and its two flavors. And last, what ranking does not promise.

## The ballot

A ranked ballot lists the same candidates a pick-one ballot would. Next to each name sits a row of columns: first, second, third, and so on.

You fill in one candidate for each column. Your favorite goes under first. Your backup goes under second. And on down the line, as far as your opinions actually go.

Maine's Secretary of State puts the rule plainly: rank as many or as few as you wish. Ranking only one candidate is a legal, countable ballot. It just means your ballot has nothing more to say if that candidate is eliminated.

[pause]

Now two things a ranked ballot is not, because these misunderstandings do real damage.

First, it is not a points system. Your second choice does not get half a vote. And your vote never counts for two candidates at once.

> One ballot is one vote, for the highest-ranked candidate still in the race.

That holds at every moment of the count. Exactly one vote, for exactly one candidate.

Second, ranking a backup is not a vote against your favorite. The count never looks at your second choice until your first choice has been eliminated. And by then your first choice can't be hurt, because they are already out.

> Ranking a backup cannot hurt your favorite.

Voting theorists call that property later-no-harm, and instant-runoff counting satisfies it. So leaving the later columns blank protects no one. It only silences your ballot early.

## The count

That is the ballot. Now the engine. Americans usually call it instant-runoff voting.

It goes like this. Count everyone's first choices. If a candidate has a majority of the ballots in play, done. That's the winner, exactly as in any election.

If nobody does, the candidate in last place is eliminated. Each of that candidate's ballots moves, at full strength, to whoever that voter ranked next. Then count again. And repeat, until someone clears a majority of the ballots still in play.

In plainer words, it is a runoff election without the second trip to the polls. The backup information for every possible runoff is collected once, on one Tuesday.

[pause]

Don't take my word for how it feels. Let's run one. It is a city race I built from one thousand ballots, with four candidates: Rivera, Okafor, Tran and Lund.

Plenty of these voters ranked only one or two names. Real voters do that, and an honest example should too.

Round one. Count the first choices.

Rivera has 380.

Okafor has 290.

Tran has 200.

And Lund has 130.

[beat]

Nobody has a majority. Lund is in last place, so Lund is eliminated, and Lund's 130 ballots move.

They split three ways, because Lund's voters had three different second preferences. 70 of them go to Tran. 40 go to Okafor. And 20 ranked nobody but Lund, so those ballots have nothing more to say.

Round two. Rivera still has 380. Okafor has climbed to 330. Tran has climbed to 270.

[beat]

Still no majority. And now Tran is last, so Tran is eliminated.

Here is where Tran's ballots point next. 190 of them name Okafor. 50 name Rivera. And 30 name no one who is still in the race.

Rivera has led both rounds. So who wins? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

Rivera gains 50, and finishes with 430.

Okafor gains 190, and finishes with 520.

[beat]

Okafor wins.

> Rivera led the first round with 380 votes, and lost.

Now let me name what you just heard, because each piece matters later in this book. Three things.

One. Rivera's support was a large fragment that never grew.

Two. The transfers followed the voters' own instructions. Not a party's, and not an official's.

Three. By the final round, 50 ballots had dropped out of the count. The word for that is exhausted. An exhausted ballot is one whose owner ranked only candidates who were eliminated. That is the price of ranking briefly, and it is a choice each voter made freely.

And those exhausted ballots change what the word majority means.

> Okafor's 520 was a majority of the 950 ballots still active.

It was not measured against all one thousand ballots cast. In this race, 520 happens to clear half of the original total too. But that is not guaranteed, and honest advocates say so out loud.

[pause]

Now replay that election in your head under pick-one rules. Same voters, same preferences, one mark each. Who wins? And what, specifically, did the ranked count change?

[long pause]

Under pick-one, Rivera wins, 380 to Okafor's 290. That is a 38 percent winner, opposed by 62 percent of the electorate.

And what changed under ranked counting was not anyone's vote total. Tran and Lund, between them, were the first choice of 330 voters. Those voters got to answer a question pick-one never asks: with your favorite gone, whom do you prefer among the rest? Most of them answered Okafor.

The ranked ballot didn't move a single vote. It collected more of each voter's judgment, and used it only when needed.

When you are back at a screen, the chapter's page has this same election as a simulator, and you can step through the rounds yourself.

## Not new, and two flavors

Ranked choice voting carries an experimental reputation. I say it has not earned one.

Australia has elected its House of Representatives this way since 1918. Ireland has elected its parliament by ranked ballot since independence, in 1922. And Cambridge, Massachusetts, has elected its city council and school committee this way since 1941. That is eighty-five years of continuous American use.

But here is the honest size of the record. A handful of full democracies have used ranked ballots nationally for generations. Dozens of governments use them somewhere. And American states adopted them only within the decade.

"Untested" is not a serious description of that record. The serious objections lie elsewhere, and we'll get to them.

[pause]

Everything so far describes the single-winner version. One mayor. One governor. One seat.

The same ranked ballot also comes in a multi-winner version, for bodies like city councils. It is called the single transferable vote, or STV.

Say a city fills five council seats at once. STV sets the winning threshold lower: just over one-sixth of the votes. And it transfers two kinds of votes. The surplus votes of winners, and the votes of eliminated candidates.

The effect is rough proportionality. A bloc with 40 percent of the votes ends up with about 40 percent of the seats, instead of everything or nothing. Cambridge's system is STV.

> Same ballot in the voter's hand. The number of seats does the rest.

So keep the two apart. Instant runoff fills one seat, by a majority. The single transferable vote fills several, in rough proportion.

## What ranking does not promise

Before the fine print, pull something back. A neighbor says to you: "If I rank a second choice, I'm voting against my first choice."

You heard the answer near the start of this chapter. What is it?

[long pause]

No. The count never looks at a second choice until the first choice has been eliminated. Ranking a backup cannot hurt your favorite. That was later-no-harm.

Now the fine print, stated by an advocate on purpose. Four limits.

The first is the largest.

> Ranked choice voting does not guarantee that the consensus candidate wins.

A candidate who would beat every rival head-to-head can still be eliminated early, for lack of first-choice votes. It happened in Alaska, in August 2022. The next chapter confronts that result, and does not hide it.

The second. Single-winner ranked choice voting does not produce proportional outcomes, and it does not end two-party dominance. Australia's century proves both points.

The third. Exhausted ballots are real. And so is the distinction you met in our count. A majority of the continuing ballots is one thing. A majority of everyone who showed up is another.

The fourth. The count is genuinely more centralized than a pick-one tally. The ballots, or their digital images, must be tabulated together. That is exactly the thread Maine's constitutional fight pulled on, and Maine's story comes two chapters from now.

[pause]

So here is a question this chapter cannot answer for you. Are those four limits a fair price for what the ranked ballot collects, or too high a price? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Hold on to that case. The question is contested, and I have not settled it here.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Instead of picking one candidate, you rank them. One ballot is one vote, for the highest-ranked candidate still in the race. And ranking a backup cannot hurt your favorite.

[beat]

Two. The count. Count first choices. If nobody has a majority, eliminate the last-place candidate, and move those ballots to each voter's next choice. Repeat.

[beat]

Three. Rivera led the first round with 380 votes, and lost. Okafor's 520 was a majority of the 950 ballots still active, because 50 ballots were exhausted.

[beat]

Four. Two flavors. Instant runoff for one seat. The single transferable vote for several. Same ballot in the voter's hand, and the number of seats does the rest.

[beat]

And five. The fine print. Ranked choice voting does not guarantee that the consensus candidate wins. It does not end two-party dominance. Exhausted ballots are real. And the count is more centralized.

[pause]

What this chapter settles is the mechanism, and where it comes from. You know what a ranked ballot collects, when each ranking is examined, and how long this machinery has run elsewhere.

What it does not settle is whether the machine delivers what I have claimed for it, under American conditions. Majority winners. No spoilers. Campaigns that reach beyond their base.

For that, we go to the proving grounds. The next chapter starts with the state that bet its entire general election on ranked ballots: Alaska.
