On October 1, 2013, New York City held an election almost nobody attended.

[beat]

The Democratic primary for public advocate had ended with no candidate above 40 percent, the threshold state law required. So, three weeks later, the city ran the top two again.

Administering that second election cost an estimated 13 million dollars. Just over 200,000 New Yorkers showed up. That was about 6.5 percent of those eligible.

Thirteen million dollars, and a fraction of the original electorate, to answer one question. If it comes down to these two, which do you prefer? [beat] The first ballot could have asked that, and answered it, for free.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about the press, and it ended by promising a ledger. This chapter is the ledger.

That runoff is the cheapest, least contested thing I have to say about money. The harder argument is the one I actually care about. It is about what the fear of a wasted vote does to who can afford to run at all.

We will take it in four steps. First, the runoff tax. Second, the money that never moves, because of spoiler fear. Third, what the research shows, and what it doesn't. And last, what ranked ballots change about money, and what they leave alone.

## The runoff tax

Wherever the law demands a majority winner, somebody has to pay for the second round.

Take Georgia. In January 2021, both of its U.S. Senate seats went to runoffs. Administering the rematch cost taxpayers roughly 75 million dollars, according to a Kennesaw State University study. And about 467,000 fewer Georgians voted in it than had voted in November.

That is the pattern everywhere runoffs exist. The government pays twice. The campaigns fundraise twice. And the electorate that shows up the second time is smaller, and less representative, than the first.

An instant runoff is exactly what its name says. It is the same majority logic, carried out in one trip to the polls, using the backup preferences that voters already wrote down.

> Where runoffs exist, the savings case for ranked ballots is bookkeeping.

New York adopted ranked choice voting in 2019, partly on the strength of that runoff math. Its citywide primaries in 2021 needed no second election.

[pause]

Now the other side of the ledger, because I mean to keep myself honest. A jurisdiction that never held runoffs doesn't pocket those savings. And switching has real one-time costs: ballot redesign, tabulation software, voter education. If a county clerk tells you the transition isn't free, she's right.

So the savings argument is for the dozens of jurisdictions already paying for second elections. For everyone else, the case rests on what the ballot does, not on what it costs.

Let's test that. A city holds a primary and a general election, and it has never used runoffs. An advocate pitches the council: "Ranked choice voting will save us millions." What is wrong with the pitch? And what is the honest version? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The savings claim belongs to runoff jurisdictions, where a ranked ballot deletes a whole election from the budget. A city with no runoffs saves nothing on administration, and it pays the one-time costs of the transition.

The honest pitch there is about outcomes, not budgets. Majority winners. No punishment for spoilers. And a runoff's benefits without a runoff's costs, the collapse in turnout included.

## The spoiler-fear subsidy

That was the easy money. Now follow the money upstream, to before a single ad is bought.

Under pick-one rules, a donation to a third candidate isn't just a long shot. It is a weapon that can fire backward. Fund the independent you actually agree with, and you may split your own side's vote, and elect the candidate you fear most.

Donors learned that lesson generations ago. It is why serious money in American politics flows almost exclusively into two channels, no matter how many candidates file.

Political scientists have a name for the regularity underneath. They call it Duverger's Law, after Maurice Duverger. His book, Political Parties, came out in 1954. It argued that plurality elections decided in a single round tend to compress competition into two parties.

Duverger was describing voters and politicians. But the logic binds donors hardest of all. They are professionals at not wasting money on arithmetic that can't win.

The result is what I'd call a donor duopoly. Since a case called Citizens United, in 2010, and the rise of the super PAC, the ceilings have come off independent spending. But the structure of campaign finance hasn't widened. It has deepened. Unlimited money chases the same two franchises, and givers who want access hedge, by writing checks to both.

The parties don't have to earn that monopoly. The ballot enforces it for them, by making every alternative a spoiler.

> The pick-one ballot makes every alternative a spoiler, and so unfundable.

Let me walk the loop once, in order, because the order is the argument.

First, pick-one ballots punish third candidacies, and everyone knows it: voters, donors, endorsers.

[beat]

Then donors consolidate into two camps. Funding an outsider risks spoiling. Funding a major party never does. This is where the duopoly is actually enforced: at the checkbook, not the ballot box.

[beat]

Then the entry price rises. A challenger without a party machine can't credibly raise money, because no one funds a spoiler. Viability becomes something parties grant, not something candidates earn.

[beat]

And ranked ballots cut the loop at step one. When a ballot can transfer, supporting an outsider can't elect your enemy. The spoiler threat stops working.

[pause]

Notice what I am claiming, and what I am not. This is a structural argument. Ranked ballots remove one specific mechanism, the one that lets two parties tax every dollar of political ambition.

It is not a claim that elections get cheaper, that dark money dries up, or that billionaires lose interest in politics. Those would be empirical claims. And the empirical record is, I'll say it plainly, thin.

## What the evidence shows, and what it doesn't

So here is the research that exists, as honestly as I can summarize it. Three pieces.

The first is about the tone of campaigns, and it is a real but modest literature. Three political scientists, Todd Donovan, Caroline Tolbert and Kellen Gracey, surveyed likely voters in ranked-choice cities, and in plurality cities matched to them by demographics. Their paper came out in 2016. People in the ranked-choice cities were significantly more satisfied with how campaigns were conducted, and less likely to call them negative.

In 2023, Donovan and Tolbert went back to the question. They found the effect real, but bounded. Voters still perceived more civility, and ordinary candidates reported gentler campaigns. The most viable candidates, the ones with the most at stake, did not.

> The civility effect is real but bounded. Front-runners still fight.

[pause]

The second piece is genuine counter-evidence. It comes from the political scientist Jesse Clark, in work gathered in a research review by New America. He studied Maine's congressional campaign of 2018, the first federal ranked-choice race in modern American history. He found it was more negative than comparable districts, with independent spending shifting sharply toward attacks.

One plausible reading is this. In a high-stakes partisan race, the super PACs doing the scorching don't need anyone's second choices. So the civility incentive binds candidates more than it binds the outside money around them.

[pause]

The third piece is money itself. Totals raised. Where it comes from. Whether small donors gain share.

> No body of evidence yet shows ranked ballots changing the money.

You will sometimes hear that ranked-choice elections produce more grassroots fundraising. That claim outruns the data, and I won't make it.

What the record does show is an entry effect. New America's review counts candidacies for the Minneapolis council nearly doubling between 2005 and 2013, under the new rules. And a study from 2018 found about a nine-point increase in candidates of color in ranked-choice cities, compared with similar plurality cities.

More people run. Whether more people can fund a run is a question the places using ranked ballots cannot yet answer.

Now a test. Here are four claims about ranked choice voting. It reduces total campaign spending. It shifts fundraising decisively toward small donors. It neutralizes super PAC spending, because attack ads stop working. And voters in ranked-choice cities perceive campaigns as less negative. Which one does the research record actually support?

[long pause]

Only the last, and with its caveat built in: the effect is weakest among the front-runners. The first two have no solid base in evidence, and advocates who claim them are ahead of the data. And the claim about super PACs is contradicted by Maine in 2018. Independent spenders don't need anyone's second choices.

## Lowering the toll, not the temperature

So why does a chapter on money belong in this book at all? Because the strongest money argument for ranked ballots was never about ad tone, or fundraising totals. It is about the gate.

Under plurality rules, the two parties hold a veto that operates before money even moves. Any candidacy outside the duopoly can be framed as a spoiler, and framed accurately. That makes it unfundable. Which makes it nonviable. Which proves the framing.

Ranked ballots dissolve that veto. Even if not one dollar fewer is spent, who is allowed to compete for the dollars changes. That is why I call this a structural reform, and not a campaign-finance reform.

> Ranked ballots change who may compete for the dollars, not how many are spent.

Put a donor in front of you. She says: "I actually prefer the independent, but I give to the major-party candidate. I'm not going to bankroll a spoiler." Under ranked ballots, what changes in her calculus? And what doesn't? Think it through.

[long pause]

What changes is that the punishment disappears. Under pick-one rules, her gift to the independent could help elect the candidate she most opposes. Under ranked ballots, the independent's supporters transfer, and they do not split the vote. Funding her sincere preference can no longer backfire.

What doesn't change is everything past the gate. Giving that is driven by access. Spending ceilings, because there are none. And outside money's freedom to attack.

It is also why reformers who care about money keep bundling disclosure rules alongside ranked ballots. Alaska's Measure 2 paired its new election system with requirements to disclose dark money, precisely because the ballot can't do that job itself.

> Ranked choice voting leaves contribution limits, disclosure law and Citizens United where it found them.

Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something I'm not.

[pause]

One question before we gather up. Early in this chapter, I said the savings argument belongs to one kind of place. Which kind?

[long pause]

A place that already holds runoffs. There, the savings are bookkeeping. Everywhere else, the case rests on what the ballot does.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The runoff tax. A second election costs millions, and draws fewer voters. Where runoffs exist, the savings case for ranked ballots is bookkeeping. Where they don't, there are no savings, and the transition isn't free.

[beat]

Two. The spoiler-fear subsidy. The pick-one ballot makes every alternative a spoiler, and so unfundable. Ranked ballots cut that loop at step one.

[beat]

Three. The evidence. The civility effect is real but bounded, and front-runners still fight. Maine in 2018 is counter-evidence. And no body of evidence yet shows ranked ballots changing the money.

[beat]

And four. The gate. More people run when spoiler fear dies. Ranked ballots change who may compete for the dollars, not how many are spent. Limits, disclosure and Citizens United stay where they were.

[pause]

Here is how I would sort it. Settled, in my view: the runoff arithmetic, which is just math. A real but bounded civility effect in the survey research. And the entry effect: more candidates, including more candidates of color.

Honestly open: whether the volume of money, its sources, or its targets change under ranked ballots, at any scale that matters. Two small states and some cities are not enough data. I'd rather concede that than torture the evidence into advocacy.

So I have made a structural argument, and conceded a thin record. Decide which of those two should weigh more. Then make the best case for the other.

[pause 5s]

One last thing. Notice the argument the opposition almost never makes anymore: that ranked choice voting is unconstitutional. They tried it, in Minnesota, in California, in Maine. How those cases went, and the one place ranked choice voting really did lose on the law, is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to New York, in October 2013. Thirteen million dollars, to ask a question the first ballot could have asked for free.

[beat]

That is the money a ranked ballot saves. The money it cannot touch is still out there.
