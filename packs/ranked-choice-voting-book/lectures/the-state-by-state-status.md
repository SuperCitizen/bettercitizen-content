June 2026. Two American capitals are telling two different stories about the same reform.

In Washington, D.C., voters are ranking candidates for mayor for the first time. They are doing it because of a ballot initiative, one that nearly 73 percent of them approved.

[beat]

Three months earlier, in Columbus, Governor Mike DeWine signed a law. It made Ohio the nineteenth state to ban ranked-choice voting. And it cuts state funds to any city that defies the ban.

Both things are true at once. Any chapter that shows you only one of them is lying by omission.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked-choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was the playbook: how a state passes this reform, and how it keeps it. This chapter is the inventory. It is the whole map, as of mid-2026, and every count in it carries that date.

We will take it in four steps. First, where ranked-choice voting is used. Second, where it is banned. Third, what the two maps have in common. And last, what is on the ballot in 2026, setbacks included.

## Where it is used

Start with the number advocates like to quote. An organization called FairVote keeps a running inventory. As of mid-2026, it counts 49 American jurisdictions that use ranked-choice voting in public elections, or have adopted it for upcoming ones.

Those 49 reach nearly 14 million voters. And they are spread across 22 states, plus D.C.

That number is worth taking apart, because its pieces do different work. There are four of them.

The biggest piece is local. It is 36 cities and 3 counties. The anchors are New York, San Francisco, Oakland, Minneapolis and St. Paul. Add Portland, Oregon, whose voters built a proportional system in 2022, one that elects several winners at once. These beachheads are urban, and they sit on the coasts and in the Mountain West.

[beat]

The second piece is the smallest, and the one that matters most. Two states use ranked ballots statewide. Maine adopted its system in 2016 and first used it in 2018. It covers state and federal primaries, and federal general elections, the presidential race included. Alaska has paired a top-four primary with a ranked general election since 2022, for everything from governor down.

[beat]

The third piece is one state, Hawaii. It quietly added ranked ballots for federal special elections, and for vacancies on county councils.

[beat]

The fourth piece is the least known, and it is my favorite. Six states and one city use ranked ballots for one group of voters only: the military, and citizens overseas. Those six include runoff states in the Deep South. They mail a ranked ballot so that a runoff does not need a second envelope, with a three-week round trip.

And here is the detail I enjoy. Several states that ban ranked ballots for everyone else still rank their soldiers' ballots. For that problem, nothing else works.

[pause]

So read the count honestly, and you see the movement's reach and its limits together.

> Forty-nine jurisdictions, not forty-nine states. Only two states use it statewide.

One more measure of what is real. In 2026 itself, 17 of those jurisdictions are electing people with ranked ballots. They run from Maine's primaries in June to general elections in November, in Portland, Maine, and in Portland, Oregon.

Let's test the breakdown. One op-ed says ranked-choice voting is used by 49 states' worth of voters. A rebuttal says only two states really use it. Both are mangling one precise statement. What is it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Forty-nine jurisdictions use it or have adopted it, reaching about 14 million voters, across 22 states and D.C. Only two states use it statewide. Most of the 49 are cities and counties.

Precision here is strategic as well as honest. Inflate the count, and you hand the opposition an easy correction. Deflate it, and you erase the experience of 14 million voters.

## Where it is banned

That is one map. Now the mirror image.

As of mid-2026, nineteen states prohibit ranked-choice voting, by statute or in a constitution. Almost all of them do it the same way: they forbid their own cities and towns to adopt it.

Listen to the pace of it. Tennessee and Florida went first, in 2022. Three more states followed in 2023. Six more came in 2024, and six more again in 2025. Then Indiana and Ohio, in early 2026.

That sequence tracks a national network, one that circulates model legislation.

Who passed these bans? Seventeen of the nineteen came from Republican trifectas. Kansas and Kentucky enacted theirs under divided government. And Ohio's ban passed with bipartisan votes.

One ban stands apart. Missouri's is the only one that voters wrote into a constitution. It passed with about 68 percent, in a package that led with language about citizenship. And it carved out the system St. Louis already had.

> As of mid-2026, nineteen states ban ranked-choice voting, mostly by overruling their cities.

## What the two maps share

Now set the two maps beside each other, and notice what they have in common.

In both, the action is states deciding for cities.

Most of the 14 million voters covered by ranked-choice voting got it through local democracy. The bans mostly exist to make sure no more of them do.

Put another way: the reform has spread from below, and it is being stopped from above.

Here is my reading of that, and it is a reading. There is a famous image of federalism as a laboratory. Whatever else these bans are, they are not that laboratory at work.

> My reading of the ban map: the laboratory is being padlocked.

[pause]

There are two honest ways to read this whole map. One says a reform that reaches 14 million voters is advancing. The other says a reform banned in nineteen states is in retreat. Take the reading you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## The 2026 ballot

Keep both readings in mind, because 2026 moves the map again, in two places. One is a debut. The other is a rubber match.

The debut is Washington, D.C. Its measure, Initiative 83, passed in November 2024. Along the way it survived two lawsuits from the D.C. Democratic Party.

But there is a catch in how the District works. An initiative there cannot appropriate money. So the D.C. Council held something like a pocket veto. In July 2025, it used half of it.

By a vote of 8 to 4, the Council funded the ranked-choice provisions for the 2026 cycle. It declined to fund the other half of the initiative. That half would have let the District's independent voters, tens of thousands of them, take part in primaries.

So in June, D.C. voters rank up to five candidates for mayor and council. The other half of what they approved sits unfunded.

Half a loaf, delivered late, is still a precedent. The nation's capital now ranks its ballots.

Here is a question on that. Nearly 73 percent of D.C. voters approved the initiative, and only half of it is operating. Why?

[long pause]

Not because a court struck it down, and not because Congress stepped in. The cause is structural. A District initiative cannot spend money, so the Council had to fund it, and the Council funded one half.

> A supermajority can pass a reform and still need the Council to fund it.

That is the last chapter's lesson again. A yes vote is the middle of the campaign.

[pause]

Now Alaska, the rubber match. On December 31, 2025, the lieutenant governor certified a new repeal initiative, for the ballot of November 3, 2026. It carried nearly 43,000 verified signatures, comfortably more than the law requires.

Alaskans have voted on this system twice. In 2020 they adopted it, with 50.55 percent. In 2024 they declined to repeal it, by 743 votes, confirmed by a recount.

Now they vote a third time, with six years of lived experience. I will not predict it. I will only note what repeal would restore: partisan primaries, and general elections won by plurality. And that would happen in a state where more voters register nonpartisan than with both major parties combined.

[pause]

And the column of setbacks, stated plainly. There are three.

Michigan. A group called Rank MI Vote suspended its drive for a 2026 constitutional amendment, in December 2025. It was more than 200,000 signatures short. It was an all-volunteer operation, and it learned that passion needs infrastructure. Its new target is 2028.

Utah. Its pilot for cities is gone. It expired on January 1, 2026, and its cities reverted.

And the states that voted no in 2024. Nevada, Oregon, Colorado, Idaho and Arizona remain "no" states.

> As of mid-2026, statewide expansion runs through one place: defending Alaska.

## Ten years on the map

Step back, and look at ten years at once.

In 2016, Maine adopts, the first state to do so. In 2018, Maine's voters defend it. In 2020, Alaska adopts, and in the same month Massachusetts votes no. Adoption was never inevitable.

In 2021, New York City holds the largest ranked-choice election in American history. In 2022, the ban era begins. November 2024 is a split decision: five statewide losses and a ban, against D.C.'s yes and Alaska's narrow survival. And by March 2026, the bans number nineteen.

The shape is plain. The usage map and the ban map have been growing at the same time.

One question before we gather up. Earlier, I took the number 49 apart. How many of those are states that use ranked ballots statewide? And which are they?

[long pause]

Two. Maine and Alaska. And one of the two is voting on repeal.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep, all of them as of mid-2026.

One. Forty-nine jurisdictions, not forty-nine states. They reach nearly 14 million voters. Most are cities and counties, and only two states use ranked ballots statewide.

[beat]

Two. Nineteen states ban ranked-choice voting, mostly by overruling their cities. Seventeen of those bans came from Republican trifectas.

[beat]

Three. In both maps, the action is states deciding for cities. My reading is that the laboratory is being padlocked.

[beat]

Four. D.C. is the year's debut, and it is half a loaf. A supermajority passed the initiative, and the Council funded only the ranked-choice half.

[beat]

And five. Alaska votes a third time, on November 3, 2026. Statewide expansion runs through one place: defending Alaska.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles: the raw geography. Fourteen million voters rank their ballots. Nineteen states forbid their cities to try.

What it does not settle is why the fight has hardened into this shape. Why does one party's national infrastructure now treat a voting method as an enemy? I think the deepest argument is not about general elections at all. It is about the elections most Americans never vote in, the ones that pick their representatives: the primaries. That machine is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to those two capitals. One is ranking its ballots for the first time. One has just made that illegal.

[beat]

Both are on the map, and an honest map shows both.
