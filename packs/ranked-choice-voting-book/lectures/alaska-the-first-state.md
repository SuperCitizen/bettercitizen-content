Don Young held Alaska's only seat in the U.S. House for forty-nine years. He had been the state's congressman since before most living Alaskans were born.

He died in March 2022. And forty-eight candidates filed to replace him.

[beat]

A former governor turned vice-presidential nominee. A Republican from the state's most famous Democratic family. A Yup'ik former state legislator, who had managed salmon runs on the Kuskokwim. And a city councilman from North Pole, legally named Santa Claus. He finished sixth.

Under the old rules, party primaries would have carved that field up before most voters ever saw it. Under Alaska's brand-new rules, all forty-eight ran on a single ballot. Every voter chose among all of them, and the four with the most votes advanced.

American politics had never run an election like it.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was the owner's manual: how a ranked ballot is marked, and how it is counted. It left one thing open. Does the machine deliver under American conditions?

So we go to the first proving ground, and we take it in four steps. First, what Alaskans actually passed. Second, the count the whole country watched. Third, the strongest point against me, which I have to answer. And last, what changed, and the fight to undo it.

## Three reforms in one package

In November 2020, Alaskans passed Ballot Measure 2 by initiative. The margin was 3,781 votes.

I won't pretend that's a landslide. Reform never wins in landslides, because it asks voters to bet on something they've never seen.

Measure 2 was three reforms in one package. It abolished party primaries. In their place came a single nonpartisan primary, where every candidate for an office appears on one ballot and the top four advance, whatever their party. It adopted ranked choice voting for the general election. And it required disclosure of the true sources of certain "dark money" flowing into Alaska campaigns.

The first two pieces are not separable, and this matters. The top-four primary moves the real decision out of low-turnout partisan primaries, and into November. Ranked choice is what makes a four-way November race workable. Without it, four candidates on a general ballot is a spoiler machine.

> The top-four primary moves the choice to November. Ranking makes four candidates workable.

The coalition behind it was deliberately cross-partisan: independents, moderate Republicans, and reform-minded Democrats. It also had significant money from national democracy-reform groups, a fact opponents have never tired of mentioning. And opponents sued. In 2022, in a case called Kohlhaas v. State, the Alaska Supreme Court rejected the challenge. Nothing in the state constitution, it held, forbade the system Alaskans had chosen.

Let's test the bundle. Suppose a state adopts ranked choice voting for the general election, but keeps its closed party primaries. What does the November ballot look like? And how much work is left for the ranking to do?

[long pause]

Not much. Each party still sends one nominee, chosen by its small primary electorate. So November is still mostly a two-candidate race, and ranking rarely changes the outcome. The pressure toward the extremes happens upstream, in the primary, untouched. That is why Measure 2 bundled the two.

## The count the country watched

Now the trial by fire.

Don Young's death meant the new system's first outing would be improvised. The special primary came in June 2022, statewide and by mail, with those forty-eight candidates. Four advanced: Sarah Palin, Nick Begich, the independent Al Gross, and Mary Peltola. Gross withdrew days later, and officials kept the August ballot at three.

The election came on August 16. Two weeks later, with the absentee ballots in, officials ran the first ranked-choice count in Alaska's history.

Here is how it went. On first choices, Peltola, the Democrat, had about 40 percent. Palin, a Republican, had about 31. Begich, also a Republican, had about 28 and a half.

Nobody had a majority. So Begich, in last place, was eliminated, and each of his ballots moved to whichever candidate that voter had ranked next.

[beat]

Look at what the transfers actually show. About 27,000 of his ballots went to Palin. About 15,000 went to Peltola. And about 11,000 ranked no one else, and dropped out of the count.

Begich's voters broke toward Palin nearly two to one. That is exactly what you'd expect from a fellow Republican's supporters. The system did not divert Republican votes. It delivered them. [beat] They simply weren't enough.

In the final count, Peltola won 51.48 percent to 48.52. She became the first Alaska Native ever elected to Congress.

Then came the screaming. Senator Tom Cotton called the result "a scam to rig elections."

But walk through what the critics were actually claiming. Nearly seven in ten voters declined to rank Palin first. She then lost a two-candidate final count. And somehow she was entitled to the seat. The old plurality rules might have handed it to her anyway. The new rules asked the one question that should decide an election: of the candidates remaining, whom do more voters prefer?

> Palin was asked the deciding question, and she lost it.

## The critique I have to answer

That is the easy objection. Here is the hard one. Honest advocacy means conceding the strongest point against you.

Two mathematicians, Adam Graham-Squire and David McCune, analyzed the ballots from that special election. They found that Begich would have beaten Peltola head to head. He would also have beaten Palin head to head.

Voting theorists have a name for a candidate like that: the Condorcet winner. In plainer words, the consensus candidate. And the count eliminated him first, because consensus candidates can be short on first-choice votes.

This is called the center squeeze.

> The center squeeze is real: the count eliminated the consensus candidate first.

I won't insult you by pretending otherwise.

[pause]

What I don't concede is that this indicts the reform. I have two reasons, and you should weigh them as an advocate's reasons.

First, the old system would never have found Begich either. Picture a closed Republican primary in 2022, with Donald Trump's endorsement behind Palin. It almost certainly nominates Palin. And a pick-one count would have given us no way to know a consensus candidate had been passed over. We only know what Begich was because ranked ballots exist.

Second, the failure was bounded, and it corrected itself. That November came the rematch: the same three candidates plus a Libertarian, with far higher turnout. Peltola took 48.8 percent of first choices. She won the final count with just under 55 percent.

Given a second look and more information, Alaskans didn't reverse the "fluke." They ratified it.

> My answer: the old ballot hid the consensus candidate. The ranked ballot revealed him.

So there are two readings of August 2022. One says the count passed over the candidate who beats everyone head to head, and that is a flaw in the method. The other says only a ranked ballot could even show you that. Take the reading you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## What changed

The race I keep coming back to isn't Peltola's. It's Lisa Murkowski's.

In February 2021, Murkowski voted to convict Trump at his second impeachment trial. Her own state party censured her for it, and Trump endorsed her challenger, Kelly Tshibaka. Murkowski is precisely the kind of incumbent the old system existed to execute.

So think it through. Under the old rules, a closed Republican primary and then a pick-one general, where does her reelection bid most likely end? And what, specifically, do the new rules change?

[long pause]

It ends in the primary, where a small, censure-minded partisan electorate gets the only vote that matters. The top-four primary made it impossible to eliminate her with partisan votes alone. And ranking let Democrats and independents support her in November, without abandoning their own first choice.

She trailed in no round. She edged Tshibaka on first choices by less than a point, and won the final count with 53.7 percent.

Hear the limit in that. The new system doesn't guarantee moderates win. It guarantees the whole electorate decides whether it wants one.

And on the very same ballots, Alaskans reelected their conservative Republican governor outright. Mike Dunleavy cleared 50 percent of first choices, with no transfers needed. Those same voters sent the Democrat Peltola back to the House.

> Voters, not the method, picked the winners.

The tone changed too, in ways I'll state carefully. The incentive is structural. When your opponent's supporters hold your fate in their second choices, scorching the earth has a price. Murkowski and Peltola, a Republican and a Democrat, publicly said they would rank each other. And after a fall of Palin and Begich attacking each other, more than a fifth of Begich's final-round ballots ranked neither remaining candidate.

Candidates who want to win under these rules learn to ask for second choices. Whether that durably lowers the temperature is a claim I'll defend with data in a later chapter. What 2022 shows is the mechanism, working as designed.

## They came back

Nobody surrenders a duopoly gracefully.

In 2024, opponents put repeal on the ballot. It failed by 743 votes, out of more than 321,000 cast, confirmed by a recount. That is the closest ballot-measure result in Alaska history.

Twice now, Alaskans have voted for this system, both times narrowly. That is not a mandate anyone can coast on. It is, however, two consecutive verdicts from the only jury that counts. And the second came after the system had actually been used, with full knowledge of everything the critics had said.

On that same 2024 ballot, the system's opponents won under it. Nick Begich, eliminated first in the 2022 special election, came back and beat Peltola for the House seat. He won the final ranked-choice round with 51.3 percent.

Whatever this system is, it is not a machine for electing Democrats.

[pause]

And the fight is happening as I write. On December 31, 2025, the lieutenant governor certified a third ranked-choice ballot measure. Repeal goes back before voters in November 2026. Governor Dunleavy signed the petition. So did Congressman Begich, who holds his seat because ranked ballots gathered him a final-round majority. A coalition called Protect Alaska's Elections, backed by unions and Alaska Native organizations, is organizing the defense.

I won't predict the outcome. After margins like those, anyone who predicts Alaska ballot measures is guessing.

One question before we gather up. Near the start, I said Measure 2 bundled two reforms that cannot be separated. Can you still name them, and say what each one does for the other?

[long pause]

The top-four primary, and the ranked general election. The primary moves the real choice to November. Ranking makes four candidates workable.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. In 2020, by 3,781 votes, Alaska passed a bundle. The top-four primary moves the choice to November. Ranking makes four candidates workable.

[beat]

Two. In August 2022, Begich's voters broke for Palin nearly two to one, and Peltola still won the final count. Palin was asked the deciding question, and she lost it.

[beat]

Three. The concession. The center squeeze is real: the count eliminated the consensus candidate first. My answer is that the old ballot hid him, and the ranked ballot revealed him.

[beat]

Four. Murkowski survived a censure because the whole electorate got to decide. A Republican governor, a Democratic congresswoman, and later Begich himself all won under these rules. Voters, not the method, picked the winners.

[beat]

And five. Repeal failed in 2024 by 743 votes. It is on the ballot again in November 2026.

[pause]

Here is what Alaska settles. It settles feasibility. "It can't work here" is no longer an argument anyone can make with a straight face. "It won't be allowed to keep working" remains a real threat.

And here is what it doesn't settle. Whether one state's experience generalizes. Whether the center squeeze will recur often enough to matter. Whether the civility is durable, or a honeymoon. I'll take those up with data, not anecdotes, in the research chapter.

First, credit where it's owed. Alaska was the first state to run its entire general election on ranked ballots. It was not the first state to adopt them. That was Maine, which got there earlier, the hard way, and nearly lost it twice. That story is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to that ballot with forty-eight names on it, and Santa Claus in sixth place.

The opponents have asked Alaskans the same question again and again. The answer, so far, has been stubborn, narrow, and twice repeated: keep it.

[beat]

If you live in Alaska, the third answer is yours to give.
