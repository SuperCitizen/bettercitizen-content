Election Day, 2014. Across America, people are standing in line to vote. And outside the polling places of Maine, volunteers are standing there too, collecting signatures.

[beat]

About 40,000 signatures, in a single day. And the reform those people signed for would not reach the ballot for two more years.

In November 2016 it did, and Maine voted 52 to 48. It became the first state in the country to adopt ranked-choice voting.

Then the legislature tried to delay the law and repeal it. So those same volunteers ran a second campaign, called a people's veto. In June 2018 they won again, 54 to 46.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked-choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter went around the world, and it ended on a question: how do we get this here? This chapter is my answer, and it is a playbook.

I open with Maine because of the single most important thing to know about passing ranked-choice voting. It is not an argument you win once.

> It is a campaign you win three times: adoption, implementation, and defense.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two doors into a state. Second, the playbook itself, in six moves. Third, the worst night this movement has had, and what it taught. And last, the campaign that begins after you win.

## Which door your state has

There are two ways in, and your state constitution has already decided which one you get.

The first door is direct democracy. About half the states, 26 of them, plus D.C., give citizens some form of it. That means the ballot initiative, the popular referendum, or both.

If you live in one of those states, you can take the question over the heads of legislators, whose own careers were built on the current rules. And it is no accident that the only two states using ranked-choice voting statewide both came in through this door. Maine and Alaska each got there by initiative.

[beat]

The second door is the legislature. Everywhere else, the path runs through it. It is slower, but it is not hopeless. It arrives in smaller packages.

Hawaii's legislature adopted ranked-choice voting in 2022, for federal special elections and for vacancies on county councils. That is a low-stakes beachhead. It lets election officials build competence before anyone's governorship rides on it.

Colorado's legislature created a local option, supported by the state, and Boulder used it to rank its first ballots for mayor in 2023. Virginia's legislature opened a local option too, and Arlington has used it since 2023.

The legislative route means accepting the politician's eternal question. "How does this affect me?" And it means designing first steps where the honest answer is "barely."

> In a legislature, the first bill puts no sitting legislator's seat at stake.

Let's test that. Your state has no initiative process, and the majority party chairs both elections committees. What does a realistic first bill look like? And what does it leave out, on purpose? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It looks like Hawaii's law, or Colorado's. Ranked ballots for special elections, for military and overseas runoff ballots, or as an option cities may choose. It leaves out statewide and legislative offices, because a bill that threatens the careers of the people voting on it dies in committee.

And the beachhead is not a consolation prize. It builds competence in the election offices and a local track record. That is the raw material every later, bigger campaign runs on.

## The playbook, with the receipts

Those are the doors. Now the campaign. Successful campaigns keep converging on the same architecture, and it has six moves. I will count them off, each with the way it fails.

One. Build a coalition across party lines. Maine's campaign was chaired by an independent former legislator. It was backed by the League of Women Voters, which had endorsed the reform in 2011 after a three-year study. Alaska's coalition in 2020 ran independents, moderate Republicans and Democrats together. A campaign that reads as one party's project loses the other party's voters two to one, and it invites a ban later.

[beat]

Two. Start local, and bank a track record. Cities are where voters meet ranking without partisan stakes, and 36 cities use it today. Boulder, Arlington and New York all became proof points for their states. Skip this step, and your statewide opposition gets to say "untested" on every mailer.

[beat]

Three. Qualify the measure, and frame it. Gathering signatures is a rehearsal for organizing at mass scale. Frame the measure around fairness and majority rule, not party advantage. Here is the failure. Michigan's all-volunteer drive for 2026 fell more than 200,000 signatures short. Passion without paid infrastructure stalls at scale.

[beat]

Four. Fund the air war, honestly. In Alaska, my side raised 6.8 million dollars. The other side raised about 580,000. National reform money wins campaigns, and then it becomes the opposition's favorite talking point. So disclose it, and own it. And know that in 2024, a far better funded opposition flipped that script in several states.

[beat]

Five. Implement like a public-works project. Education budgets. Equipment certification. Official mock elections. Outreach in many languages. I will come back to this one, because implementation failures, not arguments, are what kill reforms.

[beat]

And six. Defend it, twice if necessary. Maine beat a legislative repeal with its people's veto. Alaska beat repeal in 2024, and it faces another repeal initiative in November 2026. Budget for the defense before you celebrate the win. No defense organization, no reform.

[pause]

Walk those six moves and you will notice what they imply.

> The ballot question is the middle of the campaign, not the end.

Maine's vote in 2016 had two years of signature gathering and education before it. It had two more years of legal and political defense after it. Along the way, an advisory opinion from Maine's supreme court forced the law back to primaries and federal races only. The people's veto is what saved even that.

## What 2024 taught

That is the playbook when it works. Now the night it did not. I will not spin this.

November 2024 was the worst night the statewide movement has had. Adoption measures lost everywhere they appeared, in five states.

Colorado's took 46 percent. Idaho's took 30. Oregon's took 42, and Arizona's failed with 41. Nevada's measure had passed its first vote in 2022, and it failed its required second vote. On the same night, Missouri voters approved a constitutional ban, written by the legislature.

Only two results kept the map from being a shutout. D.C. passed its initiative with nearly 73 percent. And Alaska beat repeal, by 743 votes.

[pause]

Three lessons, learned the expensive way.

First, bundling cuts both ways. Alaska's package in 2020 worked. It put top-four primaries, ranked-choice voting and dark-money disclosure into one coherent "voters first" measure. But the 2024 measures mostly asked voters to swallow open primaries, and new general-election rules, and unfamiliar ballots, in one gulp. Idaho's drubbing suggests the dose matters.

Second, the opposition showed up funded and organized. It had model legislation, and national messaging that had matured since 2020. The network behind the bans was now playing offense.

And third, the one I would underline.

> Status-quo bias is the house edge: a confused voter votes no.

The movement's response has been visible in 2025 and 2026. Back to cities. Back to legislatures. Back to defending Alaska. That is slower, which is not the same as stopped. Reform movements in America have always advanced in waves. Women's suffrage lost dozens of state referenda before it stopped losing.

[pause]

Now put Nevada under the light. Its measure passed in 2022 with 53 percent. Two years later, on the required second vote, it failed with 47. What does that sequence teach a campaign? Think it through.

[long pause]

It does not show that Nevada's voters never wanted reform. The first vote proved a majority existed. What it shows is that a requirement to win twice gives the opposition a full cycle to organize, against a target that is now standing still. So a campaign has to budget for the defense vote, not just the first win. Adoption, implementation, defense.

## The campaign after the campaign

Here is the part most advocates skip, and I refuse to. A yes vote buys you an engineering project. Three line items decide whether your reform survives its first contact with reality.

The first is equipment. Modern tabulators from the major vendors can run ranked elections. But "can" means certified software, tested configurations, and counties that have licensed it. Readiness varies county by county. So put equipment audits in the text of the bill, with funding.

The second is education. New York is the benchmark. Before its debut in June 2021, the city spent 15 million dollars on ads, multilingual outreach and community partnerships. Afterward, 95 percent of voters called the ballot simple, in a city of eight million. Budget education as if turnout depends on it. For the most vulnerable voters, it does.

The third line item is a clause, and Utah is the cautionary tale.

In 2018, Utah created a pilot that let cities opt in. In 2021, 23 cities did, and their voters ranked happily. But pilots expire. Clerks and councils saw the end coming, and by 2023 participation had fallen to 12 cities. Bills to extend the pilot stalled in Senate committees.

On January 1, 2026, the program ended. Every participating city went back to plurality elections, whatever its own voters preferred.

[beat]

Nobody repealed ranked-choice voting in Utah. It was allowed to lapse. And for the opposition, that is victory at zero cost.

> A sunset is a death sentence. Negotiate it out.

A pilot with a sunset clause is a reform with a self-destruct already installed. And at renewal, the burden of proof will sit on you, forever.

That is my advice, and I hold it firmly. But Hawaii's small first step and Utah's pilot were both modest offers from a legislature. So make the best case you can for the organizer who takes the pilot anyway.

[pause 5s]

One more question before we gather up. At the start, I said this is a campaign you win three times. Can you still name the three?

[long pause]

Adoption. Implementation. Defense. Maine's vote sat in the middle, with two years of work before it and two more after.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Passing ranked-choice voting is a campaign you win three times: adoption, implementation, and defense.

[beat]

Two. There are two doors. About half the states allow the initiative, and Maine and Alaska both came in that way. Everywhere else, the door is the legislature, where the first bill puts no sitting legislator's seat at stake.

[beat]

Three. The playbook has six moves. A coalition across parties. A local track record. A measure that qualifies and is framed around fairness. Honest funding. Implementation. And defense. The ballot question is the middle of the campaign, not the end.

[beat]

Four. 2024, conceded. Five statewide losses, and three lessons. Bundling cuts both ways. The opposition is funded and organized. And status-quo bias is the house edge: a confused voter votes no.

[beat]

And five. After the yes vote comes equipment, education, and the clause to refuse. A sunset is a death sentence.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it does not. There is a playbook, and it has receipts. Its failures are now as well documented as its wins: signature drives without organization, measures stuffed too full, education nobody funded, and sunset clauses.

What it does not settle is where the map stands after all this winning and losing, ban by ban and debut by debut. That inventory is the next chapter, and it includes Alaska's vote in November 2026.

[pause]

For now, go back to Maine, and the volunteers outside the polling places.

They were not there to win an argument.

[beat]

They were there to start a campaign they would have to win three times.
