On a Tuesday in June 2021, nearly a million New Yorkers did something the pundit class had spent two years insisting ordinary voters couldn't handle.

[beat]

They ranked.

Thirteen candidates for mayor. A ballot that let you list up to five, in order. It was the largest test ranked choice voting had ever faced in America.

The voters were fine. The voters were better than fine. It was a city agency that fell on its face. And even that, as you'll hear, proved the reform's point more than the critics'.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was Maine, and before it, Alaska. Those are the chapters everyone quotes back to me, because states get the headlines.

But the states didn't invent this movement. They inherited it. For two decades, American cities have been the laboratories where ranked choice voting was tried, stress-tested, occasionally repealed, and steadily refined.

If you want to know whether it works, don't ask a think tank. Ask a city clerk.

We will make four stops. First, New York, the stress test. Second, the Twin Cities, for durability. Third, Oakland, where you can watch what the count actually does. And last, the growing list, with the honest part: the places that walked away.

## New York: the stress test

In 2019, New York City voters approved ranked choice voting for primary and special elections. Note the margin: 73.5 percent. That is not a squeaker engineered by activists on a sleepy ballot line. Almost three out of four voters, in the nation's largest city, said yes.

The first full-scale test was the Democratic primary for mayor, in June 2021. Thirteen candidates, nearly 940,000 ballots, and a race that was truly unsettled.

Eric Adams led the first count. He won in the eighth round, beating Kathryn Garcia by 8,426 votes. The final split was 50.4 percent to 49.6.

And here is the number the skeptics never mention. In that final round, roughly 85 percent of ballots were still in play, still expressing a preference between the last two candidates. Under the old system, everyone who picked the third- through thirteenth-place candidates would simply have been discarded.

[pause]

Now, the famous fiasco.

A week after the primary, the city's Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that was visibly wrong. Before the election, staff had done a trial run with test ballots. They failed to clear those test ballots before hitting the button.

The board retracted the tally within hours, and ran the count again, clean. Critics declared ranked choice voting too complicated to administer.

So let's test that. Based on what actually happened, what failed? And why does the distinction matter? Take a moment.

[long pause]

What failed was an agency. It left test ballots in its system: a human error in handling data, one that could have corrupted a plurality count just as easily. The ranking, the voters, and the certified count all worked as designed.

And the distinction matters because the case against ranked choice voting rests on a claim that the method is fragile. In New York, the method was sound, and an agency was sloppy.

> In New York, the method was sound. An agency was sloppy.

Then New York did the persuasive thing. It ran the system again, bigger.

The mayoral primary of June 2025 drew over a million Democratic ballots, the biggest ranked-choice contest in American history. Zohran Mamdani beat Andrew Cuomo in the final round, 56.4 to 43.6. The tabulation came out on schedule, with no test ballots anywhere near the count.

And the voters delivered their own verdict. Common Cause New York and FairVote surveyed 991 primary voters as they left the polls. Ninety-six percent said their ballot was simple to complete. And 77 percent said they want ranked choice voting in future local elections.

Four years earlier, exit polling had found nearly identical numbers. Two full-scale tests, same answer.

## The Twin Cities: durability

That is scale. Now durability.

A single election can be a fluke. Fifteen years cannot.

Minneapolis voters approved ranked choice voting in 2006, and first used it in November 2009. That was so early that no certified counting equipment existed yet. So election staff counted that first election by hand, over fifteen days. Then they invented a hand-count procedure for the next one. The city still calls it the "Minneapolis Method."

St. Paul adopted ranked choice voting by referendum in 2009, and first used it in 2011.

Since then, the Twin Cities have run ranked-choice elections over and over. In Minneapolis, every one of the twenty-two offices on the municipal ballot is now elected this way. No meltdown. No mass confusion. No exodus of bewildered voters.

The most boring fact in this book may be the most important one.

> Where ranked choice voting has been used longest, it has become unremarkable.

And if fifteen years doesn't impress you, try eighty-five. Cambridge, Massachusetts has elected its city council and school committee by ranked ballot, continuously, since 1941. Through hand counts, punch cards, and scanners. Cambridge isn't an experiment anyone is watching nervously. It's just how Cambridge votes.

## Oakland: the largest fragment

So it runs at scale, and it lasts. But what does it actually do? For that, go to the Bay Area.

San Francisco adopted ranked choice voting by initiative in 2002, and first used it in 2004. It has been the city's normal ever since. But the election that taught the country something happened across the bay.

Oakland first used ranked choice voting in 2010, in an open race for mayor. A former state senate leader, Don Perata, led the first count, with about 34 percent of first choices. A councilmember, Jean Quan, sat at 24.

Under plurality rules, Perata wins and the story ends. A candidate two-thirds of the city didn't pick takes city hall.

Instead, trailing candidates were eliminated, and their voters' backup rankings transferred. Quan consolidated the broader coalition. She won the final round, 51 to 49, and became Oakland's first Asian American woman mayor.

[beat]

Perata's supporters cried foul, and you can understand the instinct. Their candidate "came in first."

Here is a question for you. Perata led the first count, 34 to 24. I say Quan's win is the system working, not failing. What is my argument?

[long pause]

Thirty-four percent is a fragment, not a majority. When the eliminated candidates' supporters expressed their backup preferences, most of them preferred Quan. So a Perata win would have installed the candidate a majority of voters ranked below his rival.

> The reform advocate's case: 34 percent is the largest fragment, not the majority's choice.

Ranked choice voting didn't take anything from him. It asked the question plurality voting never asks: whom do most voters actually prefer?

There is a rebuttal, and you should hear it.

> The status-quo defender's rebuttal: first preferences should carry special weight.

The next chapter takes that rebuttal up. I won't settle it here.

## The growing list, and the honest part

One more stop. Look at how many different roads now lead to the same place.

Salt Lake City got ranked choice voting from a Republican state legislature. In 2018, Utah created a pilot program that cities could opt into, and in the fall of 2021, Salt Lake City and other Utah cities ran ranked-choice elections.

Santa Fe's voters approved it in 2008, with 65 percent. Then they watched their own city government stall for a decade, until the New Mexico Supreme Court cleared the way in 2018. In that first election, more than 96 percent of voters ranked at least four of the five candidates.

And in 2023, Arlington ran the first ranked-choice election in Virginia's history, under a state enabling law. Its board voted that December to keep using it.

A legislature. A court order. A state law. This reform travels.

[pause]

Now the honest part. Advocates who hide the ledger's other column deserve the skepticism they get.

A few places have walked away. Pierce County, Washington adopted ranked choice voting in 2006, and repealed it decisively in 2009, after two rocky elections. Aspen, Colorado used it once, and dropped it in 2010.

Burlington, Vermont is the case opponents cite most. It repealed ranked choice voting in 2010, 52 to 48, after a mayoral election whose winner trailed in first choices.

Utah's pilot, for all its early bipartisan promise, shrank cycle by cycle. It was allowed to expire on January 1, 2026.

And the biggest counterforce isn't cities leaving. It is states slamming the door before their cities can enter. As of this writing, nineteen states have banned ranked choice voting outright. Ohio was the most recent, in March 2026, with a law that threatened to withhold state funds from any city that uses it.

> Nineteen states have banned ranked choice voting before their cities could try it.

So why do I still write "the voters who use it keep it" with a straight face?

Because Burlington itself came back. In March 2021, eleven years after the repeal, its voters reinstated ranked choice voting for city council races, with 64 percent. The most famous repeal in the movement's history reversed itself, by a bigger margin than the repeal ever had.

And by FairVote's tally, as of early 2026, 49 jurisdictions use it or have adopted it. They are home to nearly 14 million voters.

[pause]

That is my reading of the ledger. There are two others, and they deserve to be heard at full strength.

> The status-quo defender's reading: in Burlington and Pierce County, voters rejected a confusing import.

> The alternative-reform advocate's reading: cities should be piloting different systems entirely.

Three readings of one record. Take the one you find least convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

One more question before we gather up. Back in New York, in 2021, the Board of Elections published a tally that was wrong. What had the staff left in the system?

[long pause]

Test ballots, from a trial run before the election. The method was sound. An agency was sloppy.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Ranked choice voting runs at scale. New York counted close to a million ranked ballots in a single primary, twice. In 2021 the method was sound, and an agency was sloppy.

[beat]

Two. It endures. The Twin Cities have run it for more than a decade and a half, and Cambridge since 1941. Where ranked choice voting has been used longest, it has become unremarkable.

[beat]

Three. It does what it promises. In Oakland, in 2010, Perata led 34 to 24, and Quan won the final round. Thirty-four percent is the largest fragment, not the majority's choice.

[beat]

And four. The ledger has another column. Pierce County, Aspen and Burlington repealed it. Utah's pilot expired. Nineteen states have banned it. And Burlington came back, with 64 percent.

[pause]

Here is what the cities do not settle, and I won't pretend otherwise.

Does success in nonpartisan city races transfer cleanly to high-stakes, partisan, statewide elections? That is why the Maine and Alaska chapters exist. And are the repeal stories noise, or signal?

Status-quo defenders and alternative-reform advocates both deserve a full hearing. The next chapter is called "The Opposition," and it gives them one.

[pause]

For now, go back to that Tuesday in New York, and thirteen names on a ballot.

Nearly a million people ranked them, and said it was simple.

[beat]

The open question was never whether voters can do this. It is whether they will be allowed to.
