You walk into a voting booth. You pick one person. You leave.

[beat]

That's it. That is the entirety of your democratic expression: a single mark, in a world that is anything but binary.

You may have spent weeks weighing three candidates. Admiring one. Tolerating another. Dreading the third. The ballot doesn't care. It asks exactly one question, pick one, and it discards everything else you know.

And if you voted for the person who came in third? Your voice didn't just lose. [beat] It vanished, as if you never showed up at all.

[pause]

A word about what you are hearing. This is the introduction to a book: Rank the Vote, by Chris Borgia. It is a book that argues for something, a reform called ranked choice voting. And the "I" in it is the author. The case you are about to hear is his.

[pause]

This is how nearly every American election works. And it's insane.

We will take this introduction in four steps. First, an election that could not answer the simplest question about itself. Second, the fix this book argues for. Third, where the fight stands, and I mean both halves of it. And last, what I owe you as an advocate.

## The question your ballot never asks

November 2000. The presidency of the United States came down to 537 votes.

That was George W. Bush's certified margin over Al Gore in Florida, out of nearly six million votes cast.

[beat]

Now a second number, from those same Florida ballots. 97,488 people voted for Ralph Nader.

Who were they? Were most of them Gore supporters, denied a backup choice? Were they secret Bush sympathizers? Or voters who would have stayed home in a two-man race?

[long pause]

Here is the embarrassing answer. Nobody knows. And nobody can know.

> In Florida, in 2000, the ballot never asked.

Think about what that election produced. A constitutional crisis. A Supreme Court case. A generation of recrimination. And zero information about what its own voters actually preferred.

The next chapter dissects that pathology in full. For now, just notice one thing. The most consequential American election in living memory could not answer the simplest question about itself.

Let's bring that down to one voter, someone you know.

"I like the third-party candidate best, but I'm voting for the major-party one," your friend tells you. "I can't waste my vote."

Under pick-one rules, what exactly is the system asking your friend to do? And what information dies in that booth? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is asking your friend to vote against their own judgment. To record a first choice they don't actually hold, because recording the honest one could help elect the candidate they like least.

And the lie compounds. The official tally now shows one more enthusiastic major-party voter, and one fewer third-party supporter. So the next cycle's candidates, donors and journalists all see demand that doesn't exist, and miss demand that does.

The strategy is rational. The ballot made it so.

> This book's first claim: blame the system, not the voter.

## The fix

That is the problem. Now the fix this book argues for, and it is almost embarrassingly small.

Same candidates. Same booths. Same Tuesday in November. One change. Instead of picking one candidate, you rank them. First choice, second choice, third, as far down as you care to go.

And if your favorite can't win, your ballot counts for your next choice, instead of going in the trash.

> The fix this book argues for: instead of picking one, you rank them.

That is ranked choice voting. The version used for single offices has a name of its own: the instant runoff. It does with one trip to the polls what a runoff election does with two.

Picture the two ballots side by side, each with the same four candidates: Avila, Brooks, Chen and Dimas.

On the old ballot, you mark Brooks, and everything else you think is discarded. On the ranked ballot, you put Brooks first, Avila second and Chen third. You can rank as many or as few as you want.

[beat]

So the candidates, the booth and the Tuesday stay the same. The only thing that changes is how much of your mind the ballot is allowed to record.

[pause]

My claim is that ranked choice voting fixes the pick-one pathology. Not with revolution. Not with a constitutional amendment. Not with some untested theory cooked up in a think tank.

It fixes it with arithmetic, with common sense, and with a track record. That record now runs from Canberra to Cambridge, Massachusetts, and from Maine's mill towns to every village in Alaska.

That is a claim, and you should hold me to it. Which brings us to the record.

## Where the fight stands

Twenty years ago, this book would have been a speculative pamphlet. It isn't one now, because the experiment has been run.

Three places to know. Maine's voters adopted ranked choice voting by citizen initiative in 2016, the first state in American history to do it. They have defended it at the ballot box since.

Alaska followed in 2020. It ran its entire 2022 general election on ranked ballots, governor on down.

And in June 2025, New York City counted more than a million ranked ballots in its mayoral primary, without breaking a sweat.

[beat]

Then the wider count, which comes from an organization called FairVote, as of early 2026. It finds 49 American jurisdictions that use ranked choice voting, or have adopted it for upcoming elections. They are home to nearly 14 million voters.

[pause]

And I'll tell you the other half, because you should hear it from me first. The backlash is just as real.

Nineteen states have passed laws banning ranked choice voting, most recently Ohio, in March 2026. They acted before most of their cities could try it.

Alaska's opponents lost a repeal vote in 2024, by 743 votes. They then qualified another repeal, for the ballot of November 2026.

And in April 2026, Maine's highest court advised that the state constitution still blocks ranked ballots from general elections for governor.

> Both the expansion and the backlash are real.

Anyone who tells you this reform is inevitable is selling something. And anyone who tells you it's dying should have to explain why the places that actually use it keep voting to keep it.

Let's test how well you hold both halves. Someone tells you that the courts have generally struck ranked choice voting down as unconstitutional. Think about what I just said of Maine's court. Is that what happened?

[long pause]

No. And it is the near-miss worth remembering. Federal and state courts upheld ranked choice voting, in Maine and in Alaska.

Maine's problem is narrower. It is a specific clause in the state's own constitution, a clause about plurality. That clause limits where ranked choice voting applies. It did not strike it down.

## What I owe you

One promise before we start.

I am an advocate. This book has a thesis, and the platform that carries it rightly flags it as contested. So here is the deal I'll keep with you in every chapter. It has three parts.

Real numbers, from official sources. Named opponents, quoted at their strongest. And a concession wherever the other side has a point.

Because they sometimes do. And you'll trust nothing else I say if I pretend otherwise.

> My deal with you: a concession wherever the other side has a point.

Here is what that looks like in practice. Alaska's 2022 count passed over the candidate who would have beaten either rival head-to-head. I'll show you the math. And when a city repeals the reform, I'll tell you why.

[pause]

The chapters ahead move from problem to proof to playbook.

First, the diagnosis: what pick-one voting actually measures, and the spoiler-and-extremism machine it builds. The formal name for pick-one is plurality voting. Then the mechanics of the fix, round by round.

Then the evidence. Alaska. Maine. Two decades of American cities. A century abroad. Along with it come the opposition's best arguments, and the academic data. And finally, what it takes to bring ranked choice voting to your state.

Before we gather this up, reach back to the start. I gave you two numbers from Florida: a margin, and a third candidate's vote. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The margin was 537. The Nader vote was 97,488. And the ballots could not say what those voters wanted instead, because a pick-one ballot records one mark and nothing more.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A pick-one ballot asks one question and discards everything else you know. In Florida, in 2000, the ballot never asked.

[beat]

Two. This book's first claim: blame the system, not the voter. The friend who won't waste a vote is being rational. The ballot made it so.

[beat]

Three. The fix this book argues for: instead of picking one, you rank them. If your favorite can't win, your ballot counts for your next choice. For a single office, that is the instant runoff.

[beat]

Four. Both the expansion and the backlash are real. FairVote counts 49 jurisdictions. Nineteen states have passed bans.

[beat]

And five. My deal with you: real numbers, opponents at their strongest, and a concession wherever the other side has a point.

[pause]

Here is what this introduction has not settled. [beat] Everything.

It asserted. The rest of the book shows receipts. Whether ranking really repairs what pick-one breaks is contested, and nothing you have heard so far proves it.

So before the proof begins, try something. The pick-one ballot, or the ranked one. The case for one of them probably sounds weaker to you right now. Take that one. What is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case with you. If I keep my deal, you will hear it again, from me.

We start, in the next chapter, where the damage is clearest. With the arithmetic of the one-vote ballot. And with the strange American habit of electing leaders most voters voted against.

[pause]

For now, go back to the booth. One person. One mark. And everything else you know, discarded.

[beat]

The ballot asked you one question. This book is about what it never asked.
