On election night in November 2010, Maine elected a governor with 37.6 percent of the vote.

[beat]

Paul LePage finished fewer than 10,000 votes ahead of an independent, Eliot Cutler. A Democrat, Libby Mitchell, took 18.8 percent. Two more independents split the remainder.

More than three of every five Maine voters had marked someone else.

It wasn't an upset, and it wasn't a fluke. It was the system Maine had been living under for decades. This time the result was polarizing enough that people stopped shrugging at it.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was Alaska, the first state to run its entire general election on ranked ballots. But Alaska was not the first state to adopt them. Maine was, and Maine got there the hard way.

If Alaska is the proof of concept, Maine is the proof of persistence.

We will take the story in four steps. First, the problem with Maine's governors. Second, the vote that adopted ranked choice voting, and the trap in the state constitution. Third, how the legislature tried to take the win back, and how the voters answered. And last, the congressional race that tested it, and the ceiling that still holds in 2026.

## The governor problem

LePage was the catalyst. The pattern was older than him.

From 1974 through 2014, Maine held eleven races for governor. In nine of them, the winner failed to reach a majority. In five, the winner didn't reach 40 percent.

Maine has a healthy independent streak. The state elects unaffiliated governors and senators with regularity. And that streak collided with a plurality rule, a rule built for two-candidate races, where whoever has the most votes wins. The collision produced governor after governor a majority had voted against.

> In nine of eleven races, Maine's governor won without a majority.

Now stand where a Cutler voter stood in 2014. LePage was up for reelection. A Democrat, Mike Michaud, was running, and so was Cutler, again. Vote your conscience, and risk reelecting the governor you opposed. Or abandon your favorite, and vote your fears.

LePage won again, with 48.2 percent. A stronger showing. Still not a majority.

I'm not interested in relitigating LePage, who governed exactly as 37.6 percent of the state hoped he would. I'm interested in the question his elections forced. Why does Maine's ballot keep making Maine's voters choose between honesty and effectiveness?

## Question 5, and the trap

The answer came from outside the political establishment. A volunteer coalition, led by the League of Women Voters of Maine, gathered signatures town by town, to put the question directly to voters.

On November 8, 2016, Question 5 passed, with 52.12 percent. Maine had become the first state in American history to adopt ranked choice voting. It covered primaries, the legislature, the governor, and the congressional delegation.

Fifty-two percent is not a landslide, and I won't dress it up as one. What matters is what happened over the next nineteen months, when nearly every institution in the state tried to take the win back.

[pause]

Start with the constitution, which contains a trap laid in the nineteenth century. After a string of contested races, Maine amended its constitution to elect governors, and later legislators, "by a plurality of all votes returned."

Hear the irony in that. Those clauses were written to end majority requirements, which in the 1800s had thrown deadlocked elections into the legislature. A century and a half later, they became the weapon against the majority's return.

In February 2017, the State Senate asked the Supreme Judicial Court for an advisory opinion. In May, the justices answered, unanimously. Ranked choice voting conflicts with the plurality clauses. [beat] But only where those clauses reach.

That scope is the part everyone gets wrong, so let me be exact. The opinion covered general elections, for three offices: governor, state senator, and state representative.

Primaries are not mentioned in those clauses. And federal offices, the U.S. House and the U.S. Senate, are governed by the U.S. Constitution, not by Maine's plurality language. So the law remained fully constitutional for primaries, and for congressional general elections.

> The plurality clauses reach three offices, in general elections, and nothing else.

An advisory opinion binds no one. But it told the legislature exactly where the law stood.

## The betrayal, and the people's veto

What the legislature did next is why this chapter has the name it has.

In October 2017, lawmakers met in special session. They could have fixed the constitutional conflict. They could have put the rest of the law into effect. They did neither. They passed a delay.

Ranked choice voting would be suspended until December 2021. Then it would be automatically repealed, in its entirety, unless a constitutional amendment passed by then. That amendment needed two-thirds of both chambers, and everyone understood it would never come.

The politicians had found a way to repeal a citizen initiative without ever casting a vote labeled "repeal."

[pause]

Maine's constitution offered the voters one tool. It is called the people's veto: a referendum to strike down an act of the legislature.

It required more than 61,000 valid signatures, in roughly ninety days. That is a Maine winter's worth of clipboards, outside hockey rinks and grocery stores. The campaign delivered over 66,000.

And on June 12, 2018, the people's veto passed, with about 54 percent. The delayed repeal was itself repealed.

Note the score. The voters had now approved ranked choice voting twice in nineteen months, the second time specifically to overrule their own legislature.

> Maine's voters approved ranked choice voting twice in nineteen months.

The timing carried its own poetry. On that very same June ballot, Maine ran the first statewide ranked-choice elections in American history. In a seven-way Democratic primary for governor, Janet Mills won the final round with 54.1 percent. The sky did not fall. Mills went on to win the governor's office that fall.

Let's test the boundary. It is after June 2018, and a Mainer votes in three elections. A primary for governor. The November election for governor. And the November election for U.S. Senate. Which of the three are counted by ranked ballot? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The primary and the Senate race are ranked. The November race for governor is not.

One sentence explains the pattern. Ranked choice voting applies everywhere except where the plurality clauses reach, and they reach general elections for governor and the state legislature. The line isn't a judgment about which races deserve ranking. It is the exact boundary of three sentences written in the nineteenth century.

## The race that proved it

Five months later came the first ranked-choice congressional election in American history. It was in Maine's sprawling second district, and it could not have been scripted better, or uglier.

The incumbent was a Republican, Bruce Poliquin. On first choices he led the Democrat, Jared Golden, by 2,171 votes. But he had about 46 percent, short of a majority, and two independents held the balance.

When those two were eliminated, their voters' backup rankings broke roughly two to one for Golden. Roughly eight thousand ballots ranked neither finalist, and dropped out of the count.

Golden won the final round by 3,509 votes.

[beat]

Poliquin had led the first count and lost the election. He lost it legitimately, by the counting rule Maine's voters had twice enacted, because more voters preferred Golden to him than the reverse.

So here is a question. Poliquin led the first round and lost the last. What had to be true for that to happen?

[long pause]

The only ballots that moved belonged to the two independents' voters. Each one moved to that voter's own next choice. And among those who ranked a finalist, substantially more ranked Golden above Poliquin.

No official reassigned a ballot. No ballot was counted twice. And Golden was not the first choice of a majority. His majority was assembled in the final round, which is precisely what the method is for.

> Golden's majority was assembled in the final round. That is what the method is for.

Poliquin sued in federal court, in the middle of the count. He argued that ranked choice voting violated the Constitution's first article, equal protection, due process, the First Amendment, and the Voting Rights Act. The case is called Baber v. Dunlap.

The judge was Lance Walker, a Trump appointee, for whatever that's worth. He rejected every claim. Ranked ballots treat every voter alike, he ruled, and one person, one vote is not offended when each ballot counts once per round, for one candidate.

Poliquin sought a recount, then abandoned it. Golden took the seat.

[pause]

One honest postscript. In November 2020, Maine became the first state ever to elect presidential electors by ranked ballot. And no Maine race that year needed a second round. Joe Biden cleared a majority statewide, and Susan Collins won her Senate race outright.

Opponents called that proof the system was pointless. I'd call it proof of what the system actually is.

> Ranked choice voting is an insurance policy. It speaks only when the field fractures.

When a candidate wins a true majority, it changes nothing at all. And in Maine, history says, the field fractures in about every other election.

## Still unfinished

This story has no tidy ending, because it's still being written.

Before we get to 2026, pull something back. Earlier I gave you the three offices the plurality clauses reach, in general elections. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Governor, state senator, and state representative.

In 2026, reform legislators tried again to reach them. Their bill would have extended ranked choice voting to those general elections, by redefining the count, so that "plurality" is measured at the final round.

The Supreme Judicial Court heard arguments in April. Days later, it advised that there are strong and convincing reasons the bill would be unconstitutional. The plurality clauses, the justices said, mean the count town clerks return, not the count after transfers.

The justices have now said the same thing twice, nine years apart. And they are probably right about the text.

The fix is the one available since 2017: a constitutional amendment. That takes two-thirds of each chamber, plus a majority of voters. Maine's voters have already shown they'll supply their half.

> The supermajority in Augusta is the wall.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The governor problem. In nine of eleven races, Maine's governor won without a majority, and in 2010 LePage won with 37.6 percent.

[beat]

Two. In 2016, with 52 percent, Maine became the first state to adopt ranked choice voting.

[beat]

Three. The trap. The plurality clauses reach three offices, in general elections, and nothing else. Primaries and federal races are ranked. The November race for governor is not.

[beat]

Four. The legislature passed a repeal dressed as a delay, and the people's veto struck it down. Maine's voters approved ranked choice voting twice in nineteen months.

[beat]

And five. In 2018, Poliquin led the first round and lost the last, and a federal court rejected every claim against the count. Golden's majority was assembled in the final round.

[pause]

Here is what Maine settles. A state can adopt ranked choice voting by initiative, defend it against its own political class, and run it for eight years without confusion or crisis. And voters who use the system keep choosing it.

Here is what it doesn't settle. Whether ranked ballots can ever reach the governor's race that started it all. That answer is written in a nineteenth-century clause, awaiting a twenty-first-century amendment. And whether any of this changes how campaigns behave, and how voters feel.

For that, the laboratories are the cities, and they've been running the experiment for twenty years. The next chapter goes to San Francisco, Minneapolis, and a very loud Tuesday in New York.

[pause]

For now, go back to election night in 2010, and a governor chosen by 37.6 percent.

Maine's voters have said yes to ranked ballots twice.

[beat]

The race that started the fight is the one those ballots still cannot reach.
