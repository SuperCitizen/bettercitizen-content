Two numbers, from the same election.

In June 2021, New York City ran the largest ranked-choice election in American history. Afterward, 95 percent of voters told exit pollsters the ballot was simple to complete.

[beat]

In that same election, about 140,000 ballots ranked neither of the two finalists for mayor. They sat inactive in the final round. That was roughly 15 percent.

[pause]

Advocates quote the first number. Opponents quote the second. Both are real. And a book that shows you only one of them is not a book of evidence. It's a brochure.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter gave the opposition its best arguments. This one stops arguing and starts counting.

So here is the full ledger, as the research stands. What is strong. What is modest. And what cuts against me. I think the case for ranked choice voting is strong enough to survive its own footnotes, and you can judge that for yourself.

We will take it in four steps. First, turnout. Second, civility. Third, representation. And last, the costs, measured honestly.

## Turnout

Start with the claim you have probably heard in its inflated form: ranked choice voting increases turnout. The careful version is less exciting.

David Kimball and Joseph Anthony compared ranked-choice cities with plurality cities of similar demographics. They found no statistically significant change in general-election turnout.

That is a null result. It deserves to be stated plainly.

[pause]

Now the findings layered on top of it. There are three.

Eamon McGinn studied the Twin Cities area of Minnesota, where cities adopted ranked choice voting at different times. He estimated a turnout increase of 9.6 percentage points in mayoral elections there. The increase was largest in higher-poverty precincts.

Courtney Juelich and Joseph Coll matched individual voter records, across ranked-choice and other local elections. For the general public, they found no difference. But young voters were about 9 points more likely to vote in the ranked-choice cities.

And in 2024, four researchers, Dowling, Tolbert, Micatka and Donovan, published the mechanism. Voters in ranked-choice cities report significantly more campaign contact, and more in-person outreach from candidates. The reason is not hard to see. A candidate who needs your second choice has a reason to knock on your door, even when you're wearing a rival's button.

My honest summary is this. Direct turnout effects are modest, and they depend on context. Positive in some designs. Absent in others. Concentrated among the young, and among voters campaigns rarely reach.

> Direct turnout effects are modest and mixed.

[pause]

The structural effect is a different story. It is the one I will defend without hedging.

Sometimes ranked choice voting replaces a separate runoff election. Where it does, the turnout gain is mechanical, and it is large.

FairVote examined every regularly scheduled federal primary runoff from 1994 to 2022. There were 276 of them. Turnout fell between the first round and the second in 266. That is 96 percent. And the median decline was 40 percent.

An instant runoff works differently. It asks for everyone's backup preference while everyone is still in the room. You don't need a regression to see which method counts more voters. You need subtraction.

> Where ranked choice replaces a separate runoff, the turnout gain is large.

Let's test that. A campaign flyer says, "Studies show RCV boosts turnout." A skeptic's op-ed says, "Studies show RCV does nothing for turnout." What would an honest one-sentence summary say? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Both are cherry-picking the same literature. The direct effects on general-election turnout are modest and mixed: null overall in the matched-city comparison, positive in specific settings. The large, undisputed gain comes when ranked choice voting replaces low-turnout runoffs.

The defensible claim is structural, not magical.

## Civility

Now the claim that voters volunteer first: that ranked-choice campaigns are more civil. Political scientists went looking.

Todd Donovan, Caroline Tolbert and Kellen Gracey surveyed voters in ranked-choice cities, and in matched plurality cities holding local elections. Voters in the ranked-choice cities were significantly more satisfied with the conduct of campaigns. They were also less likely to perceive those campaigns as negative.

Candidates themselves reported subtle differences. More appeals for second-choice support. More cross-endorsements.

[pause]

Then, in 2023, Donovan and Tolbert went back to the question with new data, including New York's mayoral primary of 2021. Their paper has a subtitle that should be framed on every advocate's wall. "Does Evidence Fit the Normative Narrative?"

Their answer: only partly.

[beat]

Voters do perceive less negativity. About a third report candidates explicitly asking for a second-place or third-place ranking. But the positive tone did not extend to the frontrunners. The candidates with the most at stake campaign about as hard either way.

So the incentive is real. But it is a thumb on the scale, not a personality transplant for American politics.

> The civility effect is real, and it is bounded.

That is what I will claim, because that is what the data support.

## Representation

Third, representation: who runs, and who wins. Here the numbers are striking, and they come with a caveat I will flag myself.

A group called RepresentWomen tracked a decade of municipal ranked-choice elections, from 2010 to 2019. Women won 48 percent of them.

As of its 2020 report, women held 46 percent of the mayor's offices elected by ranked choice. Among comparable mayor's offices nationally, the share was roughly a quarter.

And New York's first ranked-choice council election, in 2021, produced the first majority-women city council in the city's history.

[pause]

Then comes the study that matters most here. In 2025, Jonathan Colner published it in the American Journal of Political Science. He found a mechanism upstream of the results.

Ranked choice voting increases candidate entry. More people run, particularly women and candidates of color. Why? Because a crowded field no longer means mutually assured destruction by vote-splitting. That fits the round-by-round data, which show candidates of color gaining, not losing, as ballots transfer.

Now the caveat. Cities that adopt ranked choice voting are not random cities. They are disproportionately places already trending toward diverse candidate pools. And the observational studies cannot fully untangle that.

So let me rank my own evidence. The research on candidate entry is the strongest causal evidence we have. The raw win percentages are suggestive. They are not proof.

> The entry research is the strongest evidence. The win percentages are suggestive, not proof.

I'd rather tell you that than have an opponent tell you I didn't.

Here is a question to check it. Someone says ranked choice voting guarantees more diverse winners in any city that adopts it. Does the research support that? Think it through.

[long pause]

No. That overclaims. The cleanest finding is about entry: with vote-splitting defanged, similar candidates can run without destroying each other. The win rates are consistent with that mechanism. But they are observational, because cities that choose ranked choice voting differ from cities that don't.

## The costs, measured honestly

Two costs show up consistently in the data. You met both in the last chapter, as the opposition's arguments.

The first is ballot errors. Pettigrew and Radley, the researchers behind the error figures, put the rejection rate for ranked contests at 0.53 percent. For pick-one contests on the same ballots, it is 0.04 percent. That is about ten times higher. And the gap is concentrated in precincts with lower incomes and less education.

The mitigations are real, but partial. Cormack's follow-up study, in 2026, found New York's error rates falling with each ranked-choice cycle. And about 89 percent of the city's Democratic primary voters in 2021 successfully ranked more than one candidate in at least one race.

[pause]

The second cost is exhausted ballots: ballots that drop out of the count before the final round. The standard dataset comes from Burnett and Kogan, who analyzed more than 600,000 ballot images from four elections.

Here is the one thing their figures show. In three of the four, about a tenth of first-round ballots were exhausted before the final round. In the fourth, San Francisco in 2011, it was 27.1 percent.

And in all four, the winner finished with less than half of the total votes cast. That is the factual core of the "faux majority" charge. I won't dispute the arithmetic.

What I dispute is the baseline.

Under plurality rules, those same winners would have won with the same pluralities. And no losing voter would have had any say between the finalists. Under a traditional runoff, a median 40 percent of voters don't return.

Every system loses voters somewhere. Ranked choice voting is the only one that prints the loss in the tabulation report, where critics can quote it.

> My reply: exhaustion is the visible version of a loss every system imposes.

There is also a design lever, and it works. All four of those elections limited voters to three rankings. San Francisco squeezed a sixteen-candidate field into three slots. Where rankings are uncapped, or capped at five, exhaustion falls. Alaska's special election of August 2022 carried 94 percent of ballots into the final round.

## The scoreboard

So here is my scoreboard: six claims, and my verdict on each. Listen for how different the verdicts are.

Replacing low-turnout runoffs with one high-turnout decision. Strong.

Majority-backed winners, among the ballots still in the count. Strong, with a real asterisk.

More candidate entry, and more women and candidates of color running and winning. Promising, and partly observational.

Less negative campaigning. Modest and real, not transformative.

A direct turnout boost. Mixed, and dependent on context.

And last: can voters handle it? Yes, with an equity obligation.

[pause]

Those are my verdicts, not a referee's. A reader could weigh the same studies and lean harder on the costs. So, something to try. This ledger can be summed up two ways: that the costs are small and fixable, or that the benefits are smaller than promised. Take the one you find less convincing. What is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case. Here is my own reading. Every cost of ranked choice voting is measurable, bounded, and shrinkable by design choices. The costs of the status quo are structural and permanent: spoiled elections, December runoffs nobody attends, fields cleared by fear of vote-splitting.

One more question before we gather up. I opened with two numbers from one New York election. Can you still say what each one measured?

[long pause]

The first: 95 percent of voters called the ballot simple to complete. The second: about 15 percent of ballots ranked neither finalist, and were inactive in the final round. Both are real.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Direct turnout effects are modest and mixed. The matched-city comparison found no significant change.

[beat]

Two. Where ranked choice replaces a separate runoff, the turnout gain is large. Turnout fell in 96 percent of the federal primary runoffs studied.

[beat]

Three. The civility effect is real, and it is bounded. Voters perceive less negativity, but frontrunners campaign about as hard either way.

[beat]

Four. On representation, the entry research is the strongest evidence. The win percentages are suggestive, not proof.

[beat]

And five. The costs. Ranked contests reject about ten times as many votes, 0.53 percent, and New York's error rates are falling with experience. Exhausted ballots are real, and they fall when voters get more rankings.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. It has not settled whether these American findings hold at scale, and over decades.

For that, we leave the country. Australia has been ranking ballots since 1918. And Ireland's voters have twice refused to give them up. That evidence is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to those two numbers from New York.

Ninety-five percent found the ballot simple. Fifteen percent of ballots had no say at the end.

[beat]

Evidence is what you have when you are willing to quote both.
