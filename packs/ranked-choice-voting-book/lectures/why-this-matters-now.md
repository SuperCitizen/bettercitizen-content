Here is a strange thing to do to an idea you expect to fail.

[beat]

Pass a law against it. Then do that nineteen times.

On March 17, 2026, Governor Mike DeWine signed Ohio Senate Bill 63, without a statement. The law doesn't just decline to adopt ranked choice voting. It forbids Ohio's cities from trying it. Any city that does forfeits its state local-government funding.

Ohio was the nineteenth state to enact a ban, and the second in three months.

[pause]

Stay with the strangeness of that. Nobody passes nineteen laws against a thing that's failing on its own. You don't prohibit, with financial penalties, an idea you expect voters to reject anyway.

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter laid out the path to fifty states, and ended on a question of speed. This chapter answers a different question from the rest of the book. Not why at all. [beat] Why now.

We will take it in four steps. First, two measurements of how Americans feel about their politics. Second, what has happened to the general election. Third, the bans. And last, my argument about windows, with the two strongest positions against it.

## The stress readings

Every era thinks itself uniquely divided. So let's use instruments, not vibes.

Pew Research Center has tracked hostility between the parties for decades, and its 2022 readings were the worst in the series. 72 percent of Republicans said Democrats were more immoral than other Americans. 63 percent of Democrats said the same of Republicans.

Six years earlier, the Republican figure had been 47 percent. The Democratic figure had been 35.

[pause]

Note the word. Not wrong. Not misguided. [beat] Immoral.

> Calling the other side immoral measures contempt, not disagreement.

Now the second reading, the one that makes the first one something we can act on. Americans are not loyal partisans gleefully waging this war.

In Pew's survey of October 2025, 31 percent said both parties are too extreme. And 26 percent said neither party governs honestly and ethically.

Put the two readings side by side. Contempt for the other party, and alienation from both of them, at the same time. A political system producing both at once is not expressing what its citizens want.

Something in the machine is manufacturing this.

## The disappearing general election

I've argued throughout this book that the machine's worst gear is a pipeline: the party primary, feeding a pick-one election. This is where I show you its current dimensions.

Before I do, commit to a guess. Take every race for the U.S. House in 2024. What share of them were effectively decided in the primary, not in November?

[long pause]

87 percent. That is the finding of an analysis by Unite America.

And what share of Americans voted in those decisive primaries? [beat] About 7 percent.

If you guessed high on the first and low on the second, you've been paying attention. Most people get the direction right and the size wrong.

One more detail from that analysis. In 169 districts, the decisive primary ballot had one candidate on it.

[pause]

Now a second instrument. The Cook Political Report counts the House districts where the two parties are within five points of each other. In 1999, there were 164. Now there are 82. [beat] Exactly half.

And Cook's own analysis undercuts the comfortable villain. Most of that decline came from voters sorting themselves, not from gerrymandered lines.

Let's test that. A reformer says: "Fix gerrymandering and competition returns." Using what Cook found, why would that fix, alone, disappoint? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because the lines were the smaller part of the problem. Cook found that about 81 percent of the collapse in swing seats came from realignment: voters sorting, geographically, into like-minded places. Only about 19 percent came from redrawn lines.

Independent commissions can stop mapmakers from making it worse. They can't unsort the country. A fairly drawn map of a deep-red or deep-blue region still yields safe seats, and a safe seat's only real election is the primary.

That is why I argue for reforms that change who wins safe seats, and how. Primary reform and ballot reform attack a layer that redistricting can't reach.

So here is the general election, as it stands.

> For most Americans, in most races, the general election is a formality.

The real election is a tiny, unrepresentative primary they don't vote in. The machine manufacturing contempt has an address.

## The status quo is armed

Now back to the bans, because the timeline of mid-2026 deserves to be seen whole.

Nineteen states have now prohibited ranked choice voting. By Ballotpedia's count, seventeen of them were under unified Republican control when they did it. Kansas and Kentucky enacted theirs under divided government.

Indiana opened 2026 with a ban, in February. Ohio followed in March, with the funding penalty attached.

Then Utah. For a decade, Utah ran a pilot that let its cities rank their ballots. It was bipartisan, and locally beloved in use. The Sightline Institute's review credits it with cutting the participating cities' election costs by roughly 40 percent.

That pilot was allowed to expire on January 1, 2026. Participation had withered, from 23 cities, to 12, to 4, as the national party climate turned.

A former chair of the Utah Republican Party, Stan Lockhart, gave the honest epitaph. Once Donald Trump blamed ranked choice voting for Sarah Palin's loss in 2022, he said, "that was the end of ranked choice voting in Utah."

[pause]

In April 2026, Trump himself called for Alaskans to repeal their system, at the November election. That is round three. And in the same month, Maine's high court reaffirmed that the state constitution's plurality clause walls off any expansion to state general elections.

I want to be fair about what the ban wave is not. It is not evidence that ranked choice voting fails voters. Utah's cities and Alaska's electorate kept choosing it when asked.

The honest description is that one party's national apparatus has classified this reform as a threat. And the other party's establishment is no friend either. It was D.C.'s Democratic Party that sued to keep a ranked choice initiative from its voters. Both parties fought the 2024 measures.

The two parties disagree about nearly everything, except the rules that keep them the only two.

[pause]

Here is a question about inference. Nineteen states have banned ranked choice voting, and in most of them no jurisdiction was even using it. What is the most defensible thing to conclude from that? Think it through.

[long pause]

Not that voters in those states examined the reform and rejected it. That confuses legislatures with electorates. Where voters who actually use ranked ballots have been asked, they've kept them.

And not that the bans are empty symbolism. Ohio's carries a funding penalty.

The defensible inference is this. Political insiders perceive the reform as a real threat to the system that elected them, and they are foreclosing it ahead of time. Most bans stop cities that had never adopted it. The wave followed Alaska's 2022 results, and it tracks the positions the parties took.

One caution. The bans do not prove that ranked choice voting is good.

> The bans show one thing: the status quo is actively defending itself.

## Windows open, and windows shut

American electoral reform doesn't trickle. It comes in floods, when three things line up: public disgust, organized reformers, and a usable mechanism.

The Progressive Era stacked up the secret ballot, the direct primary, the citizen initiative, and the direct election of senators, in roughly one generation. Watergate produced the campaign-finance overhaul of 1974, within two years of the break-in.

Between floods, the channel silts up. The same proposals that passed in bunches become "unrealistic" for decades.

[pause]

Now my claim, and I label it as argument, not data. I believe we are inside such a window now, opened by exactly the disgust Pew keeps measuring. And I believe the ban wave is what the closing of a window looks like in real time.

Here is the reasoning. Adoption is slow by design. An initiative campaign needs signatures, then a majority, sometimes two majorities, then implementation. Nevada's double-passage rule erased its 2022 win in 2024.

A ban needs one legislative session.

That asymmetry matters. Every past win grew from city pilots and local options, from the capacity to experiment. And that capacity can be stripped faster than it can be used. Nineteen states' worth of laboratories are now shut.

> The reform can still win the country. It cannot win it at leisure.

[pause]

That is my inference, and it is contested. So here are three positions, each at full strength. The first is mine.

The reform advocate wants to protect a chance that may not come again. The argument: ranked choice voting is the most tested, most incremental fix available. Same offices, same Tuesday, one change to the ballot. For evidence, the advocate points to this chapter's measurements, and to two states and dozens of cities as working proof.

> The reform advocate's case: windows shut, this one is visibly closing, move now.

[pause]

The status-quo defender wants to protect a method that is simple, fast and familiar, and the right of states to set their own election rules. The argument: ranked tabulation adds steps where trust is already fragile. And exhausted ballots mean some voters' choices stop counting before the final round.

For evidence, the defender points to 2024. Those results are not a conspiracy. Five statewide electorates heard a year of argument and said no, by margins of up to 39 points. Legislatures banning a method they distrust are doing their constitutional job.

> The status-quo defender's case: a good reform can win by persuasion, without urgency.

[pause]

The alternative-reform advocate wants to protect the window itself, for a deeper fix. The political scientist Lee Drutman was once among ranked choice voting's prominent boosters. He now argues that the single-winner version delivers modest gains at best, and that the deeper disease is single-winner districts themselves.

For evidence, this position points to exhausted ballots: around 10 percent in some elections, disproportionately in lower-income precincts. If a window is truly open and truly rare, spending it on instant runoffs may crowd out proportional representation or fusion voting.

> The alternative-reform case: urgency is real, but this may be the wrong cargo.

[pause]

Three positions. Take the one you find least convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

One question before we gather up. Near the start, I gave you a single word from the Pew survey, the word that turns disagreement into contempt. Do you still have it?

[long pause]

Immoral.

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The stress readings. Majorities of each party call the other side more immoral than other Americans. And 31 percent of Americans say both parties are too extreme. Contempt and alienation, together.

[beat]

Two. The disappearing general election. 87 percent of House races were effectively decided in primaries where about 7 percent of Americans voted. And most of the lost competition came from voters sorting, not from the lines.

[beat]

Three. Nineteen bans. They are not evidence that the reform fails voters. They show one thing: the status quo is actively defending itself.

[beat]

Four. My argument. Reform comes in floods, a ban takes one session, and the reform cannot win the country at leisure.

[beat]

And five. Two answers to me. Win by persuasion, without urgency. Or spend the window on a deeper reform.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it doesn't. The measurements are settled: the Pew series, the swing-seat count, the primary arithmetic, the nineteen bans. What is contested is the inference I draw from them: that this is a closing window, demanding action on this reform, now. The alternatives are patience, or a different reform altogether.

What participation concretely looks like is the final chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to Ohio, and a governor signing a ban without a statement.

The status quo is not neutral ground that will wait politely while we deliberate. It is organized, funded, and legislating.

Whatever you decide about ranked choice voting, decide it as a participant.

[beat]

The deciding is happening either way.
