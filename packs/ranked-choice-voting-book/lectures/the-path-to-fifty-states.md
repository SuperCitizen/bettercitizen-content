The night of November 5, 2024. Five states are voting on whether to adopt ranked choice voting.

[beat]

In about six hours, the movement loses all five.

If this book were a sales brochure, this chapter would skip that night. It isn't one.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter went to the courts, and it ended on a plain conclusion: the fight over this reform is political. This chapter is about how that fight is won, state by state.

And the path to fifty states runs directly through an honest autopsy of that night. Because the same night also held, in plain sight, the evidence for how this reform actually spreads.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two doors into a state. Second, the history the movement is built on: cities first. Third, the autopsy of 2024. And last, the federal lever, and the path as I see it.

## Two doors into a state

There are exactly two ways a state adopts a new election system. Its people impose it, or its legislature passes it.

The first door is the ballot initiative: citizens with petitions, putting the question to the voters themselves. And that door only exists in some buildings. 26 states provide for a statewide initiative or referendum process.

Every statewide ranked choice victory so far has come through that door. Maine in 2016. Alaska in 2020. Both by citizen initiative, and both over the objection of the political class.

That's not a coincidence. Asking legislators to change the rules that elected them is asking the house to vote against the house. Citizens with petitions don't need permission.

> Every statewide win so far came through the citizen initiative.

The second door is the legislature. It opens rarely, and usually only partway. A pilot program here. A local option there. Ranked ballots for a narrow class of voters.

But partway matters, as you'll hear. And in roughly half the country, the legislature is the only door there is. So strategy in an initiative state and strategy in a state without one are different sports.

[pause]

And some doors are now barred from the inside. As of mid-2026, nineteen states have banned ranked choice voting by statute. In those states, the first task is repeal.

So the map of the possible has three colors.

> Three colors on the map: initiative states, legislature-only states, and ban states.

Anyone who tells you there's one national playbook hasn't looked at the map.

## The beachhead model

That is the map. Now the history the movement is built on. I'll give it with dates, because the dates are the argument.

San Francisco passed a measure called Proposition A in March 2002, 55 to 45. In November 2004, it ran the country's first big-city instant-runoff election.

[beat]

Nothing melted. Neighboring Bay Area cities followed.

Minneapolis voters adopted ranked ballots, and first used them in 2009, after the Minnesota Supreme Court waved the system through.

Maine became the first state, in 2016. And in Maine, the city of Portland had already been ranking its ballots for mayor.

New York City, the largest electorate of any American city, adopted ranked ballots in 2019, with 73.5 percent of the vote.

And Alaska went furthest, in 2020, ranking its entire general election.

At every step, the unanswerable argument was the beachhead next door. They did it. It works. Go watch.

[pause]

Let's test that. Why would a movement with national ambitions spend twenty years on city ordinances, when it could go straight for statewide ballot measures? What does a city win create that a white paper can't? Take a moment.

[long pause]

A working example, next door to the next electorate.

Statewide campaigns are won or lost on whether voters believe the thing works in practice. And the only unanswerable proof is a neighboring jurisdiction running it without drama. San Francisco for the Bay Area. Portland for Maine. Takoma Park for Washington, D.C.

A city win builds the other assets a campaign needs, too: trained volunteers, election officials with experience, and local politicians who won under the system and will say so.

Here is the model's logic in one line.

> Cities are where voters meet the reform before being asked to marry it.

People who have used ranked ballots stop finding them scary. And as 2024 is about to show, that is the whole ballgame.

## 2024: the honest accounting

Now the autopsy. Five statewide measures to adopt ranked choice voting lost in November 2024, and most of them were bundled with open primaries. The margins deserve to be stated plainly, not mumbled.

Idaho's Proposition 1 fell hardest, with 30.4 percent voting yes. Arizona's Proposition 140 took 41.3 percent. Oregon's Measure 117 took 42.3, and it was the only one that was ranked choice voting alone, unbundled. Colorado's Proposition 131 reached 46.5.

And Nevada's Question 3 had passed in 2022. Nevada's constitution required a second vote. In 2024 it failed that second vote, with 46.9 percent.

[pause]

Montana ran the movement's most informative experiment. It split its package into two measures on the same ballot. The open-primary half drew 48.9 percent. The majority-winner half drew 39.6.

Same electorate. Same night. Nine points apart.

So what does that gap most directly measure? Think it through.

[long pause]

It measures the difference in support between the two reforms themselves. Both questions went before the same voters, on the same night, amid the same campaign. Nearly everything except the content of the proposals was held constant. That is a natural experiment.

And the gap suggests that in 2024 the majority-winner mechanism, not primary reform, carried the heavier burden of persuasion. One caution: this is one state. It does not show that open primaries always poll better.

[pause]

What did the post-mortems conclude? Three things, consistently.

First, bundling hurt. Most of these measures asked voters to swallow open primaries and ranked general elections in one gulp. Montana's split result is as clean a measurement of that penalty as we will ever get.

[beat]

Second, complexity messaging worked. Opponents didn't have to win an argument that the reform was bad. They only had to plant doubt that it was confusing. And "if you're confused, vote no" is the oldest play in ballot-measure politics.

[beat]

Third, the two major parties closed ranks. Nick Troiano, of the group Unite America, said afterward that the initiatives were "largely swept up in a highly polarized climate." He described both parties and "their aligned special interests fighting tooth and nail against these initiatives."

[pause]

The Sightline Institute's review added a strategic conclusion, and I accept it. Alaska's 2020 win tempted the movement to skip the city-by-city step, in states with no beachheads.

> Sightline's conclusion, which I accept: 2024 was the bill for skipping cities.

But now the same night's fine print.

Washington, D.C. adopted ranked choice voting, with about 73 percent. Bloomington, Minnesota voted to keep its system. Alaska's repeal failed. And inside Oregon's loss, Measure 117 won in Benton and Multnomah counties, the two places already using ranked ballots.

The pattern across every 2024 contest is almost embarrassingly legible.

> Electorates that had used the reform voted for it. Electorates asked sight-unseen voted no.

Let me say that once more, in plainer words. Where people knew the ranked ballot, they kept it or expanded it. Where it was a stranger, they turned it down.

## The federal lever

There is a third door I haven't mentioned, because it's locked. It is still worth describing, since the key exists.

Congress has power, under the Elections Clause, over the manner of congressional elections. In December 2025, four members of Congress reintroduced a bill called the Ranked Choice Voting Act. They were Senators Peter Welch and Andy Kim, and Representatives Jamie Raskin and Don Beyer. The bill would require ranked ballots in congressional primaries and general elections, beginning in 2028.

I'll be straight with you: that bill is not passing this Congress. Its function, for now, is standard-setting. It puts the destination on the federal map, and it gives members from ranked choice states something to co-sponsor.

[pause]

The quieter federal story is already running, and it's my favorite fact to hand skeptics.

Six states already send ranked ballots to their military and overseas voters. Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina. A soldier abroad can vote in a federal runoff without waiting on a second envelope that may never arrive in time.

Six deep-red states, using ranked ballots routinely, without controversy. Why? Because there, the reform solves an administrative problem everyone can see. Hawaii fills certain vacancies by ranked ballot for the same practical reason.

> As a tool, not a tribal symbol, Americans use ranked ballots without flinching.

## What "fifty states" actually means

Before the path itself, one question. Early on, I said the map of the possible has three colors. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Initiative states. Legislature-only states. And ban states. Hold on to them, because the work is different in each.

So here is the path as I see it from mid-2026, stripped of prophecy. Five tasks.

One. Defend the beachheads. Alaska votes on repeal again, in November 2026, and holding it matters more than any new acquisition.

[beat]

Two. Implement well where we've won. D.C.'s rollout, in 2026, is the next national demonstration.

[beat]

Three. Rebuild the city ladder in the states that skipped it. In 2025, Greenbelt, Maryland adopted ranked choice voting with 67 percent, and Skokie, Illinois did the same with 58. Wins like those are not consolation prizes. They are the supply chain of future statewide wins.

[beat]

Four. Unbundle where bundling kills, and draft around state constitutions, not into them.

[beat]

Five. Keep the federal standard alive, for the day the count of ranked choice states makes it thinkable.

[pause]

I won't insult you with a tidy timetable, three phases ending in inevitability. Movements that mistake their roadmap for reality get 2024.

And I know the history well enough not to claim that familiarity guarantees loyalty. Burlington, Vermont repealed its instant runoff in 2010, and re-adopted it a decade later.

What I'll claim is narrower, and grounded in the most recent evidence. In the 2024 cycle, every American electorate already living with ranked ballots, and asked about them, voted to keep or expand them. Alaska. Bloomington. The Oregon counties.

A reform that wins wherever it's known, and loses mainly where it's a stranger, has a marketing problem. It does not have a product problem.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Two doors into a state: the citizen initiative and the legislature. Every statewide win so far came through the citizen initiative. And nineteen states have barred the door, so the map has three colors.

[beat]

Two. The beachhead model. Cities are where voters meet the reform before being asked to marry it.

[beat]

Three. The honest accounting. Five statewide measures lost in 2024. The post-mortems named bundling, the confusion message, and both parties closing ranks. And 2024 was the bill for skipping the city-by-city step.

[beat]

Four. The fine print of the same night. Electorates that had used the reform voted for it. Electorates asked sight-unseen voted no.

[beat]

And five. The federal bill is a standard, not a law. But six states already send ranked ballots to their troops, as a tool, without flinching.
