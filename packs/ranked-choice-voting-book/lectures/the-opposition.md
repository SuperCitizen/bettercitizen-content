March 17, 2026. In Ohio, Governor Mike DeWine signs Senate Bill 63. He makes no public statement.

With that signature, Ohio became the nineteenth state to ban ranked choice voting.

[beat]

No Ohio city had asked for ranked ballots in state races. The law does something else. It forbids Ohio's own cities from using them, and it adds a financial threat. Any charter city that tries loses its share of the state's Local Government Fund.

Cincinnati and Lakewood had been exploring ranked choice voting. They are no longer allowed to.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Rank the Vote. It is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter went to the cities that rank their ballots, and it ended on a promise: the critics would get a full hearing. This chapter is that hearing.

A reform I believe in is being banned, in state after state, faster than it is being adopted. If you want to change that, you had better understand exactly who is doing the banning, and why the smartest of them think they're right.

We will take it in four steps. First, who opposes this. Second, their three best arguments, at full strength, with no reply from me. Third, my answers. And last, what the bans themselves tell you.

## Who opposes this

Three groups. And only one of them is the cartoon you may be expecting.

The first is the organized, institutional opposition. The Heritage Foundation fired the opening shot, in an issue brief in August 2019. Its author, Hans von Spakovsky, called ranked choice voting "a scheme to disconnect elections from issues and allow candidates with marginal support from voters to win elections."

The Honest Elections Project, led by Jason Snead, has made repealing and banning ranked choice voting a headline priority. It publishes model legislation, and annual reports that celebrate each new ban and declare that "the 2024 election proved that there is no public appetite for RCV."

This is a coordinated operation, think tanks and statehouses working together, and it has been strikingly effective. Seventeen of the nineteen ban states had Republican trifectas when they acted.

[pause]

The second group is party establishments. And here I mean both parties.

It was the D.C. Democratic Party that sued, twice, to keep ranked choice voting off Washington's ballot in 2024.

It was six members of the New York City Council who sued, in December 2020, to delay the rollout the city's voters had approved. All six belonged to the council's Black, Latino and Asian Caucus. They argued the city had not prepared minority and senior voters. Their own caucus split: other Black and Latino council members, and community leaders, publicly backed the system.

And it was Senator Tom Cotton who called Alaska's first ranked-choice count "a scam to rig elections."

Parties that have mastered one set of rules do not thank you for changing the rules.

> Resisting a rule change is an incumbent instinct, not a left-right one.

[beat]

The third group deserves the most respect. These are sincere critics, who share my goals and doubt my remedy.

Take the political scientist Lee Drutman. He wrote one of the most influential pro-reform books of the last decade. In 2023, he publicly updated his views. Single-winner ranked choice voting, he argued, delivers modest moderation at best, and reformers should spend their scarce energy on proportional representation.

Some progressives have a different worry. Council Member I. Daneek Miller voiced it in New York: that ranking dilutes the influence of cohesive minority voting blocs.

These critics are not defending a duopoly. They are asking whether I've picked the right tool.

Let's test the picture so far. Ohio's bill passed the House 65 to 27, and the Senate 24 to 7, with a Democratic co-sponsor. Yet seventeen of the nineteen ban states were Republican trifectas. So what is the honest way to describe this coalition? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The organized national campaign is overwhelmingly conservative. But the bans pick up establishment votes from both parties. Kansas and Kentucky enacted theirs under divided government. Party organizations of every stripe are threatened by rules that loosen their gatekeeping.

Pretending the opposition is purely partisan would be convenient for me. [beat] It would also be false.

## Their best arguments, at full strength

Now the opposition's three strongest arguments, the way their smartest advocates make them. If my case can't survive contact with the real thing, it isn't worth your time.

Argument one. Ranked ballots cause more voter errors, and the errors are not evenly distributed.

This is no longer speculation. It is measured. Stephen Pettigrew and Dylan Radley analyzed millions of ballots. They compared ranked-choice races with single-choice races on the same ballots. In the ranked races, votes were rejected at about ten times the rate.

They also found that nearly one in twenty ranked-choice voters mismarks the ballot in at least one way.

And there is worse. The mismarks concentrate in lower-income precincts, and in precincts with fewer college degrees. So the strongest version of this argument is a claim about equity. The mental tax of ranking falls heaviest on exactly the voters that reformers claim to champion.

> The error objection: ranking's cost falls heaviest on the voters reformers claim to champion.

[pause]

Argument two. The "majority winner" is sometimes an accounting fiction.

Craig Burnett and Vladimir Kogan examined more than 600,000 ballot images, from four early ranked-choice elections. They documented something called ballot exhaustion. An exhausted ballot ranks only candidates who have been eliminated. So it carries no preference into the final round.

In the lowest of the four elections, 9.6 percent. In the highest, 27.1 percent.

And in all four, the winner's majority was a majority of the final-round ballots, but less than half of all the votes cast. In each of them, the winner finished somewhere between 43 and 46 percent of the total.

In New York's mayoral primary of 2021, roughly 140,000 ballots ranked neither finalist, and were inactive at the end. That was about 15 percent of them.

Heritage calls the result a "faux majority." And on the raw arithmetic, the phrase has bite.

> The majority objection: the winner's majority is sometimes less than half of all votes.

[pause]

Argument three. It is energy spent on the wrong reform.

This is Drutman's critique, and it comes from inside the reform world. The political-science evidence that single-winner ranked choice voting moderates politics is thin. The two-party doom loop is a problem that needs proportional representation. And every dollar and volunteer hour spent on ranked choice voting is a dollar not spent on multi-member districts, which would actually fix it.

> The ally's objection: energy spent on ranked choice belongs to proportional representation.

[pause]

Errors. Faux majorities. The wrong reform.

Before I answer, something to try. One of those three probably convinces you least. Take that one. What is the best case you could make for it, in the words of the people who hold it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case in mind while I answer.

## My answers

I'll start with a question for you. A critic tells you ranked ballots are rejected at ten times the rate of pick-one ballots. What else do you need to know before you can weigh that? Think it through.

[long pause]

You need the size of both rates, and what voters get in exchange for the difference. A multiplier means nothing without the base rate.

So here are the rates. In the ranked races, 0.53 percent of votes were rejected. In the pick-one races, 0.04 percent. Put the other way round: 99.5 percent of ranked votes count, against 99.96 percent under pick-one rules.

That is a real difference, and I won't wave it away. But it has to be weighed against what ranked ballots buy.

> Ten times a very small number is still a very small number.

And the rate is not fixed. Lindsey Cormack published a follow-up study of New York in 2026. She found error rates falling as voters gained experience. About 89 percent of Democratic primary voters in 2021 ranked more than one candidate in at least one race. And 95 percent told exit pollsters the ballot was simple to complete.

The equity concern is the serious one. My answer is the answer for every earlier ballot innovation, from the Australian ballot to vote-by-mail: money for design and education.

What the error data cannot support is the conclusion the ban states drew. You do not protect a voter who might mismark a ranked ballot by forbidding her city from letting her rank at all.

[pause]

On exhausted ballots, my answer is about the baseline.

Under plurality rules, every ballot cast for a losing candidate is exhausted the instant the polls close. It says nothing about that voter's preference between the finalists. And nobody calls that disenfranchisement.

Under a traditional runoff, it is the voters who are exhausted. Between 1994 and 2022, turnout fell between rounds in 96 percent of federal primary runoffs. The median decline was 40 percent.

Against those baselines, an instant runoff carries 73 to 90 percent of ballots into the final round. That is not a scandal. It is an improvement.

And exhaustion is partly a design choice. The elections Burnett and Kogan studied capped voters at three rankings. San Francisco's 27.1 percent came from a sixteen-candidate race with only three slots. That is why newer systems allow more: five rankings in New York, and as many as there are candidates in Maine.

Some voters will still decline to rank rivals they reject. That is their right. An abstention freely chosen is not a vote stolen.

> My answer on exhaustion: compare against the real alternative, not against perfection.

[pause]

On the wrong reform, I start by conceding. Drutman is right that single-winner ranked choice voting is not proportional representation. He is right that the moderation evidence is modest. The next chapter shows you exactly how modest.

Where I think he is wrong is in treating the two as rivals. Multi-winner ranked choice voting is a proportional system. It is how Portland, Oregon, now elects its council. And every city that learns to rank is a city where proportional ranking stops sounding exotic.

A reform that can pass, and that builds the infrastructure for the bigger one, is not a detour.

The alternative-reform case deserves your attention before you settle your own.

## What the bans tell you

Now go back to Ohio, and to the architecture of these laws.

Almost none of the nineteen states was about to adopt ranked choice voting statewide. The bans are preemption laws. They strip cities and counties of the power to choose their own local election method. These are the same cities that conservatives otherwise praise as laboratories of democracy.

> The bans are preemption laws: they forbid cities from choosing for themselves.

Ohio reached for the budget weapon. Missouri went further. Its legislature put a constitutional ban on the ballot of November 2024. It bundled the ban with a prohibition on noncitizen voting, which duplicated what Missouri law already required.

It passed, with 68.4 percent. It also carried a carve-out, protecting the existing nonpartisan system in St. Louis. To me, that suggests even the drafters knew that repealing a working local system wouldn't sell.

When one side of a debate works to make sure voters never get to run the experiment, it has told you its own estimate of how the experiment would come out. Alaska's voters were asked twice, with the experience fresh. Twice they declined to go back.

One question before we gather up. Near the start, I said three groups oppose ranked choice voting. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The organized institutions. The party establishments, in both parties. And the sincere critics, who share the goal and doubt the remedy.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The opposition is three groups: organized institutions, party establishments, and sincere reform critics. And resisting a rule change is an incumbent instinct, not a left-right one.

[beat]

Two. Their three best arguments. Ranking's cost falls heaviest on the voters reformers claim to champion. The winner's majority is sometimes less than half of all votes. And energy spent on ranked choice belongs to proportional representation.

[beat]

Three. My answers. Ten times a very small number is still a very small number. On exhaustion, compare against the real alternative, not against perfection. And the ally's critique, as I see it, is about sequencing, not substance.

[beat]

Four. The bans are preemption laws: they forbid cities from choosing for themselves.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. It has not settled whether the critics' empirical claims hold up across the full record. It has settled only who the critics are, and what their best case is.

So the next chapter stops arguing and starts counting. Turnout, civility, representation, errors. All of it, with the studies named, and the ugly numbers included.

Nineteen states have decided that their cities may not try this.
