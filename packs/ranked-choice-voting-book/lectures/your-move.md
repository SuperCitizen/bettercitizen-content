Seven hundred forty-three votes.

[beat]

That was the entire margin, after a recount, by which Alaskans kept their election system in November 2024. And the margin that created the system in the first place, in 2020, was 3,781.

Now divide numbers like those by what a single committed volunteer does in one campaign season. The hundreds of doors. The shifts at the farmers market. The letters to the editor. The twenty friends who actually vote because you asked.

Margins like Alaska's are not abstractions that happen to people. They are made, vote by vote, by people who decided the outcome was partly their job.

[pause]

This is the last chapter of Rank the Vote. The book is an argument for ranked choice voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter argued that the status quo is organized, and that the deciding is happening whether you take part or not. This chapter is what taking part looks like.

You've heard the book. You know what the pick-one ballot discards, and what the ranked ballot fixes. You know what the evidence supports, where the courts stand, and how the movement wins and loses.

The only chapter left is the one I can't write for you. But I can hand you three things: the map, the allies, and the script. Then a dare.

## First, know your terrain

Your move depends entirely on where you're standing. So start with three questions.

The first question: does your state have the initiative? 26 states offer a statewide initiative or referendum process, and a site called Ballotpedia will tell you whether yours does. If it does, you are where Maine in 2016 and Alaska in 2020 were possible. The price of admission there is signatures and organization. It is not legislators' permission.

[beat]

The second question: has your state banned ranked choice voting? As of mid-2026, nineteen have. If yours is one of them, your fight is different, but it is not hopeless. A ban is a statute, and statutes get repealed by the same majorities that passed them.

> A ban is a policy choice wearing law's clothing.

Nothing about it is permanent.

[beat]

The third question is the one most people never ask. Can your city move alone?

Much of this movement's entire history is city charters, not state law. San Francisco. Minneapolis. New York. D.C. Greenbelt. Skokie.

Whether your city can act depends on your state's home-rule rules. Some cities can amend their charters by a local vote. Others need the state to pass enabling legislation first. Two places can answer that question for you in an afternoon: your city clerk's office, and a group called the Ranked Choice Voting Resource Center.

[pause]

Let's put the three questions to work. Suppose your state has no citizen initiative, and no ban. And suppose its cities have broad home-rule powers over their own charters. Where is your leverage? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The city.

With no initiative, statewide adoption needs the legislature. And legislatures move only after local proof exists. So the highest-leverage act is building that proof where you are: a charter amendment campaign, in a home-rule city.

That is precisely the play San Francisco ran in 2002. It turned one city's working example into a region's, and then a movement's.

No ban means the laboratory is open. And no initiative only means this.

> Without an initiative, the experiment starts local, not statewide.

## Join people who are already working

That is the map. Now the allies.

None of this requires founding anything. The infrastructure exists, it's real, and I've verified it. I'll name seven groups, each by the job it does. Every one of them would put you to work this month.

One. FairVote. It has been the movement's research and advocacy hub since 1992, and it built the evidence base this book leans on. Start there for the data and the national picture.

[beat]

Two. Rank the Vote. Yes, it shares this book's name. The name is good. It is a network of volunteer-driven state groups. If your state has one, that's your local door.

[beat]

Three. RepresentUs. It runs cross-partisan campaigns for anti-corruption and election reforms, ranked choice voting among them. It is built for people who want a campaign to plug into.

[beat]

Four. Unite America. It funds and publishes the research on the primary problem, the research you heard earlier in this book.

[beat]

Five. Veterans for All Voters, founded and led by military veterans. It organizes among veterans and military families, for open primaries and majority-winner elections. Its credibility in red and purple states is exactly what 2024 showed the movement lacks.

[beat]

Six. The Ranked Choice Voting Resource Center, which does the unglamorous, load-bearing work. It gives election officials and campaigns technical help with ballot design, tabulation and voter education.

[beat]

And seven. The League of Women Voters. In many states, its state chapters have studied and endorsed ranked ballots. Yours may be one of them.

[pause]

What would they actually have you do?

Signature shifts, if your state or city has a measure in the field. Testimony at the statehouse, when a ban or a local-option bill gets its hearing. Committee rooms are emptier than you think, and a constituent who shows up is rarer than a lobbyist.

Tabling, and demonstration elections. Ranking pizza toppings converts more skeptics than any white paper. Poll-watching and education during a first rollout, which is where reforms are actually won or lost. And money, if you have it and time you don't.

[pause]

Here is how a local campaign runs, from start to finish, and where you fit. Five stages.

First, find your people. A handful of residents, a local chapter, one sympathetic councilmember. Every campaign in this book started smaller than you think.

Then, pick the vehicle. A charter amendment, a council ordinance, or a citizen petition. Which one is available depends on your state and city law. That is the terrain question again.

Then, qualify and campaign. Signatures, endorsements, press coverage, and thousands of two-minute conversations.

Then, the vote. Local measures have been winning. D.C., with 73 percent. Greenbelt, with 67. Skokie, with 58. And if you lose? Maine lost legislative fights for a decade before 2016. A loss builds the list for the next round.

And last, implement and educate. The campaign isn't over at adoption. Ballot design, training for officials, and voter education decide what the first election becomes: the proof, or the cautionary tale.

> Margins are made of volunteers.

## Make the case the way it wins

The map, the allies. Now the script.

The post-mortems of 2024 are a gift. Use them in your own conversations. They teach three habits.

The first habit: start with outcomes, not machinery. "Majority winners." "No more spoilers." "A runoff without a second election."

The mechanism comes second, after someone asks how. Rounds, transfers, eliminations. Campaigns that opened with the mechanism spent 2024 explaining. Campaigns that opened with outcomes spent it persuading.

> Outcomes first. The mechanism comes second, after someone asks how.

[pause]

The second habit: concede what must be conceded. You've watched me do it all book. The center squeeze is real. The money evidence is thin. The civility gains are bounded. And statewide voters said no five times in 2024.

Overclaiming is how advocates manufacture their own backlash. The credibility you bank by conceding is what makes your affirmative claims land.

[pause]

The third habit: answer the partisanship charge with the record, not with indignation.

Let's test that one. Your neighbor says: "Be honest — this ranked-choice thing is just a scheme to help one party." What is the strongest honest reply? What would you say?

[long pause]

You answer with facts your neighbor can verify. Alaska, 2022. The same ranked ballots elected a Republican governor outright, reelected a Republican senator, and sent a Democrat to the House. And six Southern states mail ranked ballots to their deployed troops.

One ballot, in one state, electing both parties' candidates at once. The method picks majority winners. The voters pick the parties.

Notice the replies that fail. Saying studies prove it helps both parties equally, in every election, is an overclaim. No study proves that, and partisan effects vary race by race. "Both parties oppose it, so it must be good" is an argument shaped like a conspiracy. And "trust the experts" is condescension. Those last two are the fastest ways to lose a skeptic.

## Run

The map, the allies, the script. Now the dare, and I mean it. Consider running.

School board. Library board. City council.

The whole argument of this book is that pick-one ballots rig the decision to enter a race. Ordinary people with broad appeal, and no machine behind them, are filtered out before voters ever meet them.

Where ranked ballots exist, that filter weakens. Candidacies multiplied in the cities that adopted them. If you are somewhere the reform has won, the system is now built for someone like you.

And if you run where it hasn't won, run the way the reform teaches anyway. Ask to be people's second choice, if you can't be their first. Candidates who campaign that way build the constituency for the rules that reward it.

[pause]

And 2026 hands you specific assignments, wherever you are.

Alaska votes on repeal, for the third time, on November 3, 2026. If you know Alaskans, they will decide this with a margin made of conversations.

Washington, D.C. is running its first ranked elections. A smooth rollout there is the next national proof point.

Nineteen statehouses hold bans that will someday face repeal bills. Yours may be one of them.

And FairVote keeps a tracker. It counts 49 jurisdictions, and nearly 14 million voters. It will tell you exactly where the line currently runs through your state.

## What to keep

One question before we gather up. I opened this chapter with a number: the margin by which Alaskans kept their system in 2024. Do you still have it?

[long pause]

Seven hundred forty-three votes.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The terrain. Three questions. Does your state have the initiative? Has it banned ranked choice voting? And can your city move alone?

[beat]

Two. The allies. Nothing here requires founding anything. The groups exist, and they would put you to work this month. Margins are made of volunteers.

[beat]

Three. The script. Outcomes first, and the mechanism after someone asks how. Concede what must be conceded. And answer the partisanship charge with the record.

[beat]

Four. The dare. Consider running. And wherever you run, ask to be people's second choice, if you can't be their first.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter cannot settle, and what this book cannot settle. Whether any of it happens. Alaska's repeal vote is still ahead as I write. The bans stand. Statewide voters said no five times in 2024. A book can make the argument. It cannot do the work.

[pause]

So here is the last thing I'll say. It is the thesis of the whole book, wearing work clothes.

The people who benefit from the broken system will never volunteer to fix it. Not because they're villains. Because no one audits the rules that made them winners.

Every reform in this book happened the same way. Every city, both states, all of it.

> People with no permission and no office decided the rules were their business.

[pause]

This book began in a voting booth. You walk in. You pick one person. You leave. And the ballot discards everything else you know.

[beat]

It ends outside the booth.

Seven hundred forty-three votes. The system is listening whether you speak or not. Silence is also a ballot, and it always gets counted for the status quo.

[pause]

Your move.
