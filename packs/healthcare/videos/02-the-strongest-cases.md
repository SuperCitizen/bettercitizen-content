## The day Vermont gave up

On December 17, 2014, Governor Peter Shumlin of Vermont abandoned the single-payer system he had won office promising. His own financing plan put the price at an 11.5 percent payroll tax, plus a new income tax sliding up to 9.5 percent.

- Payroll tax: 11.5%
- Income tax: up to 9.5%

Three camps read that day three ways, and all three readings are defensible.

> One collapse, three defensible readings.

## The single-payer advocate

The single-payer advocate argues that the cost gap comes from fragmentation. No payer has leverage over prices, and thousands of plan variants force every provider to keep a billing bureaucracy. The prescription is one federal payer. They point to hospital administrative costs: 25.3 percent of hospital spending in the United States, about 12 percent in Canada. The hardest evidence for this case is Vermont, and a California bill pulled in 2022 without a floor vote.

- Diagnosis: fragmentation
- Hardest evidence: Vermont 2014, California 2022

> The single-payer case: fragmentation is the overhead.

## The market-reform advocate

The market-reform advocate argues that American healthcare is not a market at all. There are no posted prices, and the patient neither sees nor cares about the price. The prescription is catastrophic coverage with savings accounts, and deregulated supply. They point to the RAND experiment, where families with substantial cost-sharing spent about 30 percent less, with no measurable harm to the average person's health. The hardest evidence for this case is in the same experiment: cost-sharing cut effective and ineffective care indiscriminately.

- Diagnosis: no prices, no shoppers
- Hardest evidence: effective care was cut too

> The market-reform case: the record indicts the absence of markets.

## The incrementalist

The ACA-expansion incrementalist argues that the framework exists, and has worked where it was fully used. The prescription is to close the coverage gap, restore subsidies and add a public option. They point to the uninsured rate, which fell from 16.3 percent in 2010 to 8.0 percent in 2024. The hardest evidence for this case is Oregon's Medicaid lottery, which found no statistically significant improvement in measured physical outcomes, and the defunding of the framework in 2026.

- Diagnosis: seams that can be fixed
- Hardest evidence: Oregon's results, 2026 defunding

> The incrementalist case: finish the job before betting the system.

## Settled and contested

Some of this is near-settled. The United States spends roughly double the peer average per person, and the gap is overwhelmingly a price gap. Which frame explains it is contested.

- Near-settled: a price gap
- Contested: which frame explains it

The lesson gives each case in full, the two great randomized experiments that every camp claims pieces of, and what would falsify each position.
