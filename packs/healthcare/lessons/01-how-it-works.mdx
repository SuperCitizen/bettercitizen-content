# How U.S. healthcare actually works

In 2009, 19,368 adults in California were hospitalized for appendicitis. Same state, same year, same routine condition, uncomplicated cases only. The lowest hospital charge was $1,529. The highest was $182,955. The median was $33,611 — and a team of UCSF researchers who published the numbers in the *Archives of Internal Medicine* could not explain most of the variation even after controlling for patient and hospital characteristics (Hsia et al. 2012). No patient could have learned the price in advance, and almost none of them paid it directly anyway: an insurer, an employer, or a government program stood between the patient and the bill. Every argument you have ever heard about American healthcare — "best in the world," "rationed by wealth," "a free market," "socialized already" — is an attempt to characterize the system that produces a number like $182,955 for an appendectomy. Before judging it, take the machine apart.

## The accident that built the system

No one designed American healthcare. Its central feature — insurance that comes from your job — is a side effect of fighting World War II.

In 1942, with inflation looming and twelve million workers headed into uniform, Congress passed the Stabilization Act and froze wages. Employers competing for scarce labor could not offer raises — so they offered benefits, and in 1943 the National War Labor Board ruled that fringe benefits like health insurance, up to about five percent of wages, did not count as wage increases. The same year, the IRS issued a special ruling that employers' contributions to group health plans were not taxable income to the worker. Congress made the exclusion permanent in the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 (§106) — and quietly dropped the wartime cap (Thomasson 2000). The result is one of the largest single [[term:subsidy]] programs in the federal tax code: excluding employer premiums from income and payroll taxes cost the Treasury an estimated $312 billion in 2022 (KFF). A dollar of employer-paid premium is untaxed; a dollar of wages is not. For eighty years that wedge has pulled working-age coverage toward the employment contract — which is why, almost uniquely among wealthy countries, losing your job in America so often means losing your insurance.

The second great accretion came in 1965, when Medicare (Social Security Act Title XVIII) and Medicaid (Title XIX) created public [[term:entitlement-program]]s for the two groups the employer system structurally excluded: retirees and the poor. The price of passage, as Paul Starr documents in *The Social Transformation of American Medicine* (1982) — the Pulitzer-winning history of how organized medicine shaped every American financing scheme to its advantage — was that Medicare initially paid hospitals their "reasonable costs" and physicians their "reasonable charges." The government bought into the existing private price structure rather than setting its own. Much of the modern cost story descends from that bargain.

> **The scholar's lens:** The founding document of health economics is Kenneth Arrow's "Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care" (*American Economic Review*, 1963). Arrow argued that medical care violates the textbook market model in predictable ways: illness is uncertain, so people demand insurance; once insured, they bear little of the marginal cost (**moral hazard**); the sick are likelier to buy coverage than the well, which can unravel insurance pools (**adverse selection**); and doctors know vastly more than patients (**information asymmetry**), so trust and professional norms substitute for shopping. Every institution in this lesson — licensure, employer group pooling, public programs, the ACA's mandate-and-subsidy architecture — is an attempted answer to a problem Arrow named. All three perspectives in Lesson 2 accept his diagnosis; they disagree about the cure.

<Checkpoint question="A software engineer quits her job to start a company and discovers that replacing her family's coverage costs far more, after taxes, than it “cost” her as an employee. Which policy choices — and from which decades — produced that gap?">
  The 1943 IRS ruling and its 1954 codification (§106): employer premium contributions are excluded from income and payroll taxes, while an individual buying her own plan uses after-tax dollars (the ACA's premium credits offset this only at lower incomes). Layer on the group-pooling advantage Arrow identified — her employer's plan priced her as part of a large pool, while the individual market prices and markets to her household — and the employment tie is doing exactly what an eighty-year-old tax wedge built it to do.
</Checkpoint>

## The five coverage sources

Where Americans actually get coverage, as of the most recent verified counts (categories overlap — several million people, for instance, are "dual eligibles" enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid):

| Source | Roughly who | How many | Mechanics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employer-sponsored insurance | Workers and dependents under 65 | about 165 million under-65s — 60% of that age group (2025, KFF) | Employer buys a group plan, splits the premium with workers; contributions untaxed |
| Medicare | 65+ and certain disabled people | about 69 million (2025) | Federal program: payroll-tax-funded Part A, premium-plus-revenue Parts B/D; 54% of eligible beneficiaries now choose private Medicare Advantage plans |
| Medicaid + CHIP | Low-income households, disabled people, most poor children | 75.7 million (Dec. 2025) | Joint federal–state program; Washington pays 50–90% of costs; states administer under federal rules |
| ACA marketplace | People without employer coverage, mostly self-employed and gig workers | 24.3 million selected 2025 plans; 23.1 million for 2026 | Subsidized private plans bought on HealthCare.gov or state exchanges, premium tax credits scaled to income |
| Uninsured | Disproportionately low-income workers; more than 8 in 10 are in working families | 26.7 million under 65 (2024) | Pay cash, rely on emergency care and safety-net clinics |

Two things to read out of the table. First, the Medicaid number is in motion: enrollment peaked at about 94 million in April 2023, when a pandemic-era rule barring states from removing anyone expired, and the "unwinding" of that rule disenrolled roughly 25 million — some no longer eligible, many dropped for paperwork reasons. For exactly that reason, the number of uninsured people under 65 rose by 1.3 million in 2024, to 26.7 million — the first increase since 2019 (KFF), partly masked in the headline Census rate (8.0 percent, statistically unchanged) by offsetting marketplace gains. Second, there is no single system here. The U.S. runs a **multi-payer** patchwork: dozens of private insurers under fifty-one different state regulatory regimes, plus three distinct public programs, each with its own eligibility rules, payment rates, and paperwork.

## What the ACA did — and what it deliberately did not

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 was the largest restructuring since 1965, and it is routinely misdescribed by both its enemies and its friends. What it actually did:

1. **Banned medical underwriting.** Insurers must cover pre-existing conditions ("guaranteed issue") and may not price on health status ("community rating," with limited age and tobacco adjustments).
2. **Created subsidized marketplaces** — HealthCare.gov plus state exchanges — with premium tax credits scaled to income for people without employer coverage.
3. **Expanded Medicaid** to all adults up to 138 percent of the federal poverty line ($21,597 for an individual in 2025) — designed as mandatory for states.
4. **Required most individuals to hold coverage** (the "individual mandate"), enforced by a tax penalty that Congress zeroed out in 2017. The requirement is still nominally on the books; the enforcement is gone.
5. **Required large employers to offer coverage** or pay a penalty, and set **essential health benefits** that all individual and small-group plans must cover — including no-cost preventive services.

The Supreme Court has reshaped and re-ratified the law four times. *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) upheld the mandate as a tax but made [[issue:medicaid-expansion]] **optional** — the reason America still has expansion and non-expansion states. As of mid-2026, 40 states plus D.C. have expanded; in the 10 holdouts, about 1.4 million adults sit in the "coverage gap," too poor for marketplace subsidies (which start at 100 percent of poverty) but ineligible for their state's unexpanded Medicaid. *King v. Burwell* (2015) preserved subsidies on the federal exchange; *California v. Texas* (2021) dismissed the last existential challenge for lack of standing; and *Kennedy v. Braidwood Management* (2025) upheld the preventive-services mandate, 6–3, holding that the task force that defines covered services is constitutionally supervised by the HHS Secretary. The result: the uninsured rate fell from 16.3 percent in 2010 to 8.8 percent by 2016, and stood at 8.0 percent in 2024.

Now the list of things the ACA did *not* do, which explains most of what Lessons 2 and 3 argue about. It created no public option and no single payer. It left the employer system — and its tax exclusion — essentially untouched. And it made almost no direct attempt to regulate the *prices* providers charge private payers.

> **Common misconception:** "Obamacare was a government takeover of healthcare." Mechanically, the ACA *expanded the customer base of private insurers* — marketplace plans are private plans, and most Medicaid expansion enrollees are in privately run managed-care plans. A government takeover was proposed (the public option) and stripped from the bill in the Senate in 2009. What the ACA nationalized was not insurance but insurance *rules*.

<Checkpoint question="A 45-year-old uninsured cook in Texas (a non-expansion state) earns $14,000 a year — below the poverty line. Her cousin in New Mexico (an expansion state) earns the same. Who can get covered, and why?">
  The cousin: New Mexico's Medicaid covers adults to 138% of poverty. The Texan falls into the coverage gap — Texas never expanded (her income exceeds its narrow pre-ACA categories or she fits none), and marketplace premium credits only begin at 100% of poverty because the ACA's drafters assumed Medicaid would catch everyone below it. *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) broke that assumption, and Congress never repaired the seam. She is one of about 1.4 million people in that position as of 2025.
</Checkpoint>

## The record: what 18 percent of GDP buys

Now the comparative data the whole debate runs on. Figures are the latest verified (2023–24, Peterson-KFF analysis of OECD data; CDC; Census):

| Measure | United States | Comparable wealthy countries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health consumption per person (2024) | $14,775 | $7,860 average; Switzerland, the runner-up, about $5,000 less than the U.S. |
| Health spending share of GDP (2024) | 17.2% | 11.2% average |
| Life expectancy at birth | 79.0 years (2024 — a record high, up from 78.4 in 2023) | about 82–84 in most peers |
| Maternal mortality (2024) | 17.9 deaths per 100,000 live births — 44.8 for Black women | the highest among high-income countries, even after falling from its 2021 peak of 32.9 |
| Uninsured | 8.0% (2024) | effectively 0–1% |

Read the table honestly in both directions. The U.S. spends roughly double per person and dies younger on average — but the 2023–24 rebound (life expectancy rose 1.5 years in two years as overdose and COVID deaths fell) shows the gap is not destiny, and the U.S. performs well on some specific measures, such as cancer screening and survival and short waits for elective specialty care. The deeper American pathology is *insecurity inside* coverage: 23 percent of insured working-age adults are **underinsured** — their out-of-pocket exposure is high relative to income (Commonwealth Fund, 2024) — and Americans owe at least $220 billion in medical debt, with 20 million people owing some and 3 million owing more than $10,000 (KFF). No peer country produces medical debt at that scale, because no peer country exposes patients to comparable cost-sharing.

Why does the spending gap exist? The most-cited answer in the literature is the title of a famous *Health Affairs* paper: "It's Still the Prices, Stupid" (Anderson, Hussey & Petrosyan 2019). Americans do not visit doctors or occupy hospital beds more than peers — utilization is unremarkable. They pay more *per unit*: U.S. prescription-drug prices average 2.78 times those of 33 OECD peers (4.22 times for brand-name drugs, per RAND's 2024 study), hospital and physician prices run far above peer levels, and a multi-payer billing apparatus adds administrative cost on top. Princeton's Anne Case and [[person:angus-deaton]], in *Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism* (2020), put the macro point bluntly: the excess cost of American healthcare functions like a tribute the economy pays off the top — extracted substantially from the wages of less-educated workers — before any care is delivered.

> **Think it through:** "It's the prices" sounds like a diagnosis, but prices are incomes — every "excess" dollar is somebody's revenue: a hospital system's, a physician's, a drugmaker's, an insurer's. Before Lesson 2, ask yourself: whose income would *your* preferred fix cut, and what do you predict they would do about it politically?

## Three models, all running at once

Reform proposals nearly always reach for one of three architectures. **Single-payer** systems (Canada, Taiwan; Britain adds public *provision* through the NHS) enroll everyone in one government plan, which gains monopsony power over prices and eliminates eligibility churn; the costs are tax-financed, and the system's generosity becomes a budget decision. **Regulated multi-payer** systems (Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland) keep competing private insurers under heavy rules — everyone must be covered, insurers must take all comers, prices are negotiated sector-wide — proving universal coverage doesn't require a single payer, at the price of permanent regulatory complexity. **Market-oriented** designs (Singapore is the closest real case; U.S. conservative proposals draw on it) push first-dollar costs back to consumers through savings accounts and catastrophic coverage, betting that price-sensitive patients will discipline providers — within, even in Singapore, a government-run backstop and price-published hospital sector.

Here is the key to American exceptionalism: the U.S. is not a fourth model — it is all three at once, plus a gap. Medicare Part A is single-payer financing; the VA is government provision, NHS-style; the ACA marketplaces are regulated multi-payer; employer plans and the cash market are the market wing; and 26.7 million people stand outside all of it.

## What this lesson is not

This lesson gave you the machine: where coverage comes from, where $5.3 trillion goes, what the ACA changed, and how the U.S. record compares. It has *not* told you what to think about any of it. The genuinely contested questions — is the cost gap caused by fragmentation, by the absence of real markets, or by fixable seams in the ACA framework? — get full steel-man treatment in Lesson 2, where each of the three perspectives (single-payer, market-reform, ACA-incrementalist) is presented as its smartest advocates argue it. The empirical record above is the shared evidence they all must explain.
