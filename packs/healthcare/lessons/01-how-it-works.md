# How U.S. healthcare actually works

The U.S. healthcare system is often described in slogans — "the most expensive in the world," "rationed by ability to pay," "a free-market system" — that all contain truth and all mislead. Start with the mechanical structure, then the cost-and-outcomes data, then the argument.

## The five coverage sources

As of 2023 (KFF data; the categories overlap slightly because some people have multiple coverages):

- **Employer-sponsored insurance** — about 156 million people (~48% of population). Employers buy group plans and split premiums with workers. Tax-advantaged: employer contributions are not counted as income to the worker, a subsidy worth ~$300B/year (CBO).
- **Medicare** — about 65 million people. Federal program for people 65+ and certain disabilities. Funded by payroll taxes (Part A) + general revenues + premiums (Parts B and D). Single-payer for hospital care; multi-payer with private "Medicare Advantage" alternatives now covering majority of enrollees.
- **Medicaid** — about 87 million people. Joint federal-state program for low-income households, the disabled, and (in expansion states) most adults below 138% of the federal poverty line. Federal government pays 50–90% of state costs depending on the program category and state demographics.
- **ACA Marketplace plans** — about 21 million people in 2024. Subsidized individual insurance bought through Healthcare.gov or state exchanges, with premium tax credits scaled to income.
- **Uninsured** — about 26 million people in 2023 (8% of population), down from ~50 million pre-ACA but still the highest rate among wealthy countries.

This is **multi-payer**: there is no single national insurer, and most coverage is mediated through one of dozens of private insurers operating under different rule sets in different states.

## What the ACA actually did

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 (the "ACA," "Obamacare") was the largest restructuring of U.S. healthcare since Medicare in 1965. The core changes:

1. **Required insurers to cover pre-existing conditions** without higher premiums.
2. **Created subsidized individual marketplaces** (Healthcare.gov + state exchanges) where people without employer coverage could buy regulated plans.
3. **Expanded Medicaid eligibility** to all adults below 138% of poverty (originally mandatory, made optional by NFIB v. Sebelius in 2012; 41 states + D.C. have expanded as of 2025).
4. **Required most individuals to have insurance** ("individual mandate," with tax penalties), with the penalty zeroed-out by the 2017 tax law but the requirement still on the books.
5. **Required most large employers to offer insurance** ("employer mandate") or pay a penalty.
6. **Set minimum benefit standards** ("essential health benefits") that all plans must cover.
7. **Created state-level "exchanges"** as the marketplace infrastructure — federally-run in 30+ states, state-run in the rest.

What the ACA did NOT do:
- It did not create a public option (a government-run insurance plan competing with private insurers).
- It did not create single-payer insurance.
- It did not change the employer-based system for the ~156M people in employer plans.
- It did not change Medicare's structure.
- It did not address provider pricing directly (the cost issue we'll come back to).

The ACA was a coverage-expansion law layered on top of the existing multi-payer system. It moved roughly 25M previously-uninsured Americans into coverage. It did not transform how care is paid for or priced.

## The cost-and-outcomes record

Comparative data (OECD 2024):

- **The U.S. spends ~$13,400 per capita per year on healthcare** — roughly **2× the OECD average**. The next-highest spender (Germany, Switzerland) is at ~$8,000. This gap is ~5% of U.S. GDP — about $1.2 trillion per year more than the U.S. would spend if it spent at OECD-peer rates.
- **Life expectancy at birth is ~76 years** (2023 data) — below every OECD peer, despite the higher spending. The gap predates the COVID-era declines and has been widening since the 1980s.
- **Maternal mortality** is roughly 3× the OECD average and rising; the U.S. is the only wealthy country where this metric has worsened in recent decades.
- **Coverage rates**: the U.S. has 8% uninsured; most wealthy countries have 0–1%. Underinsurance — having coverage that still leaves people facing major out-of-pocket bills — affects an additional ~25% of insured Americans (Commonwealth Fund tracking).

The "It's the prices, stupid" finding (Anderson, Hussey & Petrosyan 2019) is the most-cited explanation for the cost gap: U.S. prices for the same drugs, devices, and procedures are 2–4× what other wealthy countries pay. Service utilization rates are similar to peers; provider compensation is higher; administrative costs (insurance overhead, billing complexity) are dramatically higher.

This is the empirical record all three perspectives in Lesson 2 are arguing about. They don't disagree about the data; they disagree about what to do about it.

## Three model types

Most reform proposals fall into one of three categories:

- **Single-payer** (Medicare-for-All, Canada, U.K., Taiwan in different forms): one government insurer covers everyone, replaces private insurance. Provider-side can be private (Canada) or public (U.K. NHS). Pros (proponents argue): eliminates billing complexity, gives the payer monopsony power to set prices, achieves near-universal coverage. Cons (critics argue): requires large tax increase to replace premiums, removes private-insurance choice, depends on government willingness to set prices that providers will accept.

- **Multi-payer with regulation** (Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland; the ACA in milder form): private insurers compete under tight government rules — required coverage, price negotiation, sometimes risk pooling across insurers. Pros (proponents argue): preserves choice, harnesses competition, achieves universal or near-universal coverage with strong outcomes. Cons (critics argue): complex to administer, prone to insurer gaming, requires constant regulatory updating to keep working.

- **Market-based** (closer to U.S. pre-ACA, conservative reform proposals): individuals buy insurance in less-regulated markets with subsidies (often Health Savings Accounts) for catastrophic coverage. Pros (proponents argue): preserves price signals, lets innovation compete, shifts cost-sensitivity to consumers. Cons (critics argue): leaves many uninsured, creates adverse selection, asks consumers to be sophisticated buyers in a market famously hostile to consumer information.

The U.S. system is essentially a hybrid: market-based for under-65 employer plans, single-payer for Medicare hospital coverage (Part A), multi-payer-with-regulation for Marketplace plans, and outright public for Medicaid. It contains all three models running in parallel.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Single-payer advocates** argue the U.S. system's cost gap and coverage gaps are inseparable — both stem from multi-payer fragmentation, and only consolidating to a single payer fixes them.
- **Market-reform advocates** argue the cost gap stems from the *absence* of real markets in U.S. healthcare (third-party-payer distortions, lack of price transparency, regulatory capture), and that more market discipline would reduce costs and expand access.
- **ACA-expansion incrementalists** argue the ACA's framework works where states have used it fully, that the remaining gaps are a political problem (Medicaid expansion holdouts, public-option absence) rather than a structural one, and that single-payer or full marketization both underestimate the costs of disruption.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
