The night of December 31, 2025. A set of tax credits expires, on schedule.

They are the enhanced premium tax credits. They had financed a record 24.3 million enrollments on the insurance marketplaces.

Look at the weeks that led up to that night. Seven weeks earlier, a government shutdown had ended. It ran 43 days, the longest in American history, and it was fought substantially over these credits. Three weeks earlier, the Senate had rejected both parties' rescue bills.

[beat]

Then the bills arrived. The average premium payment that marketplace enrollees make jumped 58 percent. And 1.2 million fewer people signed up for coverage in 2026.

[pause]

That is the policy landscape of mid-2026, in miniature.

> Healthcare policy in mid-2026 is not a stalemate. It is a collision.

Fifteen years of coverage expansion have met the largest legislated retrenchment in the programs' history.

[pause]

The first lecture gave you the machine. The second gave you the arguments. This one maps where the fight stands, and on whose calendar.

By the end, you will be able to say what changed in 2025 and 2026, which lever changed it, and what evidence the next few years will bring.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the law of 2025, and the calendar written into it.

Second, the subsidy cliff, and what it measurably did.

Third, the one policy that kept rolling forward: drug-price negotiation.

And last, the levers: who can move what, and with how many votes.

## The 2025 law

Start with the biggest move. On July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act was signed. I will call it the 2025 law.

It passed the Senate through reconciliation, the budget procedure that needs only a simple majority. Hold on to that. It is how a chamber that cannot find 60 votes to extend marketplace subsidies could still restructure Medicaid.

The Congressional Budget Office, the CBO, gave the law its final score. It reduces federal spending on Medicaid and the marketplaces by about 1.06 trillion dollars over ten years. [beat] And it increases the number of people without health insurance by about 10 million in 2034.

[pause]

Three provisions do most of that work.

First, work requirements. The statute says "community engagement."

Adults in the ACA expansion group must document 80 hours a month of work, community service or schooling, unless they are exempt. That is verified when they apply, and again at every renewal. It begins no later than January 1, 2027.

Second, renewals every six months, for the same adults. That doubles how often they must pass through the paperwork.

Third, a squeeze on provider taxes.

States finance much of their share of Medicaid by taxing hospitals, and then returning the money as Medicaid payments. That harvests federal matching funds on the round trip. Federal watchdogs have criticized the maneuver for decades. The 2025 law freezes these taxes everywhere, and ratchets them down in expansion states.

And the law has a sweetener: a 50 billion dollar fund for rural health, aimed at the rural hospitals most exposed to that squeeze.

[pause]

Now look at when all of this takes effect, because the dates tell a story of their own.

The political science of it was written three decades ago. In 1994, Paul Pierson published a book comparing how Reagan and Thatcher tried to cut back their welfare states. He argued that cutting a popular program is a fundamentally different game from opposing its creation. The reason is that programs create constituencies.

> Programs create constituencies. Cutting one is a different game from opposing its creation.

So politicians who retrench practice what Pierson called blame avoidance. It has three tools.

Obfuscation: cut through the mechanics of eligibility, not through visible benefit levels.

[beat]

Long phase-ins: push the costs past the next election.

[beat]

And compensation: a visible fund for the most sympathetic losers.

[pause]

Let's test that. The 2025 law was signed in July. Yet through most of 2026, a Medicaid expansion enrollee in most states felt nothing. And the deepest provider-tax cuts arrive in 2031 and 2032.

Using Pierson, how would you explain that calendar? And which provision pays out before the costs land? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is blame avoidance by phase-in.

The work requirements and the six-month renewals bind only after the midterm elections of November 2026. The provider-tax ratchet spreads its pain across two more election cycles. So no single campaign season meets the full cost.

The early arrival is the rural health fund: 10 billion dollars a year, starting in fiscal 2026. That is Pierson's compensation. Visible money reaches rural hospitals, the most sympathetic potential losers, before the squeeze that endangers them begins.

And the cuts themselves run through eligibility mechanics, not through benefit levels. That is the first tool, obfuscation.

[pause]

One thing more. Even the subsidy cliff was a past Congress's deferral. The 2022 law that extended the credits scheduled their end just beyond the 2024 election.

Pierson also made a prediction: retrenchment breeds backlash, as the cuts become visible. That is the live hypothesis of the 2026 election cycle.

## Three readings of the law

So much for what the law does, and when. What should we make of it?

Start with the evidence. Work requirements have a track record to consult. Arkansas ran the experiment in 2018. About 18,000 adults lost coverage in the first seven months, before a federal court halted the policy.

The research that followed found three things.

More than 95 percent of the people targeted already met the requirement, or qualified for an exemption.

Employment did not measurably increase.

And the coverage losses flowed overwhelmingly from reporting failures: confusion, broken websites, unread mail. [beat] They did not flow from refusal to work.

[pause]

Those are findings. What the law is worth is a judgment. So read it through the three lenses from last time, each at its strongest.

> The market reformer sees overdue discipline: a gimmick curbed, and Medicaid refocused.

On this reading, provider taxes really are an accounting gimmick that inflates federal spending. A work expectation for able-bodied adults polls well, and it aligns an expanded program with norms of reciprocity. And the expansion population grew far beyond what the ACA's drafters projected. Trimming it refocuses Medicaid on the disabled, on children, and on the elderly poor.

> The single-payer advocate sees coverage removed by friction, not by an honest vote.

On this reading, the law takes the Arkansas mechanism national. Coverage is removed not by an honest vote to cut benefits, but by administrative friction. That is precisely the pathology of churn and fragmentation that the single-payer case diagnosed. Only now, on this reading, it is engineered deliberately.

> The incrementalist sees the ACA framework defunded rather than completed.

On this reading, every dollar of the 1.06 trillion comes out of the coverage architecture. That is the architecture the incrementalist strategy was counting on finishing. The plan was to complete the framework, and the law withdraws the money instead.

[pause]

Three readings of one statute. One of them probably strikes you as the weakest. Take that one. State the best case for it, as its advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## The subsidy cliff, measured

Second stop: the cliff itself.

The enhanced credits began as pandemic policy, in 2021, and were extended through 2025. Among other things, they capped what anyone had to pay for a benchmark plan at 8.5 percent of income. Under them, marketplace enrollment more than doubled.

Then came the sequence from the opening. The shutdown. A Senate vote in mid-December, where a three-year extension drew 51 votes, against the 60 that the filibuster demands. The expiration. And then a House vote, in January, that passed a three-year extension.

The Senate has let it sit. As of mid-2026, the credits remain expired, while a bipartisan group of senators negotiates a narrower version.

[pause]

Because the cliff arrived on schedule, 2026 is producing something rare in a policy debate: a measured answer.

It arrives as a puzzle. Here are two true headlines from January 2026. One says that marketplace premiums rose 26 percent. The other says that enrollees' premium payments jumped 58 percent.

A reader insists that one of them must be wrong. How can both be right? And which number does the uninsured rate respond to? Think it through.

[long pause]

The 26 percent is the gross premium: the price that insurers charge. Insurers anticipated a sicker risk pool, and raised it.

The 58 percent is the net payment: what enrollees paid, on average, after credits.

When the enhancement lapsed, the share of income that enrollees must contribute rose at every income level. So the credit absorbs less, and the net payment grows much faster than the sticker price.

And the uninsured rate responds to the net number.

> Coverage decisions ride on the net payment, not the sticker price.

Here is the rest of the measured answer. Final plan selections for 2026 came in at 23.1 million, down from the record. And the CBO attributes 2.2 million newly uninsured people in 2026 to the expiration itself.

[pause]

One caution, because partisans routinely blur this. The decade has had three coverage shocks, and they run on different clocks.

The first was the Medicaid unwinding. A pandemic rule that kept people enrolled ended, and states resumed removals. Roughly 25 million people were disenrolled. That shock is complete.

[beat]

The second is the subsidy expiration. It is hitting now, and one statute could reverse it.

[beat]

The third is the 2025 law. That wave has barely begun.

These streams add together. Combined, they are why every serious projection shows the uninsured rate climbing back toward pre-ACA territory, among the non-elderly poor, by the early 2030s.

## Drug pricing

Third stop. While coverage policy retrenched, one policy rolled forward: Medicare's negotiation of drug prices. The authority comes from the Inflation Reduction Act. And it kept rolling through a change of administration, to one that had campaigned against that law.

Take the rounds in order.

Round one. The first ten negotiated prices took effect on January 1, 2026.

Round two arrived on schedule: 15 more drugs, with prices effective in 2027. The headline drug is Ozempic. Its list price is 959 dollars a month. [beat] Its negotiated price is 274.

Round three was selected in early 2026: 15 more drugs, with prices effective in 2028. For the first time, they include drugs administered by a physician.

And so far, the manufacturers' constitutional challenges have failed in the lower federal courts.

[pause]

That persistence is itself data, and Pierson has a name for it. But first, a test of memory. Earlier, I gave you his three tools of blame avoidance. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Obfuscation. Long phase-ins. Compensation.

Those are tools for taking a benefit away. Pierson also explains why a benefit, once it flows, tends to stay. He would call it program feedback. A benefit flowing to nearly 69 million Medicare enrollees acquires defenders faster than repealers.

[pause]

And the three camps read this experiment just as the last lecture would predict.

For the single-payer advocate, it is the proof of concept for a single buyer. The only question is why a price the government can negotiate for a 65-year-old should be unavailable to a 55-year-old.

For the market reformer, it is administered price-fixing. Its costs are delayed launches and deterred research, and they arrive on a ten-year lag, invisible at the victory lap. The honest version concedes that the effect on innovation is genuinely contested.

For the incrementalist, it is exhibit A: a discrete, popular, durable mechanism, that expands round by round without betting the system.

One program, three readings. And the evidence that could sort them, the market's response to negotiated prices, is still arriving.

## The levers

Last stop: the levers. Five of them moved events in 2025 and 2026, and each takes a different number of votes.

One: a reconciliation law. Congress pulls this lever with a simple majority in the Senate, for budget items only. That was the 2025 law.

Two: a regular law. In practice, it needs 60 votes in the Senate. That was the subsidy extension: passed by the House, stalled in the Senate.

Three: rules, waivers and enforcement. The health agencies pull this lever with no votes at all, though courts review what they do. The drug-price rounds run this way.

Four: state law, made by legislatures, governors and ballot measures. Here the states diverged, as they always do. The expansion map is frozen at 40 states plus D.C. Meanwhile, Washington, Colorado and Nevada operate public-option plans inside their marketplaces. And several states are racing to implement the new work requirements early.

> When Washington gridlocks, the states become the laboratories, in both directions at once.

Five: litigation. Anyone with standing can start it, and it ends, eventually, with five justices.

In 2025, the Supreme Court upheld the ACA's mandate that plans cover preventive services at no cost. That same year, a federal judge erased a consumer rule that would have removed medical debt from credit reports. Executive-branch policy, it turns out, is only as durable as the next administration and the nearest district court.

[pause]

Now set the first two levers side by side. The asymmetry between them explains the year.

> Money can be moved by 51 votes. Program rules, and rescues, need 60.

That is why a Congress that could cut 1.06 trillion dollars could not extend a subsidy that most of its members claim to support in some form.

One last case. The governor of a non-expansion state wants the coverage gap closed, without waiting for Congress. Which levers are open to her? And what catch has been attached to each since 2025? Take a moment.

[long pause]

She has two.

The first is to adopt expansion: through the legislature, or, where it is allowed, through a ballot initiative. The catch is that the 2025 law repealed the extra federal bonus for new adopters. And any expansion population now arrives with work requirements to verify, and six-month renewals to administer.

The second is to seek a waiver for a custom program. Mississippi's expansion bills of 2026 take this route, pairing expansion with work requirements. The catch is that a waiver requires federal approval. So the design must fit whoever holds the White House. [beat] And the next one can unwind it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The 2025 law cuts about 1.06 trillion dollars from Medicaid and the marketplaces, through work requirements, six-month renewals, and a squeeze on provider taxes. The market reformer sees overdue discipline. The single-payer advocate sees coverage removed by friction. The incrementalist sees the ACA framework defunded rather than completed.

[beat]

Two. The calendar. Programs create constituencies, so cutting one is a different game from opposing its creation. It runs on blame avoidance: obfuscation, long phase-ins, compensation.

[beat]

Three. The subsidy cliff. Sticker premiums rose about 26 percent. What enrollees pay rose 58 percent. And coverage decisions ride on the net payment, not the sticker price.

[beat]

Four. Drug negotiation kept rolling, round by round, through a change of administration. Each camp reads it through its own lens: proof of concept, price-fixing, or exhibit A.

[beat]

And five. The levers. Money can be moved by 51 votes. Program rules, and rescues, need 60. And when Washington gridlocks, the states become the laboratories, in both directions at once.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. Whether the Senate extends the credits in any form. Whether national work requirements repeat Arkansas's paperwork losses at fifty-state scale, or whether implementation learns. Whether drug negotiation survives its remaining litigation. And whether Pierson's backlash arrives, in the election cycles of 2026 and 2028. Nobody knows these answers yet.

There is no next lecture in this series. From here, the argument goes to you. A defensible position on American healthcare requires three commitments. A view on which model best fits American conditions: single-payer, regulated competition, or market discipline. A view on the path, one that takes seriously what each lever can and cannot move. And an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.

The calendar from 2026 to 2028 will supply that evidence: the realized coverage losses from the subsidy cliff, the first national data on work requirements, and the market's response to negotiated drug prices. When you are back at a screen, the lesson has a place to declare your position, and a steel-man drill against the perspective you are most confident is wrong.

For now, go back to the night of December 31, 2025. A deadline arrived on schedule, and the bills followed. [beat] More deadlines are already on the calendar. You know now who wrote them, which levers can move them, and what evidence to watch for when they arrive.
