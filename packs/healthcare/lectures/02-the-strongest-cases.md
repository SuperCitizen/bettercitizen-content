Vermont. December 17, 2014. Governor Peter Shumlin steps up to a podium.

Shumlin is a Democrat. He won office promising the country's first state single-payer system. And he made that promise in what is arguably the most single-payer-friendly state in America.

[beat]

At that podium, he abandons it.

[pause]

His own financing plan had put a price on the promise. An 11.5 percent payroll tax on Vermont employers. Plus a new income tax, sliding up to 9.5 percent.

And he concluded that "the potential economic disruption and risks would be too great to small businesses, working families and the state's economy."

[pause]

Now watch what three groups make of that one day.

Single-payer advocates read it as proof that the politics, not the economics, are the obstacle.

Market reformers read it as the economics finally being written down.

And incrementalists read it as the case for never betting everything on one leap.

> One day in Vermont, three readings. All three are defensible.

That is what makes this issue contested.

Last time, I asked you to take the design you trust least, and to state the best case for it. This lecture does that work three times. Each case comes the way its smartest adherents argue it, not the way its opponents caricature it.

By the end, you will be able to state each case as its advocates would, including what each one concedes.

We will take it in three steps.

First, the three cases, one at a time. Each gets the same treatment: where it starts, what it wants to protect, its argument, and what would change its mind. Then each one goes back to that podium in Vermont.

Second, the two experiments that every side quotes.

And last, what is settled, and what is not.

One thing before the cases: the ground they stand on. The United States spends roughly double the peer average per person, with worse average outcomes. And that gap is overwhelmingly a gap in prices, not in how much care Americans use.

That much is settled, or close to it. The three cases part ways over the cause, and over the cure.

## The single-payer case

First, the single-payer case: one federal payer, covering every American. You will hear it called Medicare for All.

Where does it start? It reads the cost record as evidence about the structure of the system, not about the behavior of individuals. Americans use an ordinary amount of care. What is different, on this view, is the payment structure, and what fragmentation does to prices, to administration and to access.

What it wants to protect is coverage: universal by definition, and impossible to lose.

The argument runs in four steps.

One. Fragmentation is the overhead.

There are thousands of plan variants, each with its own payment schedules and its own rules for approval. So every provider must keep a billing bureaucracy that produces no health.

The case points to hospitals. In the United States, administration takes about a quarter of hospital spending. In Canada and Scotland, where one payer funds the hospitals, the figure is about 12 percent.

Two. One payer is one buyer.

A single buyer covering every American could do what every peer government does: set or negotiate prices across a whole industry. Americans pay 2.78 times the peer average for drugs. The reason, the case argues, is not that peers innovate less. Their payers bargain as one, and America's fragmented insurers cannot.

Three. Coverage saves lives.

The case points to a 2017 review of the evidence that expanding Medicaid reduced mortality. And under one plan, churn stops existing. Nobody loses coverage by changing jobs, or by missing a renewal form.

And four, the politics.

A system of many payers makes the insurance industry a permanent veto player. Leave the industry intact, the case argues, and you leave the opposition intact.

[pause]

On cost, the case points to an estimate from the libertarian Mercatus Center. That study was headlined everywhere for 32.6 trillion dollars in new federal costs over ten years. [beat] But it also projected that total national health spending would fall, by about 2 trillion dollars.

Both can be true, because premiums and out-of-pocket payments disappear into taxes that total slightly less.

The study's own author stressed the catch. That result assumes paying every provider at Medicare rates. That is roughly a 40 percent cut to private prices, and it assumes providers absorb the cut without reducing supply.

[pause]

What would change this position? Evidence that single-payer systems systematically produce worse outcomes: longer waits, slower innovation, degraded access to specialists, at a scale that outweighs the gains in coverage and price.

Advocates concede that wait lists are real in Canada and Britain. They argue that those are budget choices, not structural necessities.

Now take this case back to Vermont. Its advocates read that day as proof that the politics, not the economics, are the obstacle. How would they explain what went wrong? Take a moment.

[long pause]

On this reading, Vermont proves only that a small state cannot do this alone. It has no leverage over drug prices, and employers and providers can easily exit across the border.

So the lesson is federal or nothing. [beat] Not never.

> The single-payer case: fragmentation is the overhead, and one payer ends it.

## The market-reform case

Second, the market-reform case. It is made most systematically at the Cato Institute, the libertarian think tank, and by the economist John Goodman, the intellectual father of Health Savings Accounts.

Where does it start? It denies that American healthcare is a market at all. Real markets have posted prices, informed buyers, free entry, and customers who can walk away. American healthcare has none of these.

Recall the appendectomy from last time. 1,529 dollars at one hospital. [beat] 182,955 dollars at another.

So the cost record, on this view, indicts not markets, but their absence.

What it wants to protect is the patient as a buyer: someone who can see a price, compare it, and walk away. It also wants reforms that can be undone.

The argument runs in four steps.

One. The third-party-payer problem.

When an insurer pays, the patient neither sees the price nor cares about it, so providers do not compete on it. The fix is structural. Pair high-deductible, catastrophic insurance with a tax-advantaged savings account. Then routine care is bought with money the patient treats as her own.

The case points to LASIK and cosmetic surgery, where Americans do pay directly. Posted prices and falling real costs follow.

Two. Supply is throttled by regulation.

In roughly two-thirds of states, laws let incumbent hospitals object to new competitors. That, the case argues, is a moat granted by government. Deregulating supply attacks the prices themselves, the actual documented problem. Single-payer, on this view, merely changes who pays them.

Three. The uninsured problem is narrower than it looks.

Of the 26.7 million uninsured, most are eligible for something, and are not enrolled. Targeted enrollment fixes and portable subsidies could close most of that gap, the case argues, without nationalizing the financing of everyone else's care.

And four, reversibility. This is Goodman's argument.

A market reform that fails can be repealed. A consolidated national system that fails cannot be, because every inefficiency acquires a constituency.

[pause]

On innovation, the case makes a wager. The American market supplies a disproportionate share of the world's drug and device revenue, and that revenue, it argues, funds the world's research. Impose peer-level prices through a single buyer, and global innovation would slow.

The revenue share is real. The effect on innovation is genuinely contested, and the strongest market reformers concede that.

[pause]

What would change this position? Evidence that price-shopping cannot discipline healthcare, even when it is genuinely enabled. That would mean the problem Arrow named, information asymmetry, is so severe that patients can never check providers.

Now take this case back to Vermont. Its advocates read that day as the economics finally being written down. What do they mean by that? Take a moment.

[long pause]

On this reading, advocates finally priced the tax swap honestly, and voters' revealed preference killed it. Voters, in short, would not pay the sticker price.

The economics failed. [beat] Not the politics.

> The market-reform case: the cost record indicts not markets, but their absence.

## The incrementalist case

Third, the incrementalist case: build on the Affordable Care Act, the ACA, one step at a time.

Where does it start? It points at the scoreboard. The framework exists. It survived four tests at the Supreme Court. And where it has been fully used, it has worked. So finish the job, the case argues, before betting the system on either rival theory.

What it wants to protect is the ground already gained, and the coverage that people already hold.

The argument runs in four steps.

One. Expansion worked where it was tried.

In 2010, the uninsured rate was 16.3 percent. By 2024 it was 8 percent, a fall that tracks the law's implementation almost mechanically. The problems that remain, on this view, are finite and addressable: 1.4 million people in the coverage gap, and millions more who are eligible but not enrolled.

Two. The marketplaces stabilized where states leaned in.

States that ran their own exchanges, funded outreach and added subsidies of their own built stable risk pools. And in the years of enhanced subsidies, enrollment reached a record 24.3 million. The instability, the case argues, is largely a story of under-implementation.

Three. A public option.

That is a Medicare-like plan, competing inside the marketplaces, and paying prices anchored to Medicare's. The case argues that it captures most of the gains of single-payer at a fraction of the disruption. It would discipline private premiums and provide a guaranteed fallback, without dissolving the employer coverage that roughly 165 million people hold.

The case points to Washington, Colorado and Nevada, which are running state versions now.

And four, transition risk. It is, the case argues, the under-priced variable.

Single-payer requires the largest peacetime fiscal swap in American history to go right the first time. Incrementalism spreads the risk across many reversible steps. And the same caution cuts against a full market design: no wealthy country runs healthcare on consumer shopping alone.

[pause]

What would change this position? Evidence that the framework has plateaued. If the coverage gap, underinsurance and affordability worsen despite full funding of the ACA's tools, the incrementalist concedes that the structural critics, one side or the other, have the better theory.

And the live test is uncomfortable for this camp. The framework is now being defunded faster than it is being completed.

Now take this case back to Vermont. Its advocates read that day as the case for never betting everything on one leap. How does that argument run? Take a moment.

[long pause]

On this reading, a governor with a mandate, a friendly legislature and four years could not execute the transition. That is the transition-risk argument in miniature.

And it is why reversible steps beat leaps.

> The incrementalist case: the framework works where it is fully used. Finish the job.

## Two experiments

Those are the three cases. Now the evidence.

American health policy has exactly two large randomized experiments, and every perspective claims pieces of both.

The first is the RAND Health Insurance Experiment, which began in 1971. It randomly assigned thousands of families to different insurance plans. Some families got free care. Others had to pay a substantial share of their own bills, which is called cost-sharing.

The families with substantial cost-sharing spent about 30 percent less than the families with free care. [beat] And there was no measurable harm to the average person's health.

That is the crown jewel of the market-reform case. Patients can bear prices without dying.

[pause]

But RAND found two complications.

Cost-sharing cut effective care and ineffective care indiscriminately. Patients are not the discerning shoppers the model wants.

And among the poorest, sickest participants, free care produced real gains, such as better control of blood pressure.

> Cost-sharing economizes bluntly. It cuts effective care and ineffective care alike.

The second experiment ran the opposite intervention. In 2008, Oregon held a literal lottery for places in Medicaid. That created a randomized trial of coverage itself.

Two years in, Medicaid had produced no statistically significant improvement in measured physical outcomes: blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar. That is the market-reform camp's favorite finding.

[pause]

But Oregon found more.

Medicaid increased the diagnosis and treatment of diabetes. It cut depression rates by about 30 percent. It virtually eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket costs.

And it raised use of the emergency department by about 40 percent. That falsified a comfortable bipartisan story, in which coverage pays for itself by emptying emergency rooms.

Let's put Oregon to work. One senator cites it to argue that Medicaid "doesn't improve health, so expansion is waste." A second senator cites it to argue that Medicaid "is a lifesaver."

Which findings is each one cherry-picking? Think it through.

[long pause]

The first senator is citing the null result on physical health at two years. It is real. But it ignores the drop in depression, the diabetes detected and treated, and the near-elimination of catastrophic medical costs. Protecting people from those costs is insurance's actual job.

The second senator is ignoring that the null results were genuine, and that emergency use rose.

Oregon disciplines both sides. It is the best evidence we have.

> The Oregon experiment hands no one a clean win.

That is why later, larger studies of mortality carry so much weight in the coverage debate.

And here is the honest summary on coverage. It is excellent insurance for finances and for mental health. Its payoff in physical health is slower, and harder to measure, than partisans on either side promised.

## What is settled, and what is not

So where does the evidence leave the three cases? Sort the claims into two piles.

The first pile is settled, or nearly so. It holds the ground we began on: roughly double the spending, and a gap made of prices. It holds three things more.

American administrative costs are the highest in the rich world.

Coverage reliably improves financial security and mental health, and the best evidence ties expansion to lower mortality.

And coverage churn on America's scale exists nowhere else among wealthy countries.

[pause]

The second pile is genuinely contested. Four things.

Whether regulated multi-payer systems, like Germany's, match single-payer performance at lower political cost. The international data do not cleanly separate them.

Whether cost-sharing has ever been tested with the price transparency and the supply freedom that reformers say it requires. RAND predates both.

How much American pricing drives global innovation. The revenue share is documented. [beat] The counterfactual is not.

And whether a single-payer transition is politically absorbable anywhere in America, after Vermont.

[pause]

Notice one thing that all three cases did when they had their turn. Each one named the evidence that would change its mind. That evidence has a name: a falsifier.

> A position with no falsifier is a mood, not a position.

One of these three cases probably convinced you least. Take that one. State the best case for it, as its advocates would, including what it concedes.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The single-payer case: fragmentation is the overhead, and one payer ends it. One rulebook, one buyer, and coverage that nobody can lose.

[beat]

Two. The market-reform case: the cost record indicts not markets, but their absence. Let patients see prices and spend their own money, and open up supply.

[beat]

Three. The incrementalist case: the framework works where it is fully used. Finish the job, add a public option, and avoid a leap that has to go right the first time.

[beat]

Four. The two experiments. In RAND, cost-sharing cut spending by about 30 percent with no measurable harm to average health, but it economizes bluntly. In Oregon, coverage protected finances and mental health, and showed no significant gain in physical health at two years. Oregon hands no one a clean win.

[beat]

And five. Every case names what would change its mind. A position with no falsifier is a mood, not a position.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: which case is right. A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and names what is contested.

For now, go back to that podium in Vermont. One governor, one plan, one day. [beat] You can now say what each side saw there. And you can say what it would take to change each side's mind.
