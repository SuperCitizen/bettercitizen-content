California, 2009. In one year, more than 19,000 adults are hospitalized for appendicitis. Same state. Same routine condition. Uncomplicated cases only.

Now look at what the hospitals charged.

The lowest charge was 1,529 dollars.

[beat]

The highest was 182,955 dollars.

[pause]

Researchers published those numbers. Even after they controlled for the patients and for the hospitals, they could not explain most of the variation.

And here is the stranger part. No patient could have learned the price in advance. And almost none of them paid it directly. An insurer, an employer, or a government program stood between the patient and the bill.

[pause]

So before we judge the system behind that bill, let's take the machine apart. By the end, you will be able to say what kind of machine produces a bill like that.

We will take it in four steps.

First, how the system got built. Nobody planned it.

Second, the map as it stands: who is covered, and who pays.

Third, the Affordable Care Act: what it did, and what it deliberately did not do.

And last, the record: what the spending buys, why it costs so much, and the three designs that reform proposals reach for.

## The accident

Start with a blunt fact.

> No one designed American healthcare.

Its central feature is insurance that comes from your job. And that feature is a side effect of fighting World War Two.

Here is how it happened. In 1942, inflation was looming, and twelve million workers were headed into uniform. Congress froze wages. Employers were competing for scarce labor, and now they could not offer raises. [beat] So they offered benefits.

In 1943, the National War Labor Board ruled that fringe benefits like health insurance, up to about five percent of wages, did not count as wage increases. That same year, the IRS issued a ruling of its own. What an employer paid into a group health plan was not taxable income to the worker.

In 1954, Congress made that tax break permanent, and quietly dropped the wartime cap.

[pause]

Think about what that does to a paycheck.

> A dollar of employer-paid premium is untaxed. A dollar of wages is not.

Put another way, a dollar that reaches you as health insurance goes further than a dollar that reaches you as pay. That difference is a subsidy, and it is one of the largest in the federal tax code. In 2022, it cost the Treasury an estimated 312 billion dollars.

For eighty years, that tax wedge has pulled working-age coverage toward the employment contract. It is why, almost uniquely among wealthy countries, losing your job in America so often means losing your insurance.

The second great layer came in 1965, with Medicare and Medicaid. They were public programs for the two groups that the employer system shut out by its structure: retirees, and the poor.

[pause]

One name to carry with you, because the whole debate leans on him. In 1963, Kenneth Arrow wrote the founding document of health economics. He argued that medical care breaks the textbook model of a market, and breaks it in predictable ways.

Illness is uncertain, so people demand insurance. Once they are insured, they bear little of the cost of one more treatment. That is called moral hazard.

The sick are likelier to buy coverage than the well, and that can unravel an insurance pool. That is adverse selection.

And doctors know vastly more than patients do, so trust and professional norms have to stand in for shopping. That is information asymmetry.

Every institution in this lecture is an attempted answer to a problem Arrow named. And the three positions you will meet next time all accept his diagnosis. [beat] They disagree about the cure.

Let's test what we have so far. Here is a case.

A software engineer quits her job to start a company. She discovers that replacing her family's coverage costs far more, after taxes, than it ever seemed to cost her as an employee.

Which policy choices produced that gap? Think it through.

[long pause]

Start with the IRS ruling of 1943, written into the tax code in 1954. Her employer's premium contributions were excluded from income and payroll taxes. Now she is buying her own plan, with after-tax dollars.

Then add the pooling advantage that Arrow identified. Her employer's plan priced her as one member of a large pool. The individual market prices her household by itself.

So the tie between her job and her insurance did exactly what an eighty-year-old tax wedge built it to do.

## Who is covered, and who pays

That is how it was built. Now the map as it stands today.

Americans get coverage from five sources. I will count them off, with one number each.

One: the job. About 165 million people under 65 have employer-sponsored insurance.

Two: Medicare, for people 65 and older, and for certain disabled people. About 69 million.

Three: Medicaid and CHIP, for low-income households, disabled people, and most poor children. About 76 million.

Four: the marketplaces that the Affordable Care Act created. They sell subsidized private plans to people who have no employer coverage. About 24 million people chose one for 2025.

And five: no coverage at all. In 2024, that was 26.7 million people under 65. [beat] More than 8 in 10 of them are in working families.

[pause]

One thing to draw out of that list, and it is the one to keep. There is no single system here.

> American healthcare is not one system. It is a multi-payer patchwork.

There are dozens of private insurers, under fifty-one different state regulatory regimes. There are three distinct public programs on top. And each piece has its own eligibility rules, its own payment rates, and its own paperwork.

Now follow the money. In 2024, national health spending reached 5.3 trillion dollars. That is 18 percent of GDP, the output of the whole economy.

Who pays it? Private insurance pays the largest share, 31 percent. Medicare and Medicaid come next. Patients pay 11 percent out of their own pockets.

But those shares understate the public role. Count the tax break we just met, and the coverage of public employees, and government already finances roughly half of American healthcare.

And what does the money buy? Most of us carry a guess, and the guess usually has a villain in it. So here are the real shares.

Hospital care takes 31 percent. Physician and clinical services take 21 percent. And retail prescription drugs, the most politically visible cost of all? [beat] 9 percent.

> Hospitals and physicians are more than half the spending. Retail drugs are 9 percent.

Let's put that to work. A candidate promises to cut national health spending dramatically. Drug prices are bankrupting America, the candidate says, and the plan is to make every prescription drug free to produce and distribute.

Of the 5.3 trillion dollars, roughly how much could that possibly save? Take a moment.

[long pause]

At most, about 9 percent. Retail prescription drugs were 467 billion dollars of the total. The true ceiling is somewhat higher, because some drugs given in hospitals and clinics hide in other categories. But it is nowhere near a majority.

That is why health economists keep steering the conversation away from drugs and toward provider prices. Any reform that leaves hospital and physician prices untouched is, by arithmetic, working the margins.

## The Affordable Care Act

Third stop. In 2010 came the largest restructuring since 1965: the Affordable Care Act, the ACA. It is routinely misdescribed, by its enemies and by its friends. So here is what it did. Five things.

One. It banned insurers from refusing people with pre-existing conditions, and from pricing on health status.

Two. It created subsidized marketplaces, with tax credits scaled to income, for people without employer coverage.

Three. It expanded Medicaid to all adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty line. That expansion was designed to be mandatory for the states. Hold on to that word, mandatory.

Four. It required most individuals to hold coverage, and enforced that with a tax penalty. This was the individual mandate. In 2017, Congress cut the penalty to zero. The requirement is still nominally on the books, but the enforcement is gone.

Five. It required large employers to offer coverage or pay a penalty. And it set essential health benefits that individual and small-group plans must cover, including preventive services at no cost.

[pause]

The Supreme Court has reshaped and re-ratified this law four times. One of those cases you should know by name: NFIB v. Sebelius, in 2012.

The Court upheld the individual mandate, as a tax. [beat] But it made the Medicaid expansion optional.

That ruling is the reason America still has expansion states and non-expansion states. As of mid-2026, 40 states plus D.C. have expanded. 10 states have not.

And the result? In 2010, the uninsured rate was 16.3 percent. In 2024, it stood at 8 percent.

Now the other list, the one that explains most of what the next two lectures argue about. What the ACA did not do. Three things.

It created no public option, and no single payer.

It left the employer system, and its tax break, almost entirely untouched.

And it made almost no direct attempt to regulate the prices that providers charge private payers.

[pause]

So when you hear the law described as a government takeover of healthcare, check the mechanics. Marketplace plans are private plans. Most people who gained Medicaid through the expansion are in privately run managed-care plans. Mechanically, the ACA expanded the customer base of private insurers.

A government takeover was proposed. It was called the public option, and it was stripped from the bill in the Senate, in 2009.

> The ACA nationalized insurance rules, not insurance.

Here is a case to work through. A cook in Texas is 45 years old and uninsured. She earns 14,000 dollars a year, which is below the poverty line. Texas is a non-expansion state. Her cousin lives in New Mexico, an expansion state, and earns the same.

Who can get covered, and why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The cousin can. New Mexico's Medicaid covers adults up to 138 percent of poverty.

The Texan falls into what is called the coverage gap. Her state never expanded. And the marketplace tax credits only begin at 100 percent of poverty, because the law's drafters assumed that Medicaid would catch everyone below that line.

NFIB v. Sebelius broke that assumption, and Congress never repaired the seam.

So she is too poor for the marketplace subsidies, and not eligible for her own state's Medicaid. About 1.4 million people are in that position.

## What the money buys

Last stop: the record. The whole debate runs on this comparison, so let's read it honestly, in both directions.

Start with cost. The United States spends 14,775 dollars per person on health. The average for comparable wealthy countries is 7,860. [beat] Roughly double.

Now results. Life expectancy at birth is 79 years in the United States. In most peer countries, it is about 82 to 84. American maternal mortality is the highest among high-income countries. And 8 percent of Americans are uninsured, where the peer countries stand at 1 percent or less.

> The United States spends roughly double per person, and dies younger on average.

Now the other direction. That 79 is a record high. Life expectancy rose 1.5 years in two years, as deaths from overdoses and from COVID fell. So the gap is not destiny. And the United States performs well on some specific measures: cancer screening and survival, and short waits for elective specialty care.

The deeper American problem is insecurity inside coverage. 23 percent of insured working-age adults are underinsured: what they could owe out of pocket is high relative to their income. And Americans owe at least 220 billion dollars in medical debt.

No peer country produces medical debt at that scale, because no peer country exposes patients to comparable cost-sharing.

[pause]

So why does the spending gap exist? The most-cited answer in the research is the title of a famous paper: "It's Still the Prices, Stupid."

Americans do not visit doctors more than their peers do, and they do not occupy hospital beds more. What is different is the price of each unit. American prescription-drug prices average 2.78 times those of peer countries. Hospital and physician prices run far above peer levels. And a billing apparatus built for many payers adds administrative cost on top.

> Americans do not use more care than their peers. They pay more per unit.

## Three models, all at once

Reform proposals nearly always reach for one of three designs. Each gets the same treatment here: where it runs, how it works, what it offers, and what comes with it.

The first is single-payer. It runs in Canada and in Taiwan. Everyone is enrolled in one government plan. What it offers: a single buyer's power over prices, and an end to people churning in and out of eligibility. What comes with it: the costs are financed by taxes, and the system's generosity becomes a budget decision.

The second is regulated multi-payer. It runs in Germany, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Private insurers keep competing, under heavy rules: everyone must be covered, insurers must take all comers, and prices are negotiated across a whole sector. What it offers: universal coverage with no single payer. What comes with it: permanent regulatory complexity.

The third is market-oriented. Singapore is the closest real case, and American conservative proposals draw on it. It pushes the first dollars of cost back to consumers, through savings accounts and catastrophic coverage. What it offers: price-sensitive patients, who are expected to discipline providers. What comes with it, even in Singapore: a government-run backstop, and a hospital sector with published prices.

Now a test of memory. A few minutes ago I counted off five sources of American coverage. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The job. Medicare. Medicaid. The marketplaces. And nothing at all.

Hold those against the three designs, and something falls into place.

Medicare Part A is single-payer financing: one government payer, funded by a payroll tax.

The Veterans Health Administration, the VA, is government provision, in the style of Britain's NHS: public hospitals and salaried physicians.

The marketplaces are regulated multi-payer: competing private insurers, under coverage rules.

Employer plans and the cash market are the market wing.

And 26.7 million people stand outside all of it.

> The United States is all three models at once, plus a gap.

It is not a fourth model. And that means every international comparison you will ever hear is, underneath, an argument about which of America's own subsystems should absorb the others.

You may already lean toward one of those three designs. So take the one you trust least. State the best case for it, the way its defenders would.

[pause 5s]

Keep your answer. The next lecture gives each case at full strength.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. No one designed American healthcare. Insurance comes from the job because of wartime wage controls, and a tax ruling in 1943. A dollar of employer-paid premium is untaxed. A dollar of wages is not.

[beat]

Two. Coverage comes from five sources: the job, Medicare, Medicaid, the marketplaces, and nothing at all. It is not one system. It is a multi-payer patchwork.

[beat]

Three. The money: 5.3 trillion dollars a year. Hospitals and physicians are more than half the spending. Retail drugs are 9 percent.

[beat]

Four. The ACA nationalized insurance rules, not insurance. It banned pricing on health status, built the marketplaces, and expanded Medicaid, which the Supreme Court then made optional. It created no public option, and made almost no attempt to regulate prices.

[beat]

And five. The record. The United States spends roughly double per person, and dies younger on average. Americans do not use more care than their peers. They pay more per unit. And the United States is all three models at once, plus a gap.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It gave you the machine. It has not told you what to think about any of it.

Is the cost gap caused by fragmentation? By the absence of real markets? Or by fixable seams in the ACA framework? Those are three different diagnoses, and the choice among them is genuinely contested.

The next lecture takes them up as three cases: single-payer, market reform, and building on the ACA step by step. And the record you have just heard is the shared evidence that all three must explain.

For now, go back to California. One routine operation. 1,529 dollars at one hospital, and 182,955 at another. [beat] You can now say what kind of machine produces a bill like that. What to do with the machine is the argument still to come.
