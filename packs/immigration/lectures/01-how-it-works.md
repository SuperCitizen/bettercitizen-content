October 3, 1965. The foot of the Statue of Liberty. Lyndon Johnson is signing an immigration bill called the Hart-Celler Act, and he tells the country not to worry about it.

Here are his words. "This bill that we will sign today is not a revolutionary bill. It does not affect the lives of millions."

[pause]

Not a revolutionary bill.

The bill's floor manager in the Senate, Edward Kennedy, had been more specific months earlier. "Our cities will not be flooded with a million immigrants annually," he said. And "the ethnic mix of this country will not be upset."

[pause]

Both men were sincere. And both predictions failed completely.

The United States now issues roughly a million green cards a year. In 1970, people born abroad were 4.7 percent of the population, the lowest share ever. By 2023 they were 14.3 percent.

And most of the new arrivals came from Latin America and Asia, the regions the 1965 system was expected to leave at the margins.

Almost every modern immigration argument is, one way or another, an argument about the machine that law built.

> So, before the argument, the machine.

This lecture argues for nothing. It shows you the machinery, so that you can test the arguments when they come.

We will take it in four steps. First, the doors: who gets a green card, and which door is biggest. Second, how the machine was built, law by law.

Third, the humanitarian door, where the system is most jammed. And last, the people who live outside the system altogether.

## The four doors

Permanent immigration means a green card. Its formal name is lawful permanent resident status, and there are four doors to it. Their relative sizes surprise most people, so listen for the proportions.

The first door is family, and it dominates: about 65 percent of green cards over the past decade.

The family door splits in two. The spouses, the unmarried minor children and the parents of U.S. citizens are called immediate relatives, and for them there is no numerical cap at all. Qualify, and a visa exists.

That single category is nearly half of all permanent immigration.

Everyone else at the family door is called family preference: adult children, siblings, and the spouses and children of green-card holders. That group is capped, and its queues are long.

As of 2026, the most oversubscribed line, Mexican-born siblings of U.S. citizens, was only just reaching applications filed in April 2001. [beat] A quarter-century in line.

The second door is employment, at about 16 percent. It is capped at 140,000 a year, and that number has a catch: it includes the workers' spouses and children. So the number of actual workers is roughly half the headline.

The third door is humanitarian: refugees resettled from abroad, and people already here who are granted asylum. It has averaged about 11 percent. It is also the system's most volatile door, for a reason we will come to.

The fourth door is the smallest, and it is called diversity. It is a lottery, created in 1990, of up to 55,000 visas a year, for people from countries with low recent immigration.

[pause]

Family. Employment. Humanitarian. Diversity.

> Family, not employment, is the center of gravity of American immigration.

Now, the caps. Two groups have none: immediate relatives, and people granted asylum. Every other door is capped.

And one limit deserves your full attention, because it drives more of the system's trouble than any other clause.

No country's nationals may receive more than 7 percent of the capped preference visas in a year. The size of the country does not matter, and neither does the demand. Iceland and India get the same allotment.

> India and Iceland get the same ceiling: 7 percent of the capped visas.

Here is what that means in practice. An Indian software engineer, approved today in an employment category called EB-2, faces a wait measured in decades. In mid-2026, that queue was still working through filings from 2013.

An otherwise identical applicant, born almost anywhere else, waits a small fraction of that.

And notice why the rule was written. It was meant as a guarantee against discrimination: no more national favorites, as under the old quota system. Two generations later, its effect is a queue that discriminates ruthlessly by birthplace.

Good intentions, mechanical consequences. You will meet that pattern again and again in these lectures.

Let's test it. Two colleagues, both U.S. citizens, file petitions on the same day.

She petitions for her sister, who was born in Mexico. He petitions for his sister, who was born in Belgium.

Same category. Same day. [beat] Same wait? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. Siblings of citizens are family preference. That group is capped, so the 7 percent ceiling applies.

Mexico's sibling queue is massively oversubscribed, and Belgium's is not. The Mexican-born sister will likely wait many years longer for the same visa.

Now change one fact. Suppose she were the citizen's spouse, or minor child, or parent. Then she is an immediate relative.

No cap. No per-country limit. No queue beyond processing time.

## How the machine was built

That is the machine as it stands. Now, how it was built.

Five laws carry most of the story, so hold on to five years.

1924. 1965. 1986. 1990. And 1996.

Start in 1924. The Johnson-Reed Act built the first comprehensive system: quotas by national origin, keyed to the census of 1890.

The quotas were deliberately engineered to freeze the country's ethnic composition, and to nearly zero out southern and eastern Europeans and Asians.

Then 1965, and the bill Johnson signed at the Statue of Liberty. Hart-Celler abolished the quotas. In their place it put two principles for choosing immigrants: family reunification, and skills.

Two details matter, and the first helps explain why the sponsors' predictions failed so badly.

In 1965, family reunification was the principle the restrictionists preferred. They assumed it would reproduce the existing population, which was mostly European.

It did not, because chain sponsorship compounds. One arrival sponsors a relative, and that relative sponsors another.

The second detail is a new cap: the first numerical limit ever on the Western Hemisphere. That ended a long era in which Mexican workers moved across the border legally and circularly, back and forth.

Migration patterns did not change when the law did. What changed was the share of that migration the law now counted as illegal.

Hold that thought. It becomes a central piece of evidence in the next lecture.

[pause]

Now 1986, and the Immigration Reform and Control Act, known as IRCA. This was the grand bargain.

One half was legalization, for roughly 2.7 million people. The other half was enforcement: the first federal sanctions on employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers, plus more enforcement at the border.

One half of that bargain worked as designed, and the other half failed. Here is the detail that decides which.

Employers had to check a new worker's documents. They did not have to verify those documents against any database.

So, which half failed? And what is the mechanical reason? Think it through.

[long pause]

The sanctions half. When documents are checked but never verified, a market in convincing fakes satisfies the letter of the law while defeating its purpose.

Within a few years, prosecutions dwindled and enforcement agencies deprioritized audits. The unauthorized population was about 3.5 million in 1990, by Pew's estimate. By 2007 it had more than tripled.

The legalization half worked. Most of its beneficiaries became permanent residents, and many became citizens.

> A mandate without a verification system is a request.

Every camp in the immigration debate cites IRCA as proof of its own thesis. That is why the next lecture treats it as the shared diagnostic.

Two more years, more briefly. The act of 1990 raised the caps into roughly their current shape. It created the diversity lottery, and it created Temporary Protected Status, or TPS.

And the act of 1996 built the modern enforcement architecture: expedited removal, mandatory detention, a one-year deadline for filing asylum claims. Most consequential of all were the three-year and ten-year bars.

The bars work like this. A person who builds up substantial unlawful presence, and then leaves the country, becomes inadmissible for years.

The bars were meant to deter illegal presence. In practice they punish departure, and so they lock people in.

[pause]

And there the building stops. Since 1996, no comprehensive immigration statute has passed.

## The humanitarian door

Now back to the third door, the volatile one.

It rests on the Refugee Act of 1980, which the Senate passed without a single nay vote. That act wrote the standard of the 1951 Refugee Convention into American law, and it created two distinct tracks.

The first track is refugee resettlement, and it happens abroad. Applicants are screened overseas, typically for one to two years, and then admitted up to an annual ceiling. The president sets that ceiling each fall.

It is the single most elastic number in immigration policy. The Biden administration set it at 125,000. The second Trump administration suspended admissions on taking office, and then set the ceiling at 7,500, the lowest in the program's history.

Same statute. Identical text. A ceiling of 125,000 one year, and 7,500 the next.

> The refugee ceiling moves on one signature: the president's.

That is what presidential discretion means in practice.

The second track is asylum, and it runs the other way. Asylum is claimed from inside the United States, or at a port of entry, generally within one year of arrival.

The applicant must show a "well-founded fear of persecution," on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group.

Follow one claim down the pipeline. First, the claim is filed.

Then, for someone arriving at the border, comes a screening interview with an asylum officer. The officer asks whether there is a "significant possibility" that the full claim could succeed. Fail the screening, and expedited removal follows, with no court hearing.

Pass it, and the claim goes to immigration court, for a full hearing before an immigration judge. Those courts are an arm of the Justice Department, not an independent judiciary.

Last comes the decision. A grant brings protection. A denial brings a removal order, which can be appealed.

One stage of that pipeline is where nearly everyone gets stuck. [beat] It is the court.

At the end of fiscal year 2019, about one million cases were pending. By December 2024, the number had peaked at roughly 3.75 million. In spring 2026 it still stood near 3.3 million.

Multi-year waits are the norm. And the backlog is itself a force in policy, because of what the waiting does.

A person with a weak claim gains years of residence from the wait alone, and after six months, permission to work. A genuine refugee sits in the same line.

> The backlog shelters weak claims and punishes strong ones.

Every serious reform proposal, restrictionist or expansionist, starts in the same place: the capacity to decide cases.

## The unauthorized population

One group stands outside all four doors: the unauthorized population.

For years, the standard description was "about 11 million, mostly long-settled." The border surge of 2021 to 2023 broke that description.

The most-cited estimate, from the Pew Research Center, put the unauthorized population at a record 14 million as of mid-2023. That was up from 10.5 million in 2021.

Honest numbers now come with error bars. But three features of that 14 million matter for every policy argument that follows.

First, it is two populations, not one. About 4.3 million adults had been in the country for 18 years or more. More than 4.2 million adults had arrived within the previous five.

The long-settled core is who the legalization debates are about. The recent arrivals are who the border debates are about. They are different groups with different politics, and rhetoric that blurs them is usually doing it on purpose.

[beat]

Second, "unauthorized" includes millions of people with quasi-legal status. Pew counts roughly 6 million who had some protection from deportation, but no permanent status.

They had pending asylum claims, or Temporary Protected Status, or DACA, or humanitarian parole. They are here legally in the everyday sense, and unauthorized in the statistical sense, both at once.

[beat]

Third, a large minority never crossed a border unlawfully. Of the roughly 11 million people in the population before the surge, about 42 percent entered legally on a visa, and overstayed.

Let's test that with two people. A student finishes her degree, stays on, and works off the books for six years. A man crossed the Sonoran desert unlawfully in the same year she enrolled.

Who is unauthorized? And whose situation is more common, in the population before the surge? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Both are unauthorized. The law does not grade the manner of becoming so. And her situation is roughly as common as his.

That matters, because the most salient enforcement tools, border walls and Border Patrol staffing, do not address the overstay pathway at all. Verification systems in the interior, like E-Verify, do.

So let go of the picture of someone who recently snuck across the border. Many overstayed a visa. Millions have lived here for fifteen years or more.

And millions of the arrivals since 2021 presented themselves at the border to claim asylum, the opposite of evading detection.

> Unauthorized is a legal category, not a behavior.

One last turn. After 2023, the curve bent hard. Border crossings collapsed and removals rose.

By Pew's estimate, the total foreign-born population fell by nearly 1.5 million in the first half of 2025. That was the first decline since the 1960s, and the third lecture takes it up in detail.

## Who holds which lever

One more thing before we gather this up, because the whole policy debate hangs on it. Who can change each part of this machine?

Congress owns the structure: the categories, the caps, the citizenship rules, the bars. None of it has been substantially rebuilt since 1996, mostly because immigration bills die by filibuster.

The executive owns the throttle: enforcement priorities, asylum procedure, and the power to admit people case by case, which is called parole.

One more item belongs on the executive's list, and you have met it already. A number in this lecture went from 125,000 to 7,500 on one signature. Which number was it?

[long pause]

The refugee ceiling, which the president sets. And that is why policy now swings violently between administrations, without a word of the statute changing.

The states own the margins. Under a 2012 case, Arizona v. United States, they cannot run their own immigration-enforcement schemes. But they decide things like driver's licenses, in-state tuition, and how much their police cooperate with federal officers.

And under a 1982 case, Plyler v. Doe, they cannot exclude undocumented children from public schools.

> Congress owns the structure. The executive owns the throttle. The states own the margins.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. There are four doors to a green card: family, employment, humanitarian, and diversity. Family is the biggest, at about 65 percent. Family, not employment, is the center of gravity.

[beat]

Two. The caps. Immediate relatives of citizens face no cap at all. In the capped lines, India and Iceland get the same ceiling: 7 percent. That rule is where the waits of decades come from.

[beat]

Three. The machine was built by statute. Quotas in 1924. Their abolition in 1965. A bargain in 1986, whose legalization worked and whose employer sanctions failed. And since 1996, nothing comprehensive.

A mandate without a verification system is a request.

[beat]

Four. The humanitarian door has two tracks. Refugees come from abroad, under a ceiling that moves on one signature. Asylum is claimed from inside, and it runs through a court backlog in the millions.

That backlog shelters weak claims and punishes strong ones.

[beat]

And five. The unauthorized population, about 14 million in 2023, is really two populations: the long-settled, and the newly arrived. Unauthorized is a legal category, not a behavior.

[pause]

Carry one map with you as well. Congress owns the structure. The executive owns the throttle. The states own the margins.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Does this machine admit too many people, or too few? Is its composition right? What should be done about the people already here?

Those are contested questions, on which serious people divide.

The next lecture takes them on. It builds the three main positions, each at its strongest: the restrictionist, the expansionist, and the comprehensive-reform centrist.

For now, go back to the Statue of Liberty, in 1965, and to a bill that was not supposed to be revolutionary. Good intentions. Mechanical consequences. [beat] You know the machine now. Next comes the argument.
