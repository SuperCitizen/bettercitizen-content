Two numbers. Hold them side by side.

December 2023. Customs and Border Protection logs more than 300,000 encounters at the southwest border. It is the highest monthly total ever recorded.

September 2025. The Border Patrol makes fewer than 8,400 apprehensions there. And the total for that full fiscal year is the lowest since 1970.

[pause]

Now the strange part. Between those two numbers, not one word of the permanent immigration statute changed.

No new visa category. No new cap. No legalization. No rewrite of asylum law passed Congress.

The entire reversal, the sharpest in the history of the data, was built from four things. Executive action. Cooperation from foreign governments. Court fights. And one enormous appropriation.

> Immigration policy is now made almost everywhere except where the Constitution puts lawmaking power.

In other words, almost everywhere except Congress. The last lecture gave you three cases. This one is a map of where policy is actually made.

One caution before we start. Every number and every court case here is as of mid-2026, and several were still moving.

We will take it in four steps. First, the numbers, and what each one measures. Second, the money. Third, the executive actions, and how they fared in court. And last, what Congress and the states have done, and have not.

## The numbers, and what each one measures

Every political claim you will hear cites a slice of that trajectory. So begin with definitions, because these terms are routinely confused, and sometimes deliberately.

An encounter counts an event, not a person. It may be an apprehension by the Border Patrol between ports of entry, or a finding at a port that someone is inadmissible. One person who crosses three times is three encounters.

A removal is a formal deportation, with legal consequences: re-entry becomes a felony. A return is a departure without a removal order.

And net migration is the only number that captures what the others miss: people quietly leaving, or never coming. It is estimated, not counted.

[pause]

Once more, because everything after this depends on it. An encounter is an event. A removal is a formal deportation. Net migration is an estimate.

Now the picture, on those definitions.

At the border, fiscal year 2023 set the record, with nearly 2.5 million encounters. In fiscal year 2025, Border Patrol apprehensions were the lowest since 1970.

[beat]

Removals. ICE reported about 320,000 in fiscal year 2025, up from 271,000 the year before. That is substantial, and it is far below the administration's stated goal of a million a year.

Analysts dispute the government's much larger public claims, which fold in other border actions, returns, and estimates of what is called self-deportation.

[beat]

Detention. About 73,000 people were being held in January 2026, the highest number ever recorded. Analyses of the government's own data found that roughly seven in ten detainees had no criminal conviction.

[beat]

And net migration. By Pew's estimate, the foreign-born population fell by about 1.5 million in the first half of 2025, the first sustained decline since the 1960s.

Economists at Brookings and AEI estimate that net migration for 2025 was negative: the first such year in roughly five decades.

[pause]

Let's test those definitions. Three politicians are speaking.

The first says: "Encounters are down 90 percent. The problem is solved."

The second says: "Removals hit a record. It's a reign of terror."

The third says: "Net migration is negative. We're losing workers."

Can all three be accurate at once? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Yes. They measure different things.

Encounters count events at the border, and those are down to the levels of 1970. Removals count formal deportations: about 320,000, which is high but not unprecedented. Whether it is a record depends on which series and which definition you use.

And net migration sets arrivals against all departures, including voluntary ones that no agency counts directly.

> Each number is real. Each is selected for its rhetorical job.

So the first move, with any claim about immigration data, is to check which metric it rides on.

## The money

Now the money. The legislative event of this period was not an immigration bill. It was a budget bill.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July 4, 2025, was a budget reconciliation package. Inside it was roughly 170 billion dollars for immigration enforcement, the largest such investment in American history.

About 45 billion dollars of that is for detention. Before the act, 41,500 detention beds were funded. The new money, by the American Immigration Council's estimate, is enough for at least 116,000.

Tens of billions more go to border barriers, and to ICE hiring and operations. And 13.5 billion dollars is there to reimburse states and localities that help with enforcement. Remember that last figure. It comes back.

Why could this pass, when every immigration bill since 1996 has died?

[beat]

Because of the route it took. A reconciliation bill needs 51 votes in the Senate, not 60. But reconciliation can only move money.

Changes of policy, such as new visa categories, legalization, or asylum standards, generally cannot survive the limits of that process, which are known as the Byrd rule.

Both parties have met that wall. In 2021, the Senate parliamentarian ruled legalization provisions out of order in a Democratic reconciliation attempt. In 2025, the same constraint shaped what Republicans could include.

So here is the asymmetry, stated plainly. Under current Senate rules, enforcement can be funded on a party-line vote. The legal immigration system can only be rebuilt on a bipartisan one.

> Enforcement can be funded by one party. Rebuilding legal immigration takes two.

Put another way: the filibuster does not block immigration policy neutrally. It selects which kind of immigration policy is possible.

And that explains the shape of the whole landscape. Record detention funding sits beside visa caps untouched since 1990. A refugee ceiling of 7,500 sits beside a Refugee Act that was never repealed.

Whoever holds the presidency steers enforcement, with money and executive orders. The permanent statute stays frozen. So judging any actor in this system starts with knowing which levers they actually hold.

## The executive, and the courts

Third, the executive actions. The second Trump administration opened at speed: by the Migration Policy Institute's count, 181 immigration-related executive actions in its first 100 days.

What survived review by the courts is itself a civics lesson. Here is the scorecard, as of mid-2026.

Parole first. Under the Biden administration, parole programs had admitted hundreds of thousands of Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans and Venezuelans. Those programs were terminated in March 2025, and a district court blocked the mass termination.

Then Temporary Protected Status, or TPS. A court order was protecting the TPS designation for Venezuela.

In May 2025, the Supreme Court lifted both orders, and let the terminations proceed while litigation continued. So far, then, those actions have survived.

Together, they moved hundreds of thousands of people from quasi-legal status into the removable population.

[beat]

Refugee admissions. Suspended in January 2025. Then the ceiling for fiscal year 2026 was set at 7,500, the lowest ever. An emergency determination in May 2026 raised it to 17,500.

[beat]

The Alien Enemies Act. In March 2025, the administration invoked that law of 1798 to remove alleged gang members to El Salvador. The question reached the Supreme Court twice within months.

The Court required that detainees receive notice, and a chance to seek habeas review before removal. That is a due-process floor. Litigation over the act's use in peacetime continued. So that action was checked, in part.

[pause]

And then the big one, still pending: birthright citizenship.

An executive order signed on January 20, 2025, asserts that children born here to parents who are unauthorized, or only temporarily present, are not citizens.

That is a direct challenge to the settled reading of the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause, and of an 1898 case, United States v. Wong Kim Ark. Through 2025, every court to reach the merits ruled against the order.

But on June 27 of that year came a case called Trump v. CASA. The Supreme Court ruled for the administration, six to three. Listen to what it ruled on.

The question was about remedies, not about the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court held that universal injunctions, orders that block a policy for everyone, nationwide, exceed the authority Congress gave the federal courts in 1789.

So a district court could not block the executive order for everyone. It could block it only for the parties in front of it.

Now a friend tells you that the Court "upheld ending birthright citizenship." What actually happened? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The Court decided what kind of order a lower court may issue. It expressly did not rule on whether the executive order is constitutional.

> Trump v. CASA decided a question of remedies, not of citizenship.

And within weeks, plaintiffs used the avenues the Court had left open, class actions and suits by states, and the order remained blocked.

The merits case is called Trump v. Barbara. The Supreme Court heard it on April 1, 2026, with a decision expected by early July.

So state the position precisely. As of mid-2026, no court had permitted the order to take effect, and the constitutional question was awaiting the Court's answer.

[pause]

Two more cases to watch.

One is DACA, the program of deferred action for childhood arrivals. In January 2025, the Fifth Circuit ruled that the DACA regulation is unlawful. But it narrowed the relief to Texas, and it left renewals running nationwide. A final round at the Supreme Court is widely expected.

Roughly 600,000 people held DACA as of 2023. They have now spent fourteen years on two-year renewable permits, through three administrations, eight Congresses, and a dozen failed legislative fixes.

It is the clearest single illustration of what policy by executive action costs the people living under it.

The other is the asylum backlog. It peaked at roughly 3.75 million cases in December 2024, and stood around 3.3 million in spring 2026.

It did not fall because cases were decided faster. It fell because new border cases collapsed, and because the government moved to dismiss or close large numbers of pending ones.

## Congress, and the states

Last, the two players we have not yet heard from. Congress first.

Its modern record on comprehensive reform is short. In 2007, reform failed in the Senate.

In 2013, a bill with a path to citizenship, mandatory E-Verify and expanded visas passed the Senate, 68 to 32. It never got a vote in the House.

In 2024, a deliberately narrow bipartisan bill on the border and asylum failed twice in the Senate, after presidential-campaign politics turned against it.

Then 2025 demonstrated the alternative: money, on a party-line vote.

Pull something back from earlier. Why could the money pass, when those bills could not? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Reconciliation. It needs 51 votes, not 60, and it can only move money.

So the bills that would change policy stay where they are. Bills to codify DACA, to mandate E-Verify nationally, to reallocate green-card categories: all remain introduced, sponsored, and short of 60 votes.

[pause]

Now the states. A 2012 case, Arizona v. United States, still draws the boundary. Immigration enforcement is federal.

The Court struck down Arizona's state-crime provisions, while letting status checks during lawful stops stand. So states act at the margins.

But the margins have been busy.

In-state tuition for undocumented students, once available in roughly two dozen states, is contracting. Florida repealed its waiver in early 2025.

In June 2025, the Justice Department sued Texas over its law of 2001. The state declined to defend it, and a federal judge permanently blocked it within hours.

On cooperation, the states have pulled further apart. Texas and Florida have put state resources into federal enforcement, including state E-Verify mandates. Sanctuary jurisdictions have limited their cooperation with ICE, and fought in court.

The 13.5 billion dollars for cooperating states raises the stakes of that divergence.

And some things remain state choices. As of recent counts, 19 states plus D.C. issued driver's licenses regardless of status. And a case called Plyler v. Doe still guarantees schooling, from kindergarten through high school, everywhere.

Let's test the boundary. A state legislature, frustrated with federal policy, passes three laws.

One: a state crime of unlawful presence. Two: a requirement that every employer in the state use E-Verify. Three: a bar on undocumented children enrolling in public schools.

Which of them survive? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the second.

A state crime of unlawful presence is preempted under Arizona v. United States, because regulating immigration status is federal. Barring children from public school violates Plyler v. Doe.

But a mandate to verify employees falls within a state's traditional authority over licensing. The Court upheld Arizona's in 2011, and several states have them.

> States regulate at the edges, not membership itself.

Work, licenses, benefits: those are the edges.

## Three readings of one landscape

Before we gather this up, come back to the three cases from the last lecture. Each of them now points to this landscape.

The restrictionist points to the border, where Border Patrol apprehensions fell to their lowest since 1970. That collapse in unlawful crossings is the proof of concept.

> The restrictionist reading: enforcement, sufficiently resourced, works.

The expansionist points to net migration. It turned negative, and the economists who estimated it project measurably slower growth, in GDP and in consumer spending, as a consequence.

> The expansionist reading: negative net migration will bring measurably slower growth.

The centrist points to Congress. The border is, by every metric, controlled, and the sequencing theory predicts that legalization politics should now ease. The test is whether any legalization measure moves in the 2027 Congress.

> The centrist reading: a controlled border should ease the politics of legalization.

All three are pointing at the same dashboard.

Now choose the reading you find least convincing. Make its best case, the way its strongest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Know what each number measures. An encounter is an event, not a person. A removal is a formal deportation. Net migration is an estimate.

Each number is real. Each is selected for its rhetorical job.

[beat]

Two. The money. Roughly 170 billion dollars for enforcement passed by reconciliation, on a party-line vote. Enforcement can be funded by one party. Rebuilding legal immigration takes two.

[beat]

Three. Trump v. CASA decided a question of remedies, not of citizenship. As of mid-2026, no court had let the birthright order take effect, and the merits were before the Supreme Court.

[beat]

Four. Also as of mid-2026: the terminations of parole and of TPS had largely been cleared to proceed. And the Fifth Circuit had ruled the DACA regulation unlawful, with renewals still running.

[beat]

And five. States regulate at the edges, not membership itself. An E-Verify mandate survives. A state crime of unlawful presence does not, and neither does a ban from public school.

[pause]

Here is what these lectures have not settled, and will not settle for you. A defensible position on immigration needs four things.

A view on scale and composition, the structural question Congress has not touched since 1990. [beat] A view on the long-settled unauthorized population, which is the legalization question, and is distinct from the border question.

A view on enforcement: not for or against, but which tools, at what intensity, with what due-process guarantees, now that the funded capacity exists. [beat] And an honest accounting of what evidence would change each view.

Where does the argument go from here? To two experiments, both now running. Does negative net migration produce the predicted economic drag? And does control of the border unlock the politics of legalization?

When you are back at a screen, the lesson has a place to declare your own position. It also has a steel-man drill, against whichever perspective you find least persuasive.

For now, go back to the two numbers. More than 300,000 encounters in one month. Fewer than 8,400 apprehensions in another.

Between them, not one word of the statute changed. [beat] Now you know what did.
