April, 1980. Fidel Castro briefly opens the port of Mariel. By October, about 125,000 Cubans have sailed to Florida.

Most of them settle in Miami. Within a few months, the city's labor force has grown by roughly 7 percent.

Economists call a sudden shock like that, imposed from outside, a natural experiment. If immigration depresses the wages of native workers, Miami in 1980 is where you should see it.

The economist David Card looked, in a famous paper of 1990. He found next to nothing. Wages and unemployment for Miami's less-skilled workers tracked those of comparison cities, as if the boatlift had never happened.

For twenty-five years, that result anchored the economics of immigration.

Then, in 2017, the economist George Borjas reanalyzed the same episode. He found that wages for Miami's high-school dropouts, the workers most like the new arrivals themselves, had fallen by 10 to 30 percent.

[pause]

> Same city, same shock, same public data, opposite conclusions.

How is that possible? The answer matters, because the Mariel dispute is the whole empirical debate in miniature.

Last time, the machine. This time, the argument, in three steps. First, how honest economists reach opposite conclusions, and where the profession lands. Second, three cases, each as its smartest advocates argue it: the restrictionist, the expansionist, and the comprehensive-reform centrist. And last, what the evidence settles, and what it cannot.

## How honest economists disagree

Start with what each of them measured. Card compared Miami's less-skilled workers, broadly defined, against four comparison cities.

Borjas made two changes. First, he narrowed the group to high-school dropouts. At least 60 percent of the Mariel arrivals lacked a diploma, so that is where the pressure should concentrate. Second, he restricted the sample to men of prime working age.

In that thin slice, wages cratered after 1980. On the restrictionist reading, Card's broad averages had diluted a real injury to the most vulnerable native workers, until it disappeared.

Then came a third study. Michael Clemens and Jennifer Hunt found that the survey sample behind Borjas's result had changed composition at exactly the wrong moment.

Around 1980, the Census Bureau improved its coverage of Black men. Within a few years, the Black share of that tiny Miami subsample roughly doubled, from about a third to about two-thirds.

Black dropouts earned substantially less, for reasons unrelated to Mariel. So the measured wage decline could be an artifact of who was being surveyed.

Clemens and Hunt argued it was large enough to account for the entire Borjas effect. Borjas argued in reply that the wage drop persists, even after accounting for the changing racial mix.

[pause]

The exchange is sharp-elbowed but substantive, and it teaches lessons worth keeping. Small subsamples magnify survey artifacts. One treated city has no replication to fall back on. And above all:

> Which subgroup you examine is a theoretical choice, not a neutral one.

So where does the profession land? The fullest answer is a consensus report from the National Academies of Sciences. Two of its findings matter here.

The first is about wages. Immigration's effect on native wages overall is small. To the extent that negative effects exist, they concentrate on two groups: workers without a high-school education, and prior immigrants, who are the closest substitutes for new arrivals.

The second is about public budgets, and it is an asymmetry. At the state and local level, first-generation immigrants cost more than they contribute, and schooling is the big line item. At the federal level, the long-run picture is positive.

And the second generation, the immigrants' children raised in the United States, are among the strongest net fiscal contributors in the population.

[pause]

In short: small overall, concentrated at the bottom. A cost at the local level, a gain at the federal level. Hold on to both.

Let's test what we have. Card found no wage effect. Borjas found a decline of 10 to 30 percent. Neither made an arithmetic error. [beat] Which choices produced the gap? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Two were Borjas's. He narrowed the group to high-school dropouts, and he restricted the sample to prime-age men. Both choices are defensible, and both are consequential.

Clemens and Hunt added a third factor: the composition of the sample, which changed just as the boatlift hit.

The dispute is about research design, not arithmetic. That is why it has never fully resolved.

## The restrictionist case

Now the three cases, each built the same way: what it protects, its argument, its evidence, and what would change it.

Begin with the restrictionist, and with what this position is not. It is not "immigration is bad."

The strongest restrictionist position is that the current scale and composition of immigration impose concentrated costs, which the political conversation undercounts. And that a sovereign people is entitled to choose a lower number.

What it protects: the workers and communities who carry those costs, and a people's right to decide.

> The restrictionist case: concentrated costs, and a people's right to choose a lower number.

The argument has four planks.

First, wages. The restrictionist takes the National Academies' finding at face value, and argues that "small on average, concentrated at the bottom" is a distributional choice.

The gains flow broadly. The costs land on the narrow slice of workers, native and earlier immigrant, who compete directly with new arrivals. Borjas, in a book called We Wanted Workers, argues that the profession's "small effects" framing systematically launders that redistribution.

[beat]

Second, budgets. The restrictionist points to the same report: first-generation immigrants, especially those with less than a high-school education, are net costs at the state and local level.

And school boards and county hospitals cannot borrow against the second generation's future federal taxes. A federal surplus seventy-five years out does not fund a school district this year.

[beat]

Third, the rule of law. The restrictionist argues that tolerating an unauthorized population of 14 million, whatever the virtues of its individual members, teaches that immigration law is negotiable. It invites the next wave, and it sustains a labor tier beneath the law, vulnerable to exploitation.

In Mark Krikorian's version of the argument, a polity should not maintain a permanent caste outside its law.

[beat]

Fourth, social cohesion. The restrictionist points to a 2007 study by Robert Putnam. He found that in the short run, residents of more ethnically diverse American communities reported lower trust, of their neighbors and of their own group.

Putnam himself emphasized that societies rebuild broader identities over time, and he opposed restrictionist uses of his data. The restrictionist answers that the point survives the caveat.

Transitions have real costs. The pace of change is a policy variable. And dismissing cohesion concerns as disguised racism has been politically catastrophic for immigration liberalism.

[pause]

What would change this position? Its strongest holders concede that the empirics are contested. They rest the case, in the end, on sovereignty: the people's right to choose a number, which no regression can refute.

On the empirical planks, two things would change it. Evidence that wage and fiscal effects on the least-skilled are negligible, even when measured the restrictionists' preferred way. And evidence that cohesion costs dissipate quickly.

## The expansionist case

Now the expansionist, built the same way. Begin again with what the position is not. It is not "borders shouldn't exist."

The strongest expansionist position is that immigration is the United States' principal renewable resource. That the measured harms are small, localized and fixable. And that the moral claims of families and refugees deserve a weight no spreadsheet captures.

What it protects: the country's growth and future workforce, and the families and refugees with the most at stake.

> The expansionist case: large gains, fixable harms, and moral claims no spreadsheet captures.

The argument has five planks.

First, the gains, which the expansionist argues are the best-established fact in the literature. The National Academies panel called immigration "integral to the nation's economic growth," with small-to-zero long-run wage effects on natives overall.

[beat]

Second, demography, where the expansionist points to arithmetic. The U.S. fertility rate is about 1.6, far below the replacement level. Without migrants, the working-age population shrinks, and the payroll-tax base under Social Security and Medicare shrinks with it.

The moderate version of the argument needs only this: the U.S. fiscal architecture assumes positive net migration.

[beat]

Third, crime. The expansionist points to a 2018 study by Light and Miller, covering fifty states plus D.C., from 1990 to 2014. It found that undocumented immigration does not increase violent crime.

The undocumented population tripled over a period in which the national violent-crime rate roughly halved. Salient individual atrocities are real, and politically potent. But policy built on the aggregate pattern, the expansionist argues, points the other way.

[beat]

Fourth, the expansionist argues, enforcement alone has backfired before. Douglas Massey and Karen Pren found that the border buildup after 1986 increased the settled unauthorized population.

Crossing got dangerous and expensive. So seasonal Mexican migrants, who had circulated home for decades, stopped leaving, and sent for their families. Enforcement converted a circular flow of labor into a permanent resident population: the precise opposite of its goal.

[beat]

Fifth, family unity, which the expansionist calls a moral claim, not a loophole. About 4.6 million children who are U.S. citizens live with an unauthorized parent.

So mass removal is, unavoidably, a policy of separating citizens from their parents, or of exiling citizens in childhood. Expansionists insist that this cost be stated plainly in any honest accounting.

[pause]

What would change this position? Evidence that sustained high inflows durably depress wages at the low end, or overwhelm schools, housing and local budgets faster than the gains compound.

Or a demonstration that the surge of 2021 to 2023 produced exactly the local fiscal strain the National Academies' framework predicts. Critics of the expansionists argue that several big-city budgets showed it.

## The comprehensive-reform centrist case

Third, the comprehensive-reform centrist, built the same way.

The centrist position holds that the system fails on restrictionist terms and on expansionist terms at the same time. And that the fix is a package: more legal pathways, sized to labor demand. Enforcement that actually verifies. And legalization for the long-settled. All traded together, because none can pass alone.

What it protects: the bargain itself, one trade that answers what each of the other camps rejects in the system.

> The centrist case: the system fails both camps at once, so fix it together.

The argument has five planks.

First, the status quo is the worst plausible system. Restrictionists reject scale and illegality, and the system delivers both. Expansionists reject family separation and a labor tier without rights, and the system delivers both.

A design that fails this thoroughly survives, the centrist argues, only because each coalition prefers the issue to the deal.

[beat]

Second, IRCA, the bargain from the last lecture, and what exactly it showed. Legalization worked. Paper-based employer sanctions failed.

The centrist argues that the inference is not "never legalize." It is "never again legalize without a verification system that works."

Mandatory E-Verify with biometric anchoring is technically feasible. It has been blocked for decades by an alliance of employer lobbies and of civil-liberties and immigrant-rights groups.

[beat]

Third, legal pathways, which the centrist calls the pressure valve. Most unauthorized labor migration responds to real American demand. Where legal channels exist and scale, employers use them. Where channels are capped far below demand, the difference arrives anyway, unlawfully.

So cap reform tied to labor-market indicators would shrink two things at once: the unlawful flow restrictionists hate, and the exploitable workforce expansionists hate.

[beat]

Fourth, asylum triage, the centrist's test case. A backlog of millions of cases fails genuine refugees, who spend years in limbo. It also fails rule-of-law values, because the wait itself is the magnet.

More asylum officers and judges, with decisions in months, is the rare reform that both camps' logic endorses.

[beat]

Fifth, sequencing. The centrist points to a long-standing argument on the center-right: voters will accept legalization only after the border is demonstrably controlled.

The enforcement regime of 2025 and 2026, whatever one thinks of it, has produced the lowest border numbers in half a century. So the theory predicts that legalization politics should now become easier.

Whether that happens is among the most informative natural experiments American politics will run this decade.

[pause]

What would change this position? Continued proof that the grand-bargain coalition cannot assemble. The attempts of 2007, 2013 and 2024 all failed. At that point, centrism collapses into a choice between the other two camps' agendas.

## What is settled, and what is not

Three cases. Now set them against the evidence.

In the expansionist case, you heard that tougher border enforcement after 1986 grew the settled unauthorized population. Can you rebuild the mechanism? And what lesson would each camp draw from it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The mechanism first. When crossing became dangerous and costly, circular migrants stopped going home. They settled permanently, and brought their families north.

The expansionist draws this lesson: enforcement alone backfires, and legal channels for circulation would shrink the resident population.

The restrictionist draws this one: the enforcement of 1986 was fake, porous and unserious, so the test of real enforcement had not been run. Real enforcement, on that reading, is interior, employer-focused and sustained.

Both readings are about the same verified finding. The disagreement is about the counterfactual: what would have happened otherwise.

[pause]

So sort what you have heard. First, what is settled, or nearly so.

In the long run, the aggregate economic effects of immigration on natives are small and positive. [beat] Undocumented immigration does not raise violent-crime rates at the population level. [beat] And IRCA's employer sanctions failed, while its legalization held.

Now what is contested.

The size, and even the sign, of wage effects on the least-skilled. That one is unresolved by design, not by sloppiness. [beat] The fiscal balance, which flips by level of government, by education mix, and by time horizon. [beat] Whether the cohesion costs Putnam measured are transitional or durable. [beat] And whether enforcement credibility unlocks legalization politics.

Beneath all of that lies the question of values: what number, and what mix, of newcomers the country should choose.

The foreign-born share of the population traces a U. It was driven down by the quotas of 1924, and it climbed for five straight decades after 1965.

Restrictionists see the right side of that U as an unprecedented experiment. Expansionists see the left side as proof that the country has absorbed this share before, and prospered.

The historian Aristide Zolberg adds a corrective for everyone. Immigration levels in America have always been engineered policy choices.

> The U-shape is not weather. Each turn of the curve was legislated.

The present argument produces the next segment of that line. And no dataset can settle it.

> What number the country should choose is not an empirical question.

Now choose the case you found least persuasive. State it the way its smartest advocate would. Then name the evidence that advocate says would change their mind. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Mariel. Same city, same shock, same public data, opposite conclusions. The gap came from research design: which subgroup you examine is a theoretical choice, not a neutral one.

[beat]

Two. The restrictionist case: concentrated costs, and a people's right to choose a lower number. Its evidence: wage effects at the bottom, costs at the state and local level, and Putnam on trust.

[beat]

Three. The expansionist case: large gains, fixable harms, and moral claims no spreadsheet captures. Its evidence: the aggregate gains, demography, the crime findings, and 4.6 million citizen children.

[beat]

Four. The centrist case: the system fails both camps at once, so fix it together. Its evidence: IRCA, where legalization worked and paper-based sanctions failed, and a sequencing theory now being tested.

[beat]

And five. The evidence settles some things. Overall effects on natives are small and positive, and undocumented immigration does not raise violent-crime rates. It leaves the wage effect on the least-skilled contested. And it cannot settle the number.

What number the country should choose is not an empirical question.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not chosen among the three, and it will not. A defensible position picks one of the three frames, states which contested evidence it is betting on, and names what would change it.

The next lecture asks where those bets now stand: who is actually making immigration policy, and how.

For now, go back to Miami, in 1980. One city, one shock, and the same public data. [beat] You know now how honest people reached opposite conclusions there. And you can state all three cases built on that ground.
