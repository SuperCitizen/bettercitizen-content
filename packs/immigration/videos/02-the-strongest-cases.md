## Same city, opposite answers

Between April and October 1980, about 125,000 Cubans sailed from the port of Mariel to Florida. Most settled in Miami, whose labor force grew roughly 7 percent in a few months.

David Card studied it in 1990. He found that wages for Miami's less-skilled workers tracked comparison cities as if the boatlift had never happened. In 2017, George Borjas reanalyzed the same episode and found that wages for Miami's high-school dropouts had fallen 10 to 30 percent.

- Card, 1990: no wage effect
- Borjas, 2017: down 10 to 30%

> Same city, same shock, same public data, opposite conclusions.

## A dispute about design

Neither man made an arithmetic error. Borjas narrowed the group to high-school dropouts, and to prime-age men.

- Which subgroup: a choice, not neutral

Then Michael Clemens and Jennifer Hunt showed that the survey itself changed at that moment. The Census Bureau improved its coverage of Black men around 1980. The Black share of the small Miami sample roughly doubled. Black dropouts earned less for reasons unrelated to Mariel, so the measured decline could be an artifact of who was surveyed. Borjas replied that the drop persists.

- Small samples magnify survey artifacts

## The restrictionist case

The lesson gives three positions, each as its smartest advocates argue it. Restrictionists argue that the current scale and composition impose concentrated costs, on the least-skilled workers and on state and local budgets. They argue that a sovereign people is entitled to choose a lower number.

> The restrictionist case: concentrated costs, and a people's right to choose the number.

## The expansionist case

Expansionists argue that immigration is the United States' principal renewable resource, and that the measured harms are small, localized and fixable. They argue that the moral claims of families and refugees deserve weight no spreadsheet captures.

> The expansionist case: established gains, fixable harms, and the claims of families.

## The centrist case

Comprehensive-reform centrists argue that the system fails on both of those terms at once. They argue that the fix is a package: more legal pathways, enforcement that actually verifies, and legalization for the long-settled.

> The centrist case: a package, traded together, because no part passes alone.

## What no dataset can settle

Some of this is close to settled, and some is not. The size, and even the sign, of wage effects on the least-skilled is contested. So is the fiscal balance, which flips by level of government.

- Contested: wage effects on the least-skilled

Beneath it all is a values question: what number and mix of newcomers the country should choose.

- The number to choose: not an empirical question

The lesson gives each case its planks and the evidence its holders say would change their minds, and it explains why these coalitions scramble the parties.
