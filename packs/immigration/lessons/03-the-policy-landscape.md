# The current policy landscape

In December 2023, Customs and Border Protection logged 302,034 encounters at the southwest border — the highest monthly total ever recorded. In September 2025, the Border Patrol made 8,386 apprehensions there — and the full fiscal year's total, 237,538, was the lowest since 1970. Between those two numbers, not one word of the permanent immigration statute changed. No new visa category, no new cap, no legalization, no asylum rewrite passed Congress. The entire reversal — the sharpest in the history of the data — was built from executive action, foreign-government cooperation, court fights, and one enormous appropriation. That is the defining fact of the current landscape: American immigration policy is now made almost everywhere except where the Constitution puts the lawmaking power. This lesson maps where it is actually made, with the numbers as of mid-2026.

## The numbers, and what each one measures

Start with the trajectory, because every political claim you will hear cites a slice of it:

| Period | Southwest border figure | What it was |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FY2019 | 977,509 total encounters | Pre-pandemic high of the first Trump term |
| FY2023 | 2,475,669 total encounters | Record fiscal year |
| Dec. 2023 | 302,034 encounters in one month | Record month |
| FY2024 | about 2.1 million encounters | Decline begins after June 2024 asylum restrictions |
| FY2025 | 237,538 Border Patrol apprehensions | Lowest since 1970 |
| Sept. 2025 | 8,386 apprehensions (11,647 total encounters) | Monthly figures below 10,000 from spring 2025 on |

Source: CBP; CRS. Now the definitional discipline, because these terms are routinely — sometimes deliberately — confused. An **encounter** counts an event, not a person: a Border Patrol apprehension between ports, an inadmissibility determination at a port of entry, or (until May 2023) a pandemic-era expulsion; one person crossing three times is three encounters, and high encounters can coincide with high *releases* or high *returns* depending on policy. A **removal** is a formal deportation with legal consequences (re-entry becomes a felony); a **return** is a departure without a removal order. **Net migration** is the only number that captures what the others miss — people quietly leaving, or never coming — and it is estimated, not counted.

On those definitions, the mid-2026 picture: ICE reported 319,980 removals in FY2025 (the Migration Policy Institute counts about 340,000 including voluntary departures), up from 271,000 in FY2024 — substantial, but far below the administration's stated million-per-year goal, and analysts dispute the government's much larger public claims, which fold in CBP actions, returns, and estimates of "self-deportation." Detention reached about 73,400 people in January 2026, the highest ever recorded; analyses of the government's own data found that roughly seven in ten detainees had no criminal conviction. The foreign-born population fell by about 1.5 million in the first half of 2025 (Pew) — the first sustained decline since the 1960s — and economists at Brookings and AEI estimate 2025 net migration between −295,000 and −10,000: the first net-negative year in roughly five decades. Those same economists project measurably slower GDP and consumer-spending growth as a consequence — the expansionist prediction from Lesson 2, now running as a live experiment, just as the collapse in unlawful crossings is the restrictionist proof-of-concept that enforcement, sufficiently resourced, works. Both camps are pointing at the same dashboard.

<p>Yes — they measure different things. Encounters count border <em>events</em> (down to 1970 levels); removals count formal deportations (about 320,000 in FY2025, high but not unprecedented — and "record" depends on which series and definition you use); net migration nets arrivals against all departures, including voluntary ones no agency counts directly. Each number is real; each is selected for its rhetorical job. Checking <em>which metric</em> a claim rides on is the first move of immigration-data literacy.</p>

## The money: enforcement by reconciliation

The legislative event of this period was not an immigration bill. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, was a budget-[[term:reconciliation]] package — and inside it, roughly $170 billion for immigration enforcement, the largest such investment in American history. The major line items, per the bill text and the American Immigration Council's accounting: about $45 billion for detention (enough, by AIC's estimate, to expand capacity from the previously funded 41,500 beds to at least 116,000), roughly $30 billion for ICE hiring, transportation, and operations, about $46.5 billion for border-barrier construction, $13.5 billion to reimburse states and localities for enforcement help, and billions more for CBP facilities and technology.

Why could *this* pass when every immigration bill since 1996 has died? Because reconciliation needs 51 Senate votes, not 60 — but reconciliation can only move *money*. Policy changes — new visa categories, legalization, asylum standards — generally cannot survive the process's Byrd-rule limits; the Senate parliamentarian ruled legalization provisions out of order from a Democratic reconciliation attempt in 2021, and the same constraint shaped what Republicans could include in 2025. The result is a structural asymmetry worth stating plainly: under current Senate rules, *enforcement can be funded on a party-line vote, but the legal-immigration system can only be rebuilt on a bipartisan one.* The [[term:filibuster]] doesn't block immigration policy neutrally; it selects which kind of immigration policy is possible.

> **Why this matters:** Every durable feature of the current landscape — record detention funding alongside untouched visa caps from 1990, a 7,500-refugee ceiling alongside an unrepealed Refugee Act — traces to this asymmetry. Whoever holds the presidency steers enforcement with money and [[term:executive-order]]s; the permanent statute stays frozen. Judging any actor in this system starts with knowing which levers they actually hold.

## The executive actions, and their survival rate in court

The second Trump administration opened with an immigration blitz — by Pew's count, 181 executive actions in its first 100 days — and the pattern of what survived judicial review is itself the civics lesson. As of mid-2026:

- **Parole terminations: survived (so far).** The CHNV programs — which had paroled hundreds of thousands of Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, and Venezuelans under the Biden administration — were terminated in March 2025. A district court blocked the mass termination; the Supreme Court stayed that injunction on May 30, 2025, over two noted dissents, letting terminations proceed while litigation continues. The CBP One asylum-appointment system was shut down on inauguration day.
- **TPS terminations: largely cleared to proceed.** On May 19, 2025, the Court lifted the order protecting the 2023 Venezuela TPS designation; terminations and litigation over several other countries' designations continued through 2025–26. Combined, the parole and TPS actions moved hundreds of thousands of people from quasi-legal status into the removable population — the mechanism behind part of the unauthorized-population shift you saw in Lesson 1.
- **Refugee admissions: suspended, then floor-set.** Admissions were suspended in January 2025; the FY2026 Presidential Determination set the ceiling at 7,500 — the lowest ever — prioritizing Afrikaners from South Africa.
- **The Alien Enemies Act gambit: checked, partly.** The administration's March 2025 invocation of the 1798 Alien Enemies Act to remove alleged gang members to El Salvador reached the Supreme Court twice within months; the Court required that detainees receive notice and a chance to seek habeas review before removal — a [[term:due-process]] floor — while litigation over the act's peacetime use continued.
- **Birthright citizenship: the big one, still pending.** Below.

<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> In <em>Trump v. CASA</em> (June 27, 2025), the Supreme Court ruled for the administration in the birthright-citizenship litigation. A friend concludes the Court "upheld ending birthright citizenship." What actually happened?</summary>
<p>The Court decided a <em>remedies</em> question, not the Fourteenth Amendment question: 6–3, it held that universal (nationwide) injunctions exceed the equitable authority Congress gave federal courts in 1789, so district courts could not block the executive order for everyone — only for parties before them. It expressly did not rule on whether the order is constitutional. Within weeks, plaintiffs used the avenues the Court left open (class actions, state suits), and the order remained blocked. The merits case, <em>Trump v. Barbara</em>, was argued April 1, 2026, and is awaiting decision as of this writing.</p>
</details>

## The courts: three cases to watch from mid-2026

**Birthright citizenship.** Executive Order 14160 (January 20, 2025) asserts that children born here to unauthorized or temporarily present parents are not citizens — a direct challenge to the settled reading of the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause and *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* (1898). Every court to reach the merits ruled against the order through 2025; after *CASA* curtailed universal injunctions, a certified nationwide class kept it blocked. The Supreme Court heard the merits in *Trump v. Barbara* on April 1, 2026, with a decision expected by early July 2026. However it comes out, state it precisely: as of this writing, no court has permitted the order to take effect, and the constitutional question awaits the Court's answer.

**DACA.** The Fifth Circuit ruled in January 2025 that the DACA regulation is unlawful under the INA — but narrowed relief to Texas, severed work authorization from deportation forbearance, and left renewals running nationwide (initial applications are accepted but not processed). The case sits back before the district court, with a final Supreme Court round widely expected. Roughly 600,000 people held DACA as of 2023; they have now spent fourteen years on two-year renewable permits through three administrations, eight Congresses, and a dozen failed legislative fixes — the clearest single illustration of what executive-action-as-policy costs the people living under it.

**The asylum system and the backlog.** The immigration-court backlog peaked near 3.7 million cases in early 2025 and stood around 3.3 million in spring 2026 (TRAC) — down not because adjudication sped up, but because new border cases collapsed and the government moved to dismiss or close large numbers of pending ones. Meanwhile litigation continued over the asylum restrictions layered on since June 2024, and over a new front: employment-based immigration, where a September 2025 proclamation imposing a $100,000 fee on new H-1B petitions from abroad drew conflicting district-court rulings in December 2025 — one upholding it, one vacating it — teeing up appellate resolution.

## What Congress has and hasn't done

The modern record, for calibration: comprehensive reform failed in 2007 (Senate), passed the Senate 68–32 in 2013 (S. 744: citizenship path, mandatory E-Verify, expanded visas) but never got a House vote, and in 2024 a deliberately narrow bipartisan border-and-asylum bill failed [[term:cloture]] twice after presidential-campaign politics turned against it. Then 2025 demonstrated the alternative: money on a party-line vote. Bills to codify DACA, mandate E-Verify nationally, reallocate green-card categories, or move to a points-style selection system all remain introduced, sponsored — and short of 60 votes. The sequencing experiment from Lesson 2 is therefore live: the border is, by every metric, controlled; the centrist theory predicts legalization politics should now ease. Watch whether any legalization measure — Dreamers are the perennial test case — moves in the 2027 Congress. That outcome will be the single most informative data point this issue has produced in a generation.

## What states can do, and the lines that moved

*Arizona v. United States* (2012) still draws the [[term:federalism]] boundary: immigration enforcement is federal — the Court struck Arizona's state-crime provisions while letting status checks during lawful stops stand — so states act at the margins. But the margins have been busy, and in 2025–26 they moved in one direction:

- **In-state tuition for undocumented students**, once available in roughly two dozen states, is contracting: Florida repealed its waiver in early 2025, and in June 2025 the Justice Department sued Texas over its 2001 law — the state declined to defend it, and a federal judge permanently blocked it within hours, a template DOJ then aimed at other states (litigation over the maneuver continues).
- **Cooperation regimes** diverged further: states like Texas and Florida embedded state resources in federal enforcement (Operation Lone Star; state E-Verify mandates), while sanctuary jurisdictions limited ICE cooperation and fought funding-condition and National Guard deployments in court. The $13.5 billion in reconciliation money for cooperating states raises the stakes of that divergence.
- **Licenses and benefits** remain state choices: 19 states plus D.C. issued driver's licenses regardless of status as of recent counts, and *Plyler v. Doe* still guarantees K-12 schooling everywhere.

Which survive?</summary>
<p>Only (2). A state unlawful-presence crime is preempted under <em>Arizona v. United States</em> — immigration status regulation is federal. Excluding children from public school violates <em>Plyler v. Doe</em> (1982). But employer-verification mandates fall within states' traditional licensing authority (the Court upheld Arizona's in <em>Chamber of Commerce v. Whiting</em>, 2011), and several states have them. States regulate at the edges — work, licenses, benefits, schooling above K-12 — not membership itself.</p>
</details>

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. immigration as of mid-2026 requires:

1. A view on **scale and composition** (the structural question Congress hasn't touched since 1990).
2. A view on **the long-settled unauthorized population** (the legalization question — distinct, remember, from the border question).
3. A view on **enforcement** — not "for or against," but *which tools, at what intensity, with what due-process guarantees*, now that the funded capacity exists.
4. An honest accounting of **what evidence would change each** — including the two live experiments: whether negative net migration produces the predicted economic drag, and whether border control unlocks legalization politics.
