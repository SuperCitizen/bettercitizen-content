# The current policy landscape

Comprehensive immigration reform has been attempted three times in modern memory: 2007 (failed in Senate), 2013 (passed Senate 68-32, died in House), and 2024 (Senate cloture failed despite bipartisan negotiation). Between attempts, executive action and state-level experimentation have been where actual policy change has happened.

## What's on the table federally

**Comprehensive reform.** The 2013 S. 744 framework is the most-developed: pathway to citizenship for ~11M undocumented (after fines, back taxes, and waiting periods), expanded employment-based visas, mandatory E-Verify, additional border resources, and an updated visa system. Has passed the Senate once (2013); has never received a House vote. Most commentators consider this approach politically dormant.

**Border + asylum overhaul.** The 2024 bipartisan Senate negotiation (Border Act of 2024) focused narrowly on asylum and border enforcement: increased enforcement capacity, faster adjudication, raised credible-fear standard, new emergency authority to close border crossings during high-volume surges. Failed cloture despite bipartisan support after political dynamics shifted. Variants will return.

**DACA codification.** Multiple bills (American Dream and Promise Act, Dream Act variants) would convert DACA's executive-action protection into permanent statute for the ~600,000+ recipients and ~2M Dreamers (people who would qualify but didn't register). Has passed the House several times; has not cleared the Senate's 60-vote threshold.

**Skills-based selection reform.** Bills (RAISE Act and variants) to shift immigration toward skills-based selection (Canadian/Australian point system) and away from family-based. Has not passed either chamber but has consistent restrictionist and centrist support.

**Employer-verification mandates.** Bills to make E-Verify mandatory for all U.S. employers. Currently mandatory in some states and some federal contractors; voluntary nationwide. Has passed the House; has not cleared the Senate.

The political map: like healthcare, immigration is a heavily filibustered topic. The 60-vote threshold has been the binding constraint on every comprehensive proposal in modern memory.

## What the executive branch can do

**Enforcement priorities** — which categories of removal ICE prioritizes (criminal records, recent entrants, all unauthorized residents). Each administration sets these.

**Asylum procedures** — credible-fear standards within statutory limits, processing speed, where applicants wait during proceedings.

**Refugee admission ceiling** — set annually by the President. Has ranged from 18,000 (FY 2020) to 125,000 (FY 2022) in recent years.

**DACA** — currently in legal limbo. The Supreme Court has held (Regents) that rescission requires APA process; a Texas district court has held the program substantively unlawful in a ruling currently being appealed.

**Workplace enforcement** — ICE worksite operations, I-9 audits, employer prosecutions. Each administration sets the intensity.

**Discretionary parole programs** — admitting people on a case-by-case basis under §212(d)(5) of the INA. The Biden administration used this for Ukrainians, Venezuelans, Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, and Afghans; restrictionists argue this stretches the statute beyond what Congress intended.

These are all reversible. The structural system is set by Congress; the executive can shift enforcement and procedure but not the underlying law.

## What states can do (and what's preempted)

*Arizona v. United States* (2012) established that states cannot create immigration crimes or enforce federal immigration law beyond what federal law authorizes. But significant state authority remains:

- **Driver's licenses for undocumented residents** — 19 states + D.C. issue them; states can choose.
- **In-state college tuition for undocumented residents** — 24 states + D.C. permit.
- **Sanctuary policies** (limiting state/local cooperation with ICE) — varies by state and city.
- **State E-Verify mandates** — 8 states require E-Verify for some categories of employers.
- **State-level law enforcement of immigration crimes** — pre-empted broadly post-Arizona, but states retain authority over ordinary criminal law.

State-level action is where most concrete policy change has happened post-2013, both restriction-leaning (Texas Operation Lone Star, Florida's E-Verify mandate) and expansion-leaning (California's TRUST Act limiting ICE cooperation).

## The next likely fights

- **Asylum overhaul.** The 2024 Border Act framework will likely return in some form. Whether it can pass depends on the political dynamics around the next presidential administration.
- **DACA's legal status.** The Texas district court's substantive ruling is on appeal. Eventual Supreme Court review is likely. If DACA is struck down, the legalization-of-Dreamers question becomes a forcing function for legislation.
- **Title 42-equivalents.** Future emergency-authority claims to expel migrants without asylum hearings, modeled on the Title 42 pandemic-era policy.
- **Employment-verification mandates.** Mandatory E-Verify proposals may become more bipartisan if comprehensive reform stays politically blocked, because both restrictionists and centrists support stronger employer accountability.
- **Skills-based reform.** Shifting toward Canadian/Australian-style point systems has been a long-running restrictionist + centrist proposal that hasn't passed but stays on the agenda.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. immigration requires:

1. A view on **what the right scale and composition of immigration is** (the structural question).
2. A view on **what to do about the existing ~11M undocumented population** (the legalization question).
3. A view on **how enforcement should work** (the enforcement question).
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change each.

The Coach in this app will engage you on each of these. The Mirror will save your declared position; the steel-man drill will push you to construct the strongest version of the views you don't hold.
