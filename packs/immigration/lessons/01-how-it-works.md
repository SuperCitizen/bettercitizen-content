# How U.S. immigration actually works

Most arguments about U.S. immigration policy talk past each other because the participants are picturing different parts of a complex system. Start with the structure.

## The four lawful-entry categories

U.S. immigration law admits people in roughly four categories (numbers per FY 2023):

1. **Family-based** — about 65% of all permanent ("green card") immigration. Spouses, children, parents, and siblings of U.S. citizens or permanent residents. The 1965 INA reorganization made this the dominant pathway, replacing the national-origin quotas of 1924.

2. **Employment-based** — about 13% of permanent immigration. Five preference categories ranging from "extraordinary ability" (EB-1) to skilled workers (EB-3) to investor visas (EB-5). Per-country caps mean that nationals from large countries (India, China) face decade-plus waits.

3. **Refugee and asylum** — variable, around 10-15% in recent years. Refugees apply from outside the U.S. and are admitted in fixed numbers set annually by the President; asylum-seekers apply from inside the U.S. or at the border based on a "well-founded fear of persecution."

4. **Diversity visa lottery** — about 50,000 per year. Lottery for nationals of countries underrepresented in U.S. immigration. Has been a target of restrictionist proposals.

Plus large nonimmigrant (temporary) categories — H-1B specialty workers, F-1 students, B-1/B-2 tourists, H-2A/H-2B agricultural and seasonal workers — which don't lead to permanent status directly.

The total annual permanent immigration is roughly 1 million people. The U.S. foreign-born population is ~46 million (~14% of total population), the highest absolute number in U.S. history but a smaller share than the historical peak (15% in 1890).

## The undocumented population: who they actually are

The most frequently misunderstood part of the system. As of 2022 (Pew Research):

- **~11 million** undocumented people total.
- **~80% have lived in the U.S. for more than 10 years.** This is not a recent-arrival population.
- **~40-50% are visa overstays**, not unauthorized border crossings. Someone who came on an F-1 student visa, finished school, and didn't leave is undocumented in the same legal sense as someone who crossed unauthorized at the border.
- **~6 million** have U.S.-citizen children. Family separation is not a hypothetical.
- **~600,000+** are DACA recipients (brought to the U.S. as children, registered with the federal government, currently in legal limbo).

The "border crisis" framing covers a different population: people arriving recently, often seeking asylum, often from Central America, Venezuela, Cuba, or Haiti. This is a real and large flow (FY 2023 saw ~2.5M border encounters) but is largely separate from the long-resident undocumented population.

## The asylum process

Asylum is governed by the Refugee Act of 1980, which incorporated U.S. obligations under the 1951 UN Refugee Convention. The process:

1. **Apply** — at a port of entry or after entering the U.S., within one year of arrival.
2. **Credible-fear interview** — for those apprehended at the border, an asylum officer determines whether the claim has at least a "significant possibility" of succeeding. Pass rate varies by year and administration.
3. **Asylum hearing** — a full hearing before an immigration judge. The applicant must show "well-founded fear of persecution" on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group.
4. **Appeal** — to the Board of Immigration Appeals, then to a federal Court of Appeals.

Current backlog (TRAC Immigration data): ~3.5 million pending cases, average wait ~3-4 years. The backlog is itself a major policy issue — it incentivizes meritless applications (because the wait alone effectively grants years of U.S. residence) and disadvantages genuine refugees (whose claims sit in the queue alongside everyone else's).

## What the executive branch can do alone

Important constraint: the President has substantial authority over enforcement, asylum procedure, and refugee admissions (within statutory caps), but cannot change visa categories, numerical caps, or pathways to permanent status without legislation. Major recent executive actions:

- **DACA** (2012, Obama) — deferred deportation for people brought as children. Currently in legal limbo; courts have held the program substantively lawful but procedurally flawed.
- **Migrant Protection Protocols ("Remain in Mexico,"2019)** — required asylum-seekers to wait in Mexico during proceedings. Reversed by Biden, partially restored by court order, then ended again.
- **Title 42** (2020-2023) — used pandemic public-health authority to expel migrants without asylum hearings. Ended May 2023.
- **CBP One app** (2023-) — mobile-app-based scheduling for asylum-seekers.
- **Various enforcement-priority memos** — directing ICE to focus on certain categories of removal (criminal records, recent entrants) rather than long-resident undocumented.

These are reversible. The structural system — green-card categories, numerical caps, citizenship rules — has not been substantially reformed since 1986.

## The 1986 reform and what it produced

The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 (IRCA) was the last major immigration legislation. It:

1. Legalized ~3 million people who had been living in the U.S. unlawfully since 1982.
2. Made it illegal to knowingly hire undocumented workers (the first time U.S. law had imposed sanctions on employers).
3. Increased border enforcement.

The empirical record (now well-studied):

- The legalization worked technically — most of the 3M became permanent residents, then citizens.
- The employer sanctions did not work — verification was minimal, fraud was rampant, and enforcement was de-prioritized within years.
- The undocumented population grew from ~3M in 1990 to ~11M by 2007. The 1986 reform was the last "comprehensive" reform; the post-1986 growth is what successive reform proposals have tried to address.

Restrictionists read the IRCA experience as evidence that legalization without effective enforcement creates more incentive for unauthorized entry. Expansionists read it as evidence that the legalization worked and that the failure was on the enforcement side. Centrists read it as evidence that any future reform needs to genuinely deliver on enforcement to be politically sustainable.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Restrictionists** argue the current immigration level (legal + unauthorized) imposes costs on labor markets, fiscal balances, social cohesion, and rule-of-law norms that the country has been undercounting for decades.
- **Expansionists** argue immigration has been the central driver of U.S. economic dynamism and demographic stability, and that labor and family ties are core moral claims regardless of the political feelings about scale.
- **Comprehensive-reform centrists** argue both the restrictionist and expansionist positions get the structure right but the policy wrong: the path forward is more legal pathways (which addresses expansionist concerns) plus more credible enforcement (which addresses restrictionist concerns), tied together in legislation that no party currently has the votes to pass alone.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
