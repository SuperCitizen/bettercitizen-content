# How U.S. immigration actually works

On October 3, 1965, at the foot of the Statue of Liberty, Lyndon Johnson signed the Hart-Celler Act and told the country not to worry about it: "This bill that we sign today is not a revolutionary bill. It does not affect the lives of millions." The bill's Senate floor manager, Edward Kennedy, had been more specific months earlier: "our cities will not be flooded with a million immigrants annually," and "the ethnic mix of this country will not be upset." Both men were sincere. Both predictions failed completely. The United States now issues roughly a million green cards a year; the foreign-born share of the population, 4.7 percent in 1970 — its all-time low — reached 14.3 percent by 2023, within sight of the 1890 record of 14.8 percent, and most of the new arrivals came from the Latin American and Asian countries the 1965 system was expected to leave at the margins. Almost every modern immigration argument is, one way or another, an argument about the machine that law built. So before the argument: the machine.

## The four doors, and which one is biggest

Permanent immigration — a green card, formally lawful permanent resident (LPR) status — comes through four doors. Their relative sizes surprise most people, so look at the proportions first.

**Family** is the dominant door, about 65 percent of green cards over the past decade. It splits in two. *Immediate relatives* of U.S. citizens — spouses, unmarried minor children, parents — are numerically uncapped: qualify and a visa exists, which is why this single category alone is nearly half of all permanent immigration. *Family preference* covers everyone else (adult children, siblings, spouses and children of green-card holders) under a statutory floor of 226,000 a year, and the queues are long enough that the most oversubscribed category — Mexican-born siblings of U.S. citizens — is only now reaching applications filed in April 2001 (State Department Visa Bulletin, 2026): a quarter-century in line.

**Employment** is about 16 percent: five preference categories (EB-1 "extraordinary ability" through EB-5 investors) capped at 140,000 a year — a number that, counterintuitively, includes the workers' spouses and children, so the actual number of capped *workers* is roughly half the headline.

**Humanitarian** admission — [[term:refugee]]s resettled from abroad plus [[term:asylum]] grantees already here — has averaged about 11 percent, but it is the system's most volatile door, because the refugee half is set annually by the President (more below).

**Diversity** is the smallest door: a lottery, created in 1990, of up to 55,000 visas (about 51,850 effective for DV-2026, after offsets owed to other statutes) for nationals of countries with low recent immigration.

Around all of this moves a much larger *nonimmigrant* (temporary) world — F-1 students, B tourists, H-2A farmworkers, and the H-1B specialty-occupation visa — that grants no permanent status directly but feeds the green-card queues, since employers sponsor many H-1B workers for EB visas.

## What the law caps, and what the caps actually do

The Immigration and Nationality Act sets a "worldwide level" of 675,000 permanent admissions, but the cap is deliberately permeable — immediate relatives and asylees don't count against it — which is how actual admissions run near 1.1 million. The numbers worth memorizing:

| Category | Annual limit | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate relatives of U.S. citizens | None | Demand-driven; ~46% of all green cards |
| Family preference | 226,000 (floor) | Decade-plus waits in backlogged categories |
| Employment-based (EB-1 to EB-5) | 140,000 | Spouses and children count against the cap |
| Diversity lottery | 55,000 statutory (about 51,850 effective) | Created 1990; perennial repeal target |
| Per-country ceiling | 7% of preference visas | Same limit for India as for Iceland |
| H-1B (temporary) | 65,000 + 20,000 U.S. advanced-degree | Annual lottery; oversubscribed within days |

Read that per-country line again, because it drives more of the system's pathology than any other clause. No country's nationals may receive more than 7 percent of the capped preference visas in a year — roughly 25,600 — regardless of population or demand. Iceland and India get the same allotment. The result is that an Indian software engineer approved for an EB-2 visa today faces a wait measured in decades — the queue in 2026 was still working through mid-2013 filings — while an otherwise identical applicant born almost anywhere else waits a small fraction of that. The 7 percent rule was written as an anti-discrimination guarantee — no more national favorites, as under the old quota system — and its effect, two generations later, is a queue that discriminates ruthlessly by birthplace. Good intentions, mechanical consequences: a pattern you will see repeatedly in this pack.

<Checkpoint question="A U.S. citizen petitions for her Mexican-born sister; her colleague petitions the same day for his Belgian-born sister. Same category, same day — same wait?">
  No. Siblings of citizens are *family preference* (capped), and the 7 percent per-country ceiling applies. Mexico's sibling queue is massively oversubscribed, Belgium's is not, so the Mexican sister will likely wait many years longer for the same visa. If she were instead the citizen's *spouse, minor child, or parent*, she'd be an immediate relative — no cap, no per-country limit, no queue beyond processing time.
</Checkpoint>

## How the machine was built: 1924 to 1996

Five statutes explain nearly everything on the books today. Hold the sequence first; the paragraphs below unpack what each one actually did.

The **1924 Johnson-Reed Act** built the first comprehensive system: national-origin quotas keyed to the 1890 census, deliberately engineered to freeze the country's ethnic composition and to nearly zero out southern and eastern Europeans and Asians. The **1952 McCarran-Walter Act** consolidated the law into the Immigration and Nationality Act — still the statutory skeleton — but kept the quotas. The **1965 Hart-Celler Act** abolished them, substituting family reunification and skills as the selection principles. Two details matter. First, family reunification was the *restrictionists'* preferred principle in 1965 — they assumed it would reproduce the existing (mostly European) population, which is one reason the sponsors' predictions failed so badly: chain sponsorship compounds. Second, the act imposed the first-ever numerical cap on the Western Hemisphere, ending a long era in which Mexican workers moved legally and circularly across the border. Migration patterns did not change when the law did — what changed was the share of it that the law now counted as illegal. Hold that thought; it becomes a central piece of evidence in Lesson 2.

The **1986 Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA)** was the grand bargain: legalization for roughly 2.7 million people — long-resident immigrants present since before 1982, plus a parallel agricultural-worker program — paired with the first federal sanctions on employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers, plus more border enforcement. The legalization half worked as designed — most beneficiaries became permanent residents, many became citizens. The employer-sanctions half did not: verification relied on easily forged documents, prosecutions dwindled within years, and the unauthorized population, about 3.5 million in 1990 by Pew's estimate, had more than tripled by 2007. Every camp in the immigration debate cites IRCA as proof of its own thesis, which is why Lesson 2 treats it as the shared diagnostic.

The **1990 Act** raised the caps into roughly their current shape (the 675,000 framework, the 140,000 employment level, the diversity lottery) and created Temporary Protected Status (TPS). The **1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act (IIRIRA)** built the modern enforcement architecture: expedited removal, mandatory detention categories, state-federal enforcement agreements, the one-year deadline for asylum filings, and — most consequentially — the 3- and 10-year bars, which make anyone who accrues substantial unlawful presence and then *leaves* inadmissible for years. The bars were meant to deter illegal presence; in practice they punish departure, locking people in. Since 1996, no comprehensive immigration statute has passed.

<Checkpoint question="IRCA traded legalization for employer sanctions. Which half failed, and what's the mechanical reason — not just 'lack of will'?">
  The sanctions half. Employers had to check documents but not *verify* them against any database, so a market in convincing fakes satisfied the law's letter while defeating its purpose — and enforcement agencies deprioritized audits within a few years. The legalization half worked technically: roughly 2.7 million people gained status. The mechanical lesson: a mandate without a verification system is a request.
</Checkpoint>

## The humanitarian door: two tracks, one backlog

The Refugee Act of 1980 — passed by the Senate without a single nay vote, a fact worth savoring from 2026 — wrote the 1951 Refugee Convention's standard into U.S. law and created two distinct tracks.

**Refugee resettlement** happens *abroad*: applicants are screened overseas (typically for one to two years) and admitted up to an annual ceiling the President sets each fall. The ceiling is the single most elastic number in immigration policy — the annual determination averaged more than 90,000 across the program's first four and a half decades. The first Trump administration cut it to 18,000 for FY2020; the Biden administration set it at 125,000 from FY2022 through FY2025; the second Trump administration suspended admissions on taking office in January 2025 and set the FY2026 ceiling at 7,500 — the lowest in the program's history — with admissions prioritized for Afrikaners from South Africa. (A May 2026 emergency determination then raised the ceiling to 17,500, allocating the additional 10,000 places to Afrikaners as well.) Same statute, identical text; a ceiling of 125,000 one year and 7,500 the next. That is what "presidential discretion" means in practice.

**Asylum** is claimed *from inside* the United States or at a port of entry, generally within one year of arrival. The applicant must show a "well-founded fear of persecution" on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. The pipeline from claim to decision has a defined shape — and one stage where nearly everyone gets stuck:

The court stage is the system's bottleneck: pending cases nearly quadrupled in five years, from about one million at the end of FY2019 to a peak of roughly 3.75 million in December 2024, and still stood near 3.3 million in spring 2026 (TRAC) — spread across a bench of immigration judges that 2025's firings and resignations had thinned by about a quarter, to roughly 525. Multi-year waits are the norm. The backlog is itself a policy force: it shelters weak claims (the wait alone confers years of residence and, after six months, work authorization) and punishes strong ones (genuine refugees sit in the same line). Every serious reform proposal, restrictionist or expansionist, starts with adjudication capacity.

## The unauthorized population, recounted

For years the standard description was "about 11 million, mostly long-settled." The 2021–2023 border surge broke that description, and honest numbers now come with error bars. Pew Research Center's estimate — the most-cited series — put the unauthorized population at a record **14 million as of mid-2023**, up from 10.5 million in 2021, the largest two-year increase in the series' history. Three features of that 14 million matter for every policy argument downstream:

- **It is two populations, not one.** About 4.3 million adults had been in the country 18 years or more; more than 4.2 million adults had arrived within the previous five — a record for recent arrivals. The long-settled core (the people IRCA-style legalization debates are about) and the recent-arrival surge (the people border-policy debates are about) are different groups with different politics, and rhetoric that blurs them is usually doing it on purpose.
- **"Unauthorized" includes millions with quasi-legal status.** Pew counts roughly 6 million people who had some protection from deportation — pending asylum claims (about 2.6 million), TPS (about 650,000), DACA (about 610,000), humanitarian parole — but no permanent status. They are simultaneously "here legally" in the everyday sense and "unauthorized" in the statistical sense. Precision about this distinction is a reliable marker of good-faith argument.
- **A large minority never crossed a border unlawfully.** Of the roughly 11 million pre-surge population, about 42 percent entered legally on a visa and overstayed (CRS). The student who stayed after graduation and the clandestine border-crosser are in the identical legal category.

Two more numbers anchor the human stakes: about 4.6 million U.S.-born children — citizens, under *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* and the Fourteenth Amendment as currently applied — live with at least one unauthorized parent; and unauthorized workers were about 5.6 percent of the U.S. labor force in 2023. After 2023 the curve bent hard: with border crossings collapsing and removals rising, the total foreign-born population *fell* by nearly 1.5 million in the first half of 2025 by Pew's survey-based estimate — the first decline since the 1960s — and economists at Brookings and AEI estimate 2025 was the first year of net *negative* migration in at least half a century. Lesson 3 takes up that reversal in detail.

> **Common misconception:** "Unauthorized immigrant" means "recently snuck across the border." Both words of that picture fail. A plurality of the pre-surge population overstayed visas rather than crossing unlawfully, and millions are 15-plus-year residents; meanwhile, millions of the post-2021 arrivals presented themselves *at* the border to claim asylum — the opposite of evading detection. The category is legal, not behavioral.

<Checkpoint question="An F-1 student finishes her degree, stays, and works off the books for six years. A man crossed the Sonoran desert unlawfully the same year she enrolled. Who is 'unauthorized' — and whose situation is more common in the pre-surge population?">
  Both are unauthorized; the law does not grade the manner of becoming so. And hers is roughly as common as his: about 42 percent of the pre-2021 unauthorized population were visa overstays (CRS), a fact that matters because border walls and Border Patrol staffing — the most salient enforcement tools — do not address the overstay pathway at all. Interior verification systems (like E-Verify) do.
</Checkpoint>

## Who controls which lever

Keep three institutional facts straight, because the whole policy debate in Lesson 3 hangs on them. **Congress** owns the structure: categories, caps, the citizenship rules, the bars. None of it has been substantially rebuilt since 1996, mostly because immigration bills die by [[term:filibuster]]. **The executive** owns the throttle: enforcement priorities, asylum procedure, the refugee ceiling, and the parole power (case-by-case admission under INA §212(d)(5)) — which is why policy now swings violently between administrations without a word of the statute changing. **The states** own the margins: under the [[term:federalism]] settlement in *Arizona v. United States* (2012), they cannot run their own immigration-enforcement schemes, but they decide things like driver's licenses, in-state tuition, and how much their police cooperate with federal officers — and under *Plyler v. Doe* (1982), they cannot exclude undocumented children from public schools.

## What this lesson is not

It is not yet an argument. You now know the machine: four doors of very unequal size, caps that haven't moved since 1990, a humanitarian track whose ceiling swings from 125,000 to 7,500 on one signature, a court backlog in the millions, and an unauthorized population of roughly 14 million that is really two populations — long-settled and newly arrived — now shrinking for the first time in decades. Whether that machine admits too many people or too few, whether its composition is right, what to do about the people already here — those are contested questions on which serious people divide. Lesson 2 steel-mans the three main positions: restrictionist, expansionist, and comprehensive-reform centrist.
