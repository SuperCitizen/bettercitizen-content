## Three numbers, one morning

On the morning of November 8, 2016, three respected forecasters answered the same question from substantially the same polls. FiveThirtyEight gave Donald Trump about a 29 percent chance of winning. HuffPost Pollster gave him 2 percent. The Princeton Election Consortium gave him 1.

- FiveThirtyEight: 29%
- HuffPost Pollster: 2%
- Princeton Election Consortium: 1%

Trump won. Which of those three numbers was wrong, and how could you ever know?

[pause]

> A single observation cannot grade a probability.

## What 70 percent claims

A forecast of 70 percent does not mean the candidate will win, roughly. It means that in 70 of 100 elections like this one she wins, and in 30 she loses.

- 70 in 100: she wins
- 30 in 100: she loses

So when a 70 percent favorite loses, you have learned almost nothing about the forecaster.

> The 30 is not a hedge. It is content.

## The only fair test

The fair test is calibration. Take every event a forecaster called 70 percent. If about 70 percent of them happened, the numbers mean what they say. If 95 percent happened, the forecaster was underconfident. If 45 percent happened, the forecaster was selling certainty they did not have.

- Calibration: stated odds match what happened

FiveThirtyEight audited itself in 2019. Across 5,589 events it had given roughly a 70 percent chance, 71 percent occurred.

- Called 70%: happened 71%

> Grade a forecaster on a track record, never on one result.

## Three cases about the forecast

Whether a win probability should be published at all is disputed, and the lesson gives three cases.

The modeler's case argues that the alternative to a model is a pundit, and that a model can be audited: stated probabilities, archived and scored.

- Modeler: auditable, archived, scored

The model skeptic's case argues that a presidential forecast resolves once per four years, so no individual forecast is falsifiable, and a two-decimal probability is precision theater.

- Skeptic: precision theater

The institutional-numbers conservative argues that the numbers that run the republic are the boring official ones, and that they deserve trust because their methods and their errors are published.

- Institutional conservative: trust published process

Whether probabilistic election coverage is, on balance, good for self-government remains open.

The lesson gives each case in full, shows what a CBO score is and is not, and follows the 2025 firing of the BLS commissioner over a jobs report.
