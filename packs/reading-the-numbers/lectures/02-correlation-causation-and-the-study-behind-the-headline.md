Did violent crime in America rise or fall in 2022?

[beat]

The FBI's national statistics, assembled from police agencies, showed violent crime falling, by about 2 percent.

The Bureau of Justice Statistics runs a different instrument: a victimization survey. It asks a quarter-million Americans directly whether they were attacked, robbed, or assaulted. That survey showed violent victimization rising, from 16.5 incidents per 1,000 people to 23.5.

Both numbers are federal. Both are official. And both were brandished, by opposite sides of the same argument, as the crime rate.

[pause]

Neither is lying. By the end of this lecture, you will know how that is possible. And the answer is not a technicality. It is the entire discipline of reading civic numbers.

Every statistic is manufactured by a procedure. [beat] And the procedure decides what the number can mean.

[pause]

Last time, the numbers measured opinion. Now they are asked to carry a heavier word: because. We will take it in four steps.

First, a ladder of evidence, from the anecdote up to the experiment.

Second, two illusions that counterfeit causation.

Third, the published study itself, and why it can no longer be taken at its word.

And last, how to read the study behind a headline, which will bring us back to those two crime numbers.

Then we will hear three positions on what to do when clean evidence is not on offer.

## The ladder of evidence

A claim arrives wearing numbers. This policy cut crime. That law suppressed turnout. The first question is not whether the numbers are accurate. It is what design produced them.

> Design determines how much the word "because" can carry.

Researchers climb a recognizable ladder, and it has five rungs.

At the bottom, the anecdote. A constituent stands up at a town hall. Their business closed after the minimum-wage increase. That is evidence that something is possible. It is never evidence that it is typical. And it is the form of evidence human brains are built to overweight.

Next, the cross-section: compare many places at one moment. Counties with more immigrants have lower crime, say. A cross-section generates hypotheses, and headlines. But the places differ in a thousand ways besides the policy. And there is no time order, so you cannot even say which came first.

Third, the panel, which adds time. You follow the same places across years, so each one serves as its own control, and anything fixed about a place cancels out. That is real progress. But a panel still can't rule out that something else changed at the same time as the policy.

Fourth, the natural experiment. History, or an administrative accident, assigns the treatment in a way plausibly unrelated to the outcome. A draft lottery assigns military service by birthday.

Or take 1980, when 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami within months. It is called the Mariel boatlift, and no one chose it for economic reasons. The economist David Card used it to test what immigration does to native wages. Years later, George Borjas reanalyzed it, and reached the opposite conclusion.

So even a clean natural experiment leaves a human choice: compared to whom? The comparison group is where the disagreement sits.

And at the top, the randomized controlled trial. Assignment is by literal coin flip, which balances everything about the groups: measured and unmeasured, known and unknowable.

Politics has real ones. In August 2006, three researchers randomly assigned about 180,000 Michigan households to receive different get-out-the-vote mailers. A civic-duty appeal raised turnout by 1.8 percentage points over the control group. Another mailer showed the household its neighbors' voting records, and promised an update after the election. [beat] That one raised turnout by 8.1 points. About as effective as quality in-person canvassing, for the price of a stamp.

No cross-section could ever have isolated that. Randomization could, because the only systematic difference between the groups was the envelope.

So, five rungs. Anecdote. Cross-section. Panel. Natural experiment. Randomized trial. Each rung removes a family of rival explanations.

This ladder is also a scholarly revolution with a name. In 1983, the econometrician Edward Leamer charged that empirical economics ran on fragile statistical modeling: change the assumptions, change the answer. A generation later came the reply. The field's salvation had come not from fancier math, but from better research design. That turn is called the credibility revolution. Keep the name. We will meet its advocates, and its skeptics, before we finish.

## The third thing in the room

That is the ladder. Now the first illusion, the one the lower rungs cannot see through.

In the 1990s, violent crime in New York City collapsed, while the city deployed aggressive policing under the name "broken windows." For twenty years, that pairing was cited as proof the strategy worked.

Here is the inconvenient backdrop. Crime collapsed nearly everywhere in 1990s America. In cities that adopted New York's tactics. [beat] And in cities that did exactly the opposite.

A much-cited audit by Steven Levitt sorted the proposed causes. Four had real evidence. Six could not explain the timing or the geography. And among the six were the policing innovations that had claimed the credit.

The point is not that policing never matters. It is that a city, a decade, and a policy arrived bundled with an economy, a demographic wave, and a drug market.

> The raw correlation measures the bundle, not the policy.

That is confounding: the same underlying conditions drive both the supposed cause and the outcome. A third thing in the room.

Researchers answer it with controls. You will hear the phrase "controlling for income, education, and region." And controls help. But they have two limits. You can only control for what you measured, and measured well. And some variables make matters worse when controlled.

Hence the first commandment of the credibility revolution. Fix the assignment problem by design, rather than patch it afterward with arithmetic.

Let's test that. Across American cities, police officers per capita correlates positively with violent crime. More police, more crime. Does that mean police cause crime? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. High-crime cities hire more police, because city councils respond to bad years. So the causation likely runs backward, and shared conditions drive both. The name for that is reverse causation.

And well-identified studies find that more police reduce crime. So the naive sign of a cross-section can be the exact opposite of the causal effect.

## The illusion that never misses

The second illusion needs no third thing at all. It needs only luck.

Daniel Kahneman tells the story in his book Thinking, Fast and Slow. He was teaching flight instructors. The instructors had learned from experience that praising a cadet's exceptional landing led to a worse one next time. And screaming after a terrible landing led to improvement. So, they concluded, criticism works and praise corrupts.

The statistics say otherwise. An exceptional performance is partly luck, and luck doesn't repeat. So extremes are followed by more ordinary outcomes, regardless of what anyone yells.

The instructors had spent careers being rewarded by arithmetic for cruelty.

[pause]

This is regression to the mean. Once you can name it, you will see it running half of civic life, because policy targets extremes.

The crackdown follows the worst crime spike. The turnaround program goes to the lowest-scoring schools. The safety review hits the intersection with this year's most crashes. And next year the spike subsides, the scores tick up, the crashes fall, as they mostly would have anyway.

> Every program aimed at an extreme is born with a fake testimonial.

Put that to work. Your city installs gunshot-detection cameras on its 10 worst blocks, chosen because last year they had their highest shooting counts. A year later, shootings on those blocks are down 25 percent, and the mayor declares victory.

Before you credit the cameras: what is the rival explanation, and what comparison would test it?

[long pause]

Regression to the mean. Blocks selected because they just had their worst year would fall back toward their norm with no intervention at all. Last year's extreme was partly bad luck, and bad luck doesn't repeat.

The test is a comparison group, selected the same extreme way and left untreated. Compare the next ten worst blocks. Or better, randomize camera placement among the worst 20. If treated and untreated blocks fall alike, the cameras did nothing the arithmetic wasn't already doing.

## The fragile p

Third step: the published study itself.

Quantitative studies stamp their findings with a number called a p-value. So you need to know exactly what one says.

A p-value is the probability of seeing data at least this extreme, if there were truly no effect.

Take an example. A study of a civics curriculum reports a p-value of 0.04. Here is everything that number says, and it is modest. If the curriculum truly did nothing, data this extreme would arise about 4 percent of the time.

Now what a p-value is not. It is not the probability the finding is true. It is not the probability the result was chance. And it is not a measure of importance. Those confusions are common enough that in 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement warning against them.

By convention, a p-value below 0.05 earns the label "statistically significant." And that bright line built a perverse machine. Journals preferred significant results. Careers required journals. And a researcher with flexible choices could usually fish something under the line, without ever consciously cheating. Andrew Gelman calls the innocent version the garden of forking paths. Make enough defensible choices, and some path reaches significance by chance alone.

In 2005, John Ioannidis said where that machine led, in a paper titled "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False." His argument was structural arithmetic, not accusation. Combine low prior odds, small samples, flexible analysis, and a preference for positive results. Then the expected share of false published positives passes half.

A decade later, the field checked. A collaboration re-ran 100 published psychology studies, from three top journals, with the original materials. Of the originals, 97 percent had reported statistically significant results. Of the replications, [beat] 36 percent did. And the effect sizes in the replications averaged about half the originals.

[pause]

The operational rules are short. Treat any single study, however credentialed, as one draw from a noisy machine. Give weight to findings that replicate across teams. Give weight to studies that were pre-registered, with the analysis plan filed before the data arrived. And look for effect sizes with their uncertainty, rather than asterisks.

One more inversion to distrust: "significant," heard as "important." With two million observations, an effect of 0.1 points is statistically significant.

> Significance measures detectability, not size.

## The study behind the headline

Last step. Put the toolkit into the sixty seconds you will spend with a study. Three checks, in order.

First, the verb. Methods sections are honest where headlines aren't, and the tell is the verb. "Associated with" and "linked to" mean correlation. "Predicts" adds only time order. "Causes," "increases," "reduces": those are earned by the ladder's top rungs, and forfeited below them.

A careful paper says "associated with." A careless headline upgrades it to "causes" by lunchtime. No number need be altered.

Second, the size. Find the effect's magnitude, and its base rate. Suppose a headline says something "doubles the risk" of a harm that afflicts 1 person in 10,000. That means 2 in 10,000. So ask: how big, compared to what, for whom?

Third, the ghosts in the denominator. Who isn't in the data? This is selection bias, and its most famous portrait comes from the Second World War.

Abraham Wald was asked where to armor bombers, and given charts of bullet holes on returning planes. He reasoned that the holes mapped where a plane could be shot and survive. Armor belongs where the returning planes were clean. [beat] Because planes hit there never came home.

Every civic dataset has its missing planes, and crime statistics are the canonical case.

For an assault to appear in FBI figures, a chain must hold. Someone perceives a crime. Someone reports it. The department logs it as what it was. And the agency forwards it to the FBI. Every link selects. And the victimization survey consistently finds that well under half of violent crime is reported to police.

Now reach back to where we began. Two federal numbers for 2022: the FBI's count falling, the victimization survey rising. With that chain in mind, how can both be honest? Take a moment.

[long pause]

They measure different events, through different filters. The FBI's numbers count crimes that survived the full reporting chain. The survey asks households directly, and so includes the unreported majority. It has filters of its own: no homicide, no one living in an institution or without a home, and fallible memory.

So the divergence itself was the finding. Something was changing in what victims experienced, against what police came to know about.

That leaves the citizen's all-purpose question, for any number whatsoever.

> What had to happen for this event to become a data point?

## Three positions on method

One thing the ladder does not do. It ranks confidence about causation. It does not rank importance. And the questions citizens most need answered are often the ones least available to clean designs. No one will randomize democracy, war, or a border.

That gap sustains a real argument about method, and three positions hold it. Each gets its turn.

First, the credibility-revolution advocate. This position guards against confident error. Its holders argue that where the design is dirty, the scientific thing is to say "we don't know," out loud. They point to a generation of confident regressions that did real policy damage. And they point to the Mariel boatlift, where even apparently clean evidence turned on the choice of comparison group. So humility, they argue, is the scientific position.

> The credibility-revolution case: where the design is dirty, say "we don't know."

Second, the structural-model defender. This position guards the large questions. Its holders, James Heckman among them, argue that natural experiments answer only the questions nature happened to randomize. They point to what comes next: carrying a Michigan mailer, or a Miami boatlift, over to your own policy requires exactly the theory-laden modeling the revolution disdains. And design purity, they argue, pushed to a creed, becomes an excuse to study only small, answerable things.

> The structural-model case: clean designs answer only what nature happened to randomize.

Third, the evidence-consumer pragmatist. This position guards the decision that has to be made. Its holders argue that public decisions can't wait for ideal evidence, so the skill is calibrated synthesis. They point to a working method. Grade each study by its rung. Expect regression to the mean wherever extremes were selected. Treat unreplicated significance as rumor. And ask what had to happen for the data to exist.

> The pragmatist's case: public decisions can't wait for ideal evidence.

One thing none of these positions licenses: the shrug that says "you can prove anything with statistics." All three are arguments about how to weigh evidence.

You may already lean toward one of them. So take the position you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own holders would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Every statistic is manufactured by a procedure. So ask what had to happen for this event to become a data point.

[beat]

Two. The ladder: anecdote, cross-section, panel, natural experiment, randomized trial. Design determines how much the word "because" can carry.

[beat]

Three. Confounding. The raw correlation measures the bundle, not the policy. And causation can run backward.

[beat]

Four. Regression to the mean. Every program aimed at an extreme is born with a fake testimonial. The test is a comparison group, selected the same extreme way and left untreated.

[beat]

And five. A p-value is the probability of data this extreme if there were truly no effect. It is not the probability the finding is true. Significance measures detectability, not size. And any single study is one draw from a noisy machine.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. When no clean design is on offer, should we say "we don't know," build the model, or weigh what we have? That is a contested question, not a settled one.

Some numbers survive every check in this lecture. The next lecture asks a harder question, about the numbers that claim to see the future: when does a forecast deserve your trust?

For now, go back to 2022. Violent crime fell. Violent victimization rose. [beat] Two numbers, two procedures, and neither one lying. You know now what each of them was counting.
