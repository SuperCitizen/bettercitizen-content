It is the morning of November 8, 2016. Three respected statistical operations publish their final answers to the same question, from substantially the same polls.

FiveThirtyEight gives Donald Trump about a 29 percent chance of winning.

The HuffPost Pollster model gives him 2 percent.

The Princeton Election Consortium gives him 1. Its proprietor has promised, on air, to eat a bug if Trump clears 240 electoral votes.

[pause]

By midnight, Trump had won. The bug was eventually eaten. And a durable piece of folk wisdom was born: the models failed.

Here is the uncomfortable question. Which of those three numbers was wrong? [beat] And how could you ever know?

Trump winning does not refute 29 percent. Events with a 29 percent chance happen slightly more often than flipping two heads in a row. It does not even strictly refute 1 percent.

> A single observation cannot grade a probability.

And yet the question has a real answer. The three forecasts were not equally defensible, and one concept separates them. It also governs how to read a budget score, and the numbers the government publishes about itself.

We will take it in four steps.

First, what a forecast probability claims.

Second, the fair test of any forecaster, and the three kinds of model it grades.

Third, the argument over whether election forecasts should hold the microphone at all, in three cases.

And last, the numbers the government runs on: the budget score, and the agencies that do the counting.

## What a probability claims

A probabilistic forecast is not a prediction wearing decimals.

Take a forecast of Harris, 70 percent. It does not mean that Harris will win, roughly. It means this. Given the polls, their historical errors, and the assumptions of this model, Harris wins in 70 of 100 elections like this one. [beat] And in 30, she loses.

The 30 is not a hedge. It is part of the claim.

The number is also conditional. It inherits everything the first lecture taught about its inputs. Feed a model herded polls, all biased in the same direction, and it will be confidently wrong, with impeccable math.

> Models quantify only the uncertainty they are told about.

2016 shows what a model adds on top of the polls. All three operations saw the same national picture, with Clinton ahead by about 3. They diverged on two questions that polls cannot answer.

One is how often reality lands far from the polling consensus. The jargon for that is fat tails. The other is how correlated the state errors are. If the polls are wrong in Wisconsin, are they wrong in Pennsylvania and Michigan too?

FiveThirtyEight assumed fat tails, and heavily correlated errors. That is most of how the same data yielded 29 percent and not 1.

Then the Upper Midwest missed as a bloc. The 1 percent models had not merely been unlucky. They had asserted that what happened all but couldn't.

[pause]

2024 supplied the cleanest illustration. The final run of one model, the Silver Bulletin, gave Harris 50.015 percent. A coin flip, and the other major models clustered at the same shrug. Trump then swept all seven battlegrounds, and pundits scored that a humiliation.

But in a model with properly correlated errors, a sweep is what a tied forecast usually looks like when it resolves. The battlegrounds were within a couple of points of each other. So whichever way the shared polling error broke, it would tip most of them together.

## The only fair test

So how do you grade a forecaster, if one outcome teaches almost nothing?

The test was invented where forecasting grew up: the weather service. In 1950, Glenn Brier proposed scoring a probability forecast by its squared miss. Say 90 percent and the event happens, and the charge is small. Say 90 percent and it doesn't, and the charge is large. The Brier score averages that charge over every forecast you ever issued, and lower is better.

Buried in that score is a property a citizen can check by eye. Take every event a forecaster called 70 percent. If about 70 percent of them happened, that forecaster is calibrated.

> A calibrated forecaster's numbers mean what they say.

If 95 percent of them happened, the forecaster was underconfident. If 45 percent happened, the forecaster was selling certainty they didn't have.

Forecasters can be audited this way, and the good ones audit themselves. In 2019, FiveThirtyEight checked every probability it had ever published, in politics and sports, against what happened. It had given 5,589 events roughly a 70 percent chance. [beat] 71 percent of them occurred.

That is the sense in which 2016's 29 percent was defensible, and 1 percent was not. One came from a system whose stated probabilities, audited across thousands of events, mean what they say. The others asserted a precision about tail risk that the polling record flatly contradicts.

You already use one calibrated forecast fluently. A classic study from 1977 found the National Weather Service's rain probabilities strikingly well calibrated. When forecasters say 30 percent, it rains very close to 30 percent of the time. And the public has learned what that means: maybe carry the umbrella.

Elections are weather's evil twin. Millions consume the forecast, but the feedback arrives once every four years. That asymmetry, not public stupidity, is why 70 percent gets heard as a sure thing in politics, and as a maybe in weather.

Let's test it. Forecaster A calls events at 60 percent, and they happen 60 percent of the time. Forecaster B calls everything 95 percent, and those events happen 80 percent of the time. B's fans note that B is right far more often.

Who is the better forecaster, and in what sense? Think it through.

[long pause]

A is. Her numbers mean what they say, so you can act on them. B is overconfident, by 15 points on every claim, and decisions priced at B's stated certainty systematically lose.

Being right more often is the wrong test, because B also forecasts easier events. Grade the distance between the stated probability and the observed frequency.

## Three families of model

Calibration grades the forecaster. It also helps to know what kind of model is talking, because that tells you what can break. Election models come in three families.

Polling averages answer one question: what do the polls say now? They inherit every weakness of the polls, herding above all.

Fundamentals models ignore the polls early, and forecast from structural conditions: economic growth, incumbency, presidential approval. Their problem is their sample. Two dozen postwar elections is a pittance. And in 2024 the incumbent party swapped nominees in July, so which candidate was the incumbent stopped being a well-defined input.

Hybrids blend fundamentals early with polls late, then simulate thousands of elections with correlated state errors.

Each family breaks where its inputs break. Had the 2024 polls carried a fourth straight bias in the same direction, every one of the major models would have broken with them. They were spared the test. The polls were good.

So what should a citizen do with the headline number? Three disciplines.

Read the distribution, not the topline. Watch movement, not levels: a model that lurches on every poll is amplifying noise. And refuse certainty laundering, which is the conversion, after the fact, of 7 in 10 into "called it." A forecaster graded only when right will sell you confidence forever.

## Who should hold the microphone

Third step: the argument.

The sharpest published attack on election forecasting came in 2018, from Nassim Nicholas Taleb, the author of The Black Swan. His move was to treat a forecast probability as a price: the price of a bet that pays one dollar if the candidate wins.

Finance has hard rules for such prices. A legitimate price already incorporates predictable future swings, so it should not itself be forecastably volatile. When real uncertainty is high, it hugs 50 percent, and moves little on news. So a probability that swings through a wide range across a campaign is, in Taleb's terms, revealing "stark errors."

The published reply, from Aubrey Clayton, argued that the heuristic is simply false. Under mild assumptions, a forecast that updates honestly on incoming information behaves exactly as those rules require. Taleb, on this account, had confused volatility with incoherence.

Underneath the exchange sits a real civic question. When a forecaster publishes a probability in the middle of a campaign, is that information? Or is it theater?

Start on ground nobody disputes. A presidential forecast resolves once every four years. And 2016's 29 percent was widely taken to mean that Clinton had it. Three cases follow from there, and each gets its turn.

First, the modeler's case. What it protects is accountability. The modeler argues that the alternative to a model is not humility. It is a pundit. They point to Philip Tetlock's two-decade study of expert political prediction. In his own famous summary, celebrated commentators did about as well at long-range forecasts as a dart-throwing chimpanzee, and more famous experts did worse. Models, they argue, replaced that with something auditable: stated probabilities, archived, and scored. A probability is the one format that forces its author to own uncertainty in advance. And the mishearing of 29 percent is an argument for teaching the reading, not for banning the writing. Nobody proposes suppressing hurricane tracks because some residents treat the cone's edge as a wall.

> The modeler's case: the alternative to a model is not humility, but a pundit.

Second, the model skeptic's case. What it protects is honesty about how deep the uncertainty runs. The skeptic argues that even if you grant the math, the problem remains. A forecast that resolves once in four years cannot be falsified, one forecast at a time. They point to the calibration ledger, which pools easy congressional calls with the hard one everyone consumed. The forecaster collects attention up front, and can launder any outcome after the fact. They point to that 50.015 percent, and argue that a two-decimal probability is precision theater. The honest statement, they argue, compresses toward "we don't know," stated plainly. Their test is skin in the game: would the forecaster sell bets at the stated price, in size? Prediction markets at least meet that bar. And the daily twitch of the needle, they argue, is engagement product, not civic information. It demobilizes or panics voters over noise.

> The skeptic's case: when uncertainty runs this deep, a precise probability is theater.

Third, the institutional-numbers conservative. What this case protects is the shared, boring record. The conservative argues that both camps are quarreling over entertainment, and that the numbers which run the republic are the boring ones. They point to what those numbers do. Measured inflation sets Social Security checks. The jobs report steers the Federal Reserve. Census counts apportion the House. Those series, they argue, deserve a different default, which is trust. Not because official numbers are perfect: revisions prove they aren't, and prove the system is working. Because they come from published methods, on a documented schedule, from agencies that publish their own errors. And when a politician of either party says the books are cooked, the burden of proof sits with the politician. The methodology is public. Show the flaw, or withdraw the charge.

> The conservative's case: a transparent, imperfect number beats any number produced to please.

Three cases. Take the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own holders would.

[pause 5s]

## The ruler

The third case made a claim about the boring numbers. Let's look at two of them.

First, the forecast citizens most misread. The Congressional Budget Office, the CBO, scores a bill: this plan adds 3 trillion dollars to deficits over ten years. Partisans treat that number as prophecy, or as propaganda. It is neither.

A score is a disciplined comparison. CBO projects revenues and outlays for a decade, assuming existing law continues. Then it estimates how the bill would change that path. It is a ruler, deliberately common to all proposals, held by an agency that since 1975 has answered to both parties and pleased neither. It is not a prediction of actual future deficits, because Congress will change the law again.

How good is the ruler? CBO grades itself in public, and that is itself the credential. By its own evaluation, its revenue projections miss by roughly 10 percent by the sixth year. The errors come overwhelmingly from two things no scorekeeper escapes: economic turning points nobody foresaw, and later legislation.

> A ten-year score is directionally serious and decimally false.

In practice, a score of 3 trillion dollars and a score one tenth that size describe different bills. A score of 1.4 trillion against one of 1.2 trillion does not settle anything.

Try it on a scene. A senator says: CBO admits its sixth-year projections miss by about 10 percent, so its score of my bill is worthless. What is the best response? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The score's job is comparison under shared rules, not prophecy. The documented error applies to every other estimate too, including the senator's own, which lacks the audit trail. And the 10 percent figure comes from CBO's own published self-evaluation. That transparency is the credential, not the indictment.

## The count

Now the count. Every instrument in this series bottoms out in a handful of federal statistical agencies. Their product is trusted because they are boring. In 2025, that boringness met politics.

On August 1, 2025, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported just 73,000 jobs for July, and revised the two months before it down. Hours later, President Trump fired the bureau's commissioner, accusing her, without evidence, of rigging the numbers. Commissioners do not make the estimates. And revisions are routine: they fold in survey responses that arrive late.

Then the administration nominated an economist who had publicly suggested suspending the monthly jobs report. That nomination was withdrawn.

Then came a 43-day government shutdown, which halted data collection. The bureau later announced that its all-items inflation figure for October 2025 would never be published. The Federal Reserve went into its December rate decision without it.

And as of mid-2026, the bureau still lacked a Senate-confirmed commissioner.

[pause]

Nobody serious claims the bureau now fabricates numbers. The documented dangers are subtler, and there are two.

One is anticipatory bias. Statisticians who watched a commissioner fired over a revision face a quiet thumb on every future judgment call.

The other is credibility decay. Once revisions are recast as treachery, every inconvenient number becomes deniable, by either party. The shared facts dissolve into your numbers versus mine.

Against both, the agencies have one defense.

> The agencies' defense is procedure: published methods, scheduled releases, documented revisions, self-reported error.

## What to keep

Before we gather up, reach back to that November morning. Three numbers: 29, 2, and 1. Which one was defensible, and what is the test called?

[long pause]

The 29. And the test is calibration. Across thousands of events, that forecaster's numbers meant what they said.

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A forecast probability is a claim about many elections like this one. A single observation cannot grade a probability.

[beat]

Two. The fair test is calibration. Of everything a forecaster calls 70 percent, about 70 percent should happen. The Brier score keeps the tally.

[beat]

Three. Models quantify only the uncertainty they are told about. Know which family is talking: averages, fundamentals, or hybrids. Each breaks where its inputs break.

[beat]

Four. A budget score is a ruler, a comparison against current law. A ten-year score is directionally serious and decimally false.

[beat]

And five. Official statistics earn default trust through procedure, and private models earn it through audit. So before you trust a forecast, look for four things. A public track record, scored by calibration. A test on data it didn't train on. Error bars, shown, with a word on what would prove the model wrong. And its conditions, attached.

[pause]

Here is what remains open. Whether probabilistic election coverage is, on net, good for self-government: the demobilization worry is serious, and unresolved. On that, the modeler and the skeptic are both right, about different readers. Whether prediction markets, with money at stake, beat models, with their transparency. And whether the independence of the statistical agencies survives the decade. As of mid-2026, that turns on appointments, court fights, and appropriations still in motion.

This series ends here, with one distinction to carry out of it. Refusing to treat all numbers as equally suspect is not naive. And treating a calibrated forecast and a partisan guess as "both just predictions" is false equivalence, done with numbers.

Go back once more to that November morning. Three numbers, one outcome, and no way to grade them from the outcome alone. [beat] The numbers were never going to tell you what to value. Checked this way, they can tell you what it costs.
