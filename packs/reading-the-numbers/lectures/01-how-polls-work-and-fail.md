It is the autumn of 1936, and the most trusted election poll in America is run by a magazine.

The magazine is The Literary Digest. It has called five presidential races in a row, and its method is scale. It mails out about ten million straw ballots, to addresses taken from telephone directories and automobile registrations. [beat] Roughly 2.4 million come back.

The Digest's verdict is Alf Landon, over Franklin Roosevelt.

A young market researcher named George Gallup says otherwise. His sample is around 50,000 people, selected to resemble the electorate. And he says Roosevelt, in a walk.

[pause]

Roosevelt took 60.8 percent of the popular vote, and every state but Maine and Vermont. Within two years, the Digest was out of business.

The lesson everyone remembers is that bigger isn't better. The lesson that matters for reading a poll today is sharper.

> How you select people determines what your sample can say.

And the people who don't answer can matter more than the millions who do.

Bad lists, and biased silence. Those were the Digest's two diseases, and both are still polling's living problems. They have just changed clothes.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to hear a poll reported and know what its numbers promise, and what they leave out. We will take it in four steps.

First, why a thousand people can speak for a country.

Second, what the margin of error covers, and what it does not.

Third, what happens when polls agree with each other, and the record of three presidential elections.

And last, the small numbers inside a poll.

Then we will hear three serious readings of that record, and they do not agree.

## A thousand people

Start with the part that deserves the word miracle.

Suppose every member of a population has a known chance of landing in your sample, and that chance is never zero. Then a sample of about 1,000 people can estimate a percentage for the whole population. It will land within about 3 points of the truth, up or down. And it will do that 95 times out of 100.

Not a thousand per state. One thousand, in total, for a country of roughly 260 million adults.

How can that be? Think of soup. A cook judges the whole pot from one spoonful. Not because the spoon is large next to the pot. [beat] Because the pot is stirred. Random selection is the stirring.

So the size of the population drops out of the math almost entirely. A random sample of 1,000 reads the United States about as precisely as it reads Wyoming.

> Precision comes from the size of the sample, not the size of the country.

And each added person buys a little less. A sample of 100 carries a margin of nearly 10 points. A sample of 1,000 carries about 3. To get down to 1 point, you need 10,000.

Let's test that. A pollster has a sample of 1,000, and a margin of about 3 points. She wants to cut that margin roughly in half. How many people does she need? Take a moment.

[long pause]

About 4,000. Error shrinks with the square root of the sample size, so halving the error means quadrupling the sample. And the budget quadruples too, which is why national polls cluster near a thousand.

One piece of fine print follows from the same arithmetic. The margin applies to each candidate's share. The gap between two candidates is roughly twice as uncertain. So a poll showing 48 to 45, with a margin of 3, is not showing a lead in any statistical sense.

## What the margin covers

That is the miracle. Now the most consequential fine print in civic statistics.

The margin of error measures sampling error, and only sampling error. That is the bad luck in who the random draw happened to reach, assuming everything else about the survey worked perfectly.

Survey methodologists keep a wider ledger, and they call it total survey error. It has four more entries, and none of them appears in the published plus or minus 3.

One. Coverage error. Your list of people who could be sampled is not the population. The Digest's telephone and automobile lists skewed prosperous, in Depression America. Today's version is the opt-in online panel, which contains no one who doesn't do paid surveys.

[beat]

Two. Nonresponse error. The people who answer differ from the people who don't, in ways related to what you are measuring.

[beat]

Three. Measurement error. The wording of a question, the order of the questions, the social pressure of an interviewer. And in elections, the likely-voter screen, which has to guess at turnout before any vote exists to check it against.

[beat]

Four. Adjustment error. Pollsters reweight a sample to repair the skews they can name. The repair widens the real margin of error, and it does nothing about the skews they can't name.

[pause]

Coverage. Nonresponse. Measurement. Adjustment.

Nonresponse was what truly sank the Digest. A 1988 autopsy of the poll found that the mailing list alone would not have done it. Had everyone responded, the Digest would at least have called the winner. But Landon's supporters mailed their ballots back at far higher rates. [beat] The silence was partisan.

And nonresponse is the entry that has exploded. Pew Research Center ran the numbers on its own telephone surveys. In 1997, 36 percent of the households it contacted responded. By 2018, the figure was 6 percent.

A 6 percent response rate does not automatically doom a poll. Pew's own studies find that such samples, once weighted, still track benchmarks reasonably well on many measures. But it changes what a poll is.

Modern polling is no longer a matter of asking a random slice of America. It asks the unusual people willing to answer, then statistically reshapes them until they resemble America.

That reshaping is called weighting. If a sample holds too few college graduates, each graduate's answer counts slightly more, until the proportions match the electorate. Pollsters weight on education, age, race and region, and increasingly on partisanship and past vote.

So when a poll misses by more than its margin, it does not follow that the pollster lied. Usually it means the systematic errors moved together, and no sample size can fix that.

The Digest is the proof. With 2.4 million ballots, its sampling margin of error was near zero. [beat] It missed by 19 points.

> The margin of error is a floor on uncertainty, never a ceiling.

## When the polls agree

So a single poll carries errors its margin never mentions. The usual remedy is to average many polls. Here is what that remedy assumes.

Follow the polls across a campaign and you notice signatures. One firm's numbers run a point or two friendlier to Republicans than the field. Another leans Democratic. Aggregators call these house effects. They are persistent leans, produced by a firm's design choices, and not necessarily by its politics.

A house effect isn't a sin. It's a fingerprint. And averaging across houses is supposed to cancel them out.

Herding is the sin. Picture a pollster near election day, sitting on a result that looks like an outlier: one candidate up by 7, when the average says 1. She can publish, and risk being wrong alone. Or she can quietly re-weight, or shelve the poll, and be wrong with everyone.

In late October 2024, Nate Silver ran the numbers on that month's polls of the battleground states. Nearly 80 percent of them showed Harris and Trump within 2.5 points of each other. Even in truly tied races, random sampling noise should produce that only about 55 percent of the time.

What explains it? Herding is one account. The profession's own audit gives another, and both are serious.

After each election cycle, the American Association for Public Opinion Research convenes a task force to audit the polls. Its 2024 task force ran its own tests, and found no evidence that firms were copying competitors. Its explanation is that the industry has turned broadly toward weighting on political variables, such as partisanship and the 2020 vote. Many firms, independently, bolted their samples to the same template. And that mechanically compresses the spread.

That is herding's effect without herding's intent. For a citizen, the takeaway is identical either way.

> Twenty clustered polls are not twenty independent confirmations.

When methods converge, errors converge. The average gets more precise without getting more accurate.

Try it on a scene. Five polls of your state, all from the final week, show the governor's race tied at 48 to 48. Each has a margin of 3.5. Your friend says five agreeing polls make a tie five times as certain.

What is wrong with that? Think it through.

[long pause]

Averaging cancels only independent error. If the five firms weight on the same political variables, or trim toward the same consensus, their errors are correlated. Then the five polls are closer to one poll, photocopied.

The race is probably tight. But the extra certainty is false precision. In 2020, whole batteries of agreeing state polls missed in the same direction, by 4 points and more. [beat] Agreement measures consensus, not truth.

## The record

So how wrong are polls, in fact? The honest answer comes from those task force reports, the closest thing polling has to a flight recorder. Let's play back three elections.

2016 broke into two different stories. The national polls had Clinton up about 3, and she won the popular vote by about 2. The state polls were the disaster. Their average error was 5.1 points, concentrated in the Upper Midwest.

The audit found two main culprits. Voters who decided in the last week broke hard for Trump, which no earlier poll could have captured. And most state polls did not weight by education, at a moment when diplomas had begun predicting the vote. Their samples contained too many college graduates, and therefore too many Clinton voters.

And there was little evidence of shy Trump voters lying to interviewers. The polls didn't get false answers. [beat] They got the wrong people.

[pause]

2020 was worse, and stranger. The national polls overstated Biden's margin by 3.9 points, the largest national error in 40 years. No education fix could explain it: pollsters had largely adopted education weighting by then.

The task force could not definitively identify the cause. The leading suspect is one you have already met, and it is the one weighting can't reach: nonresponse. Republicans, and particularly Republicans with low social trust, were less likely to take surveys at all in the pandemic year. No demographic weight captures that, because the missingness tracked the very thing being measured.

[pause]

2024 was, by these standards, a good year. With an asterisk.

Across the polls of the final two weeks, the average error on the margin was 3.3 points. In each of the two cycles before, it had been above 5. And state presidential polls had their most accurate cycle since 1944.

Now the asterisk. For the third presidential cycle in a row, the misses pointed the same way. They overstated Democratic margins, this time by 2.7 points on average.

Three elections. Three misses. One direction.

Hold on to that pattern. All three readings of the record will have to explain it.

## Inside the poll

Last step: the small numbers inside a poll.

On election night, the most quoted numbers come overwhelmingly from the exit poll. And the exit poll is the least understood instrument in the kit.

Interviewers stand at a few hundred selected precincts, and offer questionnaires to voters as they leave. The design optimizes for speed on election night, not for describing who voted. And cooperation, on a sidewalk, skews toward the educated.

That problem is not hypothetical. The 2016 national exit poll described an electorate that was 50 percent college graduates. Pew Research Center then matched survey respondents to official state vote records, so that "voted" meant verified, not claimed. [beat] It found 37 percent.

Same election, different instruments, different facts.

The everyday version of this trap is the crosstab: a poll's results, broken out by subgroup. A national poll of 1,000 contains perhaps 150 respondents under 30.

Now reach back to the start of this lecture. A sample of 1,000 carried a margin of about 3 points. A sample of 100 carried nearly 10. So what margin belongs to those 150 people? Take a moment.

[long pause]

About 8 points, on each candidate's share. On the gap between two candidates, roughly double that.

2024 showed what this means in practice. Estimates of the Hispanic margin varied by tens of points across reputable firms.

> A subgroup number in a single poll is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The professional move is to pool many polls before believing any subgroup trend.

## Three readings of the record

So what does that record add up to? Start on ground that nobody in this argument disputes.

Response rates have collapsed. The field audits its own misses, and publishes them. The 2024 polls were more accurate than those of the two cycles before. And for three presidential cycles in a row, the misses pointed the same way.

The open question is what all of that says about polling itself. There are three serious readings, and each gets its turn.

First, the survey-methods institutionalist. This reader wants polling judged the way the field judges itself, by audit. The institutionalist reads the record as a system working. They point to the task forces: after every cycle, the field examines its own errors and publishes what it finds. They point to 2024, when accuracy was historically good. And they argue that the errors on the wider ledger are known risks, which a profession can measure and manage.

> The institutionalist's reading: total survey error is managed risk, not scandal.

Second, the structural polling skeptic. This reader does not want one good year mistaken for a cure. The skeptic reads the same record as a symptom. They point to a 6 percent response world, and argue that polls now measure the kind of person who answers. They point to weighting on past vote, and argue that it borrows accuracy from the past, at the price of missing every electorate that breaks the pattern. And they point to three misses in one direction, as the sign of a nonresponse disease no weight can cure. On this reading, 2024 looked good partly because the race sat near the polls' standing bias.

> The skeptic's reading: polls now measure the kind of person who answers.

Third, the aggregation pragmatist. This reader wants a rule that works on the polls we have. The pragmatist reads the record as a lesson about the unit of belief. They point to what averaging is for: one firm's lean is supposed to cancel against another's. They argue that no single poll is news, and that the proper unit of belief is the average, shown with its error band. And they concede a cost: converging methods quietly sap the independence that makes averaging work.

> The pragmatist's reading: the unit of belief is the average, with its error band.

Three readings of one record. The data so far refuses to retire any of them.

You may already lean toward one. So take the reading you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A random sample of about a thousand people can read a whole country, to within about 3 points. Precision comes from the size of the sample, not the size of the country.

[beat]

Two. The margin of error covers sampling error only. It leaves out coverage, nonresponse, measurement and adjustment. It is a floor on uncertainty, never a ceiling.

[beat]

Three. A house effect is a fingerprint, and herding is the sin. Either way, twenty clustered polls are not twenty independent confirmations. Agreement measures consensus, not truth.

[beat]

Four. The record. In 2016, the state polls got the wrong people. In 2020, nonresponse is the leading suspect. And 2024 was the most accurate of the three, and the third in a row to miss in the same direction.

[beat]

And five. A subgroup number in a single poll is a hypothesis, not a finding. An exit poll is built for speed, not for describing who voted.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: which of those three readings is right. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

The next lecture leaves opinion for causation. It asks what it takes for a number to support the word "because." And the lecture after that asks when a forecast, built on all of this, deserves your trust.

For now, go back to the autumn of 1936. Ten million ballots went out. 2.4 million came back. And the answer missed by 19 points. [beat] The question was never how many people answered. It was who got asked, and who stayed silent.
