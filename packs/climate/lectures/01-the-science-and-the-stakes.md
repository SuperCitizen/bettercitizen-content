March 1958. A bare lava field, 11,000 feet up the slope of Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii.

A 29-year-old geochemist named Charles David Keeling switches on a new instrument: an infrared gas analyzer. He has picked this site precisely because nothing is here. No cities. No forests. No smokestacks. Just clean mid-Pacific air.

The first reading comes back. [beat] 313 parts per million of carbon dioxide.

[pause]

Within two years, that instrument had revealed two things nobody had ever directly observed.

First, the atmosphere breathes. Carbon dioxide falls every summer in the Northern Hemisphere, as the world's forests leaf out and draw carbon in. It rises every winter, as decay returns it. Down and up, year after year, like the teeth of a saw.

Second, underneath that breathing, the baseline was climbing. [beat] It has climbed every single year since.

Keeling's first reading was 313. The annual average for 2025 was 427.

[pause]

The graph came to be called the Keeling Curve, and it is the cleanest place to start. Here is why. Arguments about climate routinely confuse two different questions.

One: is the warming real, and is it caused by people?

Two: what should be done about it?

> The warming is settled science. The response is contested policy.

This lecture pulls those two questions apart. By the end, you will be able to say what the science has established, what it leaves to human choice, and where the world's climate agreement stands.

We will take it in four steps.

First, what is settled: three claims, and one trap in how the numbers get reported.

Second, what the science cannot tell you, because it depends on choices nobody has made yet.

Third, the Paris Agreement: how it was built, and where it stands.

And last, the three kinds of response, and the three perspectives that weight them differently.

## Three settled claims

Three claims are now as solidly established as anything in observational science. Take them one at a time, each with its evidence.

Claim one. The planet has warmed significantly since pre-industrial times.

The number to hold is the long-term warming level. It now stands at about 1.3 to 1.4 degrees Celsius above the baseline of 1850 to 1900. Long-term means the average over roughly the past decade, and it is the number that matters for claims about the trend. Remember that phrase. We will need it soon.

How solid is the measurement? Independent teams keep their own records of surface temperature, among them NASA, NOAA and Berkeley Earth, and their records agree. They also agree with satellites, and with measurements of ocean heat, which involve entirely different instruments.

Claim two. The cause is primarily human emission of greenhouse gases.

The underlying physics is Victorian. John Tyndall measured how carbon dioxide absorbs infrared radiation in 1859. Svante Arrhenius computed a first estimate of the warming from doubled carbon dioxide in 1896.

And the added carbon carries a fingerprint. Before industry, the air held about 280 parts per million of carbon dioxide, and now it holds 427. The carbon that was added is ancient. It is devoid of carbon-14, and depleted in carbon-13. That is exactly what you would predict from burning plant matter that was buried long ago.

Other studies separate the human signal from the sun, from volcanoes, and from the climate's own internal swings. And the most recent comprehensive statement of the scientific consensus, the IPCC's Sixth Assessment, has one word for human influence on the warming. [beat] "Unequivocal."

Claim three. Continued emissions will produce continued warming.

Each doubling of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere yields about 3 degrees of warming, once the climate has settled. And the warming scales almost in a straight line with cumulative emissions: the running total of everything emitted. That is why net zero is the finish line with physical meaning.

> Temperatures stop rising only when net emissions stop.

So, in plainer words. The planet has warmed. People are the main cause. And more emissions will mean more warming.

None of those three claims is contested in the peer-reviewed literature. And this matters for everything that follows: disagreeing about climate policy does not require disagreeing with any of them. This series gives three perspectives on what to do, each at its strongest. All three accept the science you have just heard.

## A record year is not a warmer world

Now the trap. Here the numbers get confusing, and the confusion is worth mastering.

The World Meteorological Organization confirmed 2024 as the warmest year in the 175-year instrumental record. It ran about 1.55 degrees above the average of 1850 to 1900. That likely made it the first calendar year more than 1.5 degrees above pre-industrial.

Then Copernicus, the EU's climate service, ranked 2025 as the third-warmest year. And it reported that the three years from 2023 to 2025, averaged together, exceeded 1.5 degrees for the first time.

Now set that beside the Paris Agreement, and its target of 1.5 degrees.

A headline announces that Earth breached the 1.5-degree Paris limit in 2024. A scientist calls the headline wrong, but the situation grave. What is each of them seeing? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The headline has run two quantities together. One is a single year: 2024, at about 1.55 degrees. The other is the long-term warming level, and that is what the Paris targets actually refer to. It is currently about 1.3 to 1.4 degrees. So the limit has not formally been breached.

Why are the two so far apart? Because a single year rides on top of natural variability. The El Niño of 2023 and 2024 pushed global temperature well above the underlying trend for a time, just as a strong La Niña can pull a year below it. The Paris targets refer to the human-caused trend, averaged over decades.

> One hot year does not breach the target. It previews the trend.

And the scientist's gravity comes from that trend. The long-term level is rising by roughly 0.2 degrees per decade. On current trajectories, it is likely to cross 1.5 degrees in the 2030s.

Put another way: a record describes one year, and the Paris target describes the trend.

## A menu, not a forecast

That is the settled ground. Here is the turn.

Two kinds of statement get made about the climate, and they are easy to blur. One is established physics: more carbon dioxide produces more warming, within a known range. The other is a projection: how much carbon dioxide humanity will emit, on what timeline, under what policy.

No climate model can tell you that second thing, because it depends on choices that have not been made yet.

So the IPCC handles it with a set of standardized emissions scenarios. They are conditional projections, not predictions. If the world emits this much, then expect about this much warming.

At one end of the set is a path of steep cuts, reaching net zero by mid-century. Its best estimate for the end of this century is 1.4 degrees. At the other end is a path of very high emissions. Its best estimate is 4.4 degrees.

[beat]

The gap between those two numbers was not made by physics. It was made by human decisions.

> The scenarios are a menu, not a forecast.

So where on the menu is the world actually eating? The UN Environment Programme publishes an estimate. Its 2025 report puts warming this century at about 2.8 degrees, under current policies.

If every national pledge is fully carried out, the estimate is 2.3 to 2.5 degrees.

That is down from the 3 to 3.5 degrees the same exercise projected when Paris was signed, in 2015. It is also far above either Paris target.

Both ends of the menu now look unlikely. The real argument is over the middle.

And the budget arithmetic is unforgiving. By the estimate of the Global Carbon Budget, the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 degrees is virtually exhausted. What remains is roughly four years of emissions at the current rate.

One caution before we go on. The Paris temperature targets are themselves political judgments about tolerable risk, informed by the science but not dictated by it. Avoiding 2 degrees costs far less than avoiding 1.5. And the difference in damage between the two is real, but it is a matter of degree, not a cliff edge at a magic number.

## What the warming does

What does the warming do, in mechanical terms? The assessment documents specific consequences, each with a stated level of confidence. The confidence varies, and that is the honest way to hold this.

Sea level has risen about 20 centimeters since 1901, and the rise is accelerating.

Extreme heat is the clearest case. A heat wave that came once a decade in the pre-industrial climate now comes nearly three times as often, and runs hotter.

Tropical cyclones are different. There is no clear trend in their number. What is rising is the proportion that reach major intensity.

What remains harder is tying any single event to the climate. Take two claims. The first: climate change made this summer's heat dome more likely. The second: climate change caused one particular hurricane. Which claim should you trust more, and why?

[long pause]

The first. Warming shifts the entire distribution of temperatures, so for heat the probability calculation is direct. A hurricane involves many competing factors. The research supports trends in intensity and in rainfall. It does not support the claim that the climate caused one named storm.

The careful claim is always a probability: this event was made more likely. It is never a verdict. So treat anyone who offers certainty about one storm, in either direction, as overclaiming.

## The Paris machinery

Third stop: the agreement those targets come from.

The Paris Agreement, of 2015, was built around the failure of its predecessor. Kyoto had set binding quotas, and binding quotas collapsed on a hard problem. [beat] No world government exists to enforce them.

So Paris put pledge and review in their place. It has four parts.

A goal: hold warming well below 2 degrees, and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5.

A mechanism: each country writes its own pledge, called a Nationally Determined Contribution. The pledge is self-set, and legally nonbinding as to outcome. But every five years, each country must submit a stronger one. That is called the ratchet.

Transparency: standardized reporting and review, so that laggards are visible.

And finance: wealthy countries pledged 100 billion dollars a year to developing countries, a goal first met in 2022.

Now, those pledges are legally nonbinding. And the agreement's designers considered that a feature, not a bug. What problem were they solving? Think it through.

[long pause]

The enforcement problem. No authority can compel a sovereign state to comply. So a binding quota either deters countries from joining, or invites them to leave. The United States never ratified Kyoto. And Canada quit it.

Paris trades hard bindingness for universal participation, plus transparency, plus the ratchet. It is a bet that visibility, peer pressure and falling technology costs will do what enforcement cannot.

The economist Scott Barrett wrote the standard treatment of environmental treaty-making, and it explains why the room for design is so cramped. With no enforcer above sovereign states, he argues, a treaty must be self-enforcing: every party has to prefer staying in to walking out. That forces a trade-off between depth and breadth. Depth is how much the treaty demands. Breadth is how many countries join and comply.

Kyoto chose depth, and lost breadth.

> Paris chose breadth, and gave up enforceable depth.

There is a civics point here that Americans should not miss. The United States never ratified Paris as a treaty, through the Senate. The Obama and Biden administrations each joined it as an executive agreement. And that is exactly why American participation has swung with each presidency.

The country joined in 2016. It withdrew under the first Trump administration, rejoined under Biden, and withdrew again under the second Trump administration. That second withdrawal took effect on January 27, 2026.

So as of mid-2026, the United States is outside the agreement. It is the only country to have left it. [beat] Twice.

On Barrett's logic, an exit like that is no aberration. It is the design's predicted failure mode. The price of making membership cheap is that leaving is cheap too.

Meanwhile, American emissions tell their own story. In 2023 they were roughly 17 percent below the level of 2005. Most of that came from power plants switching from coal to gas, and from renewables. Those are market and technology forces, and they operated under every administration, in and out of Paris alike.

The next cuts are harder. Today's largest source is transportation: the sum of a hundred million dispersed decisions about vehicles and freight. And no single sector dominates. Eliminate the power sector's emissions entirely, and three-quarters of the problem is still standing.

## Three ways to respond

Last stop. Every climate policy ever proposed combines three kinds of response. Keeping them separate is the first step to combining them well.

Mitigation means reducing emissions: carbon pricing, standards for power plants and vehicles, subsidies for clean energy, halting deforestation. The costs land now. The benefits are avoided warming, decades out, shared across the globe.

Adaptation means building resilience to the warming that is already arriving: sea walls, flood-zone rules, drought-resistant crops, cooling centers. The costs land now here too. But the benefits arrive sooner, and they accrue locally.

Geoengineering means intervening in the climate system itself: reflecting sunlight with aerosols in the stratosphere, or removing carbon dioxide from the air at large scale. It is mostly unproven at scale, and its governance problems are as hard as the engineering. Nearly everyone treats it as a hedge, not a strategy.

[beat]

Mitigation. Adaptation. Geoengineering.

Before we meet the people who argue over that menu, reach back. Near the start, I gave you three settled claims. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The planet has warmed. People are the main cause. And continued emissions will produce continued warming.

Hold those three, because all three perspectives in this series accept them. Where they part is over how to weight the menu. Each gets one sentence here, built the same way.

The aggressive-mitigation advocate weights the menu toward cutting emissions, and expects to adapt along the way.

> The aggressive-mitigation advocate: mitigation first, with adaptation as the unavoidable companion.

The adaptation-first advocate inverts that priority, and weights the menu toward resilience, whose benefits arrive sooner and not in 2070.

> The adaptation-first advocate: resilience now avoids more suffering per dollar than emissions cuts.

The innovation-led centrist says the choice between those two is false, and weights the menu toward new technology.

> The innovation-led centrist: what cuts emissions is technological substitution, so accelerate it.

[pause]

Three weightings of one menu. You have heard one sentence of each, and the next lecture gives each its full case.

You may already lean toward one of them. So take the one you find least convincing. State the best case you can for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Two questions, kept apart. The warming is settled science: the planet has warmed, people are the main cause, and temperatures stop rising only when net emissions stop. The response is contested policy.

[beat]

Two. One hot year does not breach the target. It previews the trend. The Paris targets refer to the long-term warming level, now about 1.3 to 1.4 degrees, and not to any single year.

[beat]

Three. The scenarios are a menu, not a forecast. On current policies, the world is headed for about 2.8 degrees this century.

[beat]

Four. Paris chose breadth, and gave up enforceable depth. Its pledges are self-set and nonbinding. And as of mid-2026, the United States is outside it.

[beat]

And five. Three kinds of response: mitigation, adaptation, geoengineering. And three perspectives that accept the same science, and weight that menu differently.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled, and has not tried to. It has not made an argument about what to do. And it has not told you how much warming is acceptable, because that is a question of values, and the science cannot answer it.

The next lecture takes up what follows from that. How should future damages be valued against present costs? Who should bear them? And should price signals, regulation, or a push on technology carry the load? The disagreement there is genuine, which is why this series carries three perspectives, and not one.

For now, go back to the lava field. One instrument. One reading: 313. [beat] The line has climbed every year since. What that line means is settled. What to do about it is the argument.
