Two signing ceremonies, not quite three years apart.

August 16, 2022. President Biden signs the Inflation Reduction Act. As originally scored, it holds roughly 370 billion dollars in clean-energy tax credits: the largest legislated climate investment in American history.

July 4, 2025. President Trump signs the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which terminates or phases out the biggest of those credits.

[pause]

Both laws passed the same way: through budget reconciliation, on strict party-line votes. Reconciliation is the procedure that dodges the Senate filibuster, so a bare majority is enough. [beat] In both directions.

> What a bare majority can pass, the next bare majority can unwind.

That whiplash is not an accident of personalities. It is the structural fact of American climate policy today, and this lecture maps the landscape it has produced.

Last time, you heard three positions on what should be done. This time, the machinery that any of them has to work through. By the end, you will be able to say where American climate policy is actually decided, and what happens to a policy when its layer flips.

We will take it as four layers, with one stop along the way.

First, federal law: what was built, and what was unbuilt.

Second, the courts and the agencies.

Third, the states. And there we stop, to compare the three tools that every layer chooses among.

And last, the world outside.

## What was built, and what was unbuilt

Start with what the Inflation Reduction Act was. It never priced carbon. It never mandated a phase-out of fossil fuels. It was the innovation-led model from the last lecture, written into the tax code.

So it worked through credits. Credits for producing clean electricity, and for investing in it. A credit for manufacturing solar components and batteries at home. Credits for consumers, such as 7,500 dollars toward a new electric vehicle. And one stick: a fee on the methane wasted by oil and gas operations.

Modelers projected that it would push American emissions to roughly 40 percent below their 2005 level by 2030. That was most of the way to the pledge the country then had on file under Paris.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act dismantled the demand side of that machine, on a schedule. The credits for electric vehicles ended after September 30, 2025. Wind and solar projects keep their credits only if they met a construction deadline, twelve months after enactment, or enter service by the end of 2027. The methane fee was not repealed. It was deferred for a decade, to 2034.

Some things survived largely intact: credits for nuclear, geothermal and storage, on longer schedules, and a narrowed manufacturing credit.

What did that do to the outlook? The Rhodium Group publishes one: where American emissions will stand in 2035, measured against 2005. Under current policy, its answer is now 26 to 35 percent lower. A year earlier, with the credits and the rules intact, its answer was 38 to 56 percent lower.

[pause]

Call it roughly a decade of decarbonization, repriced in a single statute.

Let's test what you have. The Clean Air Act of 1970 has survived eleven presidencies. The core credits of the Inflation Reduction Act lasted three years. What structural difference best explains the gap? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The breadth of the coalition at enactment. The Clean Air Act passed with overwhelming bipartisan majorities. It cleared the Senate 73 to 0. So neither party owns repealing it, and both have constituencies inside it.

The Inflation Reduction Act passed with zero votes from the minority party. Reconciliation made that procedurally possible. It also left the law politically unentrenched. The opposing party could repeal it by the same bare-majority route, the moment it held the pen. And it did.

> Policy durability tends to track the size of the coalition that enacted it.

## The courts and the agencies

Second layer. Congress legislates on climate roughly once a decade. So most federal climate policy has lived in the agencies, in what is called the administrative state. And that means it has lived in court.

The arc runs through four landmarks. Take them in order.

The first is from 2007: a case called Massachusetts v. EPA. The Supreme Court held that greenhouse gases are "air pollutants" under the Clean Air Act. That obliged the EPA to regulate them, if it found that they endanger public health and welfare.

In 2009, the EPA made that finding. It is called the endangerment finding, and it became the load-bearing wall under every federal greenhouse-gas rule that followed: for vehicles, for power plants, for methane.

The second landmark is from 2022: West Virginia v. EPA. It capped how far that authority stretches. The Court held that the EPA could not use a marginal provision of the act to force a restructuring of the grid away from coal. And it announced the major questions doctrine: an agency needs clear authorization from Congress for an action of vast economic and political significance.

So a standard for an individual plant remained lawful. Redesigning the whole system by agency rule did not.

The third landmark is the deregulatory turn of 2025 and 2026, which went to the wall itself. In February 2026, the EPA finalized the rescission of the endangerment finding, together with every federal greenhouse-gas standard for vehicles built on it. The agency billed it as the largest deregulatory action in American history. Petitions for review were filed the day the rule was published.

Notice what changed, and what did not. Massachusetts v. EPA still stands. What changed is the agency's answer to the question that case ordered it to ask. The litigation now underway will decide whether that answer survives. Until it concludes, federal climate regulation is not rolled back so much as contested at the foundation.

The fourth landmark is California. Under the Clean Air Act, California may seek a waiver to set stricter vehicle standards, which other states may then adopt. In 2025, Congress used the Congressional Review Act to revoke the waivers behind California's electric-vehicle sales mandate and its truck rules. California and ten other states sued the same day. And a week later, the Supreme Court held that fuel producers have standing to challenge such waivers, which ensures that the fight on the merits will be heard.

[pause]

Now step back, and notice the deep structure. Statutes swing with reconciliation majorities. Rules swing with administrations. And the courts, once the referee of agency overreach, are now also the referee of agency retreat.

> The courts now referee agency retreat, as well as agency overreach.

A citizen who tracks only elections misses two of the three places where American climate policy is actually decided.

Run it forward. It is 2029, and a new administration wants federal greenhouse-gas standards for vehicles back. Assume the 2026 rescission has survived its court challenges. What is the minimum path? Think it through.

[long pause]

First, make the endangerment finding again, through full notice-and-comment rulemaking, with a record that can survive judicial review. Then issue the vehicle standards again, on top of it. Each step would be litigated, plausibly for years.

> Tearing down by rule is as slow to reverse as building by rule.

In other words, administrative policy is fragile in both directions.

## The states, and Ostrom's wager

Third layer. While the federal layer reversed, the state layer extended.

In September 2025, as Washington was finalizing repeals, California extended its economy-wide carbon market through 2045. It gave the program a new name, Cap-and-Invest, and a design detail worth remembering. Part of the auction revenue now goes to households, as credits on their utility bills. That choice is aimed squarely at the problem of political durability.

To the east, a compact called RGGI runs a cap-and-trade market for the power sector. With Virginia's return, it counts eleven states. Roughly thirty states maintain standards for renewable or clean electricity. And about two dozen governors recommitted their states to Paris-aligned targets after the federal withdrawal.

This is federalism doing what its theorists promise. And the scholar who saw it clearest was Elinor Ostrom, the only political scientist to win the economics Nobel.

The consensus of her era held that climate is a problem of the global commons, and so only a global treaty could solve it. Against that consensus, Ostrom argued for polycentric governance. That means many semi-autonomous centers of decision, such as cities, states, regional compacts and firms, each acting at its own scale.

Her case was not sentimental. A single global agreement, she observed, is brittle, slow, and starved of information. One defection can unravel it. Overlapping smaller systems experiment in parallel, copy what works, and build the trust that treaties presume. They also capture local benefits, like cleaner air and lower bills, which make action self-interested and not altruistic.

Her argument has an honest limit. Polycentric action has yet to bend national emissions as far as a binding national policy would. Leakage is real. And the states that are acting cover a minority of American emissions.

Ostrom's reply was that redundancy is the point.

> Ostrom's wager: when one center fails, the system retains working parts.

The American experience from 2022 to 2026 is about the cleanest natural experiment her theory has ever had. Whether action of this kind can substitute for a federal policy, or only bridge to one, is an open question.

## Price, mandate, or subsidy

Now the stop I promised. Strip away the acronyms, and American climate policy keeps choosing among three instruments. The choice is genuinely contested, and each of the three perspectives from last time ranks them differently. So set them side by side.

A carbon price, whether a tax or a cap-and-trade market, makes emitting expensive, and lets markets find the cheapest cuts. Consumers pay, at the pump and at the meter, where the cost is highly visible. In theory, it is the most efficient instrument per ton. It has never been enacted federally.

Regulation commands specific outcomes: a standard for a plant, a rule for a fleet. Firms pay first, and consumers pay through prices, so the cost is partly hidden. It is less efficient, but effective where prices cannot reach. And its record is whipsaw: rules issued, repealed, and relitigated with each administration.

A subsidy makes clean energy cheaper, and pulls deployment. Taxpayers pay, diffusely, almost invisibly. It is inefficient per dollar, but it builds industries and constituencies. It proved the easiest to pass. [beat] And then the easiest to repeal.

[pause]

Now read those three politically, and not only economically.

The carbon price is the economist's favorite, and the ballot box's least favorite: visible costs, diffuse benefits. Yet where its revenue is recycled locally, it has proven the most durable instrument in America.

Regulation hides its costs better, but it inherits the administrative pendulum.

Subsidies pass easiest and die easiest. But the repeal fight revealed a property of theirs that is underrated. Factories build constituencies. The loudest internal resistance to repeal came from members whose districts had won them.

> There is no dominant instrument. There are trade-offs among efficiency, visibility and survival.

Put that to work. A governor wants a climate policy that survives hostile successors, and that funds itself. Which instrument does the American record point to? And reach back to California: what design detail is doing the work?

[long pause]

A cap-and-trade program whose revenue is recycled locally. That is the model of California and of RGGI.

And the detail is the revenue. Auction proceeds pay for visible local goods, like bill credits and efficiency programs. Those create beneficiaries, and the beneficiaries defend the program. It is why California could extend its market to 2045, by a supermajority, in the same season the federal credits died.

A subsidy fails the second test, because it spends revenue. And the federal record suggests that a party-line subsidy fails the first test too.

## The international board

Last layer: the world outside.

Start with a summary, as of mid-2026.

> Paris survived the American exit. Every piece of it is strained.

The American withdrawal took effect on January 27, 2026: the second exit in a decade. And the 2025 round of national pledges arrived late, and thin.

China is now the variable that matters most. In September 2025, it announced its first absolute target for its whole economy: emissions 7 to 10 percent below their peak, by 2035. Analysts widely judged that conservative, against China's own pace of deployment. And that is the diplomatic point: underpromise, overdeliver, and let Washington own the ambition vacuum.

The fights over money continue. The finance goal for developing countries is now at least 300 billion dollars a year, by 2035. The climate talks of November 2025, in Belém, added a pledge to triple adaptation finance. They also showed the framework's limits. More than eighty countries pushed to put a roadmap for a fossil-fuel transition into the formal decision. Petrostates blocked it. It survived only as a voluntary process, outside the text.

The sharpest new instrument is European. It is called the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, and its definitive regime began on January 1, 2026. Importers of steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizer, electricity and hydrogen must account for the carbon embedded in those goods, at the EU's internal price.

In function, it is a carbon price enforced at the border, like a tariff. It is designed to stop leakage, and to export the EU's price.

For American exporters, it lands awkwardly. Their government rejects carbon pricing, so there is no domestic payment to credit against the European charge. That is a competitive argument for an American carbon price, and it arrives from trade policy, not from environmentalism.

## A position of your own

Before we gather up, one thing more. A defensible position on climate policy requires more in 2026 than it did in 2022. It requires four views.

A view on the mix of mitigation, adaptation and innovation: the three frames from last time.

A view on instruments, price, mandate or subsidy, that engages the evidence on durability, and not just the theory of efficiency.

A view on which layer you are betting on: federal statute, agency rule, the states, or pressure at the border from abroad. And on what happens to your policy when that layer flips.

And an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.

> A defensible position names its mix, instrument, layer, and what would change it.

So take the three frames once more: aggressive mitigation, adaptation first, innovation-led. Choose the one you find least persuasive. Then walk it through this landscape, and state its best case, the way its advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. What a bare majority can pass, the next bare majority can unwind. The Inflation Reduction Act built with subsidies, and the 2025 law ended or phased out the biggest of them. Policy durability tends to track the size of the coalition that enacted it.

[beat]

Two. The EPA's authority rests on Massachusetts v. EPA and the endangerment finding. West Virginia v. EPA capped it. In 2026 the agency rescinded the finding itself, and that rescission is now in court. Tearing down by rule is as slow to reverse as building by rule.

[beat]

Three. The states extended while the federal layer reversed. California's carbon market now runs to 2045. And Ostrom's wager is that when one center fails, the system retains working parts.

[beat]

Four. Price, mandate, or subsidy. There is no dominant instrument. There are trade-offs among efficiency, visibility and survival. The most durable so far has been a carbon price whose revenue stays local.

[beat]

And five. America left Paris, for the second time, on January 27, 2026. Paris survives, strained. And Europe now enforces a carbon price at its border.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Whether the endangerment rescission survives review. Whether California keeps its waiver. Whether polycentric action can substitute for a federal policy, or only bridge to one. And whether any democracy sustains visible climate costs across decades.

Those are the open questions, and they are where this argument goes next. When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position of your own, and to run the steel-man drill against the perspective you find least persuasive. That is the drill this series exists for.

For now, go back to the two signing ceremonies. One signature in August of 2022. One in July of 2025. [beat] Whatever you want climate policy to do, it has to live in one of those layers. And you know now what happens when a layer flips.
