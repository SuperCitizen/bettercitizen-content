Monday, October 8, 2018. Two announcements land, hours apart.

In Stockholm, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awards William Nordhaus the Nobel Memorial Prize in economics. He had built the first model to bolt a climate system onto a growing economy, and to ask which emissions path does most for human welfare.

In Incheon, South Korea, the IPCC releases a special report on global warming of 1.5 degrees. To hold warming there, it urges deep cuts in carbon dioxide by 2030: roughly 45 percent below the level of 2010.

Here is the awkward part. Nordhaus's own prize-winning model, run with his preferred assumptions, found the optimal amount of warming to be around 3 degrees or more. [beat] Double what the IPCC was calling the safety threshold. On the same day.

[pause]

Both documents accepted the same physics. They diverged on something else.

> The physics cannot settle how to value people who do not exist yet.

Nearly every honest disagreement in this lecture runs through that sentence.

Last time, we covered the science, which all three of our perspectives accept. This time, the argument. By the end, you should be able to state three positions the way their own advocates would, and to explain why people who share the same science and the same data still land in different places.

We will take it in three steps.

First, the fight under the fight: how much the future is worth.

Second, the three positions, one at a time. Each gets the same treatment: where it starts, the three arguments that carry it, and what evidence would change it.

And last, one set of emissions numbers, and three readings of it.

## The fight under the fight

Climate policy buys benefits for people who are decades away, with costs paid by people alive now. So any weighing of those costs and benefits needs a discount rate: the annual percentage by which future welfare is shrunk, to give it a value in the present.

Two economists show how much rides on it.

The Stern Review, commissioned by the UK Treasury, discounted the future at about 1.4 percent. Its argument was explicitly ethical. The only defensible reason to count a future person's welfare for less is the small chance that humanity will not be there at all.

Nordhaus's model, in that era, discounted at roughly 4.3 percent. His argument was from what people reveal by their choices. Discount rates should match the returns that societies actually demand from their investments, which can be observed in markets, and not a philosopher's ideal. An artificially low rate misallocates capital that could have grown into greater wealth for the future.

Stern's number told governments to decarbonize urgently. Nordhaus's recommended a modest carbon price, rising slowly.

Here is how hard the rate pulls. Take 1,000 dollars of climate damage in the year 2100, and ask what it is worth spending today to prevent it. At Stern's rate, about 350 dollars. At Nordhaus's rate, [beat] about 43.

[pause]

That is an eightfold gap, produced entirely by the choice of rate. The arithmetic is an illustration, with each rate held constant. But the two economists' own estimates of the social cost of carbon differed by roughly an order of magnitude, from identical climate science.

Notice what kind of disagreement this is. The choice between 1.4 percent and 4.3 percent is not an empirical question, waiting for better data. It is an ethical question in quantitative clothing: how much a person born in 2090 counts.

> Whoever picks the discount rate largely picks the verdict.

Then Martin Weitzman pulled the rug from under the whole exercise. In a famous paper of 2009, he argued that climate risk is fat-tailed.

Here is what that means. As outcomes get worse, they get less probable. But the probability of catastrophe, think 6 degrees or more, does not shrink fast enough for the arithmetic of expected values to behave. Push that logic to its end, and society should be willing to pay almost anything to trim the tail, whatever the discount rate. Weitzman called it his dismal theorem.

Practical economists do not take the theorem literally. But its working legacy is everywhere: climate policy framed as insurance against ruin, and not as an optimization.

Let's test all of that. An official says: "I take no ethical positions. I just discount at the market rate of return, like any investment analysis."

What would Stern say back? And Weitzman? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Stern would say that using the market rate is an ethical position. It imports savers' impatience about their own consumption into a question about other people's lives, a century out.

Weitzman would say that even granting the market rate, expected-value analysis breaks down when the losses are fat-tailed. The right frame is insurance, where we routinely pay premiums far above our expected losses.

Both deny that the official has escaped judgment. The official can defend the market rate, as Nordhaus did, but cannot call it no position at all. The judgment is still there, hidden inside a parameter.

## The aggressive-mitigation case

Now the three positions. Start with the aggressive-mitigation advocate.

This position starts from the research on damage and risk, and takes it seriously. Its conclusion: the costs of inaction now exceed the costs of decarbonization. And the damage, it adds, falls hardest on the populations that emitted least and can least afford to adapt.

Three arguments carry it.

First, damages compound. The harm at 3 degrees is far more than one and a half times the harm at 2. Stress on farming, failing infrastructure, collapsing ecosystems and migration all grow faster than the temperature does. And as researchers have counted sectors they once ignored, the damage estimates have generally been revised upward.

Second, the tail. Some impacts are plausibly irreversible at scale: the ice sheets of Greenland and West Antarctica destabilizing, the Amazon dying back, the permafrost releasing its carbon. This is Weitzman's point made concrete. When you cannot rule out ruin, optimizing for the expected outcome understates the value of prevention.

Third, the cost side collapsed. Between 2010 and the mid-2020s, solar, wind, batteries and electric vehicles fell in cost by roughly 70 to 90 percent. On this view, the old assumption that deep decarbonization meant deep sacrifice is empirically dead. And phasing out coal cuts particulate pollution, and the deaths from breathing it, on the spot.

> The aggressive-mitigation case: inaction now costs more than decarbonization. Buy the insurance.

What would change this position? Evidence that aggressive mitigation costs lower-income people more in welfare than the damage it spares them. Or evidence that adaptation can absorb several degrees of warming at acceptable cost.

Its serious advocates do not deny that the costs exist. They answer with policy design: carbon dividends, transition assistance, and international finance.

## The adaptation-first case

Now the adaptation-first advocate, built the same way.

This position starts from the warming that is already locked in. Even sharp cuts in emissions change the temperature path only marginally for decades, while adaptation pays off within years. And with the 1.5-degree target gone, it adds, honest planning is for a world of 2.5. From there, it is an argument about the marginal dollar.

Three arguments carry it.

First, adaptation demonstrably works. Compilations by Goklany show that the global death rate from extreme weather fell by roughly 98 percent between the 1920s and the 2000s, while world population more than tripled. The reasons were early-warning systems, building codes, infrastructure, and wealth. On this view, a dollar spent on flood defenses saves identifiable lives this decade, while a dollar of mitigation buys a sliver of avoided warming in 2070.

Second, the costs of mitigation are regressive, and politically explosive. Energy takes a bigger share of a poor household's budget, so carbon prices bite the poor hardest. And Roger Pielke Jr. has an iron law of climate policy: voters will tolerate only modest costs in the name of cutting emissions, and will reverse policies that exceed their tolerance. On this view, the United States of 2025 and 2026 is the demonstration: the largest climate-investment law in American history, partially repealed three years in.

Third, development is the best adaptation, and development requires energy. An average American's footprint of fossil carbon dioxide is about 14 tonnes a year. An average Indian's is about 2. Asking poor countries to slow the development that builds sea walls, grids and hospitals is, on this view, both unjust and self-defeating. [beat] Wealth is resilience.

> The adaptation-first case: warming is locked in, and resilience pays off within years.

What would change this position? Evidence that the next degree of warming produces damage that adaptation cannot absorb. Or mitigation costs falling so far that the marginal dollar flips.

Its advocates concede that the flip is underway in electricity. They argue it has not reached steel, cement, aviation or agriculture.

## The innovation-led case

And the innovation-led centrist, built the same way.

This position starts from what has actually cut emissions so far, which the frame of mitigation against adaptation misses. Its answer is technological substitution: cheaper low-carbon technology displacing fossil processes because it wins on its own terms.

Three arguments carry it.

First, the track record is technological. American and British emissions have declined since 2005, mostly through switching from coal to gas, and through renewables that fell in cost. The switch to gas was driven by the economics of fracking, not by climate law. Carbon prices, where they were implemented, produced real but smaller reductions. And a 2016 paper by Acemoglu and co-authors supplies the theory: subsidizing clean innovation directly can beat taxing dirty output, because innovation compounds.

Second, make clean cheap, because cheap is what diffuses. On this view, China and India will not adopt expensive virtue. They adopt cheap kilowatt-hours. So rich-world innovation that drops the global price of clean energy mitigates everywhere at once.

Third, fund the research. Energy research is badly underfunded relative to the stakes, and this case calls for multiplying it severalfold. A carbon price belongs in the mix, as a complement. But no price that could survive politically, on this view, drives substitution at the needed rate by itself.

> The innovation-led case: make clean energy cheap, because cheap is what diffuses.

What would change this position? Evidence that politically achievable carbon prices alone drive enough substitution, which would make the subsidies wasteful. Or evidence that innovation has hit physical limits in the hard sectors, which would make adaptation the binding strategy.

Its advocates have their own reading of the repeal you heard about earlier. The repealed law, the Inflation Reduction Act, was this position written into policy: hundreds of billions of dollars in tax credits, in place of a carbon price. On the innovation reading, its partial repeal is proof that the strategy works only if it builds durable constituencies faster than its opponents can organize.

## One set of numbers, three readings

Before the data, here is one problem that all three positions must face.

A ton of carbon dioxide that is never emitted benefits all eight billion people, each by a fraction, whoever paid for it. So each country can rationally hope that the others will pay. That is called free-riding.

Now think about a sea wall. Why does it escape the free-rider problem that haunts a carbon tax? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because its benefits stay local. The town that pays for the wall captures the protection.

Each position answers that problem in its own way. Adaptation-first advocates argue that adaptation aligns with a country's self-interest, where mitigation fights it. Innovation-led centrists argue that cheap clean energy routes around the problem entirely. And aggressive-mitigation advocates argue for treaties, finance and border mechanisms, which try to rebuild the missing incentive.

[pause]

Now the data that every one of those positions appeals to. It comes from the Global Carbon Budget, for 2025.

By share of the world's fossil carbon dioxide, China comes first, at 32 percent. The United States emits 13 percent. India emits 8.

Per person, you already have the numbers. About 14 tonnes a year for an average American. About 2 for an average Indian.

And there is a third measure. Counted up since the Industrial Revolution, the United States remains the largest cumulative emitter.

Now watch the same numbers argue three ways.

The aggressive-mitigation advocate reads the figures per person, and the cumulative ones. Americans emit nearly seven times what Indians do, on top of two centuries of banked emissions. On this reading, rich-world leadership is not charity but arrears. The countries that used the atmosphere's budget owe the steepest cuts, and the financing for everyone else's transition.

The adaptation-first advocate reads India's numbers, as destiny and not as a problem. Development will raise that figure for decades, and should, because energy is how poor countries build what survives a warmer world. On this reading, significant further warming is already baked into the arithmetic of global development. So the honest policy plans for it: resilience first.

The innovation-led centrist reads the China figure. Nearly a third of the world total is decided by the price of Chinese solar, batteries and coal power, and tomorrow's totals by what India and its successors find cheapest. On this reading, the highest-leverage American policy is whatever makes clean energy the cheap option in Asia. No treaty required.

> None of the three is misreading the numbers. They are answering different questions.

Try it on a dispute. One negotiator says China is the problem: 32 percent of emissions. Another says America is: 14 tonnes per head, and the largest cumulative total. Who is factually wrong?

[long pause]

Neither. And that is the point. Absolute emissions, emissions per person and cumulative emissions are all real measurements, and they answer different questions. Where is the most leverage now? That is the absolute figure. Who is living highest-carbon? Per person. Who used the atmosphere's budget? Cumulative.

> A careful citizen names the metric before drawing the conclusion.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The physics cannot settle how to value people who do not exist yet. That is the work of the discount rate. And whoever picks the discount rate largely picks the verdict.

[beat]

Two. The aggressive-mitigation case: inaction now costs more than decarbonization. Buy the insurance. Its arguments: damages compound, ruin cannot be ruled out, and the cost of clean energy collapsed.

[beat]

Three. The adaptation-first case: warming is locked in, and resilience pays off within years. Its arguments: adaptation demonstrably works, voters reverse costly policies, and development requires energy.

[beat]

Four. The innovation-led case: make clean energy cheap, because cheap is what diffuses. Its arguments: the track record is technological, cheap energy spreads without a treaty, and research is underfunded.

[beat]

And five. Share of the total, emissions per person, and cumulative emissions are all real measurements. A careful citizen names the metric before drawing the conclusion.

[pause]

Now, what is settled, and what is not.

Settled, or nearly so: the core science. That clean-energy costs collapsed after 2010. That 1.5 degrees is, for practical purposes, out of reach without overshoot and large-scale carbon removal. And that climate damages are already material to American infrastructure, agriculture and insurance markets.

Genuinely contested: the discount rate, which is to say the ethics of trade-offs between generations. How much weight the catastrophic tail should carry. Whether prices, regulation or subsidized innovation actually drives substitution. And whether any high-cost mitigation policy can survive democratic politics long enough to matter. On that last question, the American experience since 2022 is now Exhibit A for every side.

So this lecture gives no verdict among the three. A defensible position picks one of the three frames, states its discount-rate ethics out loud, and names the evidence that would change it.

For now, go back to October 8, 2018. Two documents, one day, the same physics. [beat] You know now what stood between them. Not the science. The worth of a person not yet born.
