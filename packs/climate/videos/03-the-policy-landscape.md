## Two signatures

Two signing ceremonies, not quite three years apart. On August 16, 2022, President Biden signed the Inflation Reduction Act, the largest legislated climate investment in American history. On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which terminated or phased out its biggest credits.

- August 2022: the IRA is signed
- July 2025: its biggest credits are ended

[beat]

That whiplash is not an accident of personalities. It is the structural fact of American climate policy.

## Built by a bare majority

Both laws passed through budget reconciliation, on strict party-line votes. That procedure dodges the Senate filibuster. But it produces statutes a bare majority can pass, and the next bare majority can unwind.

> What a bare majority passes, the next bare majority can unwind.

Compare the Clean Air Act of 1970. It cleared the Senate 73 to nothing, and it has survived eleven presidencies. The IRA's core credits lasted three years. Durability tends to track the size of the coalition that enacted a policy.

- Clean Air Act: 73–0, eleven presidencies
- IRA core credits: three years

## The layer that kept going

Federal statute is only one layer. While it reversed, the state layer extended. In September 2025, California extended its economy-wide carbon market through 2045, and routed part of the auction revenue to household utility-bill credits.

- California's carbon market: extended to 2045

The political scientist Elinor Ostrom argued for this polycentric governance: many decision centers, so that when one fails, the system retains working parts. The lesson gives the limit of her argument too. The states acting cover a minority of United States emissions.

- Ostrom: many centers, not one
- Limit: a minority of U.S. emissions

## Price, mandate or subsidy

American climate policy keeps choosing among three instruments, and the choice is genuinely contested. A carbon price puts its costs in plain sight. Yet where its revenue is recycled locally, as in California, it has proven the most durable instrument in America. Regulation hides costs better, but swings with each administration. Subsidies pass easiest and die easiest.

- Carbon price: durable where revenue stays local
- Regulation: swings with each administration
- Subsidy: easiest to pass, easiest to repeal

> No instrument dominates. Each trades efficiency, visibility and survival.

## What is still open

The lesson leaves the live questions open. Can action by many centers substitute for a federal policy, or only bridge to one? And can any democracy sustain visible climate costs across decades?

- Substitute, or only a bridge
- Visible costs, across decades

The lesson has what the 2025 law ended and what it kept, two decades of court rulings on what the EPA may regulate, and the international board after the second American exit from Paris.
