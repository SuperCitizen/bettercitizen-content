## Two announcements, one Monday

On Monday, October 8, 2018, two announcements landed hours apart. In Stockholm, William Nordhaus was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize, for a model that asks what emissions path maximizes human welfare. In South Korea, the IPCC released a report urging cuts to hold warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius.

[beat]

Nordhaus's own model, run with his preferred assumptions, found the optimal amount of warming to be around 3 degrees or more. Both documents accepted the same physics.

> Same physics, different advice. The split is over how to value the future.

## The price of the future

Climate policy buys benefits for people decades away, with costs paid by people alive now. So any cost-benefit analysis needs a discount rate: the annual percentage by which future welfare is shrunk to present value.

- Discount rate: how much the future counts

The Stern Review discounted at about 1.4 percent, on an ethical argument. Nordhaus discounted at roughly 4.3 percent, on an argument from the returns societies actually demand. Take 1,000 dollars of climate damage in 2100. Stern's rate justifies spending about 350 dollars today to prevent it. Nordhaus's rate, about 43.

- Stern, 1.4%: about $350 today
- Nordhaus, 4.3%: about $43 today

> Whoever picks the rate largely picks the verdict.

## Three cases

The lesson gives three positions. The aggressive-mitigation case argues that the costs of inaction now exceed the costs of decarbonization, and that when ruin cannot be ruled out, prevention is insurance worth buying.

- Aggressive mitigation: insurance against ruin

The adaptation-first case argues that warming is already locked in, and that a dollar spent on flood defenses saves identifiable lives this decade, while a dollar of mitigation buys a sliver of avoided warming in 2070.

- Adaptation first: resilience pays off sooner

The innovation-led case argues that what has actually cut emissions is cheaper low-carbon technology displacing fossil processes, and that cheap clean energy mitigates everywhere at once.

- Innovation led: make clean energy cheap

## One table, three readings

Every position appeals to the same emissions data. China emits 32 percent of the world's fossil carbon dioxide. An average American emits about 14.3 tonnes a year, nearly seven times an average Indian's 2.1. And the United States is the largest cumulative emitter in history.

- China: 32% of the world total
- United States: about 14.3 tonnes per person
- India: about 2.1 tonnes per person
- United States: largest cumulative emitter

> None of the three is misreading the table. They answer different questions.
