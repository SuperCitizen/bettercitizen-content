# The current policy landscape

Two signing ceremonies, not quite three years apart. On August 16, 2022, President Biden signed the Inflation Reduction Act — roughly $370 billion (as originally scored) in clean-energy tax credits, the largest legislated climate investment in American history. On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which terminated or phased out the IRA's biggest credits. Both laws passed through budget [[term:reconciliation]] on strict party-line votes — the procedure that dodges the Senate [[term:filibuster]] but produces statutes a bare majority can pass and the next bare majority can unwind. That whiplash is not an accident of personalities. It is the structural fact of contemporary American climate policy, and this lesson maps the landscape it produced: a federal layer in reverse, a judicial layer that polices both directions, a state layer that grinds on regardless, and an international layer that just watched the world's largest economy walk out — again.

## What the IRA built — and what the OBBBA unbuilt

The IRA never priced carbon and never mandated a fossil phase-out. It was the innovation-led model from Lesson 2 written into the tax code: production and investment credits for clean electricity (made technology-neutral from 2025), a 45X credit for domestic manufacturing of solar components, batteries, and processed minerals, consumer credits ($7,500 for new EVs, $4,000 used, heat-pump and efficiency credits), a clean-hydrogen credit scaled to emissions intensity, expanded Energy Department loan authority, and one stick — a fee on methane waste from oil-and-gas operations. Modelers (Princeton's REPEAT project, Rhodium Group, Energy Innovation) projected it would push U.S. emissions to roughly 40 percent below 2005 levels by 2030, most of the way to the 50–52 percent pledge then on file under Paris.

The OBBBA dismantled the demand-side core of that machine, on a schedule worth knowing precisely. The EV credits (new, used, and commercial) terminated after **September 30, 2025**; the EV-charging-infrastructure credit followed on June 30, 2026. Residential efficiency and rooftop-solar credits ended with 2025. Wind and solar projects lost the clean-electricity credits unless they began construction by **July 4, 2026** — twelve months from enactment — or are placed in service by the end of 2027. The hydrogen credit ends for projects starting construction after 2027. The methane fee was not repealed but deferred a decade, to 2034. Unobligated IRA grant money was rescinded across dozens of programs. What survived largely intact: credits for nuclear, geothermal, and storage on longer schedules, and a narrowed 45X.

The measurable consequence, per Rhodium's *Taking Stock 2025*: U.S. emissions are now projected to land **26 to 35 percent below 2005 levels in 2035** under current policy, where the same exercise a year earlier — IRA intact, EPA rules intact — projected 38 to 56 percent. Call it roughly a decade of decarbonization repriced in a single statute.

<Checkpoint question="The Clean Air Act of 1970 has survived eleven presidencies; the IRA's core credits lasted three years. What structural difference best explains the gap?">
  Coalition breadth at enactment. The Clean Air Act passed with overwhelming bipartisan majorities (it cleared the Senate 73–0), so neither party owns repealing it and both have constituencies inside it. The IRA passed through reconciliation with zero minority-party votes, which made it procedurally possible — no filibuster — but politically unentrenched: the opposing party could repeal it by the same bare-majority route the moment it held the pen (and did, in the OBBBA). Policy durability tends to track the size of the coalition that enacted it.
</Checkpoint>

## The courts and the administrative state

Because Congress legislates on climate roughly once a decade, most federal climate policy has lived in the [[term:administrative-state]] — which means it has lived in court. The arc runs through four landmarks.

***Massachusetts v. EPA* (2007)** held that greenhouse gases are "air pollutants" under the Clean Air Act, obligating EPA to regulate them if it found they endanger public health and welfare. EPA made that **endangerment finding** in 2009, and it became the load-bearing wall under every federal GHG rule that followed — vehicle standards, power-plant rules, methane rules.

***West Virginia v. EPA* (2022)** capped how far that authority stretches. The Court held EPA could not use a marginal provision of the Act to force "generation shifting" — restructuring the grid away from coal — announcing the **major questions doctrine**: agencies need clear congressional authorization for actions of vast economic and political significance. Plant-level standards remained lawful; system-wide redesign by agency rule did not. (Two years later, *Loper Bright* ended *Chevron* deference generally, further shortening every agency's leash.)

**The 2025–26 deregulatory turn** then attacked the wall itself. In June 2025, EPA proposed repealing all greenhouse-gas standards for power plants — resting primarily on a finding that the sector's emissions do not "contribute significantly" to dangerous pollution; that final rule was under White House review as of this writing in mid-2026. And in February 2026, EPA finalized the **rescission of the 2009 endangerment finding itself**, together with every federal vehicle GHG standard built on it — billed by the agency as the largest deregulatory action in U.S. history. The rule took effect April 20, 2026, and was challenged in court within days. *Massachusetts v. EPA* still stands; what changed is the agency's answer to the question that case ordered it to ask. The litigation now underway will decide whether that answer survives — and until it concludes, federal regulatory climate policy is not "rolled back" so much as *contested at the foundation*.

**California's parallel system** is the fourth front. Under the Clean Air Act, California may seek a waiver to set stricter vehicle standards, which other states may adopt — the §177 mechanism that gave Sacramento outsized national influence. In May and June 2025, Congress used the Congressional Review Act to revoke the waivers behind California's EV-sales mandate and truck rules (signed June 12, 2025); California and ten other states sued the same day, disputing whether waivers are "rules" the CRA can reach. A week later, in *Diamond Alternative Energy v. EPA* (2025), the Supreme Court held 7–2 that fuel producers have standing to challenge waivers — ensuring the merits fight will be heard. Watch this docket: it decides whether the country's most aggressive vehicle policy has a legal future.

> **Why this matters:** Notice the regime's deep structure. Statutes swing with reconciliation majorities; rules swing with administrations; and the courts — once the referee of agency overreach — are now also the referee of agency *retreat*. A citizen who only tracks elections misses two of the three places American climate policy is actually decided.

<Checkpoint question="It is 2029, and a new administration wants federal vehicle GHG standards back. Assuming the 2026 rescission survived its court challenges, what is the minimum path?">
  Re-make the endangerment finding through full notice-and-comment rulemaking (with a record that can survive judicial review), then re-issue vehicle standards on top of it — each step litigated, plausibly for years. The CRA adds a further wrinkle for California-style waivers: once a rule is disapproved, an agency may not issue one "substantially the same" without new congressional authorization. The general lesson: tearing down by rule is as slow to reverse as building by rule. Administrative policy is symmetrically fragile.
</Checkpoint>

## The states' layer — and Ostrom's wager

While the federal layer reversed, the state layer extended. Three days of September 2025 illustrate: as Washington was finalizing repeals, Sacramento enacted AB 1207 and SB 840, extending California's economy-wide carbon market through **2045** under a new name, **Cap-and-Invest** — auction revenue now partly routed to household utility-bill credits, a design choice aimed squarely at the political-durability problem. The program remains linked with Quebec's. To the east, **RGGI** — the power-sector cap-and-trade compact — counts eleven states with Virginia's return effective July 1, 2026 (enacted February 2026, reversing the prior governor's contested exit); its allowance auctions averaged about $22 per ton in 2025 and cleared in the roughly $25–35 range in 2026. Roughly thirty states maintain renewable or clean-electricity standards, several targeting 100 percent clean power — New York by 2040, California and Washington by 2045. Vermont enacted the nation's first "climate superfund" law in 2024, New York followed within months — both assess fossil producers for historical climate damages, and both are, predictably, in litigation. About two dozen governors in the U.S. Climate Alliance recommitted their states to Paris-aligned targets after the federal withdrawal.

This is [[term:federalism]] doing exactly what its theorists promise — and the political scientist who saw it clearest was **Elinor Ostrom**, the only political scientist to win the economics Nobel (2009, shared). Against the era's consensus that climate, as a global commons problem, could only be solved by a global treaty, Ostrom argued in her final years for **polycentric governance**: many semi-autonomous decision centers — cities, states, regional compacts, firms — acting at their own scales. Her case was not sentimental. Single global agreements, she observed, are brittle (one defection unravels them — see January 27, 2026), slow, and informationally starved; overlapping smaller systems experiment in parallel, copy what works, build the trust that treaties presume, and capture local co-benefits (cleaner air, lower bills) that make action self-interested rather than altruistic. California's linkage with Quebec and RGGI's auction design — later borrowed by other markets — are textbook Ostrom. So is the honest limit of her argument: polycentric action has yet to bend *national* emissions as far as a binding national policy would, leakage is real, and the states acting cover a minority of U.S. emissions. Her reply: redundancy is the point. When one center fails — as the federal center just did, twice in a decade — the system retains working parts. The 2022–2026 American experience is about the cleanest natural experiment her theory has ever gotten.

## Price, mandate, or subsidy

Strip away the acronyms and U.S. climate policy keeps choosing among three instruments. The choice is genuinely contested — each perspective from Lesson 2 ranks them differently — and the comparison is clearest side by side:

| | Carbon price (tax or cap-and-trade) | Regulation (standards) | Subsidy (tax credits) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core mechanism | Make emitting expensive; let markets find the cheapest cuts | Command specific outcomes (plant standards, fleet rules) | Make clean cheaper; pull deployment |
| Who visibly pays | Consumers, at the pump and meter — *highly* visible | Firms first; consumers via prices — partly hidden | Taxpayers, diffusely — nearly invisible |
| Raises or spends revenue | Raises (refundable as dividends or bill credits) | Neither | Spends |
| Economist's verdict | Most efficient per ton, in theory | Less efficient; effective where prices can't reach | Inefficient per dollar, but builds industries and constituencies |
| U.S. record | Never enacted federally; durable in CA (to 2045) and RGGI, where revenue stays local | Whipsawed: rules issued, repealed, relitigated each administration; endangerment finding itself rescinded 2026 | Easiest to pass (IRA 2022) — and, the OBBBA proved, easiest to repeal |

Read the table politically, not just economically. The carbon price is the economist's favorite and the ballot box's least favorite — visible costs, diffuse benefits — yet where its revenue is recycled locally it has proven the *most* durable instrument in America. Regulation hides costs better but inherits the administrative pendulum. Subsidies pass easiest and die easiest, though the OBBBA fight revealed their underrated property: factories build constituencies, and the loudest internal resistance to repeal came from members whose districts had won them. There is no dominant instrument; there are trade-offs among efficiency, visibility, and survival.

<Checkpoint question="A governor wants climate policy that (a) survives hostile successors and (b) funds itself. Which instrument does the U.S. record point to, and what design detail is doing the work?">
  A cap-and-trade program with locally recycled revenue — the California/RGGI model. Durability has come not from the instrument's efficiency but from the *revenue*: auction proceeds funding visible local goods (bill credits, efficiency programs) create beneficiaries who defend the program, which is why California could extend to 2045 by supermajority in the same season the federal credits died. A subsidy fails test (b) — it spends revenue — and the federal record suggests party-line subsidies fail test (a) too.
</Checkpoint>

## The international board

The Paris architecture survived the American exit, but mid-2026 finds every piece of it strained. The U.S. withdrawal took effect **January 27, 2026** — the second exit in a decade, executed by [[term:executive-order]] and Article 28's one-year clock. The 2025 pledge round ("NDCs 3.0") arrived late and thin: the UNEP gap numbers from Lesson 1 (2.3–2.5°C if pledges are honored; about 2.8°C on current policies) already price it in. China, now the variable that matters most, announced its first absolute economy-wide target in September 2025 — greenhouse-gas emissions 7 to 10 percent below peak by 2035, non-fossil sources above 30 percent of energy, wind and solar capacity at 3,600 gigawatts — numbers analysts widely judged conservative against China's own deployment pace, which is the diplomatic point: underpromise, overdeliver, and let Washington own the ambition vacuum.

The money fights continue on two tracks. The COP29 finance goal — at least **$300 billion per year by 2035** for developing countries, inside a $1.3 trillion mobilization call — replaced the old $100 billion goal (first met in 2022). COP30 in Belém (November 2025) added a pledge to triple adaptation finance by 2035, and showcased the framework's limits: more than eighty countries pushed to put a fossil-fuel transition roadmap in the formal decision, petrostates blocked it, and the initiative survived only as a voluntary, Brazil-led process outside the text.

The sharpest new instrument is European. The EU's **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** entered its definitive regime January 1, 2026: importers of steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizer, electricity, and hydrogen must account for embedded carbon at the EU's internal price (certificate purchases begin in 2027; a 50-tonne threshold exempts about 90 percent of importers while keeping about 99 percent of covered emissions). CBAM is, functionally, a carbon price enforced at the border like a [[term:tariff]] — Ostrom-style unilateralism at continental scale, designed to stop leakage and to export the EU's price. For U.S. exporters it lands awkwardly: their government rejects carbon pricing, so there is no domestic payment to credit against the European charge — a competitive argument for a U.S. carbon price that arrives from trade policy rather than environmentalism.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on climate policy in 2026 requires more than it did in 2022:

1. A view on the **mitigation–adaptation–innovation mix** (Lesson 2's three frames).
2. A view on **instruments** — price, mandate, or subsidy — that engages the durability evidence above, not just the efficiency theory.
3. A view on **which layer you are betting on** — federal statute, agency rule, the states, or border-pressure from abroad — and what happens to your policy when that layer flips.
4. An honest account of **what evidence would change your mind**.

What this lesson did not settle: whether the endangerment rescission survives review, whether California keeps its waiver, whether polycentric action can substitute for a federal policy or only bridge to one, and whether any democracy sustains visible climate costs across decades. Those are the live questions — which makes them exactly what the Coach will press you on.
