# The current policy landscape

The most important U.S. climate-policy event of the past decade was the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022. Understanding what it does (and doesn't do) is the prerequisite to assessing what comes next.

## The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022

The IRA is the largest U.S. climate investment ever made — roughly $370B over 10 years in tax credits, direct spending, and loan guarantees. Its core elements:

- **Production tax credits and investment tax credits** for wind, solar, geothermal, hydropower, and storage. Most credits are technology-neutral after 2025 (i.e., available to any technology meeting an emissions threshold).
- **Manufacturing tax credits (45X)** for domestic production of solar cells, wind turbines, batteries, and processed critical minerals.
- **Consumer-facing tax credits**: $7,500 for new EVs (with content rules), $4,000 used EVs, $2,000 heat pumps, etc.
- **Clean hydrogen production credit (45V)** with rates scaling by emissions intensity.
- **Direct loans and loan guarantees** through DOE's Loan Programs Office.
- **Methane fee** on oil-and-gas operations exceeding intensity thresholds.
- **Environmental Justice provisions** for disproportionately-impacted communities.

What the IRA *doesn't* do:
- It doesn't price carbon directly (no carbon tax or cap-and-trade).
- It doesn't regulate fossil-fuel use directly (no fossil-fuel phaseout mandate).
- It doesn't address adaptation in significant volume (most spending is mitigation-focused).

This is essentially the innovation-led model from Lesson 2: targeted technology subsidies + manufacturing incentives + infrastructure investment, rather than carbon pricing or regulation. The aggressive-mitigation advocates wanted more (a carbon price, fossil-fuel phaseouts); the adaptation-first advocates wanted more on resilience; the innovation-led centrists got most of what they wanted.

Independent modeling (Princeton REPEAT Project, Rhodium Group, Energy Innovation) projects U.S. emissions falling 40-44% below 2005 levels by 2030 with the IRA fully implemented, vs. the U.S. NDC of 50-52%. The IRA closes most of the gap to the U.S. Paris pledge but doesn't close all of it.

## EPA regulatory authority post-West Virginia

*West Virginia v. EPA* (2022) limited the EPA's authority to require "generation shifting" (cap-and-trade-style regulation that pushes utilities away from coal) under §111(d) of the Clean Air Act. The Court applied the "major questions doctrine" — Congress must speak clearly when authorizing agency action of major political-economic significance.

What EPA can still do post-West Virginia:
- **Set emissions standards for power plants** that are achievable through technology improvements at the plant level (e.g., carbon capture, efficiency improvements).
- **Set vehicle emissions standards** (which the EPA has done aggressively for cars and trucks).
- **Regulate methane** from oil-and-gas operations.
- **Set criteria-pollutant standards** that have climate co-benefits.

What EPA cannot easily do:
- Force utility-level fuel switching beyond what individual plants can achieve.
- Impose economy-wide carbon pricing without explicit congressional authorization.

This means most ambitious climate regulation now needs new legislation. The IRA was the largest available legislation in this congressional term; the next opportunity depends on political dynamics.

## State-level climate policy

Most aggressive U.S. climate action has been at the state level:

- **California Cap-and-Trade**: economy-wide carbon market, linked with Quebec, covering ~85% of state emissions. Carbon prices ~$30/ton; allowance auctions generate state revenue.
- **RGGI (Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative)**: 12 Northeast/Mid-Atlantic states, power-sector-only cap-and-trade. Allowance prices ~$10-15/ton.
- **Renewable Portfolio Standards (RPS)**: 30 states require utilities to source a fraction of generation from renewables; some (CA, NY, WA) have 100%-clean targets by 2045.
- **State emissions targets**: ~24 states have economy-wide emissions targets (varies in stringency); ~20 have net-zero targets by 2045-2050.
- **California's vehicle authority**: under §177 of the Clean Air Act, California can set stricter vehicle emissions standards than EPA, and other states can adopt California's. This gives California an outsized role in national vehicle regulation.

State-level action has been the primary driver of U.S. climate policy when federal legislation has stalled. The IRA changes the calculus by making federal-level investment matter more, but state-level regulation continues to be where most economy-wide pricing happens.

## What comes next

- **EPA implementation rules.** The IRA depends on Treasury and IRS regulations to implement the tax credits. Each rule (especially the 45V hydrogen rules and the 30D EV content rules) is its own political battle. Already-issued regulations have been challenged in court.

- **Paris ratchet (2025).** All Paris signatories submit updated NDCs. The U.S. update will depend heavily on the 2024-2028 administration's positioning.

- **EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM).** The EU is phasing in carbon-content tariffs on imports from countries without comparable carbon pricing. U.S. exports to the EU will face these tariffs starting in 2026 unless the U.S. has a comparable mechanism. This creates pressure for a U.S. carbon price (or carbon-intensity standard) that the IRA does not currently provide.

- **The next major U.S. legislation.** Possibilities include: a stand-alone carbon pricing bill, a clean-electricity standard, expanded transmission build-out, or further deployment subsidies. Which of these advance depends on political dynamics largely outside the climate-policy frame.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on climate policy now requires:

1. A view on **what mix of mitigation, adaptation, and innovation** the right policy package contains.
2. A view on **what role government should play** — pricing, regulation, subsidies, R&D, or some combination.
3. A view on **how international coordination** should work, given the free-rider problem.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
