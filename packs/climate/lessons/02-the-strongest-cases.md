# The strongest case for and against

The participants in climate-policy debate often agree on the science (the IPCC findings) and disagree about the response. Three positions, each presented as one its adherents would themselves endorse.

## 1. The aggressive-mitigation case

The starting move is to take the integrated-assessment economics seriously and to argue that the costs of inaction are larger than the costs of decarbonization, particularly when distributional effects and tail risks are properly accounted for.

The argument:

- **Climate damages compound nonlinearly with warming.** The damage at 3°C is much more than 1.5× the damage at 2°C — agricultural failures, infrastructure stress, ocean ecosystem collapse, and human migration scale superlinearly. The Stern Review (2007) was the first systematic accounting; subsequent literature (Nordhaus's work despite his more conservative discount rate; Stern's updated work; the IPCC AR6 WG2 chapters) converges on damages being larger than older estimates suggested. The aggressive-mitigation case argues that the standard cost-benefit analysis, when done with appropriate risk aversion, supports rapid decarbonization.

- **The cost of decarbonization has fallen dramatically.** Solar, wind, batteries, and electric vehicles have all dropped 70-90% in cost since 2010. The 2010-era assumption that decarbonization was prohibitively expensive is now empirically wrong. The cost of additional mitigation in 2025 is much lower than the cost looked in 2005, and is falling further. The economic case for acting now is stronger than at any prior time.

- **Co-benefits are large and immediate.** Mitigation policies often deliver large near-term benefits in air quality, public health, and energy security that are independent of the climate benefit. Coal phaseout produces measurable reductions in respiratory disease and premature mortality regardless of any climate benefit. The aggressive-mitigation case argues that even setting climate aside, many mitigation policies pass cost-benefit on co-benefits alone.

- **Distributional analysis favors action.** The richest and most-resilient countries can afford to adapt; the poorest and most-vulnerable cannot. Climate damage falls disproportionately on populations that have contributed least to emissions. The aggressive-mitigation case argues this distributional asymmetry is itself a moral argument for the wealthy world to lead. Klein's *This Changes Everything* makes the global-justice version of this argument; mainstream development economics largely concurs on the empirical pattern even when disagreeing about the framing.

- **Tipping-point risk justifies precaution.** Some climate impacts may be irreversible at scale (Greenland ice sheet, Amazon dieback, methane releases from permafrost). Standard cost-benefit analysis with central-tendency expectations underestimates the value of avoiding these. The aggressive-mitigation position argues for explicit risk premia in policy design — paying more now to reduce the probability of catastrophic outcomes.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that aggressive mitigation imposes welfare losses (especially on lower-income populations) larger than the avoided climate damage, or that adaptation can be scaled fast enough to absorb several degrees of warming without unacceptable losses. The aggressive-mitigation position concedes these are real concerns and tries to address them through transition assistance, carbon-dividend designs, and international finance rather than dismissing them.

## 2. The adaptation-first case

The starting move is to take the limits of mitigation seriously: even very aggressive emissions reductions only avoid future warming at the margin, and the warming already locked in (because of historical emissions and the lifetime of CO2 in the atmosphere) requires substantial adaptation regardless. From there, the argument is about resource allocation.

The argument:

- **Adaptation produces measurable, near-term reduction in suffering.** Sea walls, flood-zone restrictions, drought-resistant crops, heat-tolerance infrastructure, public-health systems for heat waves — all produce immediate, quantifiable reductions in damage from current and near-term climate events. Goklany's work documents how 20th-century mortality from extreme weather fell ~98% even as population grew, primarily because of adaptation (early warning, building codes, infrastructure). The adaptation-first case argues the marginal dollar saves more lives in adaptation than in mitigation today.

- **Mitigation costs are borne disproportionately by the poor.** Energy-price increases from carbon pricing or regulation hit low-income households harder (energy is a larger share of their budget). Coal phaseouts in Appalachia or the Ruhr produce concentrated job losses while the climate benefit is dispersed globally. The adaptation-first case argues that aggressive-mitigation rhetoric underweights these distributional costs and that, in democratic systems, ignoring them produces backlash that ultimately undoes the mitigation policy. Lomborg's *False Alarm* makes the strongest version of this case (and is controversial in the literature for that reason).

- **The 1.5°C target is already infeasible.** Current emissions trajectories make 1.5°C all-but-impossible without large-scale negative-emissions technology that doesn't yet exist at necessary scale. Continuing to insist on the target sets policies up to fail and shifts attention away from the more achievable 2.5°C-3°C reality. Adaptation, on this view, is more honest about what's coming.

- **The poorest countries' priority is development, not mitigation.** A villager in sub-Saharan Africa using firewood for cooking, with no electric grid access, contributes ~0.5 tons CO2/year. An average American contributes ~15 tons/year. Asking the villager to forgo development to avoid emissions while the American keeps emitting fails on basic justice grounds. The adaptation-first case argues that development-led emissions in poor countries is a moral right, and that mitigation policy that constrains it is unjust regardless of climate effects.

- **Global free-rider problems make mitigation harder than adaptation.** Mitigation is a pure public good (everyone benefits regardless of who paid); adaptation captures most benefits locally (a sea wall protects the people behind it). This means adaptation aligns better with sovereign interests. Pielke Jr.'s "iron law" of climate policy — that policies imposing significant near-term costs on energy users will be reversed regardless of climate benefits — is a related institutional argument.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that the next degree of warming (e.g., from 2°C to 3°C) produces damages so large or so unmanageable that adaptation is overwhelmed, or that the cost of mitigation has fallen so far that the marginal dollar is now better spent there. The adaptation-first case acknowledges that as mitigation costs fall, the balance shifts toward more mitigation — but argues this is happening too slowly to reverse the basic prioritization.

## 3. The innovation-led centrist case

The starting move is to argue the mitigation-vs.-adaptation framing is a false dichotomy, and that what actually drives emissions reductions in the wealthy world is *technological substitution* — fossil-fuel-burning processes being replaced by lower-emitting alternatives that work better and cost less.

The argument:

- **Empirical track record favors innovation-led.** The dramatic emissions reductions in the U.S. and U.K. since 2005 came primarily from coal-to-gas switching (driven by hydraulic fracturing economics, not climate policy) and renewables-and-storage cost declines (driven by R&D and deployment subsidies, not carbon pricing). Carbon pricing schemes that have been implemented (EU ETS, RGGI in the U.S. Northeast, BC's carbon tax) have produced measurable emissions reductions but smaller in magnitude than the technology-driven shifts. Acemoglu et al. (2016) develop the theoretical case for why directed technical change can produce faster decarbonization than demand-side policy alone.

- **R&D investment is dramatically underfunded.** Energy R&D as a share of GDP is far below biomedical R&D in most wealthy countries despite climate being a comparable-magnitude challenge. The Energy ARPA model (DOE's ARPA-E) and the IRA's clean-energy provisions show that targeted federal investment can shift technology trajectories. The innovation-led case argues that 5-10× larger R&D budgets would produce faster decarbonization than carbon pricing at politically realistic levels.

- **Carbon pricing is necessary but insufficient.** A revenue-neutral carbon dividend (Hansen, the Climate Leadership Council, the Baker-Shultz proposal) can correct prices and address distributional concerns simultaneously. But carbon pricing alone is unlikely to drive the rate of substitution needed; technology subsidies, performance standards, and infrastructure investment all matter. The innovation-led centrist supports carbon pricing as a complement, not the primary tool.

- **Industrial policy and deployment incentives accelerate substitution.** The IRA of 2022 — with $370B in clean-energy tax credits, manufacturing incentives, and infrastructure investment — is essentially an innovation-led model. It uses tax credits to scale solar, wind, batteries, EVs, and clean hydrogen rather than penalizing fossil-fuel use directly. Early evidence (manufacturing announcements, deployment rates) suggests this approach is producing faster build-out than carbon-pricing alternatives would have.

- **Adaptation matters but not as primary strategy.** The innovation-led case agrees that adaptation is necessary for already-locked-in warming but argues that the math doesn't work without aggressive emissions reductions at the same time. Smil's *How the World Really Works* details what physical-economic transitions actually require — it is fundamentally an innovation challenge, with adaptation as a backup.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that carbon pricing alone, at politically achievable levels, produces the rate of decarbonization needed; or that adaptation can be scaled fast enough to absorb several degrees of warming without innovation-driven mitigation. The innovation-led centrist takes both possibilities seriously and would shift toward whichever the empirical record supports.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled (or near-settled):**
- The core climate science (warming, human cause, continued warming with continued emissions).
- That mitigation costs have fallen dramatically since 2010 (renewables, batteries, EVs).
- That the 1.5°C target is now extremely difficult and 2°C is challenging but achievable.
- That climate damages are already affecting U.S. infrastructure, agriculture, and public health.

**Genuinely contested:**
- The right discount rate for valuing future damages (Stern's 1.4% vs. Nordhaus's 4.3% produces wildly different cost-benefit conclusions).
- Whether carbon pricing or technology subsidies more effectively drive emissions reductions.
- The political economy of energy transitions (whether mitigation policies are politically sustainable when they impose visible near-term costs).
- The role of climate-finance flows from wealthy to developing countries.

A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and acknowledges what's contested. Lesson 3 turns to the policy landscape — what the IRA actually did, where U.S. policy is heading, and what's at stake in the next major debate.
