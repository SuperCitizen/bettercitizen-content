Two sentences. Hold them side by side.

The first. In the 2024 election cycle, Elon Musk put more than 291 million dollars into electing the candidates he preferred. Most visibly, he did it through America PAC, the group he founded to back Donald Trump.

The second. Federal law states that no person may give a political action committee more than 5,000 dollars a year. That language has not changed since 1976.

[pause]

More than 291 million dollars. No more than 5,000 dollars a year.

Both sentences are true at the same time. And neither one involves anyone breaking the law.

[beat]

The distance between those two sentences is our subject. It took a century of statutes to build, then two landmark court decisions, then one retired admiral's lawsuit.

Other lectures cover the constitutional argument over Citizens United, and the dark-money groups that hide their donors. Here we stay with the machines themselves: what each kind of PAC may take in, what it may do with the money, and where the legal walls between them stand.

By the end, you should be able to tell four kinds of committee apart, and to explain how both of those sentences can be true.

We will take it in four steps.

First, where the PAC came from: a century of bans and workarounds.

Second, the super PAC, and the two court decisions that made it.

Third, two stranger inventions: the hybrid PAC, and the politician's second wallet.

And last, the scale, in verified numbers.

## A century of plugging holes

Start with something that surprises people. No statute invented the political action committee.

> The PAC began as a workaround.

Its history is a relay: a ban, then an invention to get around the ban, then another ban.

The relay starts in 1907. After the insurance-industry scandals of Theodore Roosevelt's second term, Congress passed the Tillman Act, and banned corporate contributions to federal campaigns. That was the first wall.

Labor unions stayed unregulated until 1943. That year, in wartime, the Smith-Connally Act barred contributions from union treasuries.

Within months, the Congress of Industrial Organizations, the CIO, had built something new: a separate fund. It was filled not from union dues, but from the voluntary, individual donations of members. And that fund could give to candidates. [beat] Why? Because it was not, legally, the union's money.

The CIO called it a Political Action Committee. It was organized in 1943, for Franklin Roosevelt's reelection in 1944, and it is the namesake and the prototype of the whole form.

Then, in 1947, the Taft-Hartley Act made the wartime ban permanent, and extended it from contributions to expenditures. That only entrenched the workaround. If the treasury was locked, the voluntary fund was the only door.

[pause]

A locked treasury, and a voluntary fund beside it. Hold on to that shape.

In the 1970s, Congress wrote the workaround into law. The Federal Election Campaign Act, FECA for short, came in 1971, and it was heavily amended in 1974, after Watergate. It authorized corporations and unions to pay the overhead of what it called "separate segregated funds." Those funds raise voluntary money from a restricted class, meaning employees, members or shareholders, and they spend it on politics.

In 1975, an FEC advisory opinion to Sun Oil confirmed that a corporation could administer such a fund with treasury money. And the population exploded. In 1974 there were roughly 600 registered PACs. A decade later there were more than 4,000.

[pause]

So for thirty-five years after FECA, the word PAC meant one thing.

> A traditional PAC: capped money in, capped money out, every dollar disclosed.

Let me put numbers on that. And listen for the order, because we will put the same four questions to every committee from here on. Money in. Money out. Who sees the donors. And whether it may work with the candidate.

Money in: a traditional PAC may take 5,000 dollars a year from any one donor.

[beat]

Money out: it may give a candidate up to 5,000 dollars per election.

[beat]

Who sees the donors: everyone. They are itemized in reports to the FEC.

[beat]

And may it work with the candidate? Yes. A contribution is the lawful, capped form of coordination.

One detail in those limits explains a great deal. An individual can also give directly to a candidate: 3,500 dollars per election, in the 2025 to 2026 cycle. That cap is indexed for inflation, so it rises. The 5,000 dollar PAC caps were fixed in the mid-1970s, and they are not indexed.

So inflation has quietly cut the traditional PAC's giving power, in real terms, by more than four-fifths. That is one reason the action moved elsewhere.

## What makes a super PAC super

That was the world for thirty-five years. Then 2010 rearranged it.

One piece of background first. In 1976, in a case called Buckley v. Valeo, the Supreme Court upheld caps on contributions, as measures against corruption, but struck down limits on independent spending. Giving to a candidate is one thing. Spending on your own is another. Every fight since 2010 builds on that distinction.

The super PAC was born in two steps, and the order matters.

Step one came in January 2010: Citizens United v. FEC. The Court held that independent expenditures do not corrupt, in the constitutional sense. An independent expenditure is spending "not coordinated with a candidate." So corporations and unions may make such expenditures, from their treasury funds.

Step two came two months later, from a different court, the D.C. Circuit, in a case called SpeechNow.org v. FEC. That court drew out a syllogism. If independent spending cannot corrupt, then contributions to a group that only spends independently cannot corrupt either. [beat] So the 5,000 dollar cap on giving to such a group is unconstitutional.

[pause]

Put it in plainer words. No corruption from the spending, so no corruption from the giving that pays for it. And so, no cap.

What resulted has a formal name: the independent-expenditure-only committee. It was promptly nicknamed [beat] the super PAC.

Now put the four questions to it.

Money in: unlimited. From individuals, from corporations, from unions.

[beat]

Money out: independent expenditures only. Not a dollar to a candidate, and not a dollar to a party.

[beat]

Who sees the donors: everyone. We will come back to that.

[beat]

And may it work with the candidate? [beat] No.

> Independence is the legal price of unlimited money.

The super PAC is a creature of one premise: that independent spending is independent in fact. Hold on to that premise. The third lecture in this series is one long fight about it.

Picture the result as two doors into a federal race.

Through the first door, money goes to a traditional PAC, and from there to a candidate's campaign. That is the contribution route, and it is capped.

Through the second door, money goes to a super PAC, and from there straight to the voters, as advertising. That is the independent route, and it has no cap.

Between the two doors stands the coordination wall. Cross it, and the so-called independent money becomes a contribution, over the cap, and illegal.

[pause]

Why does a cap survive on one route and not on the other? Because contributions are seen as a corruption risk, and SpeechNow treated truly independent spending as non-corrupting.

Both routes are disclosed. And that is the fact most often mangled in this field: super PACs itemize their donors to the FEC.

The SpeechNow plaintiffs had challenged the registration and reporting requirements too. On that point they lost. [beat] The same court that struck the cap upheld the reporting.

What a super PAC cannot tell you is who stands behind a donor, when that donor is itself a nonprofit that does not disclose, or a shell LLC. In 2024, super PACs and similar committees reported receiving over 1 billion dollars from sources like those.

> Super PACs disclose their donors. They cannot say who stands behind a donor.

That dead-end pattern is called gray money. It belongs, along with the nonprofits whose donors are not public, to the lectures on dark money.

## The hybrid, and the second wallet

Two more inventions complete the set.

The first belongs to a retired rear admiral named James Carey. In 2011, he wanted his committee, the National Defense PAC, to do two things at once. It would give capped, disclosed contributions to candidates who were veterans. And it would accept unlimited money for independent ads.

The FEC said a committee must choose. Carey went to court. Judge Rosemary Collyer granted a preliminary injunction, and that August the agency settled.

The terms were these. One committee may run both programs, if it keeps two segregated bank accounts, one for each rulebook.

> A hybrid PAC: one committee, two segregated bank accounts.

Put the four questions to a hybrid, and the answer each time is: it depends which account. One side is capped, and may work with candidates. The other side is unlimited, and may not.

The hybrid is also called a Carey committee, and it is now standard equipment. Future Forward USA PAC, the principal outside group backing Harris in 2024, ran as one, and spent about 517 million dollars.

[pause]

Let's test the field guide so far. This one takes a little working out.

A tech billionaire wants to write two checks. The first is 2 million dollars, to a group running ads for Candidate X. The second is 5,000 dollars, directly to Candidate X's campaign committee.

Which kinds of committee can take the 2 million? And can one organization handle both checks? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

Take the 2 million first. A traditional PAC cannot accept it, because it is capped at 5,000 dollars a year from each donor. The money can go to a super PAC, to the unlimited account of a hybrid PAC, or to a nonprofit that does not disclose.

The direct check cannot legally pass through any of those. Contributions to candidates must come from capped, disclosed sources. And there is a catch in the amount: the donor's own limit for direct giving is 3,500 dollars per election.

So can one organization take both checks? [beat] A hybrid PAC can. The 2 million goes into its independent-expenditure account, and a capped contribution goes into its contribution account. That is what the Carey case allows.

[pause]

The second invention is older, and stranger. It is the leadership PAC: a committee sponsored by an officeholder, and kept separate from that officeholder's own campaign.

Nearly every member of Congress has one. Mitch McConnell's Bluegrass Committee and Nancy Pelosi's PAC to the Future are two long-running examples.

Its official purpose is to build a power base, by donating to the campaigns of colleagues. That is why party leaders maintain them.

On the four questions, it looks like a traditional PAC: 5,000 dollars a year in, 5,000 dollars per election out to other candidates, and public donors.

The notorious feature is how the rest of the money may be spent. Federal law bans the conversion of campaign funds to personal use. But the FEC has interpreted that ban to reach only a candidate's own campaign committee. [beat] Not the leadership PAC. Scholars call that the personal-use gap.

Watchdogs have documented leadership PAC money flowing to resorts, to club dues, and to travel. In 2018, two groups, Campaign Legal Center and Issue One, petitioned the FEC to extend the personal-use rules. The agency took public comment. And as of mid-2026, [beat] it has never adopted a rule.

> Leadership PAC money is disclosed, capped, and almost unregulated in how it is spent.

Here is a scene. A senator's leadership PAC pays for a week at a golf resort, where the senator "met donors." If her campaign committee paid the same bill, it would plainly violate the personal-use ban.

Why is the leadership PAC version probably legal? And what would have to change to end the gap? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is probably legal because the personal-use ban applies to funds in a candidate's authorized campaign committee. A leadership PAC is, legally, a separate committee, and the FEC has not extended the ban to it by rule.

So the same expense flips from a violation to a gray zone, depending on which of the politician's two wallets pays.

[beat]

And the repair? Either a rulemaking by the FEC, the one petitioned for in 2018 and never adopted, or a statute. The fix is administrative, not constitutional. Nobody disputes that Congress could reach it.

## The scale, in verified numbers

So how big did the architecture built after 2010 become?

One definition first. Outside spending means spending by super PACs, plus other groups that are neither candidates nor parties.

> In the 2024 federal elections, outside spending reached a record: about 4.5 billion dollars.

The defining feature is concentration. Musk, at more than 291 million dollars, was the largest donor of the cycle. A handful of other individuals each crossed 100 million.

[pause]

The traditional PAC did not shrink. Corporate, union and trade-association PACs still route hundreds of millions of dollars in capped contributions. What collapsed was their relative weight.

An early assessment has aged well. It came from Richard Briffault, in a law review, in 2012. The independent-expenditure committee moved from the margins of campaign finance to its center, within two cycles of its invention. And it has stayed there.

One caution before we gather this up. It comes from two scholars, Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner, in a book of 2015. The money, they point out, did not appear from nowhere. Squeezing the regulated channels, the parties above all, redirected funds into outside groups that are less accountable. And outside groups tend to fund more ideologically extreme messaging than the pragmatic, win-seeking parties do.

That is their caution, and it comes down to one line. Whatever you conclude about super PACs, conclude it about the system: every cap reroutes, and nothing evaporates.

[pause]

Now go back to the very beginning. I gave you two sentences that seemed to collide. One donor, more than 291 million dollars. And a law that says no more than 5,000 dollars a year.

How can both be true? See whether you can say it.

[long pause]

The 5,000 dollar cap is real. It governs the first door: giving to a traditional PAC, the kind of committee that can hand money to a candidate.

Money on the scale of 291 million dollars travels through the second door. A committee that only spends independently may take any amount. The price is the wall: no working with the candidate.

Two doors. One capped, one not. Both lawful.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The PAC began as a workaround: a voluntary fund beside a locked treasury. The Tillman Act banned corporate contributions in 1907, the CIO built the first PAC in 1943, and FECA wrote the workaround into law in the 1970s.

[beat]

Two. A traditional PAC: capped money in, capped money out, every dollar disclosed. That is 5,000 dollars a year from a donor, and 5,000 dollars per election to a candidate, directly. Those caps are not indexed for inflation.

[beat]

Three. A super PAC: unlimited money in, from any source, for independent expenditures only. Nothing to candidates or parties, and no coordination. Independence is the legal price of unlimited money. It was born in 2010, in two steps: Citizens United, then SpeechNow. And super PACs disclose their donors. They cannot say who stands behind a donor.

[beat]

Four. Two more vehicles. The hybrid PAC, from the Carey case: one committee, two segregated bank accounts, a capped side and an uncapped side. And the leadership PAC: sponsored by an officeholder, giving to other candidates' campaigns, with a personal-use ban that has never been extended to it.

[beat]

And five. The scale. In the 2024 federal elections, outside spending reached a record: about 4.5 billion dollars. And it was concentrated.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not a verdict. You now own the machinery. What the machinery amounts to is exactly what three perspectives dispute.

The PAC-defender holds that this architecture is free speech, and constitutionally required.

[beat]

The PAC-restrictionist holds that it is a plutocratic capture.

[beat]

The coordination-rules reformer holds that it is an enforcement failure, and a fixable one.

[pause]

The next lecture builds the strongest case for each of the three. The one after that goes down into the coordination rules, where the regulatory fight is being fought now.

For now, go back to the two sentences. More than 291 million dollars. No more than 5,000 dollars a year. [beat] You know now how both can be true. And you know every piece of law that stands between them.
