January 2012. Newt Gingrich is running for president, and his campaign is broke. It is presumed dead.

Then a casino magnate named Sheldon Adelson writes a check. 5 million dollars, to Winning Our Future, the super PAC supporting Gingrich.

A candidacy that could not afford ads suddenly carpet-bombs South Carolina. Gingrich wins it. Two days after that victory, as the race moves to Florida, Adelson's wife Miriam writes another check, for the same amount.

In the end, the family's support reached about 21 million dollars. At the time, that was more than any super PAC donor had ever given.

[pause]

Two people had kept a major presidential candidacy alive. And they had done it entirely lawfully.

There are two ways to hear that story.

Heard one way, it is the First Amendment working. Citizens with intense preferences funded speech that voters were free to reject. And by Florida, they did.

Heard another way, it is an audition system, in which candidates survive by acquiring a billionaire.

Serious people make both of those readings, in good faith. And there is a third camp, which locates the problem somewhere else again. We will come to it.

Your job, by the end of this lecture, is to be able to make each case at full strength. Not a weak version. The version its smartest advocate would argue.

Last time, the machinery. This time, the argument. Four stops.

First, the PAC-defender. Second, the PAC-restrictionist. Third, the coordination-rules reformer. And last, what is settled, and what is contested.

Each camp gets the same treatment: what it protects, how it argues, what it points to, what it would do, and what would change its mind.

## Ground nobody owns

Before any camp speaks, notice how much is not in dispute. Four things are settled.

The scale of outside money since 2010, and how concentrated it is. The chain of doctrine that produced it. The fact that super PACs disclose their donors, while the nonprofit channel does not. And the fact that enforcement of the coordination rules has been minimal.

The scale. The doctrine. The disclosure. The thin enforcement. Keep that list of four. I will ask for it back.

> The camps disagree less about facts than about which fact is the problem.

One item on that list needs a moment more: the chain of doctrine. It has three links.

Buckley v. Valeo, in 1976, upheld limits on contributions, and struck limits on spending. It upheld caps on contributions to candidates because of the risk of quid pro quo corruption: money for a specific official act.

[beat]

Citizens United then declared that independent expenditures do not corrupt.

[beat]

And SpeechNow drew the conclusion. If a group makes only independent expenditures, then contributions to that group present no corruption interest that the law recognizes.

Buckley's distinction. Citizens United's premise. SpeechNow's syllogism. Three links. We will need them.

## The PAC-defender

Start with the PAC-defender, the free-speech maximalist. And start where the doctrine starts, with Buckley.

What this camp protects is political speech, and the freedom of citizens to pool their resources in order to be heard.

Its creed is a single line from Buckley. Listen for two words near the end: wholly foreign.

"The concept that government may restrict the speech of some elements of our society in order to enhance the relative voice of others [beat] is wholly foreign to the First Amendment."

[pause]

Wholly foreign to the First Amendment.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Effective political speech costs money: airtime, staff, postage, server time. So a cap on political spending is a cap on political speech, however it is dressed.

> The PAC-defender: a cap on political spending is a cap on political speech.

A super PAC, on this view, is nothing exotic. It is citizens pooling resources to say something together. That is the same act of association as a union local, or the NAACP, buying an ad. Cap the contributions to it, and you cap the pooled speech of every donor in it.

Three further moves give the position its strength.

First, the hydraulics argument. Money in politics behaves like water. Block a channel, and the water reroutes. It does not dry up.

The defender points to the history. The limits of 1974 produced PACs. The soft-money ban of 2002 produced groups known as 527s. Squeezing the 527s produced a migration into 501(c)(4) nonprofits.

And the super PAC, the defender argues, is the most transparent uncapped vehicle the system has ever produced. Itemized donors. Registered committees. Reported spending. Restrict it, and the same money flows somewhere darker.

[pause]

The defender also points to data from the scholars La Raja and Schaffner, published in 2015. Starving the regulated, pragmatic actors, especially the parties, has empowered outside money that is ideologically purist. On this account, regulation helped polarize the system it meant to clean.

Second, aggregation is democratization. Bradley Smith, who served as an FEC commissioner, presses this point in a book called Unfree Speech. PACs are how ordinary citizens matter. A million small donors to EMILY's List can rival one Adelson, precisely because the law lets them aggregate. And rules written in fear of billionaires also bind the bundled 50 dollar donors.

Third, incumbents write the rules. Campaign-finance regulation is drafted by sitting officeholders. Challengers must buy the name recognition that incumbents get free, and challengers are the ones outside money most often funds. It was no accident, the defender notes, who brought the SpeechNow case: a group of citizens organized to run ads against incumbents they deemed hostile to free speech.

[pause]

So the remedy is brief. Leave it alone. [beat] And deregulate the parties and the candidates too.

What would change the defender's mind? Rigorous evidence that disclosed, independent spending produces the policy distortions the restrictionists allege. And evidence that transparency and electoral competition demonstrably fail to discipline them.

The defender's bet is that transparency plus electoral competition will discipline whatever distortions disclosed, independent money produces. And fifteen years of FEC files, the defender adds, have not shown the quid pro quo.

## The PAC-restrictionist

Now the PAC-restrictionist, built the same way.

What this camp protects is representation: a government that depends on the people it represents.

Its opening move is to relocate the corruption question. Bribery, that exchange of money for a specific official act, was never the main worry, this camp says. Dependence is.

Lawrence Lessig frames it in a book of 2011, called Republic, Lost. The Framers designed a Congress "dependent on the people alone." Build a system in which a viable candidacy requires the patronage of the largest funders, and you have substituted a second dependence, an unrepresentative one.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. No envelope changes hands. [beat] The candidate knows, as everyone in the building knows, who funded the 50 million dollars in ads that saved them. And who could fund the ads that end them. Gratitude and fear do the work that bribery used to do, at scale, and legally.

> The PAC-restrictionist: the harm is dependence, not bribery.

Three supports give the position its strength.

First, the concentration is not hypothetical. The restrictionist points to the verified record. One person above 291 million dollars in 2024. A handful of others above 100 million each. Two people sustaining a presidential candidacy in 2012.

[beat]

Whatever this is, the restrictionist argues, calling it citizens pooling resources misdescribes it. The typical super PAC dollar is not small money, aggregated. It is a transfer from a single enormous fortune.

Second, representation skews measurably. The restrictionist points to a study from 2014, by Gilens and Page, which tested nearly 1,800 policy questions. Its conclusion comes in two halves. Economic elites, and organized groups representing business interests, "have substantial independent impacts on U.S. government policy." [beat] Average citizens and mass-based interest groups "have little or no independent influence."

[pause]

That study predates the super PAC question, and reaches beyond it. Its methods have been seriously contested, and the honest restrictionist says so. But it anchors the claim that the preferences of the donor class and the direction of policy travel together.

Third, the obstacle is the doctrine, not the Constitution. Richard Hasen makes the argument in a 2016 book called Plutocrats United. The Court, he argues, took a wrong turn when it treated quid pro quo corruption as the only interest that can justify limits. He proposes political equality as the constitutional value doing the real work. Nicholas Stephanopoulos supplies another frame: alignment, between what voters want and what government does. Concentrated money, on this account, degrades that alignment.

So the remedies follow. A different Court. A constitutional amendment. Or public financing, which changes what candidates depend on, and caps no one's speech.

What would change the restrictionist's mind? Replicated evidence that policy outcomes under the super PAC regime do not differ from plausible counterfactuals. Evidence, in other words, that the money buys noise, [beat] not government.

The restrictionist's bet starts from that third point: the obstacle is doctrine, not the Constitution. Recognize dependence corruption, or political equality, as interests the law may act on, or change candidates' dependence through public financing, and representation measurably improves.

[pause]

Now put the first two camps in a room. A reform bill caps contributions to super PACs at 25,000 dollars. The defender says the cap is unconstitutional. A restrictionist answers that Buckley itself upheld contribution caps.

Under current doctrine, who has the better of it, and why? You have the three links of the chain. Use them.

[long pause]

Under current law, the defender. Buckley upheld caps on contributions to candidates, because of the risk of quid pro quo. But once Citizens United declared independent expenditures non-corrupting, SpeechNow held that contributions to groups making only those expenditures present no corruption interest. So the cap fails.

[pause]

Notice what that answer covers. It says where the doctrine stands today. The restrictionist's real target is the premise underneath it: that independence prevents corruption. That is why serious restrictionists argue for overruling, or for an amendment. They do not pretend that current doctrine permits the cap.

## The coordination-rules reformer

Third, the coordination-rules reformer. Same treatment.

This camp begins from a different place. The internal logic of the doctrine, it says, is sound.

What it protects is the line that Buckley drew, between a contribution and independent spending. It wants that line policed honestly.

So go back to Buckley once more. The Court explained exactly why independent spending got more protection than contributions. Listen for two things that independence is said to do.

The absence of prearrangement and coordination, the Court wrote, "undermines the value of the expenditure to the candidate." [beat] And it "alleviates the danger that expenditures will be given as a quid pro quo for improper commitments from the candidate."

[pause]

Two things, then. Independence makes the spending less valuable to the candidate. And it makes the spending less dangerous.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. That sentence from Buckley contains a premise, and a premise can be tested. The reformer argues that this one has been falsified. Not by the Constitution. [beat] By regulation.

> The coordination-rules reformer: the doctrine is sound. Regulation has falsified its premise.

Put another way: the doctrine is fine, if independence is real. And enforcement, the reformer argues, has made it fictional.

The reformer points to the FEC's conduct standards, the rules that say what counts as coordination. They were drafted so narrowly, the reformer argues, that three things can all happen within the law. A candidate's former campaign manager can run the super PAC that supports the candidate. The campaign can post strategy and footage in public, for the super PAC to use. And the candidate can headline the super PAC's fundraisers. [beat] All without coordination, in the legal sense, ever occurring.

So the program is unglamorous, and concrete. Lengthen the cooling-off windows, which now run 120 days. Treat a super PAC that serves a single candidate, and is staffed by campaign alumni, as presumptively coordinated. Shut the public-posting workarounds. And restructure the enforcer, so that three-to-three deadlocks stop functioning as acquittals.

None of that requires amending the Constitution. All of it operates on the seam that Buckley itself drew. The blueprint is an article of 2013 by Richard Briffault, "Coordination Reconsidered." Its argument: the line between contribution and expenditure can hold only if the definition of coordination polices it honestly.

What would change the reformer's mind? Evidence that tighter conduct rules cannot be drafted in practice. Evidence that every workable definition fails in one of two ways. Either it chills groups that are independent in fact, which is the defender's fear. Or it leaves workarounds so large that the exercise is pointless, which is the restrictionist's prediction.

The reformer's bet is that tighter conduct rules can be drafted. Rules that make independence real, without chilling speech that is independent in fact. No amendment required.

## Three camps, side by side

Now hold the three side by side. They disagree about which fact is the problem. Let's see whether you can hear the difference.

Three people are arguing about 100 million dollars of super PAC spending. Each says one thing. Decide which camp is speaking.

The first: "The problem isn't the 100 million; it's that the candidate's college roommate is spending it."

[beat]

The second: "The problem is the 100 million."

[beat]

The third: "The problem is thinking 100 million of disclosed ads is a bigger threat than the same money moving somewhere you can't see it."

Three speakers, three camps. Which is which?

[long pause]

The first is the coordination-rules reformer. The objection is not to the amount. It is to an independence that is fictional.

[beat]

The second is the restrictionist. Concentration itself corrupts, through dependence, however independent the spender.

[beat]

The third is the defender, making the hydraulics argument. Restriction reroutes money to less transparent channels, so the disclosed version is the least bad equilibrium.

Being able to sort those three quickly is most of what knowing the debate means.

[pause]

Now the harder exercise. Of the three camps, choose the one you find least sympathetic. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What is settled, what is contested

Near the start, I gave you four things that are settled, and said I would ask for them back. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The scale and concentration of outside money since 2010. The chain of doctrine: Buckley's distinction, Citizens United's premise, SpeechNow's syllogism. That super PACs disclose their donors, while the nonprofit channel does not. And that enforcement of the coordination rules has been minimal.

Now the other list. Four things are contested.

One: whether independent spending changes policy outcomes, or chases them. The Gilens and Page finding and its critics mark that front.

[beat]

Two: whether dependence corruption is an interest the Constitution recognizes, or a moral framework that the First Amendment does not recognize.

[beat]

Three: whether the hydraulics are destiny. Whether any restriction merely reroutes the money, or whether well-designed rules can shrink the total.

[beat]

And four, the counterfactual that haunts every camp. What would American politics look like if the Adelson checks of January 2012 had been illegal? Better, worse, or merely different?

Those are contested questions, not settled ones.

> The scale of the money is settled. Its effect on policy is contested.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The PAC-defender: a cap on political spending is a cap on political speech. Money reroutes like water, and the disclosed super PAC is the most transparent uncapped vehicle yet built. The bet: transparency plus electoral competition will discipline the rest.

[beat]

Two. The PAC-restrictionist: the harm is dependence, not bribery. Gratitude and fear do the work of bribery, legally and at scale. The bet: recognize dependence or equality as interests the law may act on, or change what candidates depend on, and representation measurably improves.

[beat]

Three. The coordination-rules reformer: the doctrine is sound. Regulation has falsified its premise. The bet: tighter conduct rules can make independence real, and no amendment is required.

[beat]

And four. The camps disagree less about facts than about which fact is the problem. The scale of the money is settled. Its effect on policy is contested.

[pause]

This lecture has not settled which camp is right. It was built so that you could argue any of them honestly.

For now, go back to January 2012, and to two checks that kept a candidacy alive. [beat] You can hear that story three ways now. And you can make each of them at full strength.
