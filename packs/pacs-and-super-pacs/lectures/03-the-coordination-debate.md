March 20, 2024. The Federal Election Commission answers a question from a political committee in Texas.

The committee is called Texas Majority PAC. It is registered with the state, and it works to elect Texas Democrats. Its question is about knocking on doors.

Could it pay for door-to-door canvassing, the scripts, the literature, the organizers, and do it in consultation with candidates and parties? Could it share the kind of strategy that would be flagrantly illegal to share about a TV ad?

The Commission's answer: [beat] yes.

The reasoning was brief. Canvassing is not a "public communication" under the rules. So the restrictions on coordination never attach.

By that autumn, Donald Trump's campaign had handed much of its swing-state door-knocking to America PAC, Elon Musk's super PAC. NBC News chronicled it from the inside, as nervous Republican operatives watched a presidential ground game run by an outside group.

[pause]

Nothing about it was illegal.

[beat]

Does that sentence describe a sensible accommodation, or the final collapse of a constitutional premise? That is the question of this lecture. And it turns on a single word, coordination, because that one word holds up the entire system built since 2010.

By the end, you should be able to walk the legal test for coordination, name the lawful ways around it, and say why it is so rarely enforced.

Last time, three camps and their arguments. This time, the ground they fight on. Four steps.

First, why one word carries the whole structure.

Second, the test itself: three prongs.

Third, the ways around the wall, every one of them lawful.

And last, why enforcement almost never happens, and what the repairs on offer would change.

## One word

Go back to the picture from the first lecture. Two doors into a race. One is the door of capped contributions. The other is the door of uncapped independence. [beat] Between them stands the coordination wall.

That wall is built from one sentence of federal law. An expenditure made "in cooperation, consultation, or concert, with, or at the request or suggestion of" a candidate is treated as a contribution to that candidate.

[pause]

In plainer words: work with the candidate, and your spending changes its legal character. It stops being your own spending. It becomes a contribution.

Then the limits apply. Take an independent ad campaign of 40 million dollars. Cross the line, and it becomes an in-kind contribution of 40 million dollars. That is roughly 11,000 times the legal limit. [beat] And if any corporate or union money funded it, it is flatly illegal.

> Coordinated spending is a contribution. Independent spending is not.

Every uncapped dollar in American elections flows on the premise that this line is real. Buckley rested its distinction between expenditures and contributions on it. Citizens United assumed it, saying that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." And SpeechNow built the super PAC on it.

[pause]

So the working question is not philosophical. It is administrative. How does the FEC decide what counts?

The answer sits in a single regulation. And its architecture explains most of what you see in the wild.

## Three prongs

A communication is coordinated only if it meets three tests. The rule calls them prongs. Payment. [beat] Content. [beat] Conduct.

And it must meet all three. Miss any one, and the communication is independent, whatever else was going on.

> The coordination test has three prongs: payment, content, conduct. All three, or nothing.

Walk them in order.

Payment is trivial. Someone other than the campaign paid. That prong is almost always met.

[beat]

Content asks whether the communication is electoral enough to matter. There are several ways to qualify. One is any public communication that merely names a candidate within 90 days of a House or Senate election. For presidential races, the window is 120 days.

[beat]

Conduct is where the action is. The rule lists the ways a spender and a campaign can be connected. The campaign requested or suggested the ad. Or it was materially involved in decisions about the ad's content, audience, timing or placement. Or there were substantial discussions that conveyed the campaign's plans. Or the spender used a common vendor, or hired a former campaign employee, who used material knowledge of the campaign. Those last two count only within a window of 120 days.

[pause]

Then come the exits. There are two.

First, information that the campaign has made publicly available does not count.

Second, a shared vendor with a written firewall policy defeats the prong, unless evidence shows that information crossed the firewall.

[pause]

So the test is a logical "and": all three prongs, or the communication is independent. And those two exits are why the third prong almost never closes.

Let's test it. This one takes some working out.

A super PAC hires the same media firm that a Senate campaign used six months ago. It runs an ad praising the senator, 60 days before the general election. And the script comes entirely from the campaign's public speeches.

Which of the three prongs are met? Work it through.

[pause 5s]

Payment: met. The super PAC paid, not the campaign.

[beat]

Content: met. This is a public communication that names a candidate within 90 days.

[beat]

Conduct: [beat] not met. The common-vendor standard reaches back only 120 days, and six months is outside the window. And the script came from publicly available material, which the conduct standards expressly excuse.

Two prongs out of three. So the ad is legally independent.

[pause]

Now change the facts. Make the gap 90 days, with no firewall, and have the firm reuse the campaign's private targeting data. All three prongs are met. The ad becomes an in-kind contribution.

## The ways around

What comes next is not a list of scandals. Every item on it is lawful, under the rules you have just heard.

Hold two readings of that fact as we go. The reformer argues that it is precisely the point: no prong is crazy, but together they make the wall easy to walk around. The defender argues that each item reflects a deliberate choice, made to protect speech.

Four ways around. Redboxing. The personnel pipeline. The pre-candidacy buildout. And the canvassing carve-out.

First, redboxing. A campaign posts a block of text on its public website, often literally outlined in red. It is addressed to no one in particular. It says that "voters need to know" what the opponent did. It names "the message that works" with suburban women. It says the ideal channel is digital video, in these counties.

A super PAC reads the public page, [beat] and executes.

See whether you can say why that is legal. A campaign posts this on its public website: "Voters need to know Senator Y voted to cut Medicare. Digital video moves suburban women in these five counties." A super PAC reads the page and runs exactly that ad.

Which prong is not met, and why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The conduct prong. Publicly available information cannot satisfy it, however precisely the information was aimed. Posting strategy to the world is not a "substantial discussion" with anyone.

Reading the public page is exactly what the rules permit.

One study, in the Election Law Journal in 2024, documented redboxing on the websites of more than 200 federal candidates in the 2022 cycle, and found it thoroughly bipartisan. One of its authors directs campaign-finance reform at Campaign Legal Center, which has campaigned to ban the practice by name.

Campaigns post footage the same way. A separate rule covers republication: paying to disseminate a campaign's own materials is an in-kind contribution from the spender. But the candidate generally is not charged with receiving anything, unless the conduct standards are also met. So campaigns post broadcast-quality footage of the candidate, and outside groups download it and cut it into their ads.

[pause]

Second, the personnel pipeline. The cooling-off window is 120 days. So a campaign manager can leave in March, and be running the supporting super PAC by August, carrying relationships and judgment along. The rule polices information that is conveyed. It does not police accumulated wisdom.

A super PAC devoted to a single candidate, and staffed by that candidate's former aides, is a structure the authors of Buckley never imagined. It is now the default architecture of presidential politics.

[pause]

Third, the pre-candidacy buildout. The rules restrict coordination with candidates. So consider someone who has not yet declared.

Jeb Bush spent the first half of 2015 personally headlining fundraisers for a group called Right to Rise USA, which he and his aides had created. By June 30 it had banked 103 million dollars, a record at the time. And he declined to declare his candidacy until the middle of June.

Once he declared, the wall went up. By then the blueprint, the donor network and the war chest were built.

Declared candidates, for their part, may still appear and speak at their super PAC's fundraisers. FEC guidance permits it, so long as they personally solicit no more than the federal limits allow.

[pause]

Fourth, the newest, and by scale the biggest: the canvassing carve-out. This is where we began, with Texas Majority PAC.

Here is the Commission's reasoning. Canvassing scripts and literature are not public communications, because door-to-door contact involves no paid placement in mass media. And the entire coordination rule applies only to public communications. So the content prong can never be met.

The consequence: a candidate may sit down with a super PAC and plan the ground game. The turf. The scripts. The targets. [beat] All with money that no contribution limit touches.

One wall is left standing. If the canvassing data flows back to the campaign for less than its usual and normal charge, that is an in-kind contribution.

Campaign Legal Center has sued the FEC over the opinion. Meanwhile, 2024 demonstrated the model at presidential scale, and operatives worried in public about the quality control that came with renting a ground game.

[pause]

One correction before we go on, because a common belief has this mostly backwards. The belief runs: "Super PACs and campaigns coordinate illegally all the time."

> Most visible cooperation between campaigns and super PACs breaks no rule.

The rules were drafted, prong by prong and safe harbor by safe harbor, so that this cooperation is legal. That is why the reform argument is about rewriting rules. It is not, mainly, about catching cheaters.

## Why enforcement almost never happens

Now suppose conduct does cross the line, or arguably does. Even then, the enforcement machine stalls.

Look at how the FEC is built. Six commissioners. No more than three from one party. Four votes required for action.

That structure produces three-to-three deadlocks on politically sensitive matters. A deadlock functions as a dismissal. And a dismissed complaint rarely sees the inside of a courtroom.

One commissioner, Ann Ravel, put numbers on it in a report of 2017. By 2016, deadlocks on substantive enforcement votes were near 30 percent. [beat] A decade earlier, the figure had been about 3 percent.

There is a criminal route. But it requires proof of a violation that was "knowing and willful." The first such prosecution in American history concluded only in 2015. An operative named Tyler Harber pleaded guilty to directing 325,000 dollars in coordinated super PAC spending, for a 2012 congressional campaign that he managed at the same time. He drew 24 months in prison. Such cases remain rare.

> The coordination wall is patrolled by an agency built to deadlock.

And behind that agency stands a criminal standard built for the flagrant.

## The repairs on offer

The reform proposals map directly onto the holes. Four holes, four repairs.

For the personnel pipeline: lengthen the 120-day windows, to a year or more.

[beat]

For the super PAC that serves one candidate: a presumption. If a super PAC is founded or staffed by a candidate's recent aides, or funded predominantly to support one candidate, treat it as presumptively coordinated.

[beat]

For redboxing: shut the door of publicly available information, for material that a campaign itself posts. A proposed bill, the Stop Illegal Campaign Coordination Act, targets redboxing by name.

[beat]

And for the deadlocks: overhaul the enforcement machine. The Freedom to Vote Act carried coordination provisions, and a revision of the FEC's enforcement process. It never overcame a Senate filibuster. The earlier For the People Act would have shrunk the commission to five members, so that deadlocks become arithmetically impossible.

[pause]

Each proposal trades against the defender's standing objection. Every tightened standard sweeps in some speech that is independent in fact. And the First Amendment casts its shadow over the borderline cases.

Let's weigh that trade. First, pull the test back from memory. It had three prongs. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Payment. Content. Conduct.

Now two bills. Bill A extends the window for former employees, from 120 days to 2 years. Bill B makes any strategy memo that a campaign posts publicly count as conduct, if an outside group demonstrably uses it.

Which prong does each bill rewrite? And which bill faces the harder First Amendment objection? Think it through.

[long pause]

Both rewrite the conduct prong. Bill A widens a connection standard. Bill B shrinks a safe harbor.

And Bill B faces the harder objection. It penalizes outside groups for using truthful, public information. That sits near the core of the First Amendment, and it is hard to square with the principle that anyone may act on public facts.

Bill A regulates an employment relationship. Courts have historically given revolving-door rules more room.

That asymmetry shapes the drafting. Most bills, including the redboxing legislation that Campaign Legal Center endorses, target the campaign's act of signaling. They leave alone the group's act of reading.

## Three readings of one wall

So where does that leave the premise we have been testing: that independent spending is independent in fact? Three readings remain open. They belong to the three camps from last time. Each gets one line here.

The PAC-defender argues that every one of these workarounds reflects a choice to protect speech, and that every tighter rule would sweep in speech that is independent in fact.

> The PAC-defender: the erosion of independence is what robust political speech looks like.

The PAC-restrictionist argues that the independence premise was never true, and that it only waited for money on this scale to show it.

> The PAC-restrictionist: independence was always a legal fiction, awaiting this much money.

The coordination-rules reformer argues that the fault lies in the drafting, and that rewritten rules, like the repairs you just heard, would make the premise hold.

> The coordination-rules reformer: better drafting can make the independence premise true.

Now choose the reading you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its strongest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Coordinated spending is a contribution. Independent spending is not. Every uncapped dollar in American elections flows on the premise that the line is real.

[beat]

Two. The coordination test has three prongs: payment, content, conduct. All three, or nothing. And two exits explain why the third almost never closes: publicly available information, and the firewall.

[beat]

Three. The ways around are lawful. Redboxing, and public footage. The 120-day personnel window. The super PAC built before a candidacy is declared, and the candidate's appearances at its fundraisers. And since 2024, the canvassing carve-out: door-to-door canvassing is not a public communication, so a candidate and a super PAC may plan it together.

[beat]

Four. Enforcement almost never happens. The coordination wall is patrolled by an agency built to deadlock, and backed by a criminal standard built for the flagrant.

[beat]

And five. The repairs map onto the holes: longer windows, a presumption of coordination, a shut door for what campaigns post, an overhauled enforcer. And each one trades against speech that is independent in fact.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not: a resolution of the premise it tested. All three readings of Buckley's independence premise remain open.

This is the last lecture of the series, so here is where the argument goes next: to you. A defensible position on super PAC policy requires three things. A view on the empirical question: is the system built since 2010 producing dependence corruption? [beat] A view on the remedial question: which reforms, if any, fit within current doctrine? [beat] And an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.
