# How PACs and Super PACs actually work

In the 2024 election cycle, Elon Musk put more than $291 million into electing his preferred candidates — most visibly through America PAC, the group he founded to back Donald Trump. Federal law states, in language that has not changed since 1974, that no person may give a political action committee more than $5,000 a year. Both of those sentences are true at the same time, and neither involves anyone breaking the law. The distance between them is the subject of this lesson: a taxonomy of committee types that took a century of statutes, two landmark court decisions in eighteen months, and one retired admiral's lawsuit to build. The [[term:citizens-united]] pack covers the constitutional argument; the Dark Money pack covers the groups that hide their donors. This pack is about the machines themselves — what each kind of [[term:pac]] may take in, what it may do with the money, and where the legal walls between them actually stand.

## A century of plugging holes

The political action committee is not a creature of any statute. It is a workaround — and the history is a relay of bans and inventions.

Congress banned corporate contributions to federal campaigns in the Tillman Act of 1907, after the insurance-industry scandals of Theodore Roosevelt's second term. Labor unions stayed unregulated until the Smith–Connally Act of 1943 barred union treasury contributions in wartime. The Congress of Industrial Organizations responded within months by building something genuinely new: a separate fund, filled not from union dues but from members' voluntary individual donations, which could give to candidates because it was not, legally, the union's money. The CIO's Political Action Committee — CIO-PAC, organized in 1943 for Franklin Roosevelt's 1944 reelection — is the form's namesake and prototype. The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947 made the wartime ban permanent and extended it from contributions to expenditures, which only entrenched the workaround: if the treasury was locked, the voluntary fund was the only door.

The Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA, 1971, heavily amended in 1974 after Watergate) wrote the workaround into law. It authorized corporations and unions to pay the overhead of "separate segregated funds" — connected PACs — that raise voluntary money from a restricted class (employees, members, shareholders) and spend it on politics. A 1975 FEC advisory opinion to Sun Oil (the famous *SUN-PAC* opinion) confirmed that corporations could administer such funds with treasury money, and the population exploded: from roughly 600 registered PACs in 1974 to more than 4,000 a decade later. For thirty-five years after FECA, "PAC" meant this: capped money in, capped money out, every dollar disclosed.

Then 2010 rearranged the landscape in eighteen months — which is where the taxonomy comes in.

## The taxonomy: money in, money out, who sees it

Modern campaign-finance argument is mostly people talking past each other about *different vehicles*. The table below is the whole field guide; the rest of the lesson walks through it.

| Vehicle | Money in (per donor) | Money out | Donors public? | May it coordinate with candidates? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional PAC (connected or nonconnected) | $5,000/year — **not** indexed for inflation | Up to $5,000 per candidate per election (multicandidate status); may also make independent expenditures | **Yes** — itemized FEC reports | Yes — contributions are the lawful, capped form of coordination |
| Super PAC (independent-expenditure-only committee) | **Unlimited** — individuals, corporations, unions | Independent expenditures only; **$0** to candidates or parties | **Yes** — itemized FEC reports | **No** — independence is the legal price of unlimited money |
| Hybrid PAC ("Carey committee") | Both: capped into a contribution account, unlimited into a separate IE-only account | Each account by its own rules | **Yes** | Contribution side yes; IE side no |
| Leadership PAC | $5,000/year | $5,000 per candidate per election to *other* candidates; overhead, travel, polling — not the sponsor's own campaign | **Yes** | It *belongs to* a politician — but is legally separate from the campaign committee |
| Other 527 groups | Unlimited | Election-related activity outside FECA's trigger lines | **Yes** (IRS Form 8872 or FEC reports) | n/a |
| 501(c)(4) social-welfare org | Unlimited | Electioneering allowed if not its "primary" activity | **No** | Coordination rules still apply to its ads |
| 501(c)(3) charity | Unlimited (tax-deductible) | **None** — absolute candidate-campaign ban (Johnson Amendment) | No | n/a |

Read the table by columns, not rows. The "money in" column is constitutional law: caps survive where courts see a corruption risk, and fall where they don't. The "donors public?" column is the one most people get wrong — notice that **every FEC-registered committee in the table discloses its donors, including super PACs**. The anonymous-money story lives entirely in the bottom two rows, and it is the [[term:dark-money]] pack's subject, not this one's. The last column — coordination — is the load-bearing wall of the whole structure, and Lesson 3 is devoted to it.

One mechanical footnote that explains numbers you will see in FEC data: a PAC reaches **multicandidate status** (and the $5,000-per-candidate limit) once it has been registered six months, taken money from more than fifty donors, and given to at least five federal candidates. Until then it gives at the individual limit — $3,500 per candidate per election in the 2025–26 cycle. And note the asymmetry buried in the limits themselves: the individual-to-candidate cap is indexed for inflation; the $5,000 PAC caps, fixed in 1974, are not. Inflation has quietly cut the traditional PAC's giving power by more than four-fifths in real terms — one reason the action moved elsewhere.

<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> A tech billionaire wants to (a) give $2 million to a group running ads for Candidate X, and (b) give $5,000 directly to Candidate X's campaign committee. Which vehicles from the table can take the $2 million — and can the same organization handle both checks?</summary>
<p>Only a super PAC, a hybrid PAC's independent-expenditure account, or a nondisclosing nonprofit can accept the $2 million — a traditional PAC is capped at $5,000/year per donor. The direct $5,000 cannot legally pass through any of those: contributions to candidates must come from capped, disclosed sources, and the donor's own direct-giving limit is $3,500 per election. A <em>hybrid</em> PAC could take both checks — $2 million into its IE-only account, and a capped contribution into its contribution account — because Carey v. FEC lets one committee run both modes from segregated bank accounts.</p>
</details>

## What makes a super PAC "super"

The super PAC was born in two steps, and the order matters. First, *Citizens United v. FEC* (January 2010) held that independent expenditures — spending "not coordinated with a candidate" — do not corrupt in the constitutional sense, so corporations and unions may make them from treasury funds. Two months later the D.C. Circuit, sitting en banc in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC*, drew the syllogism: if independent spending cannot corrupt, then *contributions to a group that only makes independent spending* cannot corrupt either, and the $5,000 cap on giving to such a group is unconstitutional. Crucially, the same court **upheld** the registration and reporting requirements — the plaintiffs had challenged those too, and lost. The independent-expenditure-only committee, promptly nicknamed the super PAC, is therefore a creature of one premise: that "independent" spending is genuinely independent. Every fight in Lesson 3 is a fight about that premise.

The disclosure point deserves one more sentence, because it is the most commonly mangled fact in this field: **super PACs itemize their donors to the FEC**. What they cannot tell you is who stands *behind* a donor that is itself a nondisclosing nonprofit or a shell LLC — in 2024, super PACs and similar committees reported receiving over $1 billion from such sources. That dead-end pattern, "gray money," is traced hop-by-hop in the Dark Money pack.

## The hybrid, the admiral, and the politician's second wallet

Two more inventions complete the menagerie. In 2011, retired Rear Admiral James Carey wanted his National Defense PAC to do both things at once — give capped, disclosed contributions to veteran candidates *and* accept unlimited money for independent ads. The FEC said a committee must choose. In *Carey v. FEC* (D.D.C. 2011), Judge Rosemary Collyer granted a preliminary injunction, and the agency settled by stipulation that August: one committee may run both programs if it keeps **two segregated bank accounts**, one per rulebook. The resulting "hybrid PAC" or Carey committee is now standard equipment — Future Forward USA PAC, the principal pro-Harris outside group of 2024, ran as one while spending about $517 million.

The **leadership PAC** is older and stranger: a committee sponsored by an officeholder *separate from* their own campaign. Nearly every member of Congress has one (Mitch McConnell's Bluegrass Committee and Nancy Pelosi's PAC to the Future are long-running examples). The official purpose is building a power base — donating to colleagues' campaigns, which is why party leaders and committee-chair aspirants maintain them. The notorious feature is what scholars call the personal-use gap: the statutory ban on converting campaign funds to personal use (52 U.S.C. §30114) has been read by the FEC to reach only a candidate's *own* campaign committee — not the leadership PAC. Watchdogs have documented leadership-PAC money flowing to resorts, club dues, and travel; Campaign Legal Center and Issue One petitioned the FEC in 2018 to extend the personal-use rules, the agency took public comment — and, as of mid-2026, has never adopted a rule. A donor's $5,000 to a leadership PAC is disclosed, capped, and almost unregulated in how it is spent.

Her campaign committee paying the same bill would plainly violate the personal-use ban. Why is the leadership-PAC version (probably) legal — and what would have to change to close the gap?</summary>
<p>The personal-use statute applies to funds in a candidate's <em>authorized</em> campaign committee; the FEC has not extended it by rule to leadership PACs, which are legally separate committees. So the same expense flips from violation to gray zone depending on which of the politician's two wallets pays. Closing it requires either an FEC rulemaking (petitioned in 2018, never adopted) or a statute — the fix is administrative, not constitutional, since nobody disputes Congress could reach it.</p>
</details>

## The scale, in verified numbers

How big did the post-2010 architecture get? Outside spending — super PACs plus other non-candidate, non-party groups — reached a record of about **$4.5 billion** in the 2024 federal elections, within a total election price tag OpenSecrets projected at $15.9 billion. More than $2 billion of the outside money hit the presidential race alone. Concentration is the defining feature: Musk's $291 million-plus made him the cycle's largest donor, and a handful of other individuals each crossed $100 million. The traditional PAC sector did not shrink — corporate, union, and trade-association PACs still route hundreds of millions in capped contributions — but its *relative* weight collapsed. Richard Briffault's early law-review assessment ("Super PACs," *Minnesota Law Review*, 2012) has aged well: the independent-expenditure committee moved from the margins of campaign finance to its center within two cycles of its invention, and it has stayed there.

The scholar's-lens caution comes from Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner (*Campaign Finance and Political Polarization: When Purists Prevail*, 2015): the money did not appear from nowhere. Squeezing regulated channels — parties above all — redirected funds into less accountable outside groups, and outside groups tend to fund more ideologically extreme messaging than the pragmatic, win-seeking parties do. Whatever you conclude about super PACs, conclude it about the *system*: every cap reroutes; nothing evaporates.

## What this lesson is not

It is not a verdict. You now own the machinery: the century of bans and workarounds, the seven-row taxonomy, the *SpeechNow* syllogism, the Carey two-account trick, the leadership PAC's personal-use gap, and the verified 2024 scale. Whether this architecture is constitutionally required free speech, a plutocratic capture, or a fixable enforcement failure is precisely what the three perspectives on this pack dispute — Lesson 2 builds each one's strongest case, and Lesson 3 descends into the coordination rules where the live regulatory fight actually is.
