# How PACs and Super PACs actually work

The Citizens United pack covered the legal framework. This lesson dives deeper into the PAC infrastructure itself — the entities that actually spend the money, the rules they operate under, and the empirical scale of activity.

## The four major committee types

Federal Election Commission classifies political committees:

**Traditional Political Action Committees (PACs)** — what most people meant by "PAC" pre-2010. Capped contributions ($5,000/donor/year, indexed for inflation; ~$5,000 in 2024 to a non-multicandidate PAC); capped giving to candidates ($5,000 per candidate per election); coordination with candidates and parties is allowed within rules. Examples: corporate PACs (e.g., Boeing PAC), labor PACs (e.g., AFL-CIO COPE), trade-association PACs (e.g., NRA-PVF).

**Super PACs** ("Independent-Expenditure-Only Political Committees") — created by SpeechNow.org v. FEC (2010). Uncapped contributions from any source (individuals, corporations, unions, other PACs); only independent expenditures (no direct contributions to candidates or committees); coordination with candidates is forbidden in principle (with significant enforcement gaps). Examples: Priorities USA Action (D), American Crossroads (R), Senate Leadership Fund.

**Hybrid PACs** ("Carey Committees") — created by Carey v. FEC (D.C. Cir. 2011). Operate two segregated funds: one with capped contributions for direct candidate/committee giving, one with uncapped contributions for independent expenditures. The two funds must be kept legally separate. Effectively a single organizational structure with both PAC and Super PAC capabilities. Examples: NRA Political Victory Fund (operates both modes).

**Leadership PACs** — political committees set up by current or former officeholders. Capped contributions; can spend on multiple candidates' campaigns, travel, polling, and other political activities. Use of leadership PAC funds for personal expenses is a recurring concern. Examples: Mitch McConnell's Bluegrass Committee, Nancy Pelosi's PAC to the Future.

## What makes a Super PAC "Super"

The defining feature: **uncapped contributions in, independent expenditures out**.

Pre-2010, a PAC could accept up to $5,000 per donor per year and spend that money any way (within FEC rules) — including independent expenditures and direct contributions. The contribution cap was the binding constraint on volume.

Post-SpeechNow.org, a Super PAC can accept any amount from any source — including a single $50M check from one donor. The trade-off: no direct contributions to candidates. The reasoning (per the D.C. Circuit): independent expenditures don't corrupt (Citizens United), so the contribution cap that was justified by anti-corruption concerns has no application.

The result is the post-2010 landscape: a small number of mega-donors fund most Super PAC activity. Top 25 donors collectively gave more than the bottom 5M individual donors combined in recent cycles.

## The "independence" requirement

For a Super PAC to operate as a Super PAC, its expenditures must be "independent" of the campaigns it supports. Coordination is technically forbidden by FEC rules. But "coordination" has a specific legal definition that many real-world activities don't meet:

- **Public communication is allowed.** A campaign can publicly announce its strategy, target audiences, key messages — and a Super PAC can read those public communications and spend accordingly.
- **Strategic information not directly conveyed is allowed.** Polling data published in news outlets, opposition research released publicly, even campaign manager interviews — Super PACs can use any of this.
- **Personnel mobility is allowed.** A campaign manager can leave a campaign and join a Super PAC supporting that candidate (with a brief cooling-off period that's been steadily eroded).
- **Vendor sharing is allowed.** A campaign and Super PAC can use the same advertising firm, polling firm, lawyers — as long as personal-information walls are observed.

What's actually forbidden:
- **Direct strategic communication** between a campaign and its allied Super PAC.
- **Sharing of non-public information** (specific voter targeting, campaign internal polling).
- **Joint fundraising** (with limited exceptions for hybrid arrangements).

The result: the line between independent and coordinated has eroded substantially in practice. The FEC has been deadlocked on coordination cases for over a decade.

## The empirical scale

OpenSecrets data (federal, 2024 cycle):

- **Outside spending total** (Super PACs + 501(c)(4)s + others): ~$2.3B
- **Super PAC spending specifically**: ~$2B
- **Top single Super PAC spending**: $700M+ (Senate Leadership Fund and counterparts)
- **Total contributions to Super PACs**: ~$2.3B
- **Top 100 donors**: ~$1.3B+ combined (more than half of all Super PAC contributions)

For comparison, total candidate-committee spending in 2024 was ~$3.5B. Super PAC spending is roughly 2/3 of candidate spending — a major shift from the pre-2010 era when independent expenditures were a small fraction of candidate spending.

## The role of state-level PACs

State-level political action committees mirror federal structures but with significant variation:
- **Contribution limits vary widely.** Some states have no caps; others have caps lower than federal limits.
- **Disclosure varies.** Some state PACs are subject to weaker disclosure requirements than federal PACs.
- **Super PAC structures vary.** Most states allow Super-PAC equivalents, but the rules differ.

State-level activity is large and often understudied. State-level Super PACs may exceed federal-level activity in aggregate, but tracking them is harder because state disclosure regimes vary.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson sharpens:

- **PAC-defenders** argue current PACs are constitutionally protected and serve essential functions in mobilizing political activity. Restrictions face First Amendment problems and would tilt politics toward unaccountable issue-advocacy outside PAC structures.
- **PAC-restrictionists** argue the post-Citizens-United Super PAC system has produced systematic distortion of political activity in favor of mega-donors and that meaningful restrictions are necessary, even if they require constitutional amendment.
- **Coordination-rules reformers** argue the underlying problem is not Super PACs as such but the erosion of independence requirements; tighter coordination rules and better FEC enforcement would substantially address the dependence-corruption concerns without requiring sweeping restrictions.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it.
