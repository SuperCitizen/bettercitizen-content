# The coordination debate

Most active reform energy in PAC policy is around coordination rules — the legal line between "independent" and "coordinated" expenditures. This is where the post-2010 system's empirical premise is being tested.

## What the FEC currently considers "coordinated"

Under FEC regulations, a communication is "coordinated" if it satisfies all three of:

1. **Source test**: paid for by someone other than the candidate or party.
2. **Content test**: meets one of several criteria (electioneering communication; expressly advocates election/defeat; references a candidate within specific time windows).
3. **Conduct test**: meets one of several conduct factors (e.g., the spender talked to the candidate about the ad; the spender used a common vendor; a former employee of the campaign now works for the spender within 120 days).

Each test has multiple sub-tests. The cumulative effect is that *most* real-world Super PAC activity does not meet the coordination test, because the conduct factors are designed narrowly.

## Recent FEC enforcement (and non-enforcement)

Since approximately 2008, the FEC has been deadlocked 3-3 on most coordination cases. Notable patterns:

- **Joint vendors.** The FEC has not taken consistent enforcement action against shared vendors despite repeated complaints. The result: Super PACs and campaigns frequently share polling firms, ad agencies, and digital strategists.
- **Personnel mobility.** Cooling-off periods between campaign and Super PAC employment are short (often 30-120 days) and routinely evaded.
- **Public communication coordination.** The FEC has held that publicly-released strategic information doesn't trigger coordination — even when the public release is clearly designed to communicate strategy to allied Super PACs.

Mayer (2017) and the CRP/Brennan Center documented the deadlock in detail. The FEC has six commissioners (3 from each major party); most enforcement actions require 4 votes. With the partisan split, most cases produce 3-3 deadlocks.

## Reform proposals

- **Tighter conduct test.** Specific reforms would expand the cooling-off period for personnel mobility (to 1-2 years, not 30-120 days) and restrict shared-vendor arrangements.
- **Mandatory disclosure of strategic relationships.** Super PACs would have to disclose vendor and personnel relationships with campaigns more comprehensively.
- **Stronger DOJ role.** Some proposals would shift coordination enforcement from the deadlocked FEC to the DOJ, which has more enforcement authority.
- **FEC structural reform.** Changing the partisan-tied commissioner structure to break the 3-3 deadlock pattern. Has been proposed periodically but never advanced.

The Freedom to Vote Act (S. 2747) included some of these provisions. None has been enacted in recent Congresses.

## What's likely next

- **Continued FEC deadlock.** Without structural reform, the deadlock persists. Most coordination cases will not produce enforcement.
- **Litigation around specific arrangements.** Watchdog groups (Common Cause, Campaign Legal Center) periodically file complaints; some lead to court cases.
- **State-level coordination rules.** Some states have stricter coordination rules than federal law. These have been tested in state courts; rulings are mixed.
- **Periodic legislative proposals** that won't advance under current dynamics but stay on the agenda.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on PAC and Super PAC policy requires:

1. A view on **whether the post-2010 system is producing dependence-corruption** (the central empirical question).
2. A view on **what reforms (if any) would address the concerns**.
3. A view on **the constitutional limits** of available reforms.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
