Picture a phone lighting up. A fundraising email has just landed, or a candidate has just had a moment in a debate. A few minutes later, a small donation is on its way: less than the cost of a tank of gas.

[beat]

Now multiply that. In the 2024 election cycle, the Democratic side's online donation platform, ActBlue, processed about 3.8 billion dollars. Its Republican counterpart, WinRed, processed about 1.7 billion. Both figures come from the platforms' own year-end filings with the Federal Election Commission, the FEC.

And most of that money arrived just as we pictured it. In small increments, from millions of ordinary people, on phones, often within minutes of an email or a debate moment.

[pause]

Small-dollar giving on that scale is new in American history. It means the questions in this lecture are no longer questions for the wealthy.

If you have ever chipped in 25 dollars, you are a participant in the system the last two lectures mapped. The remaining question is whether you participate deliberately.

One thing should be clear from the start. This is not a sales pitch. Nothing in this lecture will tell you whom to support, and nothing here will move your money. What it can do is show you how to look before you give.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the channel: where your money lands, and what giving it puts on the public record.

Second, the research: who already funds a candidate, and what the candidate's record says.

Third, the pressure machinery that is aimed at you.

And last, a puzzle the scholars have not resolved, about what small donations do to politics.

## Know your channel

The same 100 dollars behaves very differently depending on where it lands. You have the full map already, from the first lecture. Here it is once more, from the giver's side.

Give to a candidate's campaign committee, and your gift is capped, at 3,500 dollars per election in the current cycle. It is spent under the candidate's control.

Give to a party committee, and it is capped as well, at 44,300 dollars a year for a national party. It is spent across many races.

Give to a traditional PAC, and the cap is 5,000 dollars a year. The PAC gives the money on again, in chunks of 5,000.

Give to a Super PAC, and there is no cap. Your money is spent on independent ads that you do not control, and that the candidate legally cannot direct.

Give to a 501(c)(4), and your gift is unlimited and undisclosed. But notice the catch. The same opacity that shields you shields everyone else who is funding it.

[pause]

That is the channel. Now the trade each channel makes with your privacy.

There is one threshold to know. [beat] It is 200 dollars.

Once your giving to any one committee passes 200 dollars in a cycle, four facts about you go on the record. Your name. Your address. Your employer. Your occupation. That record is permanent, public, and searchable. Anyone can look up your political giving on the FEC's website, and data firms routinely do.

> Past 200 dollars a cycle to one committee, your giving is permanently public.

Below 200 dollars, you sit in the unitemized totals: counted, but not named. And a 501(c)(4) discloses nothing about you at all, which is exactly why the first lecture called it dark money.

One more fact covers the whole list. None of these gifts is tax-deductible. A donation to politics is not charity, whatever the email implies.

Let's test the threshold. A public-school teacher gives 15 dollars a month to a Senate campaign, through a donation platform, for a full year. What ends up in the public record? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

After one year, [beat] nothing with her name on it. A year of 15 dollar gifts comes to 180 dollars, just below the threshold.

But the threshold is counted per election cycle, and a cycle runs two years. Two more months of the same recurring gift will push her past 200 dollars. So will one extra chip-in of 25. And then her name, address, employer and occupation are itemized in the campaign's FEC filings, permanently and searchably.

Neither outcome is wrong.

> Going on the public record should be chosen, not discovered.

And recurring gifts are how most donors cross thresholds without noticing.

## Follow the money, then check the record

That is what your gift does to you. Now turn to the money that is already there. Before you add to it, see who else is giving.

The disclosure system exists for you. But it only works if someone looks.

Two sources do most of the work. The first is the FEC's own data, which is the official record. There you can search for a committee and go through its actual receipts and expenditures. And you can verify any claim an ad makes about who funds whom, before you repeat it.

The second is OpenSecrets, which layers analysis on top of those filings. Industry totals. Top donors. Outside spending in the race. And the disclosure status of each group.

With those two sources open, there are two specific things to check.

First, which committee is actually asking. Fundraising emails routinely come from PACs with names that sound like a candidate's. A group called the Re-elect Smith Victory Alliance may share only a vendor with Smith. The fine print at the bottom of the email, the line that begins "Paid for by", names the real committee. That is the name to look up, before you assume.

Second, the funding picture against the story. Suppose a candidate runs as the scourge of an industry, and that candidate's top donors work in that same industry. The candidate has some explaining to do. And you can see it for yourself in an evening.

Here is a case to try.

An ad supports a Senate candidate. Its fine print says "Paid for by Prosperity Action Fund", which is a Super PAC. The Super PAC's FEC filings show a single donor: a 501(c)(4) called Citizens for American Renewal.

What do you actually know about who funded that ad? Take a moment.

[long pause]

You know the nonprofit's name. [beat] And that is all you know.

The Super PAC complied fully with disclosure law. It itemized its donor. But that donor is a vehicle whose own funders are never publicly disclosed. So the paper trail stops one hop short of a human being.

> Fully disclosed and traceable are different claims.

This is the pipeline from the first lecture: person, nonprofit, Super PAC. It is how roughly 1.3 billion dollars entered the outside spending of 2024 with no traceable origin.

[pause]

Money research tells you who is invested. It does not tell you whether the candidate is any good. For that you need the record, and two more sources.

To learn how a candidate actually voted, start with Vote Smart. It keeps roll-call votes, public statements, and interest-group ratings from across the spectrum.

To learn what a race or a ballot measure is, start with Ballotpedia. It keeps neutral profiles of candidates, districts, and ballot questions.

The goal is not to reach a particular conclusion. The goal is this.

> Give from the record, not from a 30-second spot.

And not from the most manipulable instrument ever aimed at a donor: the fundraising email.

## The pressure machinery

That email deserves a second look. Online fundraising is engineered, and the engineering is documented. Here are four of its parts.

Countdown clocks, pegged to arbitrary deadlines.

Subject lines that simulate personal mail.

Claims of 500 percent matches, which no law verifies.

And the practice that drew the most scrutiny after 2020: the pre-checked recurring box, which quietly converts a one-time gift into monthly withdrawals.

[pause]

None of this is illegal. [beat] All of it is designed to convert arousal into transactions, before reflection arrives.

In plainer words, the machinery is built to get the money moving before you have had time to think.

The recurring box matters for a second reason. Think back to the teacher. Past what amount, in a cycle, does giving go on the public record? And which four facts about the donor go with it?

[long pause]

The amount is 200 dollars. The facts are name, address, employer and occupation. A recurring box you never noticed can carry you across that line.

So there are two mechanical defenses.

> Check the "Paid for by" line. Uncheck any recurring box you did not choose.

If recurring is what you decided beforehand, the box can stay checked. The point is that you decided.

The same skepticism applies to groups with civic-sounding names that appear from nowhere around a hot issue. The first lecture's pipeline explains how astroturfing is financed. And OpenSecrets is usually the fastest way to see whether a group called Neighbors for Honest Government has any neighbors in it.

One more case, for the next time the phone lights up. The subject line reads: "Triple match active for the next 2 hours!"

You now know enough to take that sentence apart, and there are three things to ask of it. Who would have to be putting up the matching money. Which of the five channels that money would move through. And what the FEC filings would show, if the match were real. Remember that no law verifies a match.

## The small-donor paradox

Now the puzzle. It is tempting to treat small-dollar giving as the obvious cure for big money: millions of 25 dollar donors, as the democratic answer to the 290 million dollar donor.

The political-science literature complicates that comfort, and it does so from two directions.

The first comes from Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner, in a 2015 book called Campaign Finance and Political Polarization. They argue that the donors most motivated to give, at any dollar level, are ideological purists. So a system that channels money through individual donors, and not through parties, systematically advantages the most polarizing candidates. The viral fundraisers of both parties in 2024 were rarely their moderates.

> La Raja and Schaffner argue that donor-driven money advantages the most polarizing candidates.

The second is older, and it cuts the other way. In 2003, the scholars Ansolabehere, de Figueiredo and Snyder found that most giving is best understood as participation. Consumption, not investment. People give the way they cheer.

> The older finding: most giving is participation. Consumption, not investment.

On that view, your 25 dollars will not buy policy, [beat] and was never going to. Its honest function is expressive. And that is oddly liberating. It is an argument for giving on your values and your homework, not on your adrenaline.

Can both be true? [beat] They can. Your individual gift is expressive. And the whole architecture, built from millions of expressive gifts, still shapes who runs, who wins, and how they behave. That tension is unresolved in the literature. Hold it honestly.

Let's put the two together. Suppose La Raja and Schaffner are right, and individual-donor money advantages purists. Does that argue for, or against, your giving 25 dollars to a candidate you have researched and believe in?

[long pause]

Neither. [beat] It argues for the researched part.

Their claim is about the tilt of a whole system of donor-driven finance. It is not about the ethics of any one gift. Your considered 25 dollars does not become polarizing because other people give impulsively.

What the finding does counsel is awareness. Are you giving because the candidate matches your values, and your reading of the record? Or because an email made you angry at the optimal moment?

The first is participation. [beat] The second is being a purchased input.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Know your channel. The same 100 dollars is capped, and under the candidate's control, in one place. In another it is unlimited, and beyond anything the candidate can direct.

[beat]

Two. Know the trade. Past 200 dollars in a cycle to any one committee, your name, address, employer and occupation become a permanent public record. That should be chosen, not discovered. And no political gift is tax-deductible.

[beat]

Three. Follow the money, then check the record. The FEC and OpenSecrets show who funds a candidate. Vote Smart shows how the candidate voted. Ballotpedia shows what the race is. And remember that fully disclosed and traceable are different claims.

[beat]

Four. The pressure is engineered. The two defenses: check the "Paid for by" line, and uncheck any recurring box you did not choose.

[beat]

And five. The small-donor paradox. One body of work argues that donor-driven money advantages the most polarizing candidates. An older one finds that most giving is participation: consumption, not investment. Both can be true.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not a verdict on whether the small-donor revolution is good for American politics. The thesis of La Raja and Schaffner, and its critics, are an ongoing scholarly fight. The job of these three lectures was to arm you for that fight, not to settle it.

Nor is this the constitutional argument. Whether the channels should exist in their current shape is exactly what the series on Citizens United puts on trial. That is where the argument goes from here.

And the giving itself stays with you. There is no button here that gives to a candidate. No ranking of recipients, and no recommending. No cut taken of anything. Political contributions are yours to make, directly, through the recipient or a registered platform you choose.

So go back to the phone lighting up, and to the few minutes before the money moves. [beat] Those minutes are where the habit belongs.

> Channel, disclosure, record. In that order, every time, before the money moves.
