New York, 1905. Investigators working for a legislative committee make a discovery.

The country's great life-insurance companies have been quietly diverting their policyholders' premiums into Republican campaign coffers. The companies' own officers are questioned under oath. They struggle to explain the payments.

[beat]

The scandal reached the president, Theodore Roosevelt. His own campaign, in 1904, had taken corporate money. He responded by demanding that Congress ban the practice outright.

The result came in 1907, and it is called the Tillman Act. It was the first federal campaign-finance law, and it prohibited corporations and national banks from contributing to federal candidates.

Its operative prohibition runs barely two sentences. [beat] It has never been repealed.

> The Tillman Act of 1907 still binds, even after Citizens United.

Everything since has been an argument, 120 years long, about what else the Constitution lets Congress do.

[pause]

Last time we mapped the channels that political money flows through. This lecture is about the law that drew the lines between them. By the end, you should be able to say what each major statute and decision did, and what it did not do. That is the prerequisite for any opinion about the current system that is worth defending.

We will take it in four steps.

First, Congress builds: a stack of statutes, from 1907 to 1974.

Second, a case called Buckley, and the distinction everything rests on.

Third, the decades after Buckley, when the rules grew once more, and then shrank, case by case.

And last, the question underneath all of it: what kind of corruption counts?

## Congress builds

Three layers of statute stack up before the courts take over.

The first layer we have already met. The Tillman Act barred corporations and national banks from contributing to federal campaigns. In 1947, the Taft-Hartley Act extended that ban to labor unions. And, crucially, it reached past direct gifts. It also covered what corporations and unions spent independently. The term for that is an independent expenditure, and it will matter all the way through.

The second layer came in 1971. It was the Federal Election Campaign Act, known as FECA. It consolidated the disclosure rules and put the modern reporting regime in place.

The third layer is the big one. In 1974, Congress amended FECA, in the wake of Watergate. The Nixon campaign of 1972 had taken briefcases of barely traceable cash.

So Congress capped nearly everything. Listen to the list.

It capped what an individual could contribute: 1,000 dollars per candidate, per election. It capped what campaigns could spend. It capped what individuals could spend independently. It capped what candidates could spend of their own fortunes. And it created the Federal Election Commission, the FEC, to enforce it all.

[pause]

The 1974 regime regulated political money at every node. Giving, spending, even a candidate's own fortune: all of it now had a limit.

Within two years, [beat] the Supreme Court had cut that regime in half.

## Buckley: the distinction everything rests on

The case was Buckley v. Valeo, decided in 1976. It is the most important campaign-finance case in American history. And its core move is a distinction the Court has never abandoned.

The Court began with a premise. Here are its words. "Virtually every means of communicating ideas in today's mass society requires the expenditure of money."

[pause]

Follow where that leads. If communicating takes money, then limits on political money are limits on political speech. And limits on speech must answer to the First Amendment.

But the Court did not treat all political money alike. It split the regime in two.

On one side, contributions. A cap on what one donor may give a candidate, the Court said, is only a "marginal restriction" on the donor's ability to communicate. The gift is the speech act. The amount is symbolic.

And capping gifts serves a legitimate interest of the government: preventing a particular kind of corruption. Its name is quid pro quo corruption, which means dollars exchanged for official acts. To that, the Court added the words "or the appearance of corruption."

So contribution limits survive.

On the other side, expenditures. These were the caps on what a person spends independently, on their own ads and their own pamphlets, and the caps on what candidates spend of their own money. Those caps, the Court said, "impose direct and substantial restraints on the quantity of political speech."

And the anti-corruption rationale does not reach them. If the spending is not coordinated with the candidate, the Court reasoned, there is no deal to strike.

So expenditure limits fall.

> Buckley's rule: contributing can be capped. Spending independently cannot.

Now notice what that distinction quietly decides. [beat] It decides what corruption is.

> In Buckley, corruption means quid pro quo, and nothing broader.

A corrupt bargain. Nothing wider than that. And every later fight is about whether that definition is right.

Let's test the rule. Suppose Congress passes two caps. The first limits what you may give your preferred candidate. The second limits what you may spend printing your own billboards, the ones that say "Vote for her". Under Buckley, which cap survives? And on what theory? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first survives. [beat] The second falls.

The cap on your gift is a marginal restriction on speech, and it is justified by preventing quid pro quo corruption, or the appearance of it. The candidate, after all, knows who gave.

Your billboard is different. It is your own direct political speech. The candidate does not control it, so the Court saw no corrupting bargain to prevent.

That asymmetry was drawn in 1976. It is the load-bearing wall of the whole system.

## Reform, and the Court's first answers

Buckley left behind a regime of capped contributions and free spending. And two famous workarounds grew up inside it.

The first was soft money. These were unlimited donations to political parties, given in the name of party-building. By the year 2000, soft money had become a shadow system worth half a billion dollars.

The second was the sham issue ad. Corporations and unions paid for ads that said everything except "vote against". Picture one that ends, "Call Senator Smith and tell him to stop betraying seniors." It never says how to vote, so it dodged the rules on express advocacy.

In 2002, Congress attacked both. The law was the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, BCRA for short, also called McCain-Feingold.

It banned soft money for the national parties. And in a provision called section 203, it went after the ads. Corporations and unions could no longer use their treasuries to fund what the law called electioneering communications: broadcast ads that name a federal candidate in the weeks before an election.

[pause]

The Court answered twice.

The first answer came in 2003, in a case called McConnell v. FEC. By five to four, the Court upheld BCRA's principal provisions, on a record that emphasized how soft money bought access.

The second answer came four years later, after Justice O'Connor's seat had changed hands. The case was FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life. This time the Court held that section 203 could not constitutionally be applied to ads that were genuine issue advocacy.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote the controlling opinion, and gave the test. An ad may be banned only if it is, in his words, "susceptible of no reasonable interpretation other than as an appeal to vote for or against a specific candidate."

[pause]

In plainer words: if an ad can reasonably be heard as anything but an appeal to vote for or against someone, it cannot be banned.

That decision hollowed out section 203 without overruling anything. [beat] The overruling came next.

## 2010, and after

It came as a one-two punch.

The first punch was Citizens United v. FEC, decided in January 2010. The Court struck section 203 down on its face: not for some ads, but entirely. Corporations and unions, it held, have a First Amendment right to make unlimited independent expenditures from their treasuries. To get there, the Court overruled an earlier precedent, and the part of McConnell that had upheld section 203.

Now listen for what the decision left standing. It left the Tillman Act's ban on direct contributions fully intact. It left Buckley's contribution caps intact. And, by eight to one, it left the disclosure rules intact.

What moved was the doctrine. The Court declared, as a matter of law, that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption."

[pause]

Hold that sentence in mind, and watch what another court did with it.

Two months later came the second punch, SpeechNow.org v. FEC. It was decided by the D.C. Circuit: a circuit court, not the Supreme Court.

That court drew the last line of a syllogism. If independent spending cannot corrupt, then contributions to a group that does nothing but independent spending cannot corrupt either. So the 5,000 dollar cap on giving to such a group is unconstitutional.

All nine judges agreed. In the words of Judge Sentelle's opinion, "the First Amendment cannot be encroached upon for naught."

The FEC did not seek review in the Supreme Court. And the creature that resulted, a political committee that takes unlimited money and spends it independently, [beat] is the Super PAC.

Remember the division of labor between those two cases. It is the most commonly botched fact in this field.

> Citizens United freed the spending. SpeechNow freed the fundraising.

Let's test that. A friend tells you: "Citizens United created Super PACs and lets corporations give millions to candidates." The record requires two corrections. What are they?

[long pause]

Here is the first. Corporations still cannot give candidates a dime. The ban on direct corporate contributions, in the Tillman Act of 1907, survived Citizens United untouched. The case freed only independent spending.

And the second. Super PACs were created by SpeechNow, in the D.C. Circuit. That court took the no-corruption logic of Citizens United and extended it, from spending to contributions. The Supreme Court has never itself ruled on Super PAC contribution limits.

[pause]

One more wall fell, in 2014.

FECA had a second kind of cap. Beyond the limit on each gift, it set an aggregate limit: a ceiling on a donor's total giving, across all federal candidates and committees. In the 2013 to 2014 cycle, that ceiling was 123,200 dollars.

Shaun McCutcheon, an engineer from Alabama, had given to sixteen candidates. He wanted to give to a dozen more, and he sued.

In McCutcheon v. FEC, the Court struck the aggregate limits, five to four. Chief Justice Roberts reasoned this way. Each base limit already polices the risk of a quid pro quo with each individual candidate. So a second cap, on the number of candidates one donor may give the maximum, adds no protection against corruption. It just restricts participation.

> Base limits still stand. The ceiling over them is gone.

Now step back and look at the whole line. Regulation expands through 2003. Then it shrinks, in a nearly straight run, from 2007 through 2014. Buckley's regime loses its walls one at a time.

And through all of it, two things remain standing.

> No majority has yet touched base contribution limits, or disclosure.

## What kind of corruption counts?

Now the question underneath. Every case we have walked through turns on one choice of definition, made in Buckley. Can you still say what corruption meant there?

[long pause]

Quid pro quo. [beat] A corrupt bargain, dollars exchanged for official acts, and nothing broader.

The liveliest scholarship attacks that choice, and it attacks from both sides.

Start with Lawrence Lessig and his 2011 book, Republic, Lost. Lessig names what the definition misses. He calls it dependence corruption.

The Framers, Lessig argues, designed a House "dependent on the people alone". The words are from Federalist 52. But a Congress whose members spend hours a day courting the donor class has developed a second dependence. Its members are not bribed retail. They are bent wholesale, toward the concerns of the people who fund campaigns.

No prosecutor could charge it. [beat] That is precisely the point.

> Lessig's name for what Buckley's definition misses: dependence corruption.

Richard Hasen and others counter that dependence corruption is too elastic to be a judicial standard. Hasen himself argues that the Court's line is wrong for a different reason, and defends limits on grounds of political equality.

Then there is the unexpected flank. In 2015, Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner published a book called Campaign Finance and Political Polarization. With fifty states of data, they argue that the reform project partly caused the very pathologies reformers hate.

Here is their mechanism. Capping and constraining party money did not shrink political money. It rerouted it, to ideological outside groups and to donors they call purists, who fund the most polarizing candidates. Meanwhile the parties, which rationally prefer moderates who can win, lost their financial leverage.

> La Raja and Schaffner argue that capping party money rerouted it, toward purists.

So their prescription runs opposite to most reform bills. [beat] Loosen the party channels.

One scene to test all of this. A senator takes no illegal money. But the senator schedules four hours of call time a day with five-figure donors, and quietly shelves a bill those donors dislike. Which scholar's framework names the problem? And why would Buckley's framework miss it?

[long pause]

Lessig's framework names it. This is dependence corruption.

No specific exchange occurred, so there is no quid pro quo for Buckley's definition to catch. Yet the institution has developed a dependence on its funders, one that competes with its dependence "on the people alone."

Does the Constitution let Congress regulate that, and not only exchanges that look like bribery? That is the deepest disagreement running under every case in this lecture.

## Where the line is moving

Two facts frame the next round.

First, a pending case, NRSC v. FEC. It asks the Supreme Court to strike FECA's limits on what parties may spend in coordination with their candidates. The Court upheld those limits in 2001. The new challenge was argued in December 2025, and as of mid-2026 the decision is pending.

If those limits fall, the world La Raja and Schaffner prefer comes nearer, and Buckley's framework loses another wall.

Second, the referee is absent. On May 1, 2025, the FEC dropped below its voting quorum of four members. As of mid-2026, it has been unable to issue rules or resolve enforcement cases for over a year. Two replacement commissioners were nominated in February 2026, and they have not yet been confirmed.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The oldest rule is still standing. The Tillman Act of 1907 bars corporations from contributing to federal candidates, and it still binds, even after Citizens United.

[beat]

Two. In 1974, after Watergate, Congress regulated political money at every node. Two years later, Buckley cut that regime in half. Contributing can be capped. Spending independently cannot. And corruption, in Buckley, means quid pro quo, and nothing broader.

[beat]

Three. The year 2010 was a one-two punch. Citizens United freed the spending. SpeechNow, decided by a circuit court, freed the fundraising. That is where the Super PAC comes from.

[beat]

Four. In 2014, McCutcheon removed the ceiling on a donor's total giving. Base limits still stand. And no majority has yet touched base contribution limits, or disclosure.

[beat]

And five. The fight underneath is over what corruption is. The Court says quid pro quo. Lessig says dependence. La Raja and Schaffner argue that capping party money rerouted it, toward purists.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not settled whether Buckley drew its line in the right place: whether contributing and spending really differ, and whether quid pro quo is the only corruption the Constitution lets Congress fight. And it has not settled whether disclosure actually works. Disclosure is the one regulatory tool every Court majority has blessed, and the series on Citizens United takes that question up.

What this lecture should have settled is the mechanics. Who did what, in which case, in which court. Get those right, and you can argue the rest honestly.

The next lecture turns from the law to you. If you give, even a little, how do you give with your eyes open?

For now, go back to 1907. A scandal, a president, and a prohibition barely two sentences long. [beat] It has never been repealed. And the argument about what else the Constitution allows has never ended.
