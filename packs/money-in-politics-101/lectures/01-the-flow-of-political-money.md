Start with two numbers. Both come from the 2024 election, and both belong to the same man.

[beat]

The first number is 290 million dollars. That is what Elon Musk put into electing the candidates he preferred. More than that, in fact. By the year-end filings at the Federal Election Commission, it was the largest disclosed political outlay by any individual in American history.

The second number is 6,600 dollars. That was the legal ceiling on what he could hand to Donald Trump's own campaign committee: 3,300 dollars for the primary, and the same again for the general election.

[pause]

Here is the strange part. Both numbers are true at once. [beat] And neither one involved breaking any law.

The distance between them is the real subject of every argument about money in politics you have ever heard. And most of those arguments go off the rails for one reason. The people having them are not talking about the same channel.

> Money in politics is not one channel. It is five.

A political dollar can travel through five legally distinct vehicles. Each has its own caps, its own disclosure rules, and its own relationship to the candidate. By the end of this lecture you should be able to tell the five apart, and to say where a dollar's paper trail goes dark.

We will take it in four steps.

First, three questions you can ask of any political dollar.

Second, the three channels where the money is capped.

Third, the two channels where it is not, and the place where the trail disappears.

And last, a scholars' puzzle: whether all of this money is, in fact, a lot.

## Three questions

Before the channels, the framework. For any dollar moving through politics, there are three things to ask.

One. Is it capped? Some channels have hard dollar limits, which rise with inflation. Some have no limit at all.

[beat]

Two. Is it disclosed? Some channels list every meaningful donor in a public federal database. One, by design, does not.

[beat]

Three. Can it be coordinated with the candidate? Money that a candidate controls is regulated tightly. Money spent independently of the candidate has been, since 2010, unlimited.

> Three questions for any political dollar: capped, disclosed, coordinated?

The five channels are just the five different ways those three questions get answered.

And the third question does something more. It draws a line through the whole system, a line called the coordination wall. On one side of the wall, money is capped, but it can be coordinated with candidates. On the other side, money is unlimited, but it must be spent independently of them. In legal theory, at least.

Put another way, the system sets up a trade. A donor can work with the campaign, and accept a cap. Or a donor can leave the cap behind, and give up the ability to work with the campaign.

We begin on the capped side.

## The capped side of the wall

There are three channels on this side.

Channel one is the candidate's own campaign committee. Every federal candidate must register one with the Federal Election Commission, the FEC. This is the money the candidate actually controls.

An individual can give that committee 3,500 dollars per election, in the 2025 to 2026 cycle. The primary and the general count as separate elections, so the real figure is 7,000 dollars per cycle, to one candidate.

That limit rises with inflation, in odd-numbered years. In the last cycle it was 3,300 dollars, which is where our opening number came from.

Not everyone may give. Foreign nationals are barred entirely. And corporations and unions are barred too. They have been since two old laws: the Tillman Act of 1907, and the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947.

Hold on to that ban. We will need it later.

Disclosure in this channel is at its maximum. Once your contributions to a committee pass 200 dollars in total, four facts about you become a public, searchable federal record. Your name. Your address. Your employer. And your occupation.

So this is the most regulated channel in American campaign finance. [beat] And, tellingly, it is the one the biggest spenders use least.

[pause]

Channel two is the party committee. Parties are the channel that reformers forget.

An individual can give 44,300 dollars a year to a national party committee, the DNC or the RNC.

What can a party do with the money? It can give a candidate a modest 5,000 dollars per election, directly. More important, it can spend on a candidate's behalf, in coordination with the campaign. That is called a coordinated expenditure. Coordinated expenditures have a separate schedule of caps, and in 2026 the largest is about 4 million dollars, for a Senate nominee in the largest state.

Those caps on coordinated spending are under challenge. As of mid-2026 they are before the Supreme Court, in a case called NRSC v. FEC, which was argued in December of 2025. If the caps fall, the party channel widens dramatically. That is a case to watch.

[pause]

Channel three is the traditional PAC. A PAC pools money from individuals and gives it to candidates and parties. An individual can give a PAC 5,000 dollars a year. The PAC, in turn, can give a candidate 5,000 dollars per election. And its donors are itemized, under the same rules as a campaign committee's.

One detail trips up people who follow the news. A corporate PAC is not funded from the corporate treasury. It is funded by voluntary contributions from employees. The ban on treasury money survives.

So the traditional PAC is the mid-century compromise: capped, transparent, and small. When a headline says a PAC spent 40 million dollars, it almost never means this channel.

Let's test the capped side. Here is a scene.

A donor wants to put 60,000 dollars behind one Senate candidate in 2026. She will use only the channels that are capped and fully disclosed. Roughly how far can she get? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not very far, directly.

She can give 7,000 dollars to the candidate's committee: half for the primary, half for the general. She can give 5,000 a year to a PAC that supports the candidate. And she can give 44,300 a year to the national party.

But follow the money one more step. The PAC can pass only 5,000 dollars per election to the candidate. And the party spreads its money across many races.

> The capped channels keep one donor's direct stake in one candidate small.

That is what they are built to do. To put all 60,000 dollars on one race, she would have to cross the coordination wall. Over there, the cap disappears. [beat] And so does her ability to coordinate with the campaign.

## The unlimited side

Before we cross, look back for a moment. I gave you three questions to ask of any political dollar. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Is it capped? Is it disclosed? Can it be coordinated with the candidate?

On the side we are leaving, the answers were yes, yes, and yes. Now watch the answers change.

Picture a chart with a bar for every cap an individual donor faces. Two channels are missing from it, because they have no bar to draw. And both are creatures of court decisions in 2010.

Channel four is the Super PAC. Its legal name says more: an independent-expenditure-only committee.

A Super PAC can accept unlimited money, from individuals, from corporations, and from unions. And it can spend unlimited sums arguing for or against candidates. There is one condition. It may not contribute to those candidates, and it may not coordinate with them.

Where did it come from? Not directly from the case called Citizens United, whatever you may have heard. It came from a second case in 2010, SpeechNow.org v. FEC, which extended the logic of the first. The next lecture takes both cases apart.

A Super PAC's donors are itemized in its FEC filings, just like a campaign's. So run the three questions again. [beat] Not capped. Disclosed. Not coordinated.

And the scale is the story. Outside spending in 2024 hit a record of roughly 4.5 billion dollars, by the count of the tracking group OpenSecrets.

And Musk's 290 million? [beat] It traveled almost entirely through this channel. Of that sum, 239 million went to a single Super PAC, called America PAC.

[pause]

Channel five is where the lights go out. [beat] It is called a 501(c)(4).

That name is an IRS category, not an FEC one. It describes a tax-exempt nonprofit, of the type called a social welfare group. Such a group may engage in electoral politics, so long as politics is not its primary purpose.

It can take unlimited money from anyone. And under current IRS rules, its donors are never publicly disclosed.

The public sees the spending. [beat] It cannot see who paid.

This is the channel that the policy literature calls dark money. And in 2024 it set a record of its own. More than 1.9 billion dollars flowed through groups that do not disclose their donors, by the analysis of the Brennan Center for Justice.

> A Super PAC names its donors. A 501(c)(4) does not.

So far, that sounds like two separate channels with two separate answers. Here is the turn.

## Where the trail goes dark

What happens when the donor a Super PAC names is itself a 501(c)(4)?

Walk one check through it, hop by hop.

First, the check goes to a nonprofit. An individual, a corporation, or a union gives any amount to a 501(c)(4). No public filing names the giver.

Then the nonprofit moves the money to a Super PAC. That transfer is unlimited, and it is lawful, so long as politics is not the nonprofit's primary purpose.

Then the Super PAC discloses. Its FEC report dutifully itemizes the donor, and the donor it lists is the nonprofit. The person who wrote the original check appears in no public record.

Only then do the ads run. Voters see the words "Paid for by Citizens for a Stronger Tomorrow". And they have no way to trace whose money is speaking.

[pause]

Person. Nonprofit. Super PAC. Ad. That is the dark-money pipeline.

> Every hop in the pipeline is legal. The darkness comes from the sequence.

Let me say that again, because it is the heart of the matter. No link in that chain breaks a rule. The opacity is produced by the order of the links, not by any single one.

And this is no trickle. In 2024, about 1.3 billion dollars passed from such groups, and from shell companies, into Super PACs. The Super PACs disclosed the nonprofit as their donor. The original names stayed invisible.

The same pattern powers most modern astroturfing. That is a campaign made to look like the grass roots: a group with a name like Concerned Citizens for X, and no way to see whose money is behind the curtain.

Now put the whole map to work.

The chief executive of a regional grocery chain wants the company itself to support a House candidate. Not personal money. Money from the corporate treasury. Which of the five channels are open to it? And what does each open channel disclose? Think back across all five.

[pause 5s]

Three channels are closed. Remember the ban I asked you to hold on to, from 1907 and 1947. It still stands. Treasury money cannot touch the candidate's committee, a party committee, or a traditional PAC.

Two channels are open. The company can give unlimited treasury money to a Super PAC, where the company's name appears in public FEC filings. Or it can give to a 501(c)(4), where no public record ever names the company.

So the choice between those two channels is, in practice, [beat] a choice about whether the customers find out.

[pause]

One caution before we leave the channels. Money in politics often gets confused with lobbying, and the two are separate. Paying a lobbyist to argue your case to officeholders is not a campaign contribution. It is a different activity, regulated under a different law, the Lobbying Disclosure Act.

Here is what that means in practice. A company that is barred from giving treasury money to a Senate campaign can lawfully spend many times that amount lobbying the same senator's office. Keep the two ledgers distinct.

## Is it a lot?

Last stop. Step back from the channels and look at the total.

The 2024 federal elections cost about 16 billion dollars. Of that, 5.5 billion went to the presidential race. Roughly 10 billion went to the races for Congress.

That sounds enormous. Until you ask: [beat] compared to what?

In 2003, three scholars published a famous article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives. Its title was a question: "Why Is There So Little Money in U.S. Politics?" The authors were Stephen Ansolabehere, John de Figueiredo, and James Snyder.

Their argument runs like this. The federal government allocates trillions of dollars. Given those stakes, rationally self-interested investors should pour far more into elections than they do. And campaign giving, as a share of GDP, has barely moved in a century.

So how did they interpret the evidence? Most political money, they concluded, behaves like consumption, not investment. People give in order to participate, the way they donate to a church or a team. And only about one in four published studies finds contributions moving legislators' roll-call votes at all.

> One reading of the evidence: most political money is consumption, not investment.

The puzzle, they argued, is not why there is so much money in politics. [beat] It is why there is so little.

[pause]

That finding is a real constraint on lazy talk about money buying votes. But it is not the end of the argument.

The strongest reply from the reform side belongs to Lawrence Lessig, who calls his idea dependence corruption. The harm, on his account, is not votes purchased retail. It is whom candidates spend their time courting, wholesale.

> Lessig's reply: the harm is whom candidates spend their time courting.

Hold both ideas. You will need them in the next lecture, where Lessig's argument is taken up in full.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Money in politics is not one channel. It is five. Three questions tell them apart: is the dollar capped, is it disclosed, and can it be coordinated with the candidate?

[beat]

Two. Three channels are capped and disclosed. The candidate's committee takes 3,500 dollars per election from an individual, and itemizes the donor in public past 200 dollars. The national party takes 44,300 a year, and spends across many races. The traditional PAC takes 5,000 a year, and gives a candidate 5,000 per election. Corporate and union treasuries are banned from all three.

[beat]

Three. Two channels are unlimited, and both date from court decisions in 2010. A Super PAC takes unlimited money and names its donors, but its spending must stay independent of the candidate. A 501(c)(4) takes unlimited money and never publicly names its donors, and politics cannot be its primary purpose.

[beat]

Four. The trail goes dark where those two are joined. Person, nonprofit, Super PAC, ad. Every hop in the pipeline is legal. The darkness comes from the sequence.

[beat]

And five. Whether all this money is a lot is itself in dispute. One reading says most political money is consumption, not investment. The reply says the harm is whom candidates spend their time courting.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We mapped the plumbing. We did not ask whether the plumbing is good. I have offered no view on whether unlimited independent spending corrupts politics, or counts as protected speech. That fight has a series of its own, on Citizens United, where each side gets its strongest case. And nothing here was advice about your own giving. That waits for the third lecture, where the question turns practical: knowing the channels, how do you give with your eyes open?

First, though, the law. Who drew these lines, and why do they fall where they do? The next lecture takes the cases in order, from one called Buckley, through Citizens United and SpeechNow, to one called McCutcheon.

For now, go back to the two numbers. 6,600 dollars. 290 million dollars. [beat] The first is the ceiling in a channel Congress can still cap. The second traveled through a channel with no ceiling at all.

Same man. Same election. No law broken. [beat] The whole distance is the channel.
