# The legal framework — Buckley, FECA, BCRA, Citizens United, SpeechNow, McCutcheon

In 1905, investigators working for a New York legislative committee discovered that the country's great life-insurance companies had been quietly diverting policyholders' premiums into Republican campaign coffers — payments the companies' own officers struggled to explain under oath. The scandal reached President Theodore Roosevelt, whose 1904 campaign had taken corporate money, and who responded by demanding that Congress ban the practice outright. The result, in 1907, was the **Tillman Act**: the first federal campaign-finance law, prohibiting corporations and national banks from contributing to federal candidates. Its operative prohibition runs barely two sentences, it has never been repealed, and it still binds — even after *Citizens United*. Everything since has been a 120-year argument about what else the Constitution lets Congress do.

The modern shape of that argument was set by one statute and five court decisions. Knowing what each actually did — and did not do — is the prerequisite to having any opinion worth defending about the current system.

## A century of statutes: 1907–1974

Three legislative layers stack up before the courts take over:

- **Tillman Act (1907):** corporations and national banks barred from contributing to federal campaigns. The **Taft–Hartley Act (1947)** extended the ban to labor unions and — crucially — to *independent expenditures* by corporations and unions, not just direct gifts.
- **FECA (1971):** the Federal Election Campaign Act consolidated disclosure rules and put the modern reporting regime in place.
- **The 1974 FECA amendments:** the big one, passed in [[term:watergate]]'s wake — the 1972 Nixon campaign had taken briefcases of barely-traceable cash. The amendments capped individual contributions (\$1,000 per candidate per election), capped what campaigns could spend, capped what individuals could spend *independently*, capped what candidates could spend of their own fortunes, and created the **Federal Election Commission** to enforce it all.

The 1974 regime regulated political money at every node. Within two years, the Supreme Court had cut it in half.

## *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976): the distinction everything rests on

*Buckley* is the most important campaign-finance case in American history, and its core move is a distinction the Court has never abandoned. The per curiam opinion reasoned that "virtually every means of communicating ideas in today's mass society requires the expenditure of money" — so limits on political money are limits on political speech, triggering [[term:first-amendment]] scrutiny. But it split the regime in two:

- **Contribution limits survive.** A cap on what one donor may give a candidate "entails only a marginal restriction upon the contributor's ability to engage in free communication" — the gift is the speech act; the amount is symbolic. And capping gifts serves the government's legitimate interest in preventing **quid pro quo corruption** — dollars exchanged for official acts — "or the appearance of corruption."
- **Expenditure limits fall.** Caps on what a person spends *independently* — their own ads, their own pamphlets — and on what candidates spend of their own money "impose direct and substantial restraints on the quantity of political speech," and the anti-corruption rationale doesn't reach them: if the spending isn't coordinated with the candidate, the Court reasoned, there is no deal to strike.

So: **contributing is regulable; spending independently is not.** Note what the distinction quietly decides — corruption means *quid pro quo*, a corrupt bargain, and nothing broader. Every later fight is about whether that definition is right.

<Checkpoint question="Under Buckley, Congress caps (a) what you may give your preferred candidate, and (b) what you may spend printing your own “Vote for her” billboards. Which cap survives, and on what theory?">
  (a) survives; (b) falls. The contribution cap is a "marginal" speech restriction justified by preventing quid pro quo corruption or its appearance — the candidate knows who gave. Your independent billboard is your own direct political speech, and because the candidate doesn't control it, the Court saw no corrupting bargain to prevent — so capping it "imposes direct and substantial restraints on the quantity of political speech." That asymmetry, drawn in 1976, is the load-bearing wall of the whole system.
</Checkpoint>

## BCRA (2002) and the Court's first answers

*Buckley* left a regime of capped contributions and free spending — and two famous workarounds grew up inside it. **Soft money**: unlimited donations to political *parties* for "party-building," which by 2000 had become a half-billion-dollar shadow system. **Sham issue ads**: corporate- and union-funded ads that said everything but "vote against" ("Call Senator Smith and tell him to stop betraying seniors…"), thereby dodging the express-advocacy rules.

The **Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002** — McCain–Feingold — attacked both: it banned national-party soft money, and its §203 barred corporations and unions from funding "**electioneering communications**" (broadcast ads naming a federal candidate within 30 days of a primary or 60 days of a general election) from their treasuries. It also doubled the individual contribution limit to \$2,000 and indexed it to inflation — which is why the limit is \$3,500 today.

The Court's first answer came in ***McConnell v. FEC* (2003)**: 5–4, BCRA's principal provisions upheld, on a record emphasizing how soft money bought access. The second answer came four years later in ***FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life* (2007)**, after Justice O'Connor's seat changed hands: §203 could not constitutionally be applied to ads that were *genuine* issue advocacy. Chief Justice Roberts's controlling opinion gave the test — an ad may be banned only if it is "susceptible of no reasonable interpretation other than as an appeal to vote for or against a specific candidate." *WRTL* hollowed §203 without overruling anything. The overruling came next.

## 2010: the one-two punch

***Citizens United v. FEC* (January 21, 2010)** struck §203 facially: corporations and unions have a First Amendment right to make unlimited **independent expenditures** from their treasuries. It overruled *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990) and the part of *McConnell* upholding §203 — while leaving the Tillman Act's direct-contribution ban, *Buckley*'s contribution caps, and (by 8–1) the disclosure rules fully intact. The dedicated [[term:citizens-united]] pack dissects the opinion; what matters here is the doctrinal move: the Court declared, as a matter of *law*, that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption."

Two months later the D.C. Circuit, sitting en banc, drew the syllogism's last line. ***SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (March 26, 2010)**: if independent spending cannot corrupt, then *contributions to a group that does nothing but independent spending* cannot corrupt either — so the \$5,000 cap on giving to such a group is unconstitutional. All nine judges agreed; as Judge Sentelle's opinion put it, "the First Amendment cannot be encroached upon for naught." The FEC did not seek Supreme Court review. The resulting creature — a political committee that takes unlimited money and spends it independently — is the **Super PAC**. Remember the division of labor, because it is the most commonly botched fact in this field: *Citizens United* freed the *spending*; *SpeechNow* — a circuit court, not the Supreme Court — freed the *fundraising*.

<Checkpoint question="A friend says “Citizens United created Super PACs and lets corporations give millions to candidates.” What two corrections does the record require?">
  First, corporations still cannot give candidates a dime — the Tillman Act (1907) ban on direct corporate contributions survived *Citizens United* untouched; the case freed only *independent* spending. Second, Super PACs were created by *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (D.C. Circuit, en banc, March 2010), which extended *Citizens United*'s no-corruption logic from spending to *contributions* to independent-only committees. The Supreme Court has never itself ruled on Super PAC contribution limits.
</Checkpoint>

## *McCutcheon* (2014): the aggregate limits fall

FECA had a second kind of cap: an **aggregate limit** on a donor's total giving across all federal candidates and committees — \$123,200 per two-year cycle in 2013–14 (\$48,600 to candidates, \$74,600 to committees). Shaun McCutcheon, an Alabama engineer, had given \$33,088 to sixteen candidates, wanted to give to a dozen more, and sued. In ***McCutcheon v. FEC* (2014)** the Court, 5–4, struck the aggregate limits: Chief Justice Roberts's plurality reasoned that once each *base* limit polices the quid pro quo risk with each individual candidate, capping the *number* of candidates a donor may max out to adds no anti-corruption protection — it just restricts participation. Justice Thomas supplied the fifth vote while arguing the Court should have gone further and overruled *Buckley*'s contribution side entirely. Base limits still stand; the ceiling over them is gone.

Run your eye along the rail: regulation expands through 2003, then contracts in a near-straight run from 2007 through 2014 — and the open question is whether *NRSC v. FEC* extends the contraction to the party caps. Because the cases blur easily, here is the ledger of what each decision struck and what it deliberately left standing:

| Decision | What fell | What deliberately survived |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Buckley* (1976) | All expenditure limits — independent spending, candidate self-funding, campaign totals | Contribution limits; disclosure |
| *McConnell* (2003) | Nothing of substance | BCRA's soft-money ban and §203, upheld 5–4 |
| *WRTL* (2007) | §203 *as applied* to genuine issue ads | §203 on its face — for three more years |
| *Citizens United* (2010) | §203 on its face; *Austin* overruled | Tillman Act contribution ban; disclosure, 8–1 |
| *SpeechNow* (2010, D.C. Cir.) | The \$5,000 cap on giving to independent-only committees | Every base limit on giving to candidates, parties, PACs |
| *McCutcheon* (2014) | The aggregate ceiling on total giving | Every base limit |

Read the middle column top to bottom and you watch *Buckley*'s regime lose walls one at a time; read the right-hand column and you see the two things no majority has yet touched: base contribution limits and disclosure.

## The scholar's lens: what kind of corruption counts?

The entire doctrinal line above turns on one definitional choice in *Buckley*: corruption means quid pro quo. The liveliest scholarship attacks that choice from both sides.

Lawrence Lessig's *Republic, Lost* (2011) names what the definition misses: **dependence corruption**. The Framers, Lessig argues, designed a House "dependent on the people alone" (Federalist No. 52's words); a Congress whose members spend hours a day courting the donor class has developed a *second* dependence — not bribed retail, but bent wholesale toward the concerns of the people who fund campaigns. No prosecutor could charge it; that is precisely the point. Richard Hasen and others counter that "dependence corruption" is too elastic to be a judicial standard — though Hasen himself argues the Court's quid-pro-quo-only line is wrong for a different reason, defending limits on political-equality grounds.

Then there is the unexpected flank: Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner's *Campaign Finance and Political Polarization: When Purists Prevail* (2015) argues, with fifty states of data, that the reform project partly *caused* the pathologies reformers hate. Capping and constraining **party** money didn't shrink political money — it rerouted it to ideological outside groups and "purist" donors, who fund the most polarizing candidates, while parties (which rationally prefer electable moderates) lost their financial leverage. Their prescription runs opposite to most reform bills: *loosen* the party channels. Keep this argument in hand when you watch *NRSC v. FEC* — it is, in effect, the empirical brief for the side trying to lift the party caps.

<Checkpoint question="A senator takes no illegal money, but schedules four hours of call time a day with five-figure donors and quietly shelves a bill those donors dislike. Which scholar's framework names the problem — and why would Buckley's framework miss it?">
  Lessig's **dependence corruption**: no specific exchange occurred, so there is no quid pro quo for *Buckley*'s definition to catch — yet the institution has developed a dependence on funders that competes with its dependence "on the people alone." Whether the Constitution lets Congress regulate *that* — rather than only bribery-like exchanges — is the deepest disagreement running under every case in this lesson.
</Checkpoint>

## Where the line is moving now

Two live facts frame the next round. First, ***NRSC v. FEC***: the challenge — filed in 2022 by the Republican Senate and House campaign committees, joined by J.D. Vance and then-Representative Steve Chabot of Ohio — asks the Court to strike FECA's limits on what parties may spend *in coordination with* their candidates, limits the Court upheld in *FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee* (2001). It was argued December 9, 2025; as of mid-2026 the decision is pending. If the limits fall, La Raja and Schaffner's preferred world gets closer — and the *Buckley* framework loses another wall. Second, the referee is absent: the FEC dropped below its four-member voting quorum on May 1, 2025, and as of mid-2026 has been unable to issue rules or resolve enforcement cases for over a year, with two replacement commissioners nominated in February 2026 but not yet confirmed.

## What this lesson did not settle

It did not settle whether *Buckley*'s line was drawn in the right place — whether contributing and spending really differ, and whether quid pro quo is the only corruption the Constitution lets Congress fight. It did not settle whether disclosure (the one regulatory tool every Court majority has blessed) actually works; the Citizens United pack's final lesson takes that up. What it should have settled is the mechanics: who did what, in which case, in which court. Get those right and you can argue the rest honestly.
