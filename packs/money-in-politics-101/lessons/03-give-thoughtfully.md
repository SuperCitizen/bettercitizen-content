# Giving thoughtfully — research first, decide for yourself

In the 2024 cycle, ActBlue — the Democratic side's online donation platform — processed about \$3.8 billion, and its Republican counterpart WinRed about \$1.8 billion. Most of that arrived in increments smaller than a tank of gas, from millions of ordinary people, on phones, often within minutes of an email or a debate moment. Small-dollar giving at this scale is genuinely new in American history — and it means the questions in this lesson are no longer questions for the wealthy. If you have ever chipped in \$25, you are a participant in the system the first two lessons mapped. The remaining question is whether you participate deliberately.

This is not a step to skip, and it is not a sales pitch. SuperCitizen won't tell you whom to support and won't move your money. What it can do is show you how to look before you give.

## 1. Know which channel you're giving into

The same \$100 behaves very differently depending on where it lands — you now have the full map from Lesson 1:

- To a **candidate's campaign committee**: capped at \$3,500 per election (2025–26), spent under the candidate's control.
- To a **party committee**: capped (\$44,300 a year to a national party), spent across many races.
- To a **traditional PAC**: capped at \$5,000 a year; the PAC re-gives in \$5,000 chunks.
- To a **Super PAC**: unlimited; spent on independent ads you don't control and the candidate legally can't direct.
- To a **501(c)(4)**: unlimited and undisclosed — and the same opacity that shields you shields everyone else funding it.

And know the disclosure trade your channel makes. Once your giving to any one committee passes **\$200 in a cycle**, your name, address, employer, and occupation become a permanently public, searchable federal record — anyone can look up your political giving on fec.gov, and data firms routinely do. Below \$200 you are in the unitemized totals. A 501(c)(4) discloses nothing about you, which is exactly why Lesson 1 called it [[term:dark-money]]. None of these gifts, anywhere on the list, is tax-deductible (IRS Publication 529) — a "donation" to politics is not charity, whatever the email implies.

<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> A public-school teacher gives \$15 a month through a donation platform to a Senate campaign for a full year. What ends up in the public record, and why?</summary>
<p>\$180 stays below the \$200 itemization threshold — but a twelfth month's gift or one extra \$25 chip-in pushes the cycle total over \$200, and then her <strong>name, address, employer, and occupation</strong> are itemized in the campaign's FEC filings, permanently and searchably. Neither outcome is wrong — but it should be chosen, not discovered. (Recurring gifts are how most donors cross thresholds without noticing.)</p>

## 2. Follow the money before you add to it

Before giving, see who already funds the candidate or group — the disclosure system exists for you, but only works if someone looks:

- **FEC.gov data** is the official record. Search the committee, read its actual receipts and expenditures, and verify any claim an ad makes about "who funds" anyone before repeating it.
- **OpenSecrets** layers analysis on those filings: industry totals, top donors, outside spending in the race, and each group's disclosure status.

Two specific things to check. First, *which committee is actually asking*: fundraising emails routinely come from PACs with candidate-sounding names, and a "Re-elect Smith Victory Alliance" may share only a vendor with Smith. The fine print at the bottom of the email — "Paid for by…" — names the real committee; look it up before you assume. Second, *the funding picture versus the story*: a candidate running as the scourge of an industry whose top donors work in that industry has some explaining to do, and you can see it yourself in an evening.

## 3. Check the record, not the ads

Money research tells you who's invested. It doesn't tell you whether the candidate is any good. For that:

| You want to know… | Start with | What you'll find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How they actually voted | **Vote Smart** | Roll-call votes, public statements, interest-group ratings across the spectrum |
| What the race or measure is | **Ballotpedia** | Neutral profiles of candidates, districts, and ballot questions |
| Who funds them | **OpenSecrets / FEC** | Itemized donors, industries, outside spending |
| What the law lets them promise | This pack | The channel and limit architecture |

The goal isn't to reach a particular conclusion. It's to give from the record instead of from a 30-second spot — or from the most manipulable instrument ever aimed at a donor, the fundraising email.

## 4. Recognize the pressure machinery

Online fundraising is engineered, and the engineering is documented: countdown clocks pegged to arbitrary "deadlines," subject lines simulating personal mail, claims of "500% matches" that no law verifies, and — the practice that drew the most scrutiny after 2020 — **pre-checked recurring boxes** that quietly convert a one-time gift into monthly withdrawals. None of this is illegal. All of it is designed to convert arousal into transactions before reflection arrives. Two mechanical defenses: read the "Paid for by" line, and uncheck the recurring box unless recurring is what you decided beforehand. The same skepticism applies to groups with civic-sounding names that appear from nowhere around a hot issue — Lesson 1's pipeline explains how [[term:astroturfing]] is financed, and OpenSecrets is usually the fastest way to see whether "Neighbors for Honest Government" has any neighbors in it.

> **Think it through:** "Triple match active for the next 2 hours!" — who would have to be putting up the matching money, through which of the five channels, and what would the FEC filings show if the match were real?

## 5. The scholar's lens: the small-donor paradox

It is tempting to treat small-dollar giving as the obvious cure for big money — millions of \$25 donors as the democratic answer to the \$290 million donor. The political-science literature complicates the comfort. Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner (*Campaign Finance and Political Polarization*, 2015) argue that the donors most motivated to give — at any dollar level — are ideological "purists," so a system that channels money through individual donors rather than parties systematically advantages the most polarizing candidates; the viral fundraisers of both parties' 2024 cycles were rarely their moderates. Ansolabehere, de Figueiredo, and Snyder's older finding (2003) cuts the other way and is oddly liberating: most giving is best understood as **participation — consumption, not investment**. People give the way they cheer. On that view your \$25 will not buy policy, and was never going to; its honest function is expressive, which is an argument for giving on your values and your homework rather than your adrenaline.

Both things can be true: your individual gift is expressive, and the aggregate architecture of millions of expressive gifts still shapes who runs, who wins, and how they behave. That tension is unresolved in the literature — hold it honestly.

<details class="checkpoint">
<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> If La Raja and Schaffner are right that individual-donor money advantages "purists," does that argue for or against your giving \$25 to a candidate you've researched and believe in?</summary>
<p>Neither — it argues for the <em>researched</em> part. Their claim is about the system-level tilt of donor-driven finance, not the ethics of any one gift; your considered \$25 doesn't become polarizing because other people give impulsively. What the finding does counsel is awareness: ask whether you're giving because the candidate matches your values and record-reading, or because an email made you angry at the optimal moment. The first is participation; the second is being a purchased input.</p>
</details>

## 6. Decide for yourself

SuperCitizen is nonpartisan by design. We don't host a "give to this candidate" button, we don't rank or recommend recipients, and we take no cut of anything. Political contributions are yours to make — directly, through the recipient or a registered platform you choose, informed by what you found above.

If you want to support nonpartisan civic education *itself*, you can support SuperCitizen at [supercitizen.org](https://supercitizen.org) — the home of our nonprofit mission. That keeps this work free and independent.

## What this lesson is not

It is not a verdict on whether the small-donor revolution is good for American politics — the La Raja–Schaffner thesis and its critics are a live scholarly fight, and this pack's job was to arm you for it, not settle it. It is also not the constitutional argument: whether the channels *should* exist in their current shape is exactly what the [[term:citizens-united]] pack puts on trial. What it should leave you with is a habit: channel, disclosure, record — checked in that order, every time, before the money moves.
