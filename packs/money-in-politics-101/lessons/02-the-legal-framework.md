# The legal framework — Buckley, FECA, BCRA, Citizens United, SpeechNow

The shape of money in politics today is the product of five legal events spanning four decades. Knowing what each one did and did not do is the prerequisite to having any opinion about whether the current system is good.

## 1. FECA (1971, amended 1974)

The **Federal Election Campaign Act** created the modern federal regime. After Watergate, Congress passed substantial amendments in 1974 that:

- Limited individual contributions to candidates and committees.
- Limited campaign expenditures (including by candidates from their own funds).
- Limited "independent expenditures" — money spent advocating for or against a candidate without coordinating.
- Created the **Federal Election Commission (FEC)** to administer the law.

FECA, as amended, is the statutory backbone. Most subsequent litigation is about whether specific limits in FECA survive constitutional review.

## 2. *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) — the foundational distinction

Buckley is the most important campaign-finance case in U.S. history. The Supreme Court drew a line that has held, with refinement, ever since:

- **Contribution limits are constitutional.** A cap on what any one donor can give to a candidate or committee is a permissible regulation. The government's interest in preventing *quid pro quo corruption* (or its appearance) outweighs the speech burden, because giving money to someone else to spend is a less direct form of expression.
- **Independent expenditure limits are NOT constitutional.** A cap on what a person spends *independently* — buying their own ads, putting up their own lawn signs, running their own newspaper editorial — is a direct restriction on political speech and fails First Amendment review.
- **Self-funding limits are NOT constitutional.** A candidate spending their own money on their own campaign is also direct speech.

Buckley sets the frame: **contributing is regulable; spending independently is not.** Everything that follows is downstream of this distinction.

## 3. BCRA / McCain-Feingold (2002)

The **Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act** of 2002 tightened FECA in two main ways:

- **Banned "soft money"** — unlimited donations to political parties, which had grown to be a major loophole.
- **Restricted "electioneering communications"** — ads naming a federal candidate within 60 days of a general election (30 days of a primary), funded from corporate or union treasuries.

BCRA passed with bipartisan support. Most of it was upheld in *McConnell v. FEC* (2003). The electioneering-communications rule for corporations and unions is what *Citizens United* later struck.

## 4. *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010)

The case that's commonly invoked but often misunderstood. What Citizens United did:

- **Held BCRA's ban on independent campaign expenditures by corporations and unions unconstitutional.** Corporations and unions, like individuals, have a First Amendment right to spend independently on political speech.
- **Affirmed the disclosure regime.** The Court explicitly preserved the requirement that political ads disclose their funders.

What Citizens United did *not* do:

- It **did not** strike contribution limits to candidates or PACs — those remain.
- It **did not** create Super PACs directly. (The next case did.)
- It **did not** legalize anonymous political spending. (That comes from a separate IRS rule about 501(c)(4) donor disclosure.)

The 5–4 majority is associated with the politically right side of the Court; the four dissenters argued that corporate political speech threatens the integrity of the electoral process in ways the majority underweighted. Both sides claim Buckley supports their view.

## 5. *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (D.C. Cir. 2010)

The case that **actually created Super PACs** — decided two months after *Citizens United* by the D.C. Circuit (not the Supreme Court). The court held:

- If contributing to a committee that makes only *independent* expenditures cannot corrupt a candidate (per Buckley + Citizens United logic), then **contribution limits to such committees are unconstitutional**.

The result: a new vehicle that can take unlimited sums from anyone (individual, corporation, union) and spend unlimited sums on independent advocacy. The FEC declined to appeal. Super PACs — formally "independent expenditure-only committees" — went live for the 2010 cycle.

## Where the live debate is

Two genuine, consequential disagreements remain among legal scholars and the public:

1. **Was the Buckley line drawn in the right place?** Some argue the contribution / independent-expenditure distinction was always artificial and that *all* election-related spending implicates the same speech interests. Others argue that direct giving to a candidate is qualitatively different and that Buckley got it right but that subsequent cases (Citizens United, McCutcheon) eroded the contribution-side regulation too aggressively.

2. **Should disclosure of 501(c)(4) donors be required?** Citizens United explicitly relied on disclosure as a partial cure for the speech freedoms it expanded. The IRS rule that lets 501(c)(4)s keep donors anonymous is not constitutionally compelled — it's a regulatory choice. Reform proposals (the DISCLOSE Act and successors) periodically attempt to close this gap. Opponents argue forced disclosure chills donor privacy and can be weaponized.

These are the questions to take to a contested-policy Socratic session — neither side is silly, and the Constitution genuinely permits the policy to go either way.
