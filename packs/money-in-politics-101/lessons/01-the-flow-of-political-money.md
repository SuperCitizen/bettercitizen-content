# The flow of political money — who can give what to whom

Most arguments about "money in politics" go off the rails because the people having them are not arguing about the same channel. There are four legally distinct vehicles a dollar can flow through, each with different rules, different disclosure requirements, and different effects on a campaign.

This lesson maps the four. The next lesson covers the law that drew the lines.

## 1. Candidate campaign committees

The dollar a candidate puts in their own ad on TV came through a **campaign committee** — every federal candidate is required to register one with the FEC.

- **Who can give:** individual U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents; PACs; party committees. Not corporations, not unions, not foreign nationals.
- **Per-cycle limits (federal, current schedule):** an individual can give a candidate **\$3,500 per election** (primary and general count separately, so \$7,000 total). PACs can give **\$5,000 per election**. Limits are indexed and adjust each cycle — see the FEC source for the current table.
- **Disclosure:** itemized donor names, addresses, employers, and occupations are reported to the FEC for any donor giving \$200+ aggregate.
- **What it can spend on:** anything that promotes the candidate, *coordinated* with the candidate.

## 2. Political Action Committees (PACs)

A **PAC** ("traditional PAC" or "non-connected PAC") is a separate committee that raises money from individuals and gives it to candidates and parties.

- **Who can give:** individuals (\$5,000/yr aggregate to a PAC).
- **What it can spend:** \$5,000 per election to a candidate, \$15,000/yr to a national party.
- **Disclosure:** same itemized reporting as campaign committees.
- **The catch:** a PAC's contribution power is much smaller than its independent expenditure power. So most "PAC influence" stories you read are usually about Super PACs.

## 3. Super PACs (independent-expenditure-only committees)

The **Super PAC** is the post-2010 vehicle. It can raise *unlimited* sums from individuals, corporations, and unions. It can spend unlimited sums on independent advocacy. It cannot give money directly to a candidate or coordinate with one.

- **Who can give:** individuals, corporations, unions, other PACs — no aggregate limit.
- **What it can spend on:** ads, mailers, organizing — independent of the candidate's campaign.
- **Disclosure:** itemized donors disclosed to the FEC.
- **The legal source:** *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (D.C. Cir. 2010), decided in the wake of *Citizens United*. We cover the legal pathway in the next lesson.

## 4. 501(c)(4) social-welfare organizations ("dark money")

A **501(c)(4)** is an IRS classification, not an FEC classification. It's a tax-exempt nonprofit whose primary purpose must be social welfare, but which is allowed to spend on political activity as a *secondary* purpose.

- **Who can give:** individuals, corporations, unions — no aggregate limit.
- **Disclosure:** **donors are NOT publicly disclosed** under current IRS rules. This is why this channel is called "dark money" — the public can see what was spent, but not who funded it.
- **What it can spend on:** issue advocacy, electioneering communications, voter mobilization. Direct campaign expenditures are limited by IRS rules but the line is often litigated.
- **Why it grew:** the post-Citizens United legal landscape made independent expenditure unlimited, and the IRS rule on 501(c)(4) donor disclosure created a legal pathway for unlimited *and* anonymous political spending.

## A worked example

You read a news story that says "a billionaire spent \$50 million to influence the Senate race in [state]." Where did that money go?

- If \$3,500 of it went to the candidate's campaign committee, that's the contribution limit per election.
- If \$5,000 of it went to a candidate-aligned traditional PAC, that's the cap for *that* vehicle.
- If \$50 million went to a Super PAC running ads supporting the candidate without coordinating, that's legal under *SpeechNow* — donors itemized at FEC.
- If \$50 million went to a 501(c)(4) running "issue" ads in the closing weeks, that's also legal under current law — and the donor's name is *not* disclosed.

Most of the story you're reading is almost certainly about channels 3 or 4. The argument about whether channels 3 and 4 should exist in their current form is the live policy question. The next lesson covers the law that put them in place.
