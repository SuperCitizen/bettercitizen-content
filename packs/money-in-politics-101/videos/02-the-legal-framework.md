## Two sentences from 1907

In 1905, investigators in New York discovered that the great life-insurance companies had been quietly diverting policyholders' premiums into Republican campaign coffers. President Theodore Roosevelt demanded that Congress ban the practice.

The result, in 1907, was the Tillman Act: the first federal campaign-finance law. It barred corporations from contributing to federal candidates. It has never been repealed, and it still binds.

- The Tillman Act, 1907: still in force

Everything since has been an argument about what else the Constitution lets Congress do.

## The distinction everything rests on

In 1974, after Watergate, Congress capped political money at every point: what people could give, and what anyone could spend. Two years later, in Buckley versus Valeo, the Supreme Court cut that in half.

Limits on what one donor may give a candidate survive. They guard against quid pro quo corruption: dollars exchanged for official acts.

- Contribution limits: survive

Limits on what a person spends independently, and on what candidates spend of their own money, fall. If the spending is not coordinated with the candidate, the Court reasoned, there is no deal to strike.

- Spending limits: fall

> Contributing is regulable. Spending independently is not.

## 2010: the one-two punch

In January 2010, in Citizens United, the Supreme Court held that corporations and unions may make unlimited independent expenditures. The Tillman Act's ban on direct contributions stayed intact.

- Citizens United: freed the spending

Two months later, the D.C. Circuit, a circuit court and not the Supreme Court, took the next step. If independent spending cannot corrupt, then contributions to a group that does nothing else cannot be capped either. That case is SpeechNow, and its creature is the Super PAC.

- SpeechNow: freed the fundraising

> Citizens United freed the spending. A circuit court freed the fundraising.

## What kind of corruption counts?

The whole line of cases turns on one choice in Buckley: corruption means quid pro quo. Lawrence Lessig names what that definition misses: dependence corruption, a Congress not bribed, but bent toward the people who fund campaigns. Critics counter that the idea is too elastic to be a standard for judges.

- Quid pro quo, or dependence?

The lesson does not settle it. It gives the full ledger: what each decision struck and what it left standing, from the 2002 reform act to the case on party spending still pending as of mid-2026.
