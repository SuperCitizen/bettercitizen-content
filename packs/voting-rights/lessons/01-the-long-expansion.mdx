# The long expansion: how the franchise grew

In 1965, 6.7 percent of voting-age Black citizens in Mississippi were registered to vote. Not 67 — six point seven, a figure later entered into the Supreme Court's own record. That number existed *ninety-five years after* the Constitution was amended to declare that the right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged … on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Hold both facts in your head at once, because together they teach the central lesson of this pack: in America, the right to vote has never been a single grant. It is a long, unfinished argument — expanded by amendment, hollowed out by state law and administrative practice, and re-secured, when it was re-secured at all, by statute and litigation. This lesson walks the expansion. The next one walks the enforcement.

## A republic of property holders

The Constitution of 1787 never says who may vote. Article I ties the House electorate to whatever each state requires for "the most numerous Branch of the State Legislature" — and the states, at the founding, mostly required the same thing: be white, be male, and own property or pay taxes above a threshold. The theory, inherited from England, was that only men with a "stake in society" possessed the independence to vote freely; tenants and laborers, dependent on landlords and employers, could be coerced.

That theory collapsed under two generations of pressure. New states entering the union competed for settlers by offering broad white manhood [[term:suffrage]]; older states, embarrassed by comparison and pushed by Jeffersonian and then Jacksonian movements, rewrote their constitutions. By 1828 most states had dropped the property requirement; North Carolina, the last holdout, abandoned it in 1856. On the eve of the Civil War, the property-holder's republic was gone — for white men.

The expansion had a shadow, and naming it matters for everything that follows: several states that democratized the franchise for white men in those same decades wrote racial exclusions into their constitutions explicitly, narrowing or eliminating the Black male franchise that had existed in places at the founding. Alexander Keyssar's *The Right to Vote* (2000), the standard history, presses this point hard — the American franchise has never moved in one direction. Expansion and contraction run together, often in the same document.

## The 15th Amendment and the half-century workaround

The Civil War settled slavery; [[term:reconstruction]] tried to settle citizenship. The third of the [[term:civil-war-amendments]], ratified in 1870, is one sentence: the right of citizens to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Under federal protection, Black men registered, voted, and won office across the South.

Then federal protection left, and the South discovered the amendment's structural weakness. The 15th Amendment is a *negative* command — it forbids denying the vote *on account of race*. It does not grant anyone a vote, and it says nothing about devices that never mention race. Beginning in 1890, Southern constitutional conventions built the [[term:jim-crow]] electoral order on exactly that gap:

| Device | How it worked on paper | How it worked in practice | What finally killed it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Poll tax | A small annual fee to vote, race-neutral on its face | Cumulative back taxes and selective collection priced out Black voters (and many poor whites) | 24th Amendment (1964, federal elections); *Harper* (1966, state elections) |
| Literacy / "understanding" test | Read and interpret a constitutional passage | The registrar chose the passage and graded the answer — discretion did the discriminating | Suspended by the Voting Rights Act of 1965 |
| Grandfather clause | Literacy test waived for those entitled to vote before 1866 — and their descendants | Exempted illiterate whites; left Black applicants to face the test alone | *Guinn v. United States* (1915) |
| White primary | The Democratic Party, as a "private" club, limited its primary to whites | In a one-party South, the primary *was* the election | *Smith v. Allwright* (1944) |

Read the right-hand column closely, because it teaches the tempo of this fight. *Guinn* struck Oklahoma's grandfather clause in 1915 as an obvious end-run around the 15th Amendment — but the Court left the literacy test itself standing as "independently legal," and registrar discretion ground on for another fifty years. The white primary took three decades of litigation: in *Grovey v. Townsend* (1935) the Court accepted the "private club" theory; in *Smith v. Allwright* (1944) it reversed itself, holding that when a state builds its election machinery around a party primary, the party's racial exclusion *is* state action. Each victory closed one door; the system opened another. [[term:disenfranchisement]] was not a law that could be struck down once. It was an administrative practice, renewable indefinitely.

<Checkpoint question="Mississippi's literacy test never mentioned race, and most Black registrants it rejected were never told a racial reason. Why couldn't the 15th Amendment — on its own — stop it?">
  Because the amendment forbids denial *on account of race*, and a facially neutral test administered with registrar discretion forces every rejected applicant to prove discriminatory administration case by case, registrar by registrar. The device's power was discretion, not text. That's why the eventual fix (next lesson) didn't argue with the tests — the Voting Rights Act of 1965 simply suspended them and made covered states ask permission before inventing replacements.
</Checkpoint>

## Citizenship is not a ballot

The 19th Amendment (1920) used the 15th's exact grammar — the vote "shall not be denied or abridged … on account of sex" — and roughly doubled the eligible electorate at a stroke. But its grammar also inherited the 15th's limits. A Black woman in 1921 Mississippi faced the same poll tax, the same literacy test, and the same registrar as her husband; the 19th Amendment removed *sex* as a lawful ground of denial and left every other device intact.

Native Americans illustrate the same principle from a different angle. Roughly 12,000 Native Americans served in the U.S. Army in World War I, yet a large share of Native people were not even citizens. The Snyder Act — the Indian Citizenship Act of June 2, 1924, named for Representative Homer P. Snyder — granted citizenship to all Native Americans born in the United States. It did not grant a single vote, because voter qualifications belonged to the states — and some states, using devices like "Indians not taxed" clauses and reservation-residency rules, kept Native citizens from the polls for another generation. The Library of Congress's account is blunt: some states barred Native Americans from voting until 1957.

> **Why this matters:** Citizenship, in the American design, is federal; the franchise is mostly state. Every group in this lesson discovered that gap — freedmen after 1870, women after 1920, Native Americans after 1924. Whenever you hear a modern proposal framed as "only citizens should vote" or "all citizens can vote," the first analytical question is the same one: *who administers the rule, and what discretion do they hold?*

## The poll tax dies twice

By the early 1960s the poll tax survived in a handful of Southern states, and killing it took two separate instruments, two years apart — a fact that makes an excellent test of whether you understand American federalism.

The **24th Amendment** (ratified 1964) abolished the poll tax in *federal* elections only: primaries and elections for President, Vice President, presidential electors, senators, and representatives. Its drafters chose the narrow path deliberately, betting that a federal-elections-only amendment could actually be ratified. That left states free to tax the vote in elections for governor, state legislature, and everything else local.

The **Supreme Court** closed that half two years later. In *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections* (1966), the Court held that *any* poll tax in *any* election violates the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment: "a State violates the Equal Protection Clause … whenever it makes the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard." *Harper* overruled *Breedlove v. Suttles* (1937), which had blessed the poll tax three decades earlier — a reminder that constitutional meaning here moved not by amendment but by the Court changing its mind.

## Old enough to fight

The last great expansion ran on a war and a deadline. With 18-year-olds being drafted for Vietnam, "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" moved Congress to lower the voting age to 18 by statute in the 1970 Voting Rights Act extensions. In *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970), a fractured Supreme Court held that Congress could do that for *federal* elections but not for *state* ones — threatening every state with two sets of voter rolls, two ballots, and an administrative nightmare for the 1972 election.

Congress answered with the 26th Amendment: citizens eighteen or older shall not be denied the vote "on account of age." Proposed in March 1971 — the Senate voted 94–0, the House 401–19 — it was ratified by July 1, 1971: under four months, the fastest ratification of any constitutional amendment. Notice the pattern repeating one last time: the same negative grammar ("shall not be denied … on account of"), adopted because the affirmative alternative — a federal right to vote — was never on the table.

The timeline rewards a second look: every *date on it* is an expansion, and nearly every *gap between dates* contains a contraction — 1890s disenfranchisement after 1870, the white primary after *Guinn*, state barriers after 1924. The rail reads like steady progress only if you skip the spaces.

<Checkpoint question="After Oregon v. Mitchell (1970), an 18-year-old in many states could vote for President but not for governor. Why was that legally possible — and what does the fix tell you about where the franchise lives?">
  Because Congress's statute lowering the voting age was upheld only for *federal* elections; state qualifications remained the states' to set, so a state could keep 21 for its own offices. The fix had to be a constitutional amendment — the 26th — because nothing short of one can override state control of state-election qualifications. Same lesson as the poll tax: the franchise lives in state law except where the Constitution carves out a specific prohibited ground.
</Checkpoint>

## What's settled here — and what isn't

Settled: the arc of formal eligibility. Property tests are gone (by the 1850s for white men), and race (1870), sex (1920), wealth (1964 and 1966), and age above eighteen (1971) are constitutionally prohibited grounds for denying the vote. Citizenship without the franchise — the freedmen's, Black women's, Native Americans' experience — is the recurring American pattern, and the case law that ended each device (*Guinn*, *Smith*, *Harper*) is good law.

Not settled — and the live argument of the rest of this pack: the Constitution still contains **no affirmative right to vote**. Every voting amendment is a negative command forbidding particular *grounds* of denial, which leaves the machinery — registration, rolls, IDs, deadlines, polling places — in state hands, where discretion has always lived. Whether today's facially neutral rules are sensible administration or the modern descendants of the literacy test's discretion is precisely what divides the ballot-access expander from the election-integrity conservative, and this lesson has *not* answered it. What history shows is narrower but firm: paper guarantees without enforcement machinery failed for ninety-five years. The machinery Congress finally built in 1965 — what it did, and what the Supreme Court has since unbuilt — is the next lesson. Modern debates about [[term:voter-suppression]] make no sense without it.
