Selma, Alabama. March 7, 1965. Some six hundred peaceful marchers reach the foot of the Edmund Pettus Bridge. State troopers are waiting for them. And in front of television cameras, the troopers beat them.

[pause]

Five months later, on August 6, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act.

By the end of that year, roughly 250,000 new Black voters were on the rolls in the South. A third of them had been registered by federal examiners.

[pause]

Last time, we ended on a number. Ninety-five years of constitutional guarantee had moved registration in Mississippi to 6.7 percent. Now one statute moved it more in five months than the Fifteenth Amendment had in a generation.

How? [beat] To answer that, look at the Act the way an engineer would.

> The Voting Rights Act was not a declaration of rights. It was a machine.

And every part of the machine existed because some earlier remedy had failed.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to name the parts, say what each one did, and say exactly which part the Supreme Court removed in 2013.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the machine: three parts, and how they locked together.

Second, whether it worked.

Third, how the Court switched it off, and what still runs.

And last, the argument over building it again.

## The machine

Start with the failure the designers had studied.

In the decade before 1965, voting discrimination was fought one lawsuit at a time, under the civil rights acts of 1957 and 1960. It was a losing race. The Department of Justice would spend years proving that one county's registrar discriminated. It would win. And then it would watch the county adopt a new device.

The courts came to see it as a game of cat and mouse. [beat] A game the cat always lost.

So the 1965 Act went after the tempo of the problem, with three parts that interlocked.

Part one is Section Two. It is the nationwide, permanent piece. It bans any voting qualification or practice that denies or abridges the right to vote on account of race. It never expires. And it is enforced the old way: someone sues, and a court decides. Think of Section Two as the Fifteenth Amendment, with a right to sue attached.

Part two is Section Four, and it did two jobs. It suspended literacy tests, and similar tests or devices, in the places where they were doing the damage. And it defined which places those were. That definition is the famous coverage formula.

The formula had two conditions. A state, or a part of a state, was covered if it kept a test or device as of November 1, 1964. And if its registration or its turnout was below 50 percent in that year's presidential election.

Notice what the formula left out. It required no finding of bad intent. It was deliberately mechanical: the numbers themselves identified the suspects.

Part three is Section Five, and it had no precedent in American law. Its name is preclearance.

Here is the rule. A covered jurisdiction could not enforce any change to its voting rules until federal authorities had cleared it. Not a redistricting plan. Not an annexation. Not the move of a single polling place.

Walk through that last one. A covered county wants to move a polling place.

First, the change is frozen. It cannot be enforced at all, so no new device gets even one election as a head start.

Then the county submits the change, in most cases to the Attorney General, who has 60 days to object. And the county carries the burden. It must show that the change has neither the purpose nor the effect of denying or abridging the vote on account of race.

If no objection comes within the 60 days, the change takes effect. If one comes, the change is blocked. Full stop.

[pause]

Now see the reversal, because it is what makes Section Five remarkable. In an ordinary lawsuit, the challenger carries the burden of proof, and review comes after the rule is already in force. Under preclearance, the jurisdiction carried the burden, and review came first.

> Preclearance flipped two things: who carries the burden, and when review happens.

The cat finally moved first.

## Did it work?

That is the machine. Now, did it work?

This is the rare statute whose effects you can look up in a table. The table comes from the Supreme Court's own opinions, in a case we are about to reach, and the majority and the dissent both treated its numbers as common ground.

Take Mississippi. In 1965, Black registration stood at 6.7 percent. In 2004, it stood at 76.1 percent.

[pause]

What the justices disputed is what those numbers mean. The majority took them as mission accomplished. The dissent took them as the machine working exactly as designed, and as a reason not to switch it off.

Two more numbers complete the picture, and they point in opposite directions, depending on your theory of the case.

The first is the Attorney General's objection rate. In the Act's first decade, it was 14.2 percent of the changes submitted. In the decade before 2006, it was 0.16 percent. [beat] A small fraction of one percent.

The second number. Even at that low rate, preclearance blocked more than 700 voting changes between 1982 and 2006. And in 2006, Congress renewed the Act for 25 years, by lopsided bipartisan margins.

So here is the hinge of everything that follows. Is a 0.16 percent objection rate a cured disease, or a working vaccine?

[pause]

Every argument in the rest of this lecture is a position on that question.

Let's work on it. A skeptic says: by 2006, the Justice Department objected to almost nothing, so clearly preclearance was no longer doing any work. What is the strongest reply, using only the numbers you have just heard? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The reply is that deterrence does not show up in an objection rate. Even at 0.16 percent, more than 700 changes were blocked, changes that would otherwise have governed real elections. And no one can count the changes that were never proposed, because preclearance existed.

But the reply does not settle it either. The registration table is consistent with both readings. A cured disease and a working vaccine produce the same chart. And that ambiguity is precisely what the justices divided over in 2013.

## The formula falls

The case is Shelby County v. Holder. Shelby County, Alabama, sued to have preclearance declared unconstitutional, and in 2013 it mostly succeeded. But be careful with the word mostly.

The Court divided five to four. And it did not strike down Section Five. [beat] It struck down the coverage formula, in Section Four.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the majority. His logic began from one sentence: the Act "imposes current burdens and must be justified by current needs." The formula, he reasoned, failed that test. It was keyed to election data from 1964, 1968 and 1972, and Congress had left it untouched in the 2006 renewal. So it singled out nine fully covered states, and parts of others, on the basis of a forty-year-old snapshot. And that, for the majority, was in tension with the states' "equal sovereignty."

[pause]

Current burdens. Current needs.

Justice Ginsburg wrote the dissent. She answered with the theory of deterrence, and with the most quoted line in modern voting-rights law. Listen for the image in it.

"Throwing out preclearance when it has worked and is continuing to work to stop discriminatory changes is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet."

[pause]

An umbrella, in a rainstorm.

The majority reasoned from the age of the formula. The dissent reasoned from deterrence. Keep both, because the argument over rebuilding still runs on those two lines.

Now the result. Be precise about it.

> Shelby County struck the formula, and only the formula.

What that leaves is a machine with its ignition removed. Section Five remains on the books. It is fully constitutional, and fully inert. With no formula, no jurisdiction is covered. And where no one is covered, nothing has to be precleared.

The Court explicitly invited Congress to write a new formula, one "based on current conditions." [beat] Congress has not.

So since 2013, every voting change, everywhere in the country, takes effect immediately. And challengers carry the burden of suing afterward. It is the posture from before 1965, with better statutes.

Test that. After Shelby County, a city with a long record of voting discrimination redraws its council districts. Plaintiffs believe the new map dilutes minority votes. What is their legal position? Think it through.

[long pause]

The map takes effect. The plaintiffs must sue under Section Two, and they bear the burden of proving it unlawful.

The map is not frozen, whatever the city's record: with no formula, Section Five binds no one. And the plaintiffs are not left without a remedy: Shelby County did not strike down the Voting Rights Act, and Section Two survives in full. But it works the old way. The map governs elections while they litigate.

So 2013 did not change whether a remedy exists. It changed the burden, and the timing.

## What Section Two can still do

With preclearance dormant, Section Two carries the whole load. And since 2013, the Supreme Court has marked out its edges, in two directions.

The first case narrowed it. Brnovich v. Democratic National Committee, in 2021, concerned vote denial: challenges to the rules for casting and counting ballots. Arizona discarded ballots cast in the wrong precinct, and it made it a crime for most third parties to collect mail ballots. The Court upheld both rules, six to three.

The majority did not announce a complete test. It listed guideposts instead. Among them: the size of the burden, where the "usual burdens of voting" do not count. The size of the racial disparity. And the strength of state interests, such as preventing fraud.

The practical message: a bare statistical disparity, plus inconvenience, will rarely be enough.

The second case went the other way, and it surprised nearly everyone. Allen v. Milligan, in 2023, concerned vote dilution: the drawing of district lines.

Alabama is roughly a quarter Black. Its congressional map had seven districts, and one of them had a Black majority. Defending that map, Alabama asked the Court to remake this part of the law around a "race-neutral benchmark."

The Court refused, five to four. The opinion was by Chief Justice Roberts, the author of Shelby County. It reaffirmed the framework of an earlier case, called Gingles, which had governed for 37 years. And it affirmed the order that Alabama draw a second district where Black voters could elect a candidate of their choice.

Districting claims under Section Two, the Court said, remain exactly as strong as they were. No stronger. [beat] But no weaker.

Hold the two cases side by side, and the law after 2013 comes into focus.

> Section Two today: strong on district lines, weaker on procedures, always after the fact.

Against discriminatory district lines, Section Two remains a serious weapon. Against discriminatory voting procedures, it is a much weaker one. And in every case, the remedy arrives only after the contested rule has already governed elections. Lawsuits take years. Elections happen on schedule.

Now pull something back from earlier. I said preclearance flipped two things. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Who carries the burden. And when review happens.

Those two explain a puzzle. Compare an objection under Section Five in 1980 with a victory under Section Two in 2026. The legal outcome is identical: the practice is blocked. Yet the later victory may matter less to the people who won it.

The objection stopped the practice before any election was held under it. The lawsuit runs while the practice is in force. By the final judgment, officials may have been elected under rules later held unlawful, and no court can rerun those elections.

The same outcome on paper, and a very different remedy in practice. That asymmetry is the core of the argument for restoration, and that argument is our last step.

## Should the machine be rebuilt?

Begin on ground nobody disputes. The Court struck a formula, and invited a new one. So the proposals to rebuild answer Shelby County on its own terms: if the fault was a stale formula, write a fresh one.

The chief proposal is the John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act. It would tie coverage to recent voting-rights violations, ones that have been decided in court, on a rolling basis. A jurisdiction would earn coverage by its current conduct, and age out of it with a clean record. The bill passed the House in 2021. It was blocked in the Senate, by the filibuster. It has been reintroduced since.

Two camps argue over it: restorationists, and skeptics. Restorationists want to protect review before the election. Skeptics want to protect a state's power to enforce its own laws without asking first. And the debate between them divides on three honest questions.

Question one. Is the rainstorm over?

Restorationists argue that it is not. They point to the wave of state voting legislation after 2013. And they point to the asymmetry you just worked through.

> The restorationist case: a remedy that comes after the election comes too late.

Skeptics argue that it largely is. They point to registration and turnout in the formerly covered states, where the gaps have largely closed. That is the Shelby County majority's own table. And they point to Milligan, as proof that Section Two still has teeth where it matters most.

[pause]

Question two. Can it ever be the constitutional norm for a state to need permission in advance?

Skeptics argue that it cannot. Even a formula built on current conditions makes states ask federal permission to enforce their own laws. The Shelby County majority called that extraordinary medicine, appropriate only for extraordinary conditions.

> The skeptic case: federal permission is extraordinary medicine, for extraordinary conditions.

Restorationists argue that the Fifteenth Amendment already answers this. It has an enforcement clause, and that clause exists precisely because ordinary remedies failed for a century. That is the last lecture, in one sentence.

[pause]

Question three belongs to neither camp. It is a design problem. Can a formula based on violations be both effective and stable?

A formula triggered by violations proved in court is only as fast as litigation itself. And slow litigation is the very weakness that preclearance existed to fix. Make the trigger stricter, and it invites the same objection about staleness, on a delay.

One exercise before we gather up, and it is the one question I will not answer. Take the camp you find less convincing, restorationist or skeptic. State its case as well as its best advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Voting Rights Act was a machine, with three parts. Section Two: a nationwide ban, enforced by lawsuit. Section Four: the coverage formula. Section Five: preclearance.

[beat]

Two. Preclearance flipped two things: who carries the burden, and when review happens. A covered jurisdiction had to prove its change clean before the change took effect.

[beat]

Three. The registration numbers are common ground. Black registration in Mississippi rose from 6.7 percent to 76.1. What divided the Court was what those numbers mean: a cured disease, or a working vaccine.

[beat]

Four. Shelby County struck the formula, and only the formula. Section Five is still law, and it is inert. Since 2013, a change takes effect first, and challengers sue afterward.

[beat]

Five. Section Two today: strong on district lines, after Milligan. Weaker on procedures, after Brnovich. And always after the fact.

[pause]

Here is what is settled, and what is not.

The mechanics and the record are settled: the design of the Act, its measured effect, and what the three cases held.

What is not settled is everything that depends on how you read a 0.16 percent objection rate. Whether deterrence was doing invisible work in 2013. Whether a lawsuit under Section Two is an adequate substitute for review before the fact. And whether any new formula can satisfy both "current needs" and the demand for speed.

On those questions, the umbrella and the principle of equal sovereignty simply collide.

And we have not yet touched the rules being fought over now, jurisdiction by jurisdiction: registration systems, the maintenance of the rolls, ID, proof of citizenship. In that fight, every position claims this history as its own. The next lecture gives each of them its turn: the ballot-access expander, the election-integrity conservative, and the state-administration pragmatist.

For now, go back to Selma, and to what was signed five months after the bridge. Not a declaration. A machine. [beat] Its parts are still on the books, with the ignition removed.

Whether to start it again is the argument.
