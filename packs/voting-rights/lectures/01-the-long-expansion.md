Mississippi, 1965. Take every Black citizen of voting age in the state, and ask how many of them are registered to vote.

[beat]

The answer is 6.7 percent.

Not 67. [beat] Six point seven. That figure was later entered into the Supreme Court's own record.

[pause]

Now set a second fact beside it. Ninety-five years earlier, the Constitution had been amended to declare that the right to vote shall not be denied "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

Ninety-five years of that sentence. [beat] And 6.7 percent.

[pause]

Hold both facts in your head at once, because together they teach the central point of this whole series.

> The right to vote in America has never been a single grant.

It is a long, unfinished argument. It was expanded by amendment. It was hollowed out by state law and by administrative practice. And it was secured again, when it was secured at all, by statute and by lawsuit.

Our subject is the franchise: the right to vote, and who holds it. By the end of this lecture, you should be able to walk its expansion from the founding to 1971. And you should be able to explain how a guarantee can sit in the Constitution for most of a century without reaching the people it names.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the republic of property holders, and how it ended.

Second, the Fifteenth Amendment, and the workaround that was built in its gap.

Third, why citizenship is not a ballot.

And last, two fixes that each had to be made twice.

## A republic of property holders

Start with what the Constitution of 1787 says about who may vote. [beat] It never says.

Article One ties the voters for the House to whatever each state requires for the most numerous branch of its own legislature. So the states decided. And at the founding, they mostly required the same thing: be white, be male, and own property, or pay taxes above a threshold.

The theory was inherited from England. Only a man with a "stake in society" had the independence to vote freely. A tenant depended on his landlord, and a laborer depended on his employer, and a man who depends on someone can be coerced.

That theory collapsed, under two generations of pressure. New states entering the union competed for settlers, and one thing they offered was a broad vote for white men. The older states were embarrassed by the comparison. Jeffersonian movements pushed them, and then Jacksonian ones, and they rewrote their constitutions.

By 1828, most states had dropped the property requirement. North Carolina was the last holdout. It gave way in 1856.

So on the eve of the Civil War, the property holder's republic was gone. [beat] For white men.

[pause]

Because the expansion had a shadow, and naming it matters for everything that follows.

In those same decades, several of the states that opened the vote to white men wrote racial exclusions into their constitutions, explicitly. Black men had been able to vote in some places at the founding. These states narrowed that vote, or eliminated it.

Alexander Keyssar wrote the standard history of this subject, a book called The Right to Vote, and he presses this point hard. The American franchise has never moved in one direction. Expansion and contraction run together. [beat] Often in the same document.

Keep that pattern in mind. We are about to watch it repeat.

## The Fifteenth Amendment, and the workaround

The Civil War settled slavery. Reconstruction tried to settle citizenship.

The Fifteenth Amendment was ratified in 1870, and it is one sentence long. The right of citizens to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude."

[pause]

For a time, it worked. Under federal protection, Black men registered, voted, and won office across the South.

Then federal protection left. And the South discovered a weakness in the way the amendment is built.

Listen to its grammar again. Shall not be denied, on account of race. That is a negative command. It forbids denying the vote for one particular reason. It does not hand a vote to anyone. And it says nothing at all about a device that never mentions race.

> The Fifteenth Amendment forbids one ground of denial. It grants no one a vote.

Beginning in 1890, Southern constitutional conventions built an entire electoral order on exactly that gap. It is the order we call Jim Crow. It ran on four devices, and every one of them looked neutral on paper.

One: the poll tax. On paper, it was a small yearly fee to vote, the same for everyone. In practice, back taxes piled up, and collection was selective, and together they priced out Black voters, along with many poor white ones.

Two: the literacy test. On paper, you were asked to read a passage of the constitution and interpret it. In practice, the registrar chose the passage, and the registrar graded the answer.

Three: the grandfather clause. On paper, the literacy test was waived for anyone entitled to vote before 1866, and for his descendants. In practice, that exempted white men who could not read, and it left Black applicants to face the test alone.

Four: the white primary. The Democratic Party called itself a private club, and it limited its primary to white voters. And in a one-party South, the primary was the election.

[pause]

Now watch how slowly those devices fell, because the tempo is the point.

In 1915, in a case called Guinn, the Supreme Court struck down Oklahoma's grandfather clause as an obvious end-run around the Fifteenth Amendment. But the Court left the literacy test itself standing. The test, it said, was "independently legal." And the registrar's discretion ground on for another fifty years.

The white primary took three decades of lawsuits. In 1935, the Court accepted the private club theory. In 1944, in Smith v. Allwright, it reversed itself. When a state builds its election machinery around a party primary, the Court held, the party's racial exclusion is the state's own action.

Each victory closed one door. [beat] And the system opened another.

There is a word for what that system did. Disenfranchisement: taking the vote away. And disenfranchisement was not a law that could be struck down once. It was an administrative practice, and it could be renewed indefinitely.

Let's test that. Mississippi's literacy test never mentioned race. Most of the Black applicants it rejected were never told a racial reason. So why couldn't the Fifteenth Amendment, on its own, stop it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because the amendment forbids denial on account of race. A test that is neutral on its face, and run at a registrar's discretion, puts the work on the person turned away. Every rejected applicant has to prove discriminatory administration. Case by case. Registrar by registrar.

The power of that device was discretion, not text.

And that is why the eventual fix did not argue with the tests at all. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 suspended them, and it made the covered states ask permission before inventing replacements. That story belongs to the next lecture.

## Citizenship is not a ballot

Here is the turn. The same grammar was used again, and it carried the same limits with it.

The Nineteenth Amendment came in 1920. It borrowed the Fifteenth's exact form: the vote shall not be denied "on account of sex." And at a stroke, it roughly doubled the number of people eligible to vote.

But think about a Black woman in Mississippi in 1921. She faced the same poll tax as her husband. The same literacy test. The same registrar. The Nineteenth Amendment removed sex as a lawful ground for denying the vote, and it left every other device intact.

[pause]

Native Americans show the same principle from a different angle.

Roughly 12,000 Native Americans served in the United States Army in World War One. Yet a large share of Native people were not even citizens.

In 1924, Congress passed the Snyder Act, also called the Indian Citizenship Act. It granted citizenship to all Native Americans born in the United States.

[beat]

It did not grant a single vote.

The reason is the one we keep meeting. The qualifications for voting belonged to the states. And some states kept Native citizens from the polls for another generation, with clauses about "Indians not taxed," and with rules about residence on a reservation. The Library of Congress puts it bluntly: some states barred Native Americans from voting until 1957.

Citizens in 1924. And in some states, no ballot until 1957.

So here is the American design, in two short sentences.

> Citizenship is federal. The franchise is mostly state.

Every group in this lecture discovered that gap. Freedmen, after 1870. Women, after 1920. Native Americans, after 1924.

And the gap hands you a tool. Sooner or later you will hear a modern proposal framed as "only citizens should vote," or as "all citizens can vote." Whichever it is, the first question to ask is the same.

> Ask who administers the rule, and what discretion they hold.

## Two fixes, each made twice

Last step. Two more barriers came down in the 1960s and the 1970s: wealth, and age. Each of them took two separate instruments. And the reason is the gap we have just named.

Begin with the poll tax. By the early 1960s, it survived in a handful of Southern states.

The Twenty-Fourth Amendment was ratified in 1964. It abolished the poll tax in federal elections only: primaries and elections for president, for vice president, and for Congress. Its drafters chose that narrow path deliberately. They were betting that an amendment limited to federal elections could actually be ratified.

And that left the states free to tax the vote in everything else: elections for governor, for the state legislature, for every local office.

So test yourself. It is 1965. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment is in force, and Virginia still has a poll tax. Is that tax unconstitutional everywhere, lawful everywhere, or something in between? Think it through.

[long pause]

Something in between. Virginia's poll tax was unconstitutional in federal elections, and still lawful in state and local ones. The amendment reached only half of the ballot.

The Supreme Court closed the other half, two years later. The case was Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections, in 1966. The Court held that any poll tax, in any election, violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. In the Court's words, a state violates that clause "whenever it makes the affluence of the voter or payment of any fee an electoral standard."

[pause]

To get there, Harper had to overrule a decision from 1937, one that had blessed the poll tax. So on this half of the question, the Constitution's meaning did not move by amendment. It moved because the Court changed its mind.

> The poll tax died twice: once for federal elections, once for state ones.

Two instruments. Two years. Two halves of federalism.

Now the last great expansion. It ran on a war, and on a deadline.

Eighteen-year-olds were being drafted for Vietnam, and the slogan was "old enough to fight, old enough to vote." In 1970, Congress lowered the voting age to 18 by statute, as part of that year's extension of the Voting Rights Act.

Then came a case called Oregon v. Mitchell, in the same year. A fractured Supreme Court held that Congress could lower the age for federal elections, but not for state ones.

Picture what that meant. In many states, an 18-year-old could vote for president, and could not vote for governor. Every state was threatened with two sets of voter rolls, two ballots, and an administrative nightmare, with the 1972 election on its way.

It is the poll tax story over again. The qualifications for a state's own elections were the state's to set, so a state could keep 21 for its own offices. And nothing short of a constitutional amendment can override that.

So Congress answered with one: the Twenty-Sixth. It was proposed in March 1971. The Senate voted 94 to 0. And it was ratified by July 1, in under four months, the fastest ratification of any constitutional amendment.

Here is what it says. Citizens 18 or older shall not be denied the vote "on account of age."

[beat]

On account of. You have heard that grammar twice already in this lecture. Can you name the two amendments, and the ground each one removed?

[long pause]

The Fifteenth, in 1870: on account of race. The Nineteenth, in 1920: on account of sex. And now the Twenty-Sixth: on account of age.

The same negative grammar, one last time. And it was adopted for a reason. The affirmative alternative, a federal right to vote, was never on the table.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The franchise has never moved in one direction. Property tests for white men were gone by 1856. And in those same decades, several states wrote Black voters out.

[beat]

Two. The Fifteenth Amendment forbids one ground of denial. It grants no one a vote. So the poll tax, the literacy test, the grandfather clause and the white primary were built in its gap, and their power was discretion, not text.

[beat]

Three. Citizenship is federal. The franchise is mostly state. Women met that gap after 1920, and Native Americans after 1924.

[beat]

Four. The poll tax died twice: once for federal elections, by the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, and once for state ones, in Harper. The voting age took a statute, and then the Twenty-Sixth Amendment.

[beat]

Five. Every voting amendment speaks the same negative grammar: shall not be denied, on account of. And when a rule looks neutral, ask who administers it, and what discretion they hold.

[pause]

One more way to hold all of it. Set this lecture's dates on a line, from 1856 to 1971. Every date on that line is an expansion. And nearly every space between two dates holds a contraction: the Jim Crow devices after 1870, the white primary after Guinn, the state barriers after 1924. The line looks like steady progress only if you skip the spaces.

## What is settled, and what is not

Some of this is settled. The arc of formal eligibility is settled: property tests are gone, and race, sex, wealth, and age above 18 are grounds on which the Constitution forbids denying the vote. The cases that ended each device are good law.

Here is what is not settled. It is the open argument of the next two lectures, and it starts from one structural fact.

> The Constitution still contains no affirmative right to vote.

Every voting amendment forbids a particular ground of denial. That leaves the machinery in the hands of the states: registration, rolls, IDs, deadlines, polling places. And the machinery is where discretion has always lived.

So what are today's rules, the ones that are neutral on their face? There are two readings, and this lecture has not chosen between them.

> One reading: today's neutral rules are sensible administration.

> The other reading: today's neutral rules descend from the literacy test's discretion.

That is precisely what divides the ballot-access expander from the election-integrity conservative. Those are two of the three positions this series will hear out. The third is the state-administration pragmatist.

Before we close, one exercise, and it is the only question I will leave unanswered. Take whichever of those two readings you find less convincing. Make its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

The history you have just heard does not choose between those readings. What it does show is narrower, but it is firm. Paper guarantees, without machinery to enforce them, failed for ninety-five years.

In 1965, Congress finally built that machinery. What it did, and what the Supreme Court has since unbuilt, is the next lecture.

For now, go back to Mississippi. One sentence, added to the Constitution in 1870. [beat] And ninety-five years later, 6.7 percent.

You know now how both of those could be true at once.
