November 2024. A presidential election has just been held, and the Census Bureau surveys the country and reports two numbers.

Of all the citizens old enough to vote, 73.6 percent were registered.

[beat]

And 65.3 percent voted.

[pause]

Now run the subtraction, twice. Between registered and voted, the gap is about eight points. Between eligible and registered, the gap is more than twenty-six.

[beat]

More than twenty-six points. That is upwards of sixty million adult citizens who were not on the rolls at all.

[pause]

Last time we ended on the rules now being fought over, jurisdiction by jurisdiction. This is that fight. And it is where the franchise actually lives now.

> The franchise now lives in the pipeline between you and a counted ballot.

Not in whether you may vote. In the administrative steps that come after: registration, the maintenance of the lists, the rules for proving who you are.

And every fight in this lecture is, underneath, the same fight. The system is 50 states and thousands of counties, so errors are inevitable. The question is which direction of error you prefer, and who pays for it.

By the end, you should be able to state the rules on four fronts, and each of three positions on them, the way its smartest advocate would.

We will take it in four steps.

First, getting on the rolls.

Second, staying on them.

Third, two fights over eligibility: voting after a felony conviction, and proof of citizenship.

And last, the three positions, each with its turn.

## Getting on the rolls

Among democracies, the United States is unusual in making registration chiefly the voter's job, and not the state's. That structural choice dates to the 19th century, and it explains much of that sixty-million gap.

The modern floor was built in 1993, by the National Voter Registration Act, often called the motor voter law. States must offer registration at driver's-license agencies, and by a standard federal form sent in by mail. Remember that federal form. It comes back.

The argument today is over what sits on top of that floor, and it comes in two main models.

The first is automatic voter registration, and it flips the default. Under the older design, an eligible citizen at the DMV is asked to opt in. Under the automatic design, the agency registers that citizen, unless the citizen opts out. Some states ask on the spot. Others mail a notice afterward, and a person who ignores the notice stays registered.

By the count of the National Conference of State Legislatures, about half the states, plus the District of Columbia, had some version as of 2025.

Nobody is compelled to register. The argument is entirely about defaults, and about decades of behavioral evidence that defaults do enormous, quiet work.

The second model is same-day registration, and it attacks the deadline instead. As of 2026, 24 states and the District of Columbia let an eligible citizen register and vote in one visit.

The research consensus, as that same conference summarizes it, is that same-day registration raises turnout by roughly 3 to 7 percentage points. [beat] With an honest caveat: the states that adopted it tended to be high-turnout states already, so the causal share is hard to isolate.

> Automatic registration changes the default. Same-day registration removes the deadline.

Put another way, each design puts the risk of error in a different place. Take the deadline. One reading: it is a safeguard, the buffer in which records get checked. The other reading: it is a trap for movers and first-time voters.

Each model divides the risk between two people: the citizen who did everything late, and the administrator who must verify in real time.

## Staying on the rolls

Registration has a second half. It gets less attention, and it generates more lawsuits: staying registered.

That same 1993 law gives the states two commands here, and they are in deliberate tension. Command one: conduct a "general program" of list maintenance, to remove voters who die or move. Command two: do not remove anyone "by reason of the person's failure to vote."

[pause]

Clean the list. But remove no one for staying home.

For a voter who may have moved, the statute prescribes a slow and specific ritual. First, the state sends a notice card, by mail that can be forwarded. Then it waits. If the voter neither returns the card nor votes through the second federal general election that follows, the registration may be canceled. That wait is roughly four years.

Notice first. [beat] Then waiting.

The fight is over the trigger: what may start that process. Ohio started it for anyone who missed a single two-year cycle. And in 2018, in a case called Husted, the Supreme Court upheld Ohio's process, five to four.

The majority's reading was that the statute forbids removal solely for nonvoting. And Ohio's removals rested on more than that: nonvoting, plus the unreturned notice, plus four more years of silence.

The dissenters took the same word to mean something stricter: nonvoting cannot be the proximate trigger at all. Their reason was that the overwhelming majority of people simply do not return government postcards.

The majority's reading is the law.

> Not voting may start a removal. It may never finish one alone.

Let's test that. A voter misses her second federal election in a row. The day after, the state removes her from the rolls. No notice was ever sent. Is that removal lawful, after Husted? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is not. And it violates the statute under every reading.

The ritual is notice first, then waiting. Here there was no notice, and no waiting period. Nonvoting did all of the work alone. What Husted blessed is narrower: a missed election as the trigger to send the notice.

## The felony patchwork, and proof of citizenship

Third step. Begin with felony convictions. An estimated few million Americans are barred from voting by one. But the word barred hides the real story. [beat] The real story is variance.

The constitutional anchor is a case from 1974, Richardson v. Ramirez. Section Two of the Fourteenth Amendment penalizes a state for abridging the vote, with an exception: "participation in rebellion, or other crime." The Court took that exception as "affirmative sanction."

In plainer words, a state may take the vote away for a crime without violating equal protection.

So the question is purely one of state policy. And the states have answered it every possible way. As of May 2026, the conference of state legislatures sorts them into four groups.

In the first group, the vote is never withdrawn. That is Maine, Vermont, and the District of Columbia. People there vote from prison.

In the second, 23 states restore the vote automatically at release.

In the third, 15 states wait until parole and probation end. Some of them also require that outstanding fines, fees or restitution be paid first. Critics argue that this is a wealth test on the restoration of a right, an echo of poll-tax logic. Supporters argue that it is simply completing the sentence.

In the fourth, 10 states require something more: a waiting period, a petition, or executive clemency.

Now work on a case. Two people are convicted of the same federal felony, and they serve identical sentences. In 2026, one finishes parole in a state that restores the vote on release. The other lives in a state that requires an individual petition for clemency.

One has a ballot. The other has a petition to the governor. What single fact explains how the same Constitution permits both? Think it through.

[long pause]

Richardson v. Ramirez. The Court held the practice affirmatively sanctioned. So there is no federal constitutional floor for restoration, and each state's policy governs its own residents.

> After a felony conviction, the vote is pure state law.

Now the second fight. Voter ID has a series of its own, so we take the newer front: proof of citizenship.

Federal law already requires every registrant to attest to citizenship, under penalty of perjury. The proposal now in play would require documentary proof to register: a passport, or a birth certificate.

The governing precedent is from 2013: Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Arizona. Arizona demanded documents from people registering with the federal form, the one from the 1993 law. The Supreme Court held that it could not.

The reasoning turns on three words. The law commands states to "accept and use" the federal form, and that command, the Court held, preempts Arizona's demand.

Put another way: a state acting alone cannot bolt documentary proof onto the federal form.

But the holding has seams, and today's fight runs along them. It governs the federal form, and federal elections. States retain more room over registrations on their own state forms. And Congress could change the statute itself.

A bill called the SAVE Act would do that, by requiring documentary proof nationwide. It passed the House on April 10, 2025. [beat] It has not become law.

## Three positions

Before the positions, pull something back from the start. I gave you two gaps: one between eligible and registered, one between registered and voted. Which was the larger, and by roughly how much?

[long pause]

Eligible to registered. More than twenty-six points, against about eight. That gap is measured, not asserted.

Now the ground that nobody owns. All three positions claim the history of the last two lectures as their own. None of them defends fraud. None of them defends suppressing turnout. They disagree about default settings, and about the direction of tolerable error.

First, the ballot-access expander. What this advocate is protecting is the eligible citizen's counted ballot.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. History's lesson is that administrative friction is the modern franchise fight. The literacy test was paperwork too. Every hurdle between an eligible citizen and a counted ballot filters out real voters at the margins: a registration deadline, a purge notice, a demand for documents. And the margins are never distributed evenly.

> The ballot-access expander's case: friction is the modern franchise fight.

Now what this advocate points to. Two things.

First, who bears the burden. Movers, renters, the poor, the young, and minority citizens bear it first, and the expander points to the Census registration gap as the evidence.

Second, the fraud that ID rules and citizenship-document rules are aimed at. Documented fraud of that kind is vanishingly rare. So, the expander argues, these rules buy near-zero integrity, at a real cost in participation.

And the expander names a default, the one already used for jury rolls and Selective Service: the state builds the list, automatically and accurately. A citizen's failure to file paperwork on time is never the reason an eligible vote goes uncounted.

[pause]

Second, the election-integrity conservative. What this advocate is protecting is public confidence.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Elections run on public confidence, and confidence is a function of verifiable rules, not of assurances after the fact. Attestation under penalty of perjury is an honor system. And a perjury prosecution, after an illegal ballot is counted, un-rings no bells.

> The election-integrity conservative's case: confidence needs verifiable rules, not assurances afterward.

Now what this advocate points to. Two things.

First, the logic of prevention. Rare fraud, the conservative argues, is an argument for cheap prevention, not against it. We lock doors in low-burglary neighborhoods.

Second, the rolls themselves: millions of entries for the moved and the dead. Inflated rolls are an attack surface, and a drain on confidence, even when they are never exploited. So maintenance of the kind upheld in Husted is hygiene, not suppression.

And the conservative names a standard, the guideposts from Brnovich, which you met last time. The usual burdens of voting are civic responsibilities, not disenfranchisement: producing the ID you need for daily life, registering before a deadline, updating an address. States are entitled to prefer prevention over remediation.

[pause]

Third, the state-administration pragmatist. What this advocate is protecting is a system that is built competently.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Both of the other camps argue about defaults, while the real action is in the data plumbing. Most of what each side wants is compatible, if you build it competently. Most of what each side fears comes from building it badly, on no budget.

> The state-administration pragmatist's case: judge every proposal by its errors in both directions.

Now what this advocate points to. Two things.

First, automatic registration is a tool for list quality. Records verified by the government beat handwritten cards. So, the pragmatist argues, conservatives should love it.

Second, aggressive purges are a machine for generating errors, and integrity advocates should fear them. A false removal of an eligible voter is detected on Election Day, in public, one angry voter at a time.

And the pragmatist names rules. Judge every proposal by its error rates in both directions, not by its slogan. Fund the boring infrastructure that makes both access and integrity cheap, such as data shared between states. And stop legislating in the dark, when measurement is possible.

[pause]

Now put all three to work. A state with automatic registration registers a customer at the DMV. A notice is mailed to him, and he ignores it. He never asked to be registered, and he never votes.

Who bears the cost here, and of what error? Work out what each of the three would say. Take a moment.

[long pause]

The access expander says there is no cost. An extra accurate registration burdens no one, and the default captured an eligible citizen the old system would have missed.

The integrity conservative says the cost is roll inflation. An entry created without an affirmative act is an entry no one is curating, and inflated rolls are where bad things start.

The pragmatist says the real question is data quality. A record from the DMV, with a verified address, is typically cleaner than a handwritten form. So the error being traded is legibility, not eligibility.

[pause]

Notice that nobody disputes the facts. They dispute which error matters.

One exercise, and it is the one question I will not answer. Of those three positions, take the one you find least convincing. State its case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The franchise now lives in the pipeline between you and a counted ballot. In 2024, a quarter of eligible citizens were off the rolls. And every fight over the pipeline asks which direction of error you prefer, and who pays for it.

[beat]

Two. The 1993 registration law is the floor. On top of it, automatic registration changes the default, and same-day registration removes the deadline.

[beat]

Three. Staying on the rolls is notice first, then waiting through two federal general elections. After Husted, not voting may start a removal. It may never finish one alone.

[beat]

Four. After a felony conviction, the vote is pure state law, and the states fall into four groups. And a state acting alone cannot bolt documentary proof of citizenship onto the federal form.

[beat]

Five. Three positions. The ballot-access expander's case: friction is the modern franchise fight. The election-integrity conservative's case: confidence needs verifiable rules, not assurances afterward. The state-administration pragmatist's case: judge every proposal by its errors in both directions.

[pause]

Here is what is settled, and what is not.

The legal architecture is settled, and so are the descriptive facts: what the three cases held, the four groups of states, the registration gap.

What is not settled is every question of degree that the settled law leaves open. Four of them.

Whether defaults that raise registration also change who governs. The evidence on partisan effects is far thinner than partisans assume.

Whether rules shaped for prevention are worth their cost in participation, when the event they prevent is rare.

Whether a condition of fines and fees is completed punishment, or a wealth test.

And whether Congress will move the line on proof of citizenship that the Arizona case drew.

[pause]

This series closes where it opened, with the structural fact from the first lecture. There is still no affirmative constitutional right to vote. So all of this, the defaults, the purges, the documents, is decided statute by statute, state by state, term by term.

And this lecture leaves you a test for any position, your own included.

A defensible position names the error it tolerates. It names the error it refuses. And it names the evidence that would change its mind.

Go back, then, to the two numbers from the Census. About eight points between registered and voted. More than twenty-six between eligible and registered. [beat] You know now what stands inside that second gap: a registration, a notice card, a document.

And the rules for each of them are still being written.
