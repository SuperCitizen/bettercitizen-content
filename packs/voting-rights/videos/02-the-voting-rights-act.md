## Five months

On March 7, 1965, Alabama state troopers beat some six hundred peaceful marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, in front of television cameras. Five months later, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act. By the end of that year, roughly 250,000 new Black voters were on Southern rolls.

- Selma: March 7, 1965
- Signed: August 6, 1965
- About 250,000 new Black voters

> The Voting Rights Act was not a declaration of rights. It was a machine.

## The cat moves first

Before 1965, the Department of Justice would spend years proving that one county's registrar discriminated, win, and watch the county adopt a new device.

Section 5 of the Act changed the order. It is called preclearance. A covered jurisdiction could not enforce any change to its voting rules until it proved to federal authorities that the change did not deny the vote on account of race.

- The jurisdiction carries the burden
- Review comes before the change

> Preclearance reversed the burden, and it reversed the timing.

## The formula falls

Which places were covered? Section 4(b) answered with a formula, keyed to election data from 1964, 1968 and 1972. In 2013, in Shelby County against Holder, the Supreme Court ruled five to four. It did not strike down Section 5. It struck down the formula.

- Shelby County, 2013: five to four
- Struck: the coverage formula

With no formula, no jurisdiction is covered, so nothing must be precleared. Every voting change now takes effect immediately, and challengers sue afterward.

> Section 5 is still on the books, fully constitutional and fully inert.

## Cured disease, or working vaccine

By the decade before 2006, the Attorney General objected to 0.16 percent of submitted changes, down from 14.2 percent in the Act's first decade.

- Objections: 14.2%, then 0.16%

Chief Justice Roberts, for the majority, wrote that the Act imposes current burdens and must be justified by current needs, and a forty-year-old formula failed that test.

- The majority: current burdens, current needs

Justice Ginsburg, for the dissent, wrote that throwing out preclearance while it is working is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet.

- The dissent: an umbrella in a rainstorm

The lesson has the registration numbers both sides treated as common ground, what the Act's Section 2 can still do after two later cases, and the debate over rebuilding the formula.
