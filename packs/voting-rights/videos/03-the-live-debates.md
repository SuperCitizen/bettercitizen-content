## Sixty million

In November 2024, by the Census Bureau's survey count, 73.6 percent of America's citizen voting-age population was registered to vote, and 65.3 percent voted. The larger gap is the first one. Upwards of sixty million adult citizens were not on the rolls at all.

- Registered: 73.6%
- Voted: 65.3%

Errors are inevitable in a system of 50 states and thousands of counties. So every fight here is the same fight.

> Which direction of error do you prefer, and who pays for it?

## Getting on the rolls

One answer attacks the deadline. 24 states plus D.C. let an eligible citizen register and vote in one visit. Research finds that same-day registration raises turnout by roughly 3 to 7 percentage points. The caveat is that the states adopting it tended to be high-turnout states already, so the causal share is hard to isolate.

- Same-day registration: 24 states + D.C.
- Turnout: roughly 3 to 7 points higher

## Staying on the rolls

Federal law tells states to remove voters who die or move, and not to remove anyone for failing to vote. Ohio started its removal process for anyone who missed a single two-year cycle. It sent a notice, then waited four more years. In 2018, in Husted, the Supreme Court upheld that, five to four. The statute forbids removal solely for nonvoting.

- Husted, 2018: five to four

> Nonvoting may start the removal process. It may not be the only basis.

## Three positions

Ballot-access expanders argue that administrative friction is the modern franchise fight, and that every hurdle filters out real voters at the margins. They would have the state build the list, automatically and accurately.

- Ballot-access expander: the state builds the list

Election-integrity conservatives argue that elections run on public confidence, and that confidence comes from verifiable rules. They would have states prefer prevention over remediation, even when fraud is rare.

- Election-integrity conservative: prevention over remediation

State-administration pragmatists argue that both camps debate defaults while the real action is in data plumbing. They would judge every proposal by its error rates in both directions.

- State-administration pragmatist: error rates, both directions

All three claim the history, and none of them defends fraud or turnout suppression.
