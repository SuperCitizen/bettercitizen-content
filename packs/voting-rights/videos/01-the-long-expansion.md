## Six point seven

In 1965, 6.7 percent of voting-age Black citizens in Mississippi were registered to vote. Not 67. Six point seven. That was ninety-five years after the Constitution was amended to say the vote shall not be denied on account of race.

- 15th Amendment: 1870
- Mississippi, 1965: 6.7% registered

> In America, the right to vote has never been a single grant.

## A negative command

The 15th Amendment forbids denying the vote on account of race. It does not grant anyone a vote, and it says nothing about devices that never mention race. Beginning in 1890, Southern states built an electoral order on exactly that gap.

- Forbids a ground; grants no vote

Take the literacy test. The registrar chose the passage and graded the answer, so discretion did the discriminating. In 1915, in Guinn against United States, the Supreme Court struck Oklahoma's grandfather clause, which had spared whites from the test. It left the test itself standing, and registrar discretion ground on for another fifty years.

- Guinn, 1915: clause struck, test survives

> Each victory closed one door. The system opened another.

## Citizenship is not a ballot

The Snyder Act of 1924 granted citizenship to all Native Americans born in the United States. It did not grant a single vote, because voter qualifications belonged to the states. Some states barred Native Americans from voting until 1957.

- Snyder Act, 1924: citizenship
- Some states barred Native voters until 1957

> Citizenship is federal. The franchise is mostly state.

## The same grammar, every time

Race in 1870. Sex in 1920. The poll tax in federal elections in 1964. Age above eighteen in 1971. Every voting amendment is a negative command, forbidding one ground for denying the vote. The machinery stays in state hands: registration, rolls, IDs, deadlines, polling places.

- Race, sex, poll tax, age
- The machinery stays with the states

> The Constitution still contains no affirmative right to vote.

## What history leaves open

Are today's facially neutral rules sensible administration, or the modern descendants of the literacy test's discretion? That question divides the ballot-access expander from the election-integrity conservative, and the lesson does not answer it. What history shows is narrower: paper guarantees without enforcement machinery failed for ninety-five years.

- Sensible administration, or the old discretion?

The lesson has each device and what finally killed it, the poll tax that had to die twice, and the fastest amendment ever ratified.
