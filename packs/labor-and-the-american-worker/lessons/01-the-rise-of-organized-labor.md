# The rise of organized labor

For most of the colonial and early national period, Americans worked as farmers, independent artisans, household producers, indentured servants, or — for millions of enslaved people — as forced, unpaid labor. Industrialization changed that. As factories and large corporations spread in the nineteenth century, a growing share of Americans became wage workers who sold their time to an employer. Out of that new reality came the American labor movement.

## From craft to wage labor

The independent craftsman who owned his tools and sold his finished goods gradually gave way to the wage earner who tended a machine he did not own and made a product he did not sell. The historian David Montgomery described this as a long contest over control of the workplace, in which skilled workers fought to preserve craft autonomy against employers and managers seeking to set the pace and method of work. As that autonomy eroded, workers turned to organization. Early local craft unions appeared before the Civil War, and the National Labor Union (1866) was an early attempt at a federation, but durable national organization came later.

## The Knights of Labor and the AFL

Two very different models competed in the 1880s. The Knights of Labor, founded in 1869 and led by Terence V. Powderly, sought to unite all "producers" — skilled and unskilled, regardless of trade, and including many women and Black workers — into one broad organization aimed at reforming the wage system itself. The Knights grew explosively to perhaps 700,000 members by 1886, then collapsed nearly as fast.

The rival model proved more durable. The American Federation of Labor (AFL), founded in 1886 under Samuel Gompers, organized skilled workers along craft lines and pursued "pure and simple" unionism — concrete gains in wages, hours, and conditions through collective bargaining rather than sweeping social reform. The AFL's craft approach left most unskilled and immigrant factory workers unorganized for decades.

## Strikes and violence

Labor's struggles in the Gilded Age were often violent. The Haymarket affair of 1886 began as a Chicago rally for the eight-hour day; a bomb killed police officers, and the trial and execution of anarchist defendants set back the eight-hour movement and the Knights. The Homestead strike of 1892 pitted Andrew Carnegie's steel managers, led by Henry Clay Frick, against the Amalgamated Association; an armed battle with Pinkerton guards left people dead and broke the union at the plant. The Pullman strike of 1894, a nationwide railroad boycott led by Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union, was crushed when federal troops intervened and a court injunction jailed Debs. These defeats showed how often courts and government power sided with employers.

## The Triangle fire and workplace safety

On March 25, 1911, a fire at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in New York City killed 146 garment workers, most of them young immigrant women, many trapped behind locked exit doors. The disaster shocked the public and energized reformers and the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union. New York's resulting Factory Investigating Commission produced a wave of fire-safety, building, and labor laws, marking an important step toward government regulation of workplace safety.

## The New Deal labor settlement

The Great Depression and the New Deal transformed labor law. The National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act of 1935 guaranteed most private-sector workers the right to organize, bargain collectively, and strike, and created the National Labor Relations Board to enforce those rights and oversee union elections. Three years later, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established a federal minimum wage (initially 25 cents an hour), required overtime pay above 40 hours per week — helping cement the eight-hour day and the 40-hour standard — and sharply restricted child labor.

This new legal framework coincided with a new kind of union. In 1935 the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) broke from the AFL to organize entire industries — steel, autos, rubber — rather than single crafts. Dramatic sit-down strikes, especially the Flint sit-down against General Motors in 1936-37, forced major manufacturers to recognize industrial unions.

## Peak and decline

Union membership surged, reaching roughly a third of nonfarm wage and salary workers in the mid-1950s, when the AFL and CIO merged in 1955. But the tide turned. The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, passed over President Truman's veto, banned certain tactics, permitted states to enact "right-to-work" laws barring union-security agreements, and otherwise rebalanced the Wagner framework toward employers. Over the following decades, deindustrialization, the shift to services, employer resistance, and globalization eroded the unionized share of the workforce. By the 2020s, Bureau of Labor Statistics data put union membership at around 10 percent of wage and salary workers — and only about 6 percent in the private sector — a long fall from the postwar peak.
