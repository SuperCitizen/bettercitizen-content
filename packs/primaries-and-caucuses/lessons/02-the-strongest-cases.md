# The strongest case for and against

Three positions on what the U.S. nominating process should look like.

## 1. The status-quo defender case

Core argument: the current system, despite complexity, has produced strong nominees and accommodates the federal structure of American politics.

- **Sequential primaries surface candidates organically.** Early small states (Iowa, New Hampshire) allow lesser-known candidates to compete via personal campaigning before huge media markets dominate. A Pete Buttigieg or Mike Huckabee can break through in Iowa; without sequential primaries they couldn't.
- **The party-elite role checks pure plebiscitary danger.** Cohen et al.'s *The Party Decides* documents that party endorsements still substantially shape primary outcomes. This is a feature: pure popularity contests can produce nominees who can't govern (Trump 2016 is debated as evidence either way). Some elite filtering produces accountability.
- **Federalism is preserved.** State parties, state primary rules, and state-level electoral institutions all operate. Replacing this with a national primary would centralize power in Washington and weaken state-level political organization.
- **Voter participation is high.** Modern primary turnout (roughly 30-40% of party registrants in competitive primaries) is substantial. Caucus turnout is lower, but the total voter participation in presidential nominations is the highest it has been in U.S. history.
- **Reformer alternatives have problems.** A national primary day favors well-funded candidates with national name recognition (mostly current officeholders). RCV in primaries is administratively complex. Top-two primaries can produce same-party general elections that disenfranchise minority partisans.

What evidence would change this position? Persuasive evidence that the current system systematically produces unrepresentative nominees, or that the early-state structure produces enough harm to outweigh the benefits.

## 2. The national-primary reformer case

Core argument: the current system is too long, too expensive, and too dependent on early-state idiosyncrasies. A single national primary day (or regional primary structure) would be more representative and democratic.

- **Length and cost favor wealthy candidates.** A 9-month primary campaign costs $100M+. Only candidates with extensive networks or self-funding can afford it. National primary or compressed regional primary cycles would reduce these advantages.
- **Iowa and New Hampshire are unrepresentative.** Both are smaller, whiter, more rural than the country. Their outsized influence shapes which candidates emerge. The Democratic Party's 2024 reforms (replacing Iowa with South Carolina) acknowledged this.
- **Sequential primaries let early-state results unduly bias later voters.** Voters in March states are influenced by January results. A national primary would let voters everywhere weigh in based on the same information at the same time.
- **The cost of complexity.** Different states have different rules, different ballot-access requirements, different dates. The complexity favors candidates with sophisticated legal and organizational infrastructure.
- **Existing institutions can be coordinated.** A national primary day in March or April, regional primary clusters separated by 2-3 weeks (Northeast, South, Midwest, West) — these are administrable proposals that don't require constitutional amendment.

What evidence would change this position? Persuasive evidence that a compressed national primary would produce worse nominees (e.g., favor only well-known incumbents) or undermine state-party organization in ways that hurt overall political participation.

## 3. The ranked-choice / open-primary advocate case

Core argument: the larger problem is not the schedule or geography but the structure of primary voting itself. In safe-seat partisan districts (which most are after gerrymandering), the primary is the real election; current low-turnout primaries dominated by activists produce nominees unrepresentative of the broader electorate.

- **Primary-driven polarization is real.** Brady & Han (2006) and McGhee et al. (2014) document the empirical relationship between primary-driven nominations and Congressional polarization. In safe-seat districts, candidates fear losing primaries to more-extreme challengers, pulling them away from the median voter.
- **Ranked-choice voting would help.** RCV in primaries (used in Maine, Alaska's general-then-RCV system, several local jurisdictions) lets voters express preferences across multiple candidates and reduces the spoiler effect that favors extremists. Empirical evidence is limited but suggestive.
- **Top-two and top-four open primaries reduce partisan capture.** Alaska's "top four with RCV" system (since 2020) elected Mary Peltola (D) in 2022, the first time a Democrat won a statewide Alaska election in decades. The system rewards broad-coalition candidates over narrow-base candidates. California's top-two has produced more competitive general elections in some cases.
- **Independent voters are the missing constituency.** ~40% of U.S. voters identify as independent. Closed primaries (and to a lesser extent semi-closed) exclude them from the most consequential elections in safe-seat districts. Open primaries (in any form) restore their voice.
- **The reforms are state-specific and don't require federal action.** Each state can change its primary structure under existing constitutional authority (subject to party rules). Alaska, California, Washington, and Louisiana have done so without national coordination.

What evidence would change this position? Persuasive evidence that open or RCV primaries reduce political accountability (because nominees no longer need to satisfy active partisans) or that they're administratively too complex to scale.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled:**
- Primary turnout is substantial in competitive races but much lower than general-election turnout.
- Iowa and New Hampshire are demographically unrepresentative of the country.
- Closed primaries exclude independent voters.
- Sequential primaries advantage candidates with early-state organization.

**Genuinely contested:**
- Whether the current system produces nominees who reflect their party's voters or only its activists.
- Whether RCV and top-two primaries actually reduce polarization at scale.
- Whether the costs of a national primary outweigh the benefits.
- Whether party-elite influence is a feature (preventing extremist nominees) or a bug (entrenching insiders).

A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and acknowledges what's contested. Lesson 3 turns to current reform proposals and where the politics is.
