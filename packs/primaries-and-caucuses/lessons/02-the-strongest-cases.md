# The strongest case for and against

In the spring of 2015, the smart money in political science said Donald Trump could not be nominated. The reigning account of presidential nominations — Cohen, Karol, Noel, and Zaller's *The Party Decides* (2008) — had shown that since the 1980s, the candidate who won the "invisible primary" of elite endorsements almost always won the nomination; voters, on this view, mostly ratified a choice the extended party network had already converged on. Trump entered with almost no endorsements, was opposed by nearly every organ of his party, and won anyway. By October 2016 the book's own authors had published a reassessment in *PS: Political Science & Politics*, arguing that new media, early money, and factional warfare had weakened the party's coordinating power. Keep that arc in mind as you read what follows, because every position below is, in part, a theory of *who should decide* — and 2016 is the test case each must explain.

Three positions, each at its strongest. Note as you read that they largely agree on the facts from Lesson 1; they disagree about which facts are decisive.

## 1. The status-quo defender case

Core claim: the current system — sequential contests, mixed rules, substantial-but-not-total party influence — survives every comparison with its real alternatives, as opposed to idealized ones.

- **Sequential voting is a vetting technology.** The scholarly version of this argument is Redlawsk, Tolbert, and Donovan's *Why Iowa?* (2011): early small states force candidates into retail politics that tests stamina and breadth cheaply, and — critically — later voters *learn from earlier results*, correcting first impressions in a way a single national vote never could. The track record of breakthroughs is real: Jimmy Carter in 1976, John McCain's 2008 resurrection, Pete Buttigieg essentially tying for first in Iowa 2020 from a standing start. Under a national primary, every one of them loses to whoever begins with the most money and name recognition.
- **Party influence is a feature, not a scandal.** *The Party Decides* described elite coordination as the system *working*: the extended party — officeholders, activists, allied groups — screens for governing competence and general-election viability in ways low-information primary electorates cannot. The 2016 counterexample cuts both ways here: defenders read Trump's nomination not as proof the system is fine but as a warning of what happens when the party's filter *fails* — an argument for more party influence, not less.
- **Participation is historically high.** The reformed system's primaries drew 28.5 percent of all eligible voters in 2016 (just under the 2008 record of 30.4 percent) — tens of millions of participants in what was, before 1972, a decision made in actual smoke-filled rooms. Judged against its own history rather than against an imagined ideal, the system has never been more open.
- **Federalism does real work.** Fifty state laboratories produced the taxonomy in Lesson 1; parties retain First Amendment associational rights; no single point of failure (or capture) exists. A national primary centralizes all of it.
- **The alternatives have known defects.** A national primary day favors celebrity and self-funding; plurality winners in a ten-candidate national field could claim the nomination with 25 percent; and the runoff or RCV machinery needed to fix *that* imports every controversy from the voting-systems debate.

What would change this position: evidence that the sequential system now systematically nominates candidates their own party's median voter opposes, or that early-state distortion (in who gets vetted at all) outweighs the vetting benefit.

## 2. The national-primary reformer case

Core claim: the calendar is an accident of the 1970s that no one would design on purpose, and its costs — length, expense, early-state veto power, momentum distortion — now exceed whatever vetting it provides.

- **The sequence is arbitrary and unrepresentative.** Iowa and New Hampshire are whiter, older, and more rural than the country; their gatekeeping role was never chosen by anyone. The DNC's own 2024 reordering — South Carolina first, precisely to put Black Democratic voters at the front — concedes the unrepresentativeness critique outright. But reformers note what the reordering did *not* fix: somebody still votes first, and New Hampshire's open defiance showed the parties cannot fully control their own calendar anyway.
- **Momentum is information cascade, not deliberation.** The defender's "later voters learn" is the reformer's "later voters are herded": a few hundred thousand caucus-goers move polls, money, and media for everyone else, and candidates die before 95 percent of the country votes. In 2024 both nominations were clinched by March 12. A national or regional-rotation primary lets all voters decide on the same information.
- **Length and cost select for the wrong traits.** The effective campaign now runs more than a year and costs hundreds of millions; it tests fundraising endurance, not governing ability — and hands disproportionate influence to early money, the very force the *Party Versus Faction* reassessment identified as eroding party coordination.
- **The proposals are administrable.** A single national primary day with a majority requirement (runoff or ranked ballot); or four regional clusters voting at two-to-three-week intervals, rotating which region leads each cycle (the National Association of Secretaries of State has endorsed a rotating regional plan since 1999). None requires a constitutional amendment — only the thing that has actually blocked them, which is that neither national party will unilaterally disarm its early-state arrangements.

What would change this position: evidence that compressed calendars reliably nominate worse candidates (the 25-percent-plurality problem), or that killing retail-politics vetting measurably degrades nominee quality.

> **Think it through:** The defender says later voters *learn* from early states; the reformer says they are *herded* by them. Both describe the identical mechanism — early results changing later votes. What observation, even in principle, could tell learning apart from herding?

## 3. The ranked-choice / open-primary advocate case

Core claim: both other camps are arguing about the *presidential* calendar while the deeper damage is structural and local — in safe seats, the primary **is** the election, and the rules of that election quietly decide who governs.

- **Safe seats make primaries decisive.** Through a combination of [[term:gerrymandering]] and geographic self-sorting, only a few dozen of 435 House districts are competitive in November. Everywhere else, the November winner was actually chosen in a spring primary — typically by a fraction of the electorate. In the 2022 midterms, 21.3 percent of eligible voters participated in primaries (Bipartisan Policy Center), versus about 64 percent [[term:voter-turnout]] in the 2024 general election. The real electorate, for most American offices, is that 21 percent.
- **Primary electorates pull candidates toward the poles — with an honest asterisk.** Brady, Han, and Pope (2007) showed congressional candidates position themselves closer to their primary electorate than to their district's median voter. But the advocate must also own the complication: McGhee, Masket, Shor, Rogers, and McCarty (2014) found that *how open* a state's primary is has little measurable effect on legislator ideology — so simply opening closed primaries, by itself, is weak medicine. The advocate's strongest reading: the problem is the combination of low turnout, partisan filtering, AND plurality voting, which is why the remedy is structural (all-candidate primaries, ranked ballots), not just looser registration rules.
- **The all-candidate model has a real-world record.** Alaska's top-four primary with a ranked general (adopted 2020) elected Mary Peltola in 2022 — the first Alaska Native in Congress, taking a seat Republicans had held for 49 years — by rewarding cross-party second choices; two years later the *same rules* elected Republican Nick Begich III over Peltola. Advocates count both as wins: the system selects coalition-builders rather than favoring a party. Washington and California's top-two systems show moderation effects that are real but modest and entangled with same-cycle reforms (the fairest reading of the evidence, per McGhee and Shor's 2017 assessment).
- **The excluded plurality.** A record 45 percent of American adults identified as independents in Gallup's 2025 averages. Closed primaries exclude them from the decisive round entirely; in safe seats that is exclusion from self-government, full stop.
- **No federal action required.** Every reform in this bucket — top-two, top-four, ranked primaries, [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] generals — has been adopted by state law or citizen initiative, within the constitutional lane *Washington State Grange* opened.

What would change this position: evidence that all-candidate primaries reduce accountability (nominees no longer answerable to a coherent party electorate), that ballot complexity suppresses participation among exactly the marginal voters the reform claims to serve, or continued repeal votes showing the public itself rejects the trade.

## What's settled, what's contested, what's overclaimed

The three camps share more empirical ground than their rhetoric suggests. It helps to sort the load-bearing claims explicitly:

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Primary turnout is far below general-election turnout (21.3% in 2022 vs. about 64% in 2024) | Settled |
| Iowa and New Hampshire are demographically unrepresentative of the country | Settled |
| Closed primaries exclude independents — now the largest identification bloc | Settled |
| Sequential contests advantage candidates with early-state organization and early money | Settled |
| Candidates position toward primary electorates (Brady–Han–Pope) | Strong evidence |
| Party-elite coordination still controls nominations | Contested — *The Party Decides* vs. its authors' own post-2016 reassessment |
| Opening primary *eligibility* reduces polarization | Overclaimed — McGhee et al. (2014) find little effect |
| All-candidate primaries + ranked generals elect more coalition-minded candidates | Suggestive (Alaska, top-two studies); too few cases to settle |
| A national primary would favor celebrity and money | Plausible, untested |

The honest summary: the *facts* of the system are mostly settled; the *weights* are not. Whether party influence is a safeguard or a usurpation, whether 21 percent turnout is a scandal or just a smaller-but-adequate jury, whether Alaska generalizes — those are judgment calls that evidence narrows but cannot close.

A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and names what's contested inside it. Lesson 3 turns to the live reform fights — what has actually passed, failed, and been repealed since 2020 — where each of these arguments meets the voters.
