# How U.S. primaries and caucuses actually work

On the night of January 15, 2024, with wind chills near 35 below zero, about 110,000 Iowans drove through blowing snow to school gyms and church basements — the lowest Republican caucus turnout in a quarter-century — and handed Donald Trump a 30-point win, the largest margin a non-incumbent has ever posted there. Eight days later, New Hampshire held a presidential primary the Democratic National Committee had formally declared "meaningless." The DNC had ordered New Hampshire to vote later; state law commands the secretary of state to schedule the primary first in the nation; New Hampshire obeyed its law and defied the party. President Biden, following party rules, did not put his name on the ballot — and won anyway, with more than 63 percent as a write-in. The state's delegates were stripped, then quietly seated at the convention months later.

Hold both scenes in mind, because together they teach the deepest fact about the American nominating process: it is run by **two sovereigns at once**. Political parties — formally private associations — write the rules for who becomes a nominee. State governments, which answer to their own laws and not to any party, administer most of the voting. When the two cooperate the seams are invisible; when they collide, as in New Hampshire, you can see exactly where party authority ends and state authority begins. Start with the mechanics.

## Primaries vs. caucuses

A **[[term:primary]]** is a state-administered election: a secret ballot at ordinary polling places (or by mail), counted by election officials — the machinery of a [[term:general-election]] asking a different question: not *who holds the office* but *who gets the party's nomination*.

A **[[term:caucus]]** is a party-administered meeting. The classic Democratic form, used in Iowa for half a century: at a fixed hour, neighbors gather in a precinct meeting place and physically sort themselves into corners by candidate preference — the "first alignment." Any candidate group below a **viability threshold** (15 percent of the room in most precincts; 25 percent where only two delegates are at stake; one-sixth where there are three) is declared non-viable, and its members get one chance to **realign**: join a viable group, combine with another non-viable group to reach 15 percent, or go home. Then the final count is converted into county-convention delegates by formula. It is public, time-consuming politics: no secret ballot, and a voter who is sick, working a night shift, or short of child care simply does not vote.

That cost structure explains the long decline. Caucus [[term:voter-turnout]] runs far below primary turnout, and after Iowa's 2020 Democratic count melted down on national television, the migration accelerated. In 2024, Republicans held caucuses in only a handful of states — Iowa, Nevada, Idaho, Missouri, North Dakota, Utah, and Hawaii among them — while Democrats abandoned the in-person caucus almost entirely: Iowa Democrats met on January 15 only to conduct party business, having moved the actual presidential preference to a seven-week mail vote announced on Super Tuesday. Why do any caucuses survive? Control: a caucus is run *by the party, on the party's schedule, under the party's rules*, at the party's expense — which is precisely how Nevada Republicans could hold a binding caucus in 2024 two days after a state-run primary their rules ignored.

<Checkpoint question="Your state party wants maximum participation in its presidential contest, but it also wants to keep control of the date and the rules. Primary or caucus — and what exactly is it trading away with each choice?">
  A **primary** buys participation (secret ballot, all-day voting, mail options, state-funded administration) at the price of control: the legislature sets the date and the rules of access. A **caucus** keeps the party sovereign over timing and procedure — useful when party and legislature disagree, as for Nevada Republicans in 2024 — at the price of mass participation. The 2024 migration shows both parties mostly deciding that participation matters more.
</Checkpoint>

## Who gets to vote: the taxonomy

For every primary, someone must decide which voters may participate — and that decision is made jointly by state law and party rule. The National Conference of State Legislatures, whose classification is the standard reference, sorts the states into seven bins (counts as of its May 2026 update):

| Type | Rule | States (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed (8 states) | Only registered party members | New York, Florida, Pennsylvania, New Jersey |
| Partially closed (9) | Party may choose, each cycle, whether to admit unaffiliated voters | Maryland, Oregon, Connecticut, Utah |
| Open to unaffiliated voters (8) | Party members stay in their party's primary; independents pick one | Massachusetts, North Carolina, Colorado, New Hampshire |
| Partially open (5) | Any voter may cross over, but doing so is recorded or rebinds registration | Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio |
| Open (15) | Any voter picks either party's primary, privately | Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, Vermont |
| Top-two (2) | All candidates on one ballot; top two advance regardless of party | California, Washington |
| Top-four (1) | Same, but four advance — to a ranked-choice general | Alaska |

Louisiana remains its own category: for most offices it skips primaries entirely — every candidate, every party, appears on one ballot, with a runoff if no one clears a majority. (Nebraska, NCSL's other special case, elects its officially nonpartisan legislature the top-two way.) But in 2026 Louisiana began moving its biggest offices — U.S. House and Senate, state supreme court, and two state boards — into closed party primaries open to party members and unaffiliated voters, though not to members of other parties: a major unwinding of the 50-year-old "jungle" system. The first round ran in May 2026 minus its main event — the Supreme Court's April 2026 ruling in *Louisiana v. Callais* voided the state's congressional map, and the governor suspended the U.S. House primaries days before the vote.

The stakes of the taxonomy are straightforward. Closed systems maximize the party's control over its own label and exclude the unaffiliated — now the largest bloc in the country, at a record 45 percent of adults in Gallup's 2025 averages. Open systems enfranchise independents at the price of **crossover voting** — including organized raiding of the other party's primary, much discussed and rarely decisive in the research. Top-two and top-four systems abolish the party primary as such: the first round is just a winnowing election, which is why two Democrats or two Republicans can face each other in November in a lopsided district.

## The constitutional frame: private clubs, public elections

Whether a state *may* impose any of these rules on a party took a century of litigation to settle, and the three landmark cases form one coherent story.

The first is **Smith v. Allwright** (1944). Texas Democrats, calling themselves a private association, barred Black voters from their primary — and in the one-party South, the Democratic primary *was* the election. The Supreme Court, 8–1, demolished the private-club theory: when a state builds its election system around party primaries, the primary becomes **state action**, and the Fifteenth Amendment applies. Lonnie Smith, a Houston dentist, won the right to vote in the only contest that mattered. The case is the floor under everything: parties are private, but their nominating contests turn constitutionally public the moment they are integral to the election.

The ceiling came in **California Democratic Party v. Jones** (2000). California voters had adopted a "blanket" primary — every voter receives every party's candidates and may pick a Democrat for governor, a Republican for senator, line by line. The parties themselves sued, and the Court, 7–2 (Scalia writing), struck it down: forcing a party to let nonmembers choose its *nominee* violates its First Amendment freedom of association. States cannot simply conscript a party's label.

Then the synthesis: **Washington State Grange v. Washington State Republican Party** (2008) upheld Washington's top-two system, 7–2, against the same parties' objections. The trick is that top-two does not nominate anyone: candidates merely state a party *preference*, and the top two advance as individuals, so the party is never forced to associate with anyone. Read the three cases together and you get the modern rule of thumb: states have wide latitude to design election systems *around* parties, and much less latitude to reach *inside* them.

<Checkpoint question="A state passes a law letting every voter vote in every party's primary, office by office. A second state abolishes party primaries and sends the top two finishers from a single all-candidate ballot to the general. Which law survives a First Amendment challenge, and why?">
  The second. The first is a blanket primary — *California Democratic Party v. Jones* (2000) struck exactly that, because it forces a party to let nonmembers pick its nominee. The second is the top-two design upheld in *Washington State Grange* (2008): since no one is nominated and candidates only declare a "preference," the party's associational rights aren't burdened. The constitutional line is not how open the voting is — it's whether the state is commandeering the party's **nomination**.
</Checkpoint>

## Delegate math: how the race actually ends

Presidential nominations are won in **delegates**, not raw votes. Each state contest determines how many of that state's national-convention delegates each candidate receives; a convention majority nominates. The two parties run stage three — allocation — under philosophically different rules, and the difference shapes how every race unfolds:

| | Democratic Party | Republican Party |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Allocation method | Proportional everywhere | State option: proportional, winner-take-all, or hybrids |
| Threshold | 15% (statewide and by district) to win any delegates | States choose, capped at 20% during the early window |
| Timing rule | Same rules all season | March 1–14 contests must be proportional (RNC Rule 16); winner-take-all allowed from March 15; the four February carve-outs set their own |
| Party leaders | About 700 "automatic" delegates (superdelegates) — since the 2018 reform, barred from the first ballot unless the outcome is already mathematically settled | No equivalent bloc; a small number of RNC officials are delegates |
| Effect on races | Slow, grinding accumulation; trailing candidates with 20–30% keep earning delegates | Early proportionality, then a March winner-take-all avalanche that ends races fast |

The Democratic 15 percent threshold does heavy, underappreciated work: a candidate polling 12 percent everywhere wins literally zero delegates while a rival at 35 percent banks them in every state and district. The Republican calendar rule works toward the same end from the other side — once winner-take-all kicks in on March 15, a front-runner can convert 40-percent wins into 100-percent delegate hauls, which is how Trump outran a divided field's combined vote share in 2016. The superdelegate rule is the residue of 2016's other fight: after Sanders supporters objected that party officials had publicly committed to Clinton before anyone voted, the DNC voted in August 2018 to bar automatic delegates from the first nominating ballot unless pledged delegates have already decided the outcome. In 2024 none of this machinery faced stress — both races were effectively over by Super Tuesday, and both nominations were mathematically clinched the same night, March 12.

<Checkpoint question="In a Democratic primary, the statewide vote is: Adams 40%, Baker 34%, Cole 14%, Dunn 12%. Roughly how do the statewide delegates split — and how would the same vote play in a Republican winner-take-all state after March 15?">
  Cole and Dunn, both under 15%, get **nothing**. Adams and Baker split the statewide delegates in proportion to their share of the qualifying vote (40/74 and 34/74 — roughly 54% and 46% of the delegates). In a post-March-15 GOP winner-take-all state, Adams takes **every** delegate with the same 40%. That single contrast explains why Democratic races grind on and Republican races snap shut.
</Checkpoint>

## The calendar: front-loading and the 2024 reordering

None of this math matters equally everywhere, because the contests are *sequential* — and sequence is power. The modern calendar is the unintended product of the **McGovern–Fraser Commission**, created after the catastrophic 1968 Democratic convention in Chicago, where Hubert Humphrey won the nomination without contesting a single primary while police fought protesters outside. The commission's report, *Mandate for Reform* (1971), required state parties to select delegates through open, timely, demonstrably fair processes — and state parties, finding compliance easiest via state-run primaries, stampeded: 17 states held Democratic primaries in 1968, 23 by 1972, and today more than three-quarters of states use primaries. Nobody designed that result — the central argument of Nelson Polsby's *Consequences of Party Reform* (1983) is that the primary-dominated system was the reform's largely unintended byproduct.

Sequence created its own pathologies. Jimmy Carter's 1976 run — an obscure one-term governor camping in Iowa for a year, converting a caucus win into magazine covers and money — taught every candidate since that early states are leverage. States responded by **front-loading**, racing their contests forward, and the parties responded with rules penalizing queue-jumpers. The equilibrium for a generation: Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada, South Carolina, then a March avalanche.

The 2024 cycle broke the equilibrium, on one side. At President Biden's urging, the DNC voted in early 2023 to demote Iowa and New Hampshire — both demographically unrepresentative, one operationally discredited — and lead with South Carolina (February 3), then Nevada and New Hampshire (February 6), Georgia (February 13), and Michigan (February 27). Reality complied only partially: Georgia's Republican secretary of state declined to move the date, and New Hampshire, bound by its first-in-the-nation statute, voted January 23 anyway — the write-in spectacle this lesson opened with. Iowa Democrats threaded the needle by mail; Republicans kept the traditional order entirely. Whether the reordering survives to 2028 is, as of mid-2026, an open question inside the party — the calendar is re-litigated every cycle.

> **Why this matters:** Front-loading means the nomination is usually decided while most states' voters are still waiting their turn — in 2024, before roughly half the country's primaries. Turnout follows the live contest: 28.5 percent of eligible Americans voted in the competitive 2016 primaries (near the 2008 record of 30.4 percent), but participation collapses in states that vote after the race is over.

## Who is actually in charge

Step back and the system's strangest feature comes into focus: no federal law dictates how parties choose presidential nominees, and the Constitution never mentions primaries — or parties. The national committees set the broad rules — calendar windows, allocation formulas, the convention itself — and enforce them not through courts but through **delegate seating**: defy the calendar and your delegation shrinks or vanishes, as New Hampshire Democrats briefly experienced. State legislatures decide whether to run a primary at all, on what date, with which voters eligible. State parties fill the gaps — choosing caucuses over primaries, writing the delegate-selection plans the national party must approve.

The scholar's lens here belongs to V. O. Key Jr., the great mid-century student of American parties. In *American State Politics* (1956), Key examined the direct primary — a Progressive Era invention designed to break boss control — and issued the warning that still organizes the modern debate: primary electorates are small and unrepresentative even of their own party, and in lopsided states the primary quietly *replaces* the general election as the real contest. Both observations were about state politics in the 1950s; both are the live wires in the arguments ahead — whether unrepresentative primary electorates drive polarization, and who should be allowed into the only election that matters in a safe seat.

## What this lesson is not

It is not yet an argument about whether any of this is good. The machinery — caucus mechanics, the taxonomy, the case law, the delegate math, the calendar — is the shared ground. The argument is over what the machinery *does* to American politics, and Lesson 2 steel-mans the three live positions: the **status-quo defender** (sequential contests vet candidates; party influence is a feature; federalism is preserved), the **national-primary reformer** (the calendar is an accident that privileges two small states and front-loaded money), and the **ranked-choice / open-primary advocate** (the deeper defect is *who votes* in primaries and *how*, especially where the primary is the whole election). A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it.
