# How U.S. primaries and caucuses actually work

The presidential nominating process is privately controlled by political parties operating under their own rules, but those rules are administered through state election machinery. The result is one of the most complicated electoral systems in any democracy. Start with the mechanics.

## Primaries vs. caucuses

A **primary** is a state-administered election. Voters go to polling places (or vote by mail), mark a ballot, and the votes are counted by election officials. Most states use primaries.

A **caucus** is a party-administered meeting. Voters gather in person, often at schools or community centers, and physically separate into groups by candidate preference. After multiple rounds and re-shuffling, a final tally is taken. Caucuses generally produce lower turnout than primaries (the time commitment is much higher) and have been declining in use — Iowa's 2024 Democratic-side switch to a primary was the most recent major change.

As of 2024, only a handful of states still use caucuses (Iowa Republicans, Nevada Republicans, North Dakota and Wyoming for both parties). Most states have moved to primaries.

## Open, closed, and semi-closed

State law and party rules together determine who can vote in a given party's primary:

- **Closed primary**: only registered party members can vote. New York, Florida, Pennsylvania, Maryland are examples. Independents and unaffiliated voters are excluded.
- **Open primary**: any registered voter can choose either party's primary on election day. Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, Vermont are examples.
- **Semi-closed**: registered party members can vote in their own party's primary; independents can choose. New Jersey, North Carolina, Massachusetts are examples.
- **Top-two / blanket / "jungle" primary**: all candidates of all parties on a single ballot; top two finishers (regardless of party) advance to general election. California (most offices), Washington State are the major examples. Louisiana has a variant.

The choice has substantial policy effects. Closed primaries give the party more control over who can influence nomination but exclude growing populations of unaffiliated voters. Open primaries allow cross-party voting that, in some cases, can be strategic — voting in the other party's primary to weaken its strongest candidate. Top-two primaries can produce general elections between two same-party candidates if a state is heavily one-party.

## Delegate allocation

The actual presidential nomination is determined by **delegate count**, not raw vote totals. Each state's primary or caucus determines how many delegates from that state go to the national convention pledged to which candidates. The candidate with a majority of delegates at the convention wins the nomination.

The two parties allocate delegates differently:

**Republican Party (since 2020):**
- Most states use winner-take-all or modified winner-take-all (e.g., 50% threshold).
- Some states use proportional allocation.
- The Republican National Committee sets minimum thresholds and broad guidelines; state parties have substantial discretion.

**Democratic Party:**
- Proportional allocation in all states (a candidate must reach 15% in a district or statewide to receive delegates).
- "Superdelegates" (party officials and elected leaders) historically had voting power at the convention; post-2018 reforms restricted them to voting only on the second ballot in contested conventions.

The proportional-vs.-winner-take-all difference matters: Republican primaries can produce decisive winners faster (a 30% lead in a winner-take-all state gives 100% of delegates), while Democratic primaries produce more gradual delegate accumulation.

## "Front-loading" and the early state structure

The order of state primaries matters enormously. The first few states (Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada, South Carolina) shape the field by:
- Producing media attention that affects later voters.
- Demonstrating campaign viability (or its absence).
- Producing the first delegate counts that signal momentum.

States have engaged in "front-loading" — moving their primaries earlier to gain more attention. The result is that by Super Tuesday (early March), often more than half the delegates have been allocated. Candidates who haven't built campaigns ready by January are at significant disadvantage.

The early-state structure has been criticized for several reasons:
- Iowa and New Hampshire are demographically unrepresentative (smaller, whiter, more rural than the country).
- Their disproportionate influence shapes which candidates emerge.
- The compressed front-loaded calendar advantages candidates with money and existing organization.

The Democratic Party reformed its early-state schedule in 2024, replacing Iowa with South Carolina as the first contest. Republicans retained Iowa as first.

## The role of the parties (national vs. state)

The presidential nomination process is governed by **party rules**, not federal election law. The Constitution doesn't specify how parties choose nominees; political parties are formally private associations.

The Republican National Committee and Democratic National Committee set broad rules:
- Delegate-allocation methods.
- Eligibility (who can be a candidate).
- Schedule (which state primaries can happen when).
- Rules of the convention itself.

State parties operate within these rules. They have substantial discretion on specifics — Iowa's caucus structure, for example, was an Iowa Democratic Party choice; the specific delegate-allocation formula in California Democratic primaries is California Democratic Party's choice.

This private-party control distinguishes presidential nomination from general elections. State election boards administer the voting (counting ballots, opening polling places), but the rules they administer come from the parties.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Status-quo defenders** argue the current system, despite its complexity, has produced strong nominees, allows substantial voter participation, and distributes power between national parties and states in a way that respects federalism.
- **National-primary reformers** argue the current system is too long, too expensive, too dependent on early-state idiosyncrasies, and should be replaced by a national primary day or a regional primary structure.
- **Ranked-choice / open-primary advocates** argue the current system, especially in safe-seat partisan districts, eliminates competition by selecting nominees in low-turnout primaries dominated by activists; opening primaries (top-two or top-five) and using ranked-choice voting at the primary stage would produce more representative nominees.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
