# The current reform debate

In December 2024, election workers in Juneau finished a hand recount and certified that Alaska would keep its top-four primary and ranked-choice general election — by 743 votes out of more than 320,000 cast. The system had been adopted in 2020 by a margin under 4,000 votes; it survived repeal in 2024 by a margin under 800; and within a year, opponents had gathered enough signatures to put repeal on the ballot a third time, in November 2026. That is what the American primary-reform debate actually looks like up close: not a tide moving one direction, but trench warfare, fought state by state, initiative by initiative, in margins a strong wind could flip.

This lesson is the scoreboard and the map — what has actually passed, failed, and been repealed since 2020, and which lever controls each kind of change. Bring the three positions from Lesson 2 with you; every entry below is one of them winning or losing a round.

## The three levers

Every proposal in this debate moves through one of three doors, and mistaking the door is the most common analytical error in popular coverage.

**State law (including citizen [[term:ballot-initiative]]s)** controls the structure of the election itself: whether a primary exists, who may vote in it, whether it is partisan or all-candidate, and what counting rule is used. This is where nearly all real action has been — Alaska, California, Washington, Maine, Louisiana, and every state ban all came through this door. The constitutional lane is the one Lesson 1 mapped: states may design systems *around* parties (*Washington State Grange*), but may not hand a party's nomination to nonmembers (*California Democratic Party v. Jones*).

**National party rules** control the presidential calendar and delegate allocation — the DNC's 2024 reordering and superdelegate reform, the RNC's proportionality window. Enforcement is delegate seating, not law, and as New Hampshire demonstrated in 2024, a determined state can simply absorb the penalty.

**Federal law** could, in principle, reach congressional elections through the Elections Clause — and has not. The Voter Choice Act (Senators Bennet and King), which would fund state transitions to [[term:ranked-choice-voting]], and the Fair Representation Act (Representative Beyer), which would mandate RCV and multi-member House districts, are serious drafting exercises with no path to passage as of mid-2026. Presidential nominations are even further from federal reach: a statutory national primary would collide with both party autonomy precedents and state administration of elections.

## The scoreboard, 2020–2026

Here is the record, corrected to mid-2026. It rewards close reading — almost every line cuts against some camp's preferred narrative:

| Year | Jurisdiction | What happened | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2020 | Alaska | Top-four primary + RCV general (Measure 2) | Adopted, 50.55% |
| 2020 | St. Louis | Approval-voting primary + runoff (Prop D) | Adopted, 68% |
| 2022 | Nevada | Top-five primary + RCV general (Question 3), first vote | Passed, 53% |
| 2022 | Florida, Tennessee | Statewide RCV bans (preempting cities) | Enacted |
| 2024 | Nevada | Question 3, required second vote | **Failed** — reform dead |
| 2024 | Alaska | Repeal of top-four + RCV | Failed by 743 votes — system survives |
| 2024 | Missouri | Constitutional ban on RCV (Amendment 7) | Passed, about 68%; St. Louis grandfathered |
| 2024 | Washington, D.C. | Initiative 83: semi-open primaries + RCV | Passed, about 73%; Council funded only the RCV half — first ranked primary June 2026 |
| 2024 | Maine | First RCV *presidential* primary; semi-open rules take effect | Implemented |
| 2024–26 | Louisiana | Closed party primaries (U.S. House/Senate, state supreme court, two boards) replace the jungle system for those offices | First held May 2026 — minus the U.S. House races, suspended after the Supreme Court's April 2026 *Callais* ruling voided the map |
| 2025 | North Dakota | HB 1297 bans approval voting and RCV | Enacted — Fargo's approval voting (adopted 2018, used 2020–24) voided |
| 2026 | Ohio | 19th state RCV ban | Enacted (March) |
| 2026 | Alaska | Third ballot fight on repeal | Qualified for November ballot |

Three honest readings of the table. First, **the all-candidate model is resilient where it exists and stalled where it doesn't**: Alaska's system has twice survived razor-thin statewide votes (adoption in 2020, attempted repeal in 2024), California and Washington's top-two systems are politically settled — but no new state has joined them since 2020, and Nevada's failure killed the movement's flagship expansion. Second, **the ban wave is the bigger structural story by raw count**: nineteen states, nearly all Republican-led, have now preemptively outlawed RCV, often where no jurisdiction was using it — reform has become *coded*, absorbed into partisan identity in a way it was not a decade ago. Third, **the direction is not uniform even within a party coalition**: deep-blue D.C. adopted RCV over its own Democratic Party organization's objection, while Louisiana, under a Republican governor, moved *away* from its open all-comers system toward closed primaries — the rare recent reform in the *closing* direction.

## RCV at the primary stage, specifically

Because this pack is about nominations, isolate that thread. Maine is the working example: ranked ballots have decided its state and federal *primaries* since June 2018, and in March 2024 it ran its first ranked presidential primary, under newly semi-open rules admitting unenrolled voters. Alaska's top-four primary pairs with ranked generals rather than ranked primaries — the winnowing round stays single-choice. A handful of localities rank primary ballots, and Virginia's local-option law has brought RCV to county elections including Arlington's. The 2020 cycle also saw several state Democratic parties use ranked ballots in party-run presidential contests — a reminder that a party can adopt RCV for its own nomination *without any change in state law*, the most underused lever on the board.

The evidence base remains thin and honest advocates say so. Maine's primaries have produced majority nominees without spoiler panics, and exhausted-ballot rates have been modest; whether ranked primaries select more broadly acceptable nominees at scale is exactly the kind of question that needs more states and more cycles. The best-studied adjacent reform — California and Washington's top-two — shows, per McGhee and Shor's careful 2017 assessment, moderation effects that are real but modest in California and weaker in Washington, entangled with same-cycle redistricting changes. Treat anyone claiming dramatic, proven effects (in either direction) as selling something.

## National and regional primary proposals

The boldest reform family is also the most dormant. A single national primary day polls well in the abstract and has been proposed in Congress repeatedly for over a century; rotating regional plans (four clusters voting at intervals, lead rotating each cycle) have carried the National Association of Secretaries of State's endorsement since 1999. Nothing has moved. The obstacles are structural, not rhetorical: a national primary requires either federal legislation that would test the limits of congressional power over what are formally party processes, or coordinated rule changes by two national parties, each of which would be surrendering its early-state vetting machinery in exchange for a system that — as Lesson 2's defender argued — rewards front-runners with money and name recognition. The likeliest path for calendar change remains what actually happened in 2024: incremental, one-party reordering of the early window, re-fought every four years. As of mid-2026, the DNC's 2028 calendar deliberations are again live, with no guarantee South Carolina keeps its new perch.

## What a learner should take away

The reform debate is not one argument but three, running on different clocks. The **counting-rule fight** (RCV, approval, top-N) is fast-moving, state-by-state, and now sharply partisan-coded — expect more bans, more initiative fights, and a decisive Alaska rubber match in November 2026. The **calendar fight** is slow and intra-party, re-opened every cycle, with 2028's Democratic order genuinely unsettled. The **structural fight** over who votes in the decisive round — closed versus open versus all-candidate — moves slowest of all, because it runs through both constitutional doctrine and each party's self-interest, and it just produced movement in *both* directions: D.C. opening, Louisiana closing.

A defensible position on primaries and caucuses therefore needs three components: a view on **whether the current system produces representative nominees** (Lesson 2's central contest), a view on **which reforms would help and at what cost**, and a view on **which lever — state, party, or federal — should be doing the work**, with an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.
