# The current reform debate

Three categories of reform are live: state-by-state primary structure changes, ranked-choice voting adoption, and the long-running but largely dormant national-primary proposals.

## State-by-state changes

Most reform happens at the state level. Recent or active changes:

- **Alaska** (2020 ballot initiative, took effect 2022): top-four open primary with RCV in the general election. Combines two reforms; results so far suggest broader-coalition candidates are advantaged.
- **California** (2010 ballot initiative, in effect since 2012): top-two open primary. Has produced same-party general elections in some heavily-partisan districts.
- **Washington State** (since 2008): top-two open primary, similar to California's.
- **Louisiana**: traditional jungle primary with November runoff if no majority.
- **Maine** (since 2018 for federal offices): RCV. Used in 2018, 2020, 2022 federal races.
- **Nevada** (2022 ballot initiative passed; constitutional amendment requires 2024 second vote, did pass): top-five open primary with RCV in general.

The trend has been toward more open and ranked-choice systems, but the pace is slow and reversals are possible.

## RCV at the primary level

A subset of reform proposals would adopt ranked-choice voting specifically at the primary stage:

- **Maine Democratic primary** has used RCV since 2020.
- **Several local jurisdictions** use RCV in primaries.
- **Federal proposals** (Voter Choice Act, Fair Representation Act) would expand RCV but have not advanced.

Empirical evidence on RCV primaries is limited. Maine's experience suggests it produces broader-coalition candidates and reduces strategic-voting concerns; whether this scales to national elections is contested.

## National primary proposals

Various proposals (Sen. Carl Levin's earlier work, regional-primary plans from the National Association of Secretaries of State, scholarly proposals) would replace the current calendar with:

- **Single national primary day** (early March or June): all states vote simultaneously.
- **Regional primary structure**: 4-5 regional clusters voting at 2-3-week intervals.
- **Rotating regional system**: regions rotate which goes first each cycle.

None has been seriously advanced in Congress. Implementation would require either federal legislation (constitutional questions about state authority over elections) or coordinated party-rule changes (which neither party has been willing to make).

## What the parties are actually doing

The Democratic Party's 2024 calendar reforms (replacing Iowa with South Carolina as first contest) is the most-significant party-level reform in years. The Republican Party retained its traditional schedule.

State parties are making smaller adjustments — adding RCV in some primaries, adjusting delegate-allocation thresholds, changing eligibility rules. These are reform-in-incrementalism that doesn't capture media attention but accumulates over time.

## What's likely next

- **More state-level top-N + RCV combinations.** The Alaska model (top-four open primary + RCV general) may spread to other states. Nevada's 2024 vote was important; results elsewhere depend on ballot-initiative activity.
- **DNC/RNC calendar tweaks.** Periodic adjustments to the early-state structure are likely; major restructuring is not.
- **Federal RCV legislation** is theoretically on the agenda (Sanders, Khanna proposals) but has not advanced.
- **Defensive litigation.** Where states have passed reforms, opponents have litigated. Florida's recent attempts to nullify RCV adoption are an example. Expect more.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on primaries and caucuses requires:

1. A view on **whether the current system produces representative nominees** (the central empirical question).
2. A view on **what reforms (if any) would help** — and at what cost.
3. A view on **how to balance national consistency, state experimentation, and party autonomy**.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
