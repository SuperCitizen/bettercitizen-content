January 15, 2024. Iowa, after dark. The wind chill is near 35 below zero.

About 110,000 Iowans drive through blowing snow, to school gyms and church basements. It is the lowest turnout for a Republican caucus in a quarter-century. And they hand Donald Trump a 30-point win.

[pause]

Eight days later, New Hampshire holds a presidential primary. And the Democratic National Committee has formally declared that primary "meaningless."

The national party had ordered New Hampshire to vote later. But state law commands the secretary of state to schedule the primary first in the nation. So New Hampshire obeyed its law, and defied the party.

President Biden followed the party's rules, and kept his name off the ballot. [beat] He won anyway, as a write-in, with more than 63 percent.

The party stripped the state's delegates. Months later, it quietly seated them at the convention.

[pause]

Hold both scenes in mind. Together they teach the deepest fact about how America chooses its nominees.

> The nominating process is run by two sovereigns at once.

The first sovereign is the party. Political parties are formally private associations, and they write the rules for who becomes a nominee. The second sovereign is the state. State governments answer to their own laws, not to any party, and they administer most of the voting.

When the two cooperate, the seams are invisible. When they collide, as they did in New Hampshire, you can see exactly where party authority ends and state authority begins.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to follow a nomination from the first contest to the convention, and say who is in charge at each step. We will take it in four stops.

First, the two kinds of contest: the primary and the caucus.

Second, who gets to vote, and how far a state may go in telling a party who that is.

Third, the delegate math, which decides how a race ends.

And last, the calendar, because the order in which the states vote is a form of power.

## Primary and caucus

Start with the primary, which is an election run by the state. You vote by secret ballot, at an ordinary polling place or by mail, and election officials count the result.

It is the machinery of a general election, asking a different question. Not who holds the office. [beat] Who gets the party's nomination.

A caucus is something else: a meeting run by the party.

Picture the classic Democratic form, which Iowa used for half a century. At a fixed hour, neighbors gather in a precinct meeting place. Then they physically sort themselves into corners of the room, by the candidate they prefer. That sorting is called the first alignment.

Next comes a test. In most precincts, any group below 15 percent of the room is declared non-viable. That line is called the viability threshold.

The members of a non-viable group get one chance to realign. They can join a viable group. They can combine with another small group, to reach the threshold together. Or they can go home. Only then is the final count converted into delegates.

[pause]

Notice what kind of politics that is. It is public and time-consuming, with no secret ballot. And a voter who is sick, or working a night shift, or short of child care, simply does not vote.

That cost explains a long decline. Caucus turnout runs far below primary turnout. By 2024, Republicans held caucuses in only a handful of states, and Democrats had abandoned the in-person caucus almost entirely.

So why does any caucus survive? [beat] Control.

A caucus is run by the party, on the party's schedule, under the party's rules, at the party's expense. That is how Nevada Republicans could hold a binding caucus in 2024, two days after a state-run primary that their rules ignored.

Let's test that. Suppose your state party wants the most participation it can get in its presidential contest. But it also wants to keep control of the date and the rules. Primary, or caucus? And what is it trading away with each choice? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Each choice has a price. A primary buys participation: a secret ballot, voting all day, options by mail, and the state paying to run it. The price is control, because the legislature sets the date and the rules of access.

A caucus keeps the party sovereign over timing and procedure. The price is mass participation.

> A primary buys participation. A caucus keeps control.

And in 2024, both parties mostly decided that participation matters more.

## Who gets to vote

That is how the voting happens. Now, who is allowed in?

For every primary, someone has to decide which voters may take part. That decision is made jointly, by state law and by party rule. [beat] Two sovereigns again.

The National Conference of State Legislatures keeps the standard classification, and it sorts the states into seven types. You do not need to memorize all seven. You need the direction they run in: from closed, to open, to systems that leave the party primary behind.

The first type is closed: only registered party members may vote. New York and Florida work this way.

[beat]

Next, partially closed: the party itself may choose, each cycle, whether to admit unaffiliated voters.

[beat]

Third, open to unaffiliated voters: party members stay in their own party's primary, and independents pick one. That is New Hampshire's rule.

[beat]

Fourth, partially open: any voter may cross over, but the crossing is recorded, or it changes the voter's registration.

[beat]

And fifth, open: any voter picks either party's primary, and picks privately.

[pause]

The last two types are different in kind. In a top-two system, all the candidates appear on one ballot, and the top two advance, regardless of party. California and Washington use it. Alaska uses top-four, where four candidates advance to a ranked-choice general election.

Here is what is at stake.

Closed systems give a party the most control over its own label. They also exclude the unaffiliated, who are now the largest bloc in the country. In Gallup's averages for 2025, they were a record 45 percent of adults.

Open systems give independents a vote. The price is crossover voting, which includes organized raiding of the other party's primary. In the research, raiding is much discussed, and rarely decisive.

And top-two and top-four abolish the party primary as such. The first round is just a winnowing election. That is why, in a lopsided district, two Democrats or two Republicans can face each other in November.

## Private clubs, public elections

Now a harder question. A party is a private association. May a state impose rules like these on it at all?

Settling that took a century of litigation, and three landmark cases tell one story. Think of them as a floor, a ceiling, and the room in between.

The floor is a case from 1944, Smith v. Allwright.

Texas Democrats, calling themselves a private association, barred Black voters from their primary. And in the one-party South, the Democratic primary was the election.

The Supreme Court rejected the private-club theory, eight to one. When a state builds its election system around party primaries, the Court held, the primary becomes state action, and the Fifteenth Amendment applies.

That case is the floor under everything else. Parties are private, but their nominating contests turn constitutionally public the moment they are integral to the election.

[pause]

The ceiling came in 2000, in California Democratic Party v. Jones.

California's voters had adopted a blanket primary. Every voter received every party's candidates, and could pick a Democrat for governor and a Republican for senator, line by line. The parties themselves sued, and the Court struck the system down.

Its reason was the First Amendment: forcing a party to let nonmembers choose its nominee violates the party's freedom of association.

[pause]

Then, in 2008, came the room in between. In a case called Washington State Grange, the Court upheld Washington's top-two system, against the same parties' objections.

How does that fit with Jones? [beat] Top-two does not nominate anyone. Candidates merely state a party preference, and the top two advance as individuals. So the party is never forced to associate with anyone.

Put the three cases together, and you get the modern rule of thumb.

> States have wide latitude around parties, and much less latitude inside them.

Let's test that with two laws.

One state lets every voter vote in every party's primary, office by office. A second state abolishes party primaries. It sends the top two finishers, from a single ballot of all the candidates, to the general election.

Which law survives a First Amendment challenge? And why? Think it through.

[long pause]

The second. The first law is a blanket primary, and Jones struck exactly that, because it forces a party to let nonmembers pick its nominee. The second is the top-two design, upheld in Washington State Grange. No one is nominated, so the party's rights are not burdened.

Notice what the line is not. It is not how open the voting is. The line is whether the state is commandeering the party's nomination.

## Delegate math

So far, we have been counting voters. But voters do not nominate a president directly.

Presidential nominations are won in delegates, not raw votes. Each state's contest decides how many of that state's delegates each candidate receives, and a majority at the national convention nominates.

Every nomination since 1972 has followed the same path. First comes an invisible primary, in the year or more before any voting: endorsements, donors, polling. Then come the state contests, in sequence, until one candidate holds a majority of the delegates. Only then comes the convention, which in the modern era ratifies what the state contests already decided.

[pause]

The step that turns votes into delegates is where the two parties part ways.

Democrats divide delegates in proportion to the vote, everywhere, under the same rules all season. But there is a threshold. [beat] A candidate needs 15 percent to win any delegates at all.

That threshold does heavy work. A candidate polling 12 percent everywhere wins literally zero delegates.

Republicans leave more to the states, but their calendar sets a rule. Contests in the first two weeks of March must be proportional, and from March 15, winner-take-all is allowed. After that, a front-runner can turn a win with 40 percent of the vote into every one of a state's delegates.

Now work a problem through. Picture a Democratic primary with four candidates. Adams wins 40 percent of the statewide vote. Baker wins 34. Cole wins 14. And Dunn wins 12.

Who gets statewide delegates, and who gets none? And what would that same vote do in a Republican state with winner-take-all rules?

[pause 5s]

Cole and Dunn are both under 15 percent, so they get nothing. Adams and Baker split the statewide delegates, in proportion to their shares of the vote that qualified. That gives Adams roughly 54 percent of the delegates. It gives Baker roughly 46.

In the Republican winner-take-all state, Adams takes every delegate. [beat] With the same 40 percent.

> Democratic races grind on. Republican races snap shut.

In 2024, both nominations were mathematically clinched on the same night, March 12.

## The calendar

None of that math matters equally everywhere, because the contests come one after another.

> Sequence is power.

And nobody designed the sequence we have. The modern calendar is the unintended product of a reform.

In 1968, the Democratic convention met in Chicago. Hubert Humphrey won the nomination without contesting a single primary, while police fought protesters outside. Out of that convention came a commission, known as McGovern-Fraser.

Its report required state parties to select their delegates through processes that were open, timely and demonstrably fair. State parties found that the easiest way to comply was a primary run by the state. And they stampeded. Today, more than three-quarters of the states use primaries.

In 1976, Jimmy Carter was an obscure one-term governor. He camped in Iowa for a year, and converted a caucus win into magazine covers and money. Every candidate since has known that early states are leverage.

The states knew it too. They raced their contests forward, which is called front-loading. For a generation the order held: Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada, South Carolina, and then an avalanche in March.

[pause]

In 2024, that order broke, on one side. At President Biden's urging, the Democratic National Committee voted to demote Iowa and New Hampshire, and to put South Carolina first. New Hampshire, bound by its first-in-the-nation law, voted in January anyway. That was the write-in we began with. And Republicans kept the traditional order entirely.

Will the new order survive to 2028? As of mid-2026, that is an open question inside the party.

Here is why the calendar matters to a voter. Front-loading means the nomination is usually decided while most states' voters are still waiting their turn. In 2024, it was decided before roughly half the country's primaries.

Now step back. At the very start, I said this process is run by two sovereigns. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The parties, and the states. And notice who is missing. No federal law dictates how parties choose presidential nominees. The Constitution never mentions primaries. [beat] It never mentions parties.

The national committees set the broad rules, and they enforce them through delegate seating, not through courts. Defy the calendar, and your delegation shrinks or vanishes. State legislatures decide whether to run a primary at all, on what date, and with which voters eligible. And state parties fill the gaps.

One scholar's warning belongs here. V. O. Key Jr. was the great mid-century student of American parties. In 1956, he examined the direct primary, and he made two observations. Primary electorates are small, and unrepresentative even of their own party. And in lopsided states, the primary quietly replaces the general election as the real contest.

Both observations sit at the center of the arguments ahead.

## Not yet an argument

Everything so far is machinery, and the machinery is shared ground. It is not yet an argument about whether any of it is good.

The argument is over what the machinery does to American politics. It has three positions, and here is each one in brief.

The status-quo defender argues that sequential contests vet candidates, that party influence is a feature, and that federalism is preserved.

> The status-quo defender: sequential contests vet candidates, and party influence is a feature.

The national-primary reformer argues that the calendar is an accident, and that it privileges two small states and front-loaded money.

> The national-primary reformer: the calendar is an accident that privileges two small states.

The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate argues that the deeper defect is who votes in primaries, and how, especially where the primary is the whole election.

> The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate: the deeper defect is who votes, and how.

[pause]

Three positions, and you may already lean toward one. So take the one you find least convincing, and state the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

A defensible position takes one of those views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The nominating process is run by two sovereigns at once. Parties write the rules, and states run most of the voting. A primary buys participation. A caucus keeps control.

[beat]

Two. Who may vote runs from closed, to open, to top-two and top-four, which abolish the party primary as such.

[beat]

Three. States have wide latitude around parties, and much less latitude inside them. The line is not how open the voting is. It is whether the state commandeers the party's nomination.

[beat]

Four. Presidential nominations are won in delegates, not raw votes. Democrats divide them in proportion, above a threshold of 15 percent. Republicans allow winner-take-all from March 15. So Democratic races grind on, and Republican races snap shut.

[beat]

And five. Sequence is power. The calendar is the unintended product of a reform, and it usually decides the nomination while most states' voters are still waiting their turn.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has described the machinery, and it has not judged it. Whether any of this is good for American politics is the question it leaves open.

The next lecture gives each of the three positions its strongest case, the way its smartest holder would make it.

For now, go back to those two nights in January. A caucus in Iowa, run by a party. A primary in New Hampshire, run by a state, against its own party's orders. [beat] Two sovereigns. You know now where one ends, and where the other begins.
