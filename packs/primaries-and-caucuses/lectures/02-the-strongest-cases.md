The spring of 2015. In political science, the smart money says Donald Trump cannot be nominated.

The smart money has a book behind it. Its title is The Party Decides, and it is the reigning account of presidential nominations. It had shown that since the 1980s, nominations turned on an invisible primary: a contest for the endorsements of party elites. The candidate who won that contest almost always won the nomination. Voters, on this view, mostly ratified a choice the party's network had already converged on.

[beat]

Trump entered with almost no endorsements. Nearly every organ of his party opposed him. [beat] And he won anyway.

[pause]

By October 2016, the book's own authors had published a reassessment. New media, early money and factional warfare, they argued, had weakened the party's coordinating power.

Keep that arc in mind. Every position you are about to hear is, in part, a theory of who should decide. And 2016 is the test case each one must explain.

Last time, the machinery. This time, the argument: three positions, each at its strongest. And notice how much ground they share.

> The camps largely agree on the facts. They disagree about which facts are decisive.

We will take it in four stops. First, the status-quo defender. Second, the national-primary reformer. Third, the ranked-choice and open-primary advocate. And last, a sorting: what is settled, what is contested, and what is overclaimed.

Each position gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, the evidence it points to, and what would change its holder's mind.

## The status-quo defender

Begin with the status-quo defender. What this position protects is the system as it stands: contests in sequence, mixed rules, and party influence that is substantial but not total.

Its core claim is a comparison. The current system, the defender argues, survives every comparison with its real alternatives, as opposed to idealized ones.

> The status-quo defender's case: the system survives comparison with its real alternatives.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Sequential voting is a vetting technology. Early small states force candidates into retail politics, which tests stamina and breadth cheaply. And later voters learn from earlier results, correcting first impressions in a way a single national vote never could.

Party influence, on this view, is a feature, not a scandal. The extended party is officeholders, activists and allied groups. It screens for governing competence, and for viability in a general election, in ways that low-information primary electorates cannot.

And federalism does real work. There are fifty state laboratories. Parties keep their First Amendment rights of association. And no single point of failure, or of capture, exists. A national primary centralizes all of it.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, the scholarship. A book of 2011 makes the scholarly version of the vetting argument, and its title is a question: Why Iowa? And The Party Decides described elite coordination as the system working.

[beat]

Second, a track record of breakthroughs. Jimmy Carter in 1976. John McCain's resurrection in 2008. Pete Buttigieg, who all but tied for first in Iowa in 2020, from a standing start. Under a national primary, the defender argues, every one of them loses to whoever begins with the most money and name recognition.

[beat]

Third, participation. In 2016, the primaries drew 28.5 percent of all eligible voters. That is tens of millions of people, taking part in what was, before 1972, a decision made in actual smoke-filled rooms. Judged against its own history, the defender argues, and not against an imagined ideal, the system has never been more open.

The defender also argues that the alternatives have known defects. A national primary day favors celebrity and self-funding. In a national field of ten candidates, a plurality winner could claim the nomination with 25 percent. And the runoff or ranked ballot needed to fix that imports every controversy from the debate over voting systems.

Then there is 2016. The defender does not read Trump's nomination as proof that the system is fine. The defender reads it as a warning of what happens when the party's filter fails: an argument for more party influence, not less.

And what would change this position? Evidence that the sequential system now systematically nominates candidates their own party's median voter opposes. Or evidence that the distortion from the early states, in who gets vetted at all, outweighs the vetting benefit.

## The national-primary reformer

Now the national-primary reformer, built the same way. What this position protects is the voter who comes late in the sequence. It wants all voters to decide on the same information.

Its core claim is about the calendar. The calendar, the reformer argues, is an accident of the 1970s that no one would design on purpose. And its costs now exceed whatever vetting it provides: length, expense, a veto for the early states, and the distortion of momentum.

> The national-primary reformer's case: the calendar is an accident no one would design.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The sequence is arbitrary and unrepresentative. Iowa and New Hampshire are whiter, older and more rural than the country, and their role as gatekeepers was never chosen by anyone.

Momentum, on this view, is an information cascade, not deliberation. Where the defender says later voters learn, the reformer says later voters are herded. A few hundred thousand caucus-goers move the polls, the money and the media for everyone else.

And length and cost select for the wrong traits. The effective campaign now runs more than a year, and costs hundreds of millions. It tests fundraising endurance, the reformer argues, not governing ability.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, the Democratic National Committee's own reordering in 2024. It put South Carolina first, precisely to put Black Democratic voters at the front. That, the reformer argues, concedes the critique outright. And the reordering left two things unfixed. Somebody still votes first. And New Hampshire's open defiance showed that the parties cannot fully control their own calendar anyway.

[beat]

Second, how fast the races end. Candidacies die before 95 percent of the country votes. And in 2024, both nominations were clinched by March 12.

[beat]

Third, proposals that are ready to administer. One is a single national primary day, with a majority requirement, met by a runoff or a ranked ballot. Another is four regional clusters, voting two to three weeks apart, with a different region leading each cycle. The National Association of Secretaries of State has endorsed a rotating regional plan since 1999.

The reformer also argues that none of this requires a constitutional amendment. What has actually blocked it is that neither national party will unilaterally disarm its early-state arrangements.

Then there is 2016. A long and costly campaign hands disproportionate influence to early money. And early money is the very force that the authors' reassessment identified as eroding party coordination.

And what would change this position? Evidence that compressed calendars reliably nominate worse candidates, which is the problem of the 25-percent plurality. Or evidence that killing retail-politics vetting measurably degrades the quality of nominees.

[pause]

Before the third position, a test that needs the first two. One fact appears in the defender's brief, and in the reformer's brief. In 2016, 28.5 percent of eligible voters took part in the primaries.

How would the defender put that fact in a sentence? And how would the reformer? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The defender would put it this way. Nearly three in ten eligible Americans helped choose the nominees. That is more than at almost any point in history, in a system that was pure elite property fifty years earlier.

The reformer would put it this way. More than seven in ten eligible Americans had no say in choosing the only two candidates with a real chance at the presidency. And even that 28.5 percent was concentrated in early states, voting before the field had been winnowed for everyone else.

The same number, against opposite benchmarks: history, versus the full electorate.

> Most primary-reform arguments turn on the choice of denominator.

## The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate

Now the ranked-choice and open-primary advocate, built the same way. What this position protects is the voter who is shut out of the round that decides.

Its core claim is that both other camps are arguing about the presidential calendar, while the deeper damage is structural and local. In safe seats, the primary is the election. And the rules of that election quietly decide who governs.

> The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate's case: in safe seats, the primary is the election.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Gerrymandering and geographic self-sorting have left only a few dozen of the 435 House districts competitive in November. Everywhere else, the November winner was actually chosen in a spring primary, typically by a fraction of the electorate.

Primary electorates, on this view, pull candidates toward the poles. The advocate attaches an honest asterisk to that claim, and you will hear it in a moment.

And closed primaries exclude independents from the decisive round entirely. In safe seats, the advocate argues, that is exclusion from self-government.

Now the evidence this position points to. Three things.

First, who votes, and who cannot. In the midterm primaries of 2022, 21.3 percent of eligible voters took part. In the general election of 2024, turnout was about 64 percent. The real electorate for most American offices, the advocate argues, is that 21 percent. And a record 45 percent of American adults identified as independents, in Gallup's averages for 2025.

[beat]

Second, the research, with its asterisk. A study of 2007 showed congressional candidates positioning themselves closer to their primary electorate than to their district's median voter. But a study of 2014 found that how open a state's primary is has little measurable effect on legislators' ideology. So opening closed primaries, by itself, is weak medicine.

The advocate's strongest reading is that the problem is a combination: low turnout, partisan filtering, and plurality voting. That is why the remedy is structural: all-candidate primaries and ranked ballots, not just looser registration rules.

[beat]

Third, a real-world record. Alaska adopted a top-four primary with a ranked general election in 2020. In 2022, it elected Mary Peltola, the first Alaska Native in Congress, to a seat Republicans had held for 49 years. Two years later, the same rules elected a Republican, Nick Begich the Third, over Peltola. The advocate counts both as wins: the system selects coalition-builders, and does not favor a party.

The advocate also argues that no federal action is required. Every reform in this family has been adopted by state law or citizen initiative, within the constitutional lane that Washington State Grange opened.

Then there is 2016. For this position, the presidential calendar is a sideshow. The damage it points to is in the safe seats.

And what would change this position? Evidence that all-candidate primaries reduce accountability, with nominees no longer answerable to a coherent party electorate. Evidence that ballot complexity suppresses participation, among exactly the marginal voters the reform claims to serve. Or continued repeal votes, showing that the public itself rejects the trade.

[pause]

Test this position too. Alaska's system elected a Democrat in 2022, and a Republican in 2024, to the same House seat. What does that pair of results prove for the advocate? And what does it not prove? Take a moment.

[long pause]

For the advocate, the pair is the best evidence that the system is not a partisan trick. Under identical rules, voters replaced a Democrat with a Republican. That supports the claim that the system rewards broad coalitions, not one party.

What it does not prove is that the system reduces polarization generally. It is two races, in one state, confounded by candidate quality and national tides. And it does not prove the system would work identically in larger, more polarized states.

> Two data points discipline a theory. They do not confirm it.

## Settled, contested, overclaimed

Three positions, and they share more empirical ground than their rhetoric suggests. So sort the claims that carry the weight.

Four are settled. Primary turnout is far below general-election turnout. [beat] Iowa and New Hampshire are demographically unrepresentative of the country. [beat] Closed primaries exclude independents, who are now the largest bloc by identification. [beat] And sequential contests advantage candidates with early-state organization and early money.

One has strong evidence behind it: candidates position themselves toward primary electorates.

One is contested: whether party elites still control nominations. That is The Party Decides, against its own authors' reassessment after 2016.

And three need care. That opening primary eligibility reduces polarization is overclaimed, because the study of 2014 found little effect. That all-candidate primaries with ranked generals elect more coalition-minded candidates is suggestive, with too few cases to settle it. And that a national primary would favor celebrity and money is plausible, and untested.

[pause]

Now pull something back. A while ago, I gave you one number that two camps both claim. Can you still give the number, and the two benchmarks?

[long pause]

28.5 percent. Measured against history, it belongs to the defender. Measured against the full electorate, it belongs to the reformer.

And that is the honest summary of this whole debate.

> The facts of the system are mostly settled. The weights are not.

Is party influence a safeguard, or a usurpation? Is 21 percent turnout a scandal, or just a smaller but adequate jury? Does Alaska generalize? [beat] Those are judgment calls. Evidence narrows them. It cannot close them.

So take the position you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest holder would, and name what would change that holder's mind. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The camps largely agree on the facts. They disagree about which facts are decisive. And each position is, in part, a theory of who should decide.

[beat]

Two. The status-quo defender's case: the system survives comparison with its real alternatives. Sequential contests are a vetting technology, and party influence screens for governing competence. What would change it: evidence that the sequence nominates candidates their own party's median voter opposes.

[beat]

Three. The national-primary reformer's case: the calendar is an accident no one would design. Two unrepresentative states hold a gatekeeping power no one assigned them, and momentum herds later voters. What would change it: evidence that compressed calendars reliably nominate worse candidates.

[beat]

Four. The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate's case: in safe seats, the primary is the election. Roughly a fifth of eligible voters decide it, and closed primaries lock independents out. What would change it: evidence that all-candidate systems reduce accountability.

[beat]

And five. The facts of the system are mostly settled. The weights are not. Most primary-reform arguments turn on the choice of denominator.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not chosen among the three, and it will not. A defensible position picks one of the three frames, says why, and names what is contested inside it.

One puzzle stays open too. The defender says later voters learn from the early states, and the reformer says they are herded by them. Both describe the identical mechanism: early results changing later votes. What observation could tell learning apart from herding, even in principle, is a question to carry with you.

The next lecture turns to the reform fights themselves: what has actually passed, failed and been repealed since 2020. That is where each of these arguments meets the voters.

For now, go back to the spring of 2015. A book said the party decides, and the smart money believed it. Then the book's own authors looked again. [beat] Who should decide is still the question under all three positions.
