December 2024. In Juneau, election workers finish a recount by hand. Then they certify the result. Alaska will keep its top-four primary, and its ranked-choice general election.

The margin is 743 votes. [beat] Out of more than 320,000 cast.

[pause]

Now put that margin in its sequence. Alaska adopted the system in 2020, by a margin under 4,000 votes. The system survived repeal in 2024, by a margin under 800. And within a year, opponents had gathered enough signatures to put repeal on the ballot again: a third ballot fight, set for November 2026.

That is what the American debate over primary reform looks like, up close. It is not a tide moving in one direction. It is trench warfare, fought state by state, initiative by initiative, in margins a strong wind could flip.

[pause]

Last time, three positions. This time, the scoreboard and the map: what has actually passed, failed and been repealed since 2020, and which lever controls each kind of change. By the end, you should be able to take any proposal in this debate and say which door it has to go through.

Bring the three positions with you. The status-quo defender. The national-primary reformer. The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate. Every entry on the scoreboard is one of them winning or losing a round.

We will take it in four stops. First, the three levers. Second, the scoreboard since 2020. Third, ranked ballots in primaries, specifically. And last, the national primary, and where the fight goes next.

## Three doors

Every proposal in this debate has to move through a door. And mistaking the door is the most common analytical error in popular coverage.

> Every proposal moves through one of three doors: state law, party rules, federal law.

The first door is state law, which includes citizen ballot initiatives. State law controls the structure of the election itself: whether a primary exists, who may vote in it, whether it is partisan or all-candidate, and what counting rule is used.

This is where nearly all the real action has been. Alaska, California, Washington, Maine, Louisiana, and every state ban, all came through this door.

Its constitutional lane was mapped in the first lecture. States may design systems around the parties. That was Washington State Grange. They may not hand a party's nomination to nonmembers. That was California Democratic Party v. Jones.

[beat]

The second door is national party rules. They control the presidential calendar, and the allocation of delegates. The Democratic reordering of 2024 came through this door. So did the Republican rule on proportional contests.

Enforcement here is delegate seating, not law. And as New Hampshire demonstrated in 2024, a determined state can simply absorb the penalty.

[beat]

The third door is federal law. In principle, Congress could reach congressional elections through the Elections Clause. [beat] It has not.

Two bills show what that would look like. The Voter Choice Act would fund states' transitions to ranked-choice voting. The Fair Representation Act would mandate ranked-choice voting, and multi-member districts for the House. Both are serious drafting exercises. As of mid-2026, neither has a path to passage.

And presidential nominations are even further from federal reach. A national primary made by statute would collide with the precedents on party autonomy, and with state administration of elections.

Let's test the doors. A bill in Congress would require every state to elect its U.S. House members by ranked-choice voting. Which lever is being pulled? And is it constitutionally available? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Federal law. [beat] And yes, it is available. The Elections Clause lets Congress make or alter the rules for the times, places and manner of congressional elections. No amendment is required.

The hard case is presidential nominations, which run through party processes and state administration. So keep two ideas apart. Politically dead is true of these bills. Constitutionally unavailable is not true, for congressional elections.

## The scoreboard

Now the record, corrected to mid-2026. It rewards close attention, because almost every line cuts against some camp's preferred narrative. I will give you three honest readings of it, with the entries behind each one.

Reading one.

> The all-candidate model is resilient where it exists, and stalled where it doesn't.

Resilient, because Alaska's system has twice survived razor-thin statewide votes: adoption in 2020, and the attempted repeal in 2024. And the top-two systems of California and Washington are politically settled.

Stalled, because no new state has joined them since 2020. Nevada tried. Its voters passed a top-five primary with a ranked-choice general in 2022, with 53 percent. But the measure required a second vote, and in 2024 it failed. That failure killed the movement's flagship expansion.

[pause]

Reading two. By raw count, the bigger structural story is the wave of bans.

Nineteen states have now outlawed ranked-choice voting, preemptively. Nearly all of them are Republican-led. And often, no jurisdiction in the state was using it.

Follow the wave. Florida and Tennessee enacted statewide bans in 2022. Missouri put a ban into its constitution in 2024, with about 68 percent of the vote. North Dakota's ban, in 2025, went further: it voided approval voting in Fargo, a system already in use. And in 2026, Indiana and Ohio became the eighteenth and nineteenth.

What that shows is that reform has become coded. It has been absorbed into partisan identity, in a way it was not a decade ago.

[pause]

Reading three. The direction is not uniform, even within a party coalition.

Deep-blue Washington, D.C., adopted ranked-choice voting in 2024, by initiative, with about 73 percent of the vote. It did so over the objection of its own Democratic Party organization. The same initiative called for semi-open primaries, but the Council funded only the ranked-choice half.

Louisiana went the other way. Under a Republican governor, it moved away from its open, all-comers system, toward closed party primaries for certain offices. That is the rare recent reform in the closing direction.

Now test the scoreboard. A columnist writes: "Since 2020, America has been steadily moving toward ranked-choice voting."

From what you have just heard, what are the two strongest points for that claim? And the two strongest against it? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

For the claim. D.C. adopted with 73 percent. And Alaska's system survived both its adoption vote and the repeal attempt, which failed by 743 votes.

[beat]

Against the claim. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, and North Dakota's law erased an existing system in Fargo. And Nevada's initiative died at its required second vote.

So "steadily moving toward" misdescribes a fight that is intensifying in both directions at once.

> The accurate claim is polarization of the reform itself.

## Ranked ballots in primaries

These lectures are about nominations, so isolate that thread: ranked ballots at the primary stage itself.

Maine is the working example. Ranked ballots have decided its state and federal primaries since June 2018. And in March 2024, Maine ran its first ranked presidential primary.

Alaska is a different design. Its top-four primary pairs with a ranked general election, not a ranked primary. The winnowing round stays single-choice.

And then there is the most underused lever on the board. In the 2020 cycle, several state Democratic parties used ranked ballots in presidential contests that they ran themselves.

> A party can adopt ranked ballots for its own nomination without any change in state law.

What does the evidence show? The evidence base remains thin, and honest advocates say so.

Maine's primaries have produced majority nominees without spoiler panics, and rates of exhausted ballots have been modest. But do ranked primaries select more broadly acceptable nominees, at scale? That is exactly the kind of question that needs more states and more cycles.

The best-studied neighboring reform is top-two, in California and Washington. A careful assessment of 2017 found moderation effects that are real but modest in California, and weaker in Washington. And those effects are entangled with redistricting changes made in the same cycle.

So treat anyone who claims dramatic, proven effects, in either direction, as selling something.

Now put the doors to work. Suppose you want your state's presidential primary to use ranked ballots in 2028. At the start, I gave you three doors. Which two would you have to walk through? And which one actor could do it alone, if the legislature refuses? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Door one is state law. The legislature, or an initiative where one is available, must authorize ranked ballots on the primary the state administers.

[beat]

Door two is party rules. The state party's delegate-selection plan must allocate delegates from that ranked count, and the national party must approve the plan.

[beat]

And the actor who could do it alone is the state party itself. It can run its own contest with ranked ballots, with no statute required. It trades the state's administrative machinery for full control of the rules.

## The national primary, and three clocks

The boldest reform family is also the most dormant.

A single national primary day polls well in the abstract, and it has been proposed in Congress repeatedly, for over a century. Rotating regional plans would have four clusters of states vote at intervals, with a different cluster going first each cycle. They have carried the endorsement of the National Association of Secretaries of State since 1999.

[beat]

Nothing has moved.

The obstacles are structural, not rhetorical. A national primary requires one of two things. One is federal legislation, which would test the limits of Congress's power over what are formally party processes. The other is coordinated rule changes by two national parties.

And each party would be surrendering its early-state vetting machinery. In exchange it would get a system that, as the status-quo defender argued last time, rewards front-runners with money and name recognition.

So the likeliest path for calendar change remains what actually happened in 2024: an incremental reordering of the early window, by one party, fought again every four years. As of mid-2026, the Democratic National Committee's deliberations over the 2028 calendar are open again, with no guarantee that South Carolina keeps its new perch.

[pause]

Step back from all of it, and you can hear that this is not one argument.

> The reform debate is three arguments, running on different clocks.

The fight over counting rules is the fast one: ranked-choice, approval voting, top-two and top-four. It moves state by state, and it is now sharply coded by party. As of mid-2026, the forecast was more bans, more initiative fights, and a decisive rubber match in Alaska that November.

[beat]

The fight over the calendar is slow, and it happens inside the parties. It reopens every cycle, and the Democratic order for 2028 is genuinely unsettled.

[beat]

The structural fight is the slowest of all. It is over who votes in the decisive round: closed, versus open, versus all-candidate. It runs through both constitutional doctrine and each party's self-interest. And it has just produced movement in both directions. [beat] D.C. voting to open. Louisiana closing.

## Three positions, one map

Those three fights are where the three positions meet the voters. So put each position on the map, in one line.

The status-quo defender would keep the sequence of early states, and its vetting machinery.

> The status-quo defender: keep the sequence of early states, and the vetting it provides.

The national-primary reformer would replace that sequence with one national primary day, or with regions voting in rotation.

> The national-primary reformer: one national primary day, or regions voting in rotation.

The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate would change the counting rule, and change who votes in the decisive round.

> The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate: ranked ballots, and all-candidate primaries.

[pause]

Three positions. Take the one you find least persuasive. State its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Every proposal moves through one of three doors: state law, party rules, federal law. Nearly all the real action has come through state law.

[beat]

Two. The all-candidate model is resilient where it exists, and stalled where it doesn't. Alaska kept its system by 743 votes. Nevada's expansion failed. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting. The accurate claim is polarization of the reform itself.

[beat]

Three. A party can adopt ranked ballots for its own nomination without any change in state law. And the evidence on ranked primaries remains thin.

[beat]

And four. The reform debate is three arguments, running on different clocks. Counting rules move fast. The calendar moves slowly, and the national primary, the boldest idea, is the most dormant. And who votes in the decisive round moves slowest of all.

[pause]

Here is what these three lectures have not settled, and have left to you. A defensible position on primaries and caucuses needs three parts. A view on whether the current system produces representative nominees. A view on which reforms would help, and at what cost. And a view on which lever should be doing the work: state, party, or federal. With all three goes an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position, and then to run a steel-man drill against the perspective you find least persuasive.

For now, go back to Juneau. A recount by hand, and a margin of 743 votes. [beat] Not a tide. Trench warfare. And within a year, the next round had qualified for the ballot.
