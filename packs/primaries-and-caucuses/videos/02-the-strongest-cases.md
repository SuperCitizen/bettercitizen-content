## The smart money, 2015

In the spring of 2015, the smart money in political science said Donald Trump could not be nominated. A 2008 book, The Party Decides, had shown that the candidate who won the contest for elite endorsements almost always won the nomination. Trump entered with almost no endorsements, and won anyway. By October 2016, the book's own authors had published a reassessment.

- The Party Decides, 2008
- Its authors' reassessment, 2016

> Every position here is, in part, a theory of who should decide.

## The status-quo defender

The status-quo defender argues that contests held in sequence are a vetting technology. Small early states test candidates cheaply, and later voters learn from earlier results. The defender points to breakthroughs like Jimmy Carter in 1976, and to participation: 28.5 percent of eligible voters in the 2016 primaries, in a decision once made in smoke-filled rooms. On this view party influence is a feature, and 2016 warns of what happens when the party's filter fails.

- Sequence as vetting

> The defender: later voters learn from earlier results.

## The national-primary reformer

The national-primary reformer argues that the calendar is an accident of the 1970s that no one would design on purpose. Iowa and New Hampshire are whiter, older and more rural than the country. Where the defender says later voters learn, the reformer says they are herded: momentum is an information cascade, not deliberation. The reformer points to 2024, when both nominations were clinched by March 12. The remedy is one national primary day, or regions voting in rotation.

- A national day, or rotating regions

> The reformer: later voters are herded, not informed.

## The ranked-choice, open-primary advocate

The ranked-choice and open-primary advocate argues that the other two are arguing about the presidential calendar, while in safe seats the primary is the election. The advocate points to turnout: 21.3 percent of eligible voters took part in the 2022 primaries. The advocate also owns a complication. A 2014 study found that how open a primary is has little measurable effect on legislators' ideology. So the remedy is structural: all-candidate primaries and ranked ballots, as in Alaska.

- Opening eligibility alone: little effect
- All-candidate primaries, ranked ballots

> The advocate: in safe seats, the primary is the election.

## Facts and weights

The three camps largely agree on the facts. They disagree about which facts are decisive.

> The facts of the system are mostly settled. The weights are not.
