## 743 votes

In December 2024, election workers in Juneau finished a hand recount. Alaska would keep its top-four primary and its ranked-choice general election, by 743 votes out of more than 320,000 cast. The system had been adopted in 2020 by a margin under 4,000 votes. Repeal is on the ballot a third time, in November 2026.

- Alaska, 2024: kept by 743 votes

> Not a tide moving one direction, but trench warfare, state by state.

## Both directions at once

The record since 2020 cuts against every tidy story. Nevada passed a top-five primary with ranked-choice voting in 2022, and it failed its required second vote in 2024. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, often where nobody was using it.

- Nevada: passed 2022, failed 2024
- Ranked-choice bans: 19 states

Yet Washington, D.C. adopted it with about 73 percent. And Louisiana moved away from its all-comers ballot toward closed party primaries, the rare recent reform in the closing direction.

- Washington, D.C.: adopted, about 73%
- Louisiana: toward closed primaries

## Three doors

Every proposal moves through one of three doors. State law controls the structure of the election: whether a primary exists, who may vote in it, and how the votes are counted. Nearly all the real action has been here. National party rules control the presidential calendar and the delegates. Federal law could reach congressional elections, and has not.

- State law
- National party rules
- Federal law

There is one lever more. A state party can run its own presidential contest, under its own rules, with ranked ballots. Several state Democratic parties did so in 2020.

> A party can change its own nomination without any change in state law.

## Three fights, three clocks

What these reforms do is less settled than who is winning. The evidence base is thin, and honest advocates say so. The best-studied case, the top-two primary, shows moderation effects that are real but modest.

- Evidence: thin, effects modest

And the debate is three fights on different clocks. The counting-rule fight moves fast. The calendar fight is slow. The fight over who votes in the decisive round is slowest of all.

- Counting rules: fast
- The calendar: slow
- Who votes: slowest
