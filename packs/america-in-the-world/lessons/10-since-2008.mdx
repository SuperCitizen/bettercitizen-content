# Since 2008: is America's lead slipping or holding?

In November 2008, two months after the investment bank Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy, the National Intelligence Council, the US intelligence community's long-range forecaster, published *Global Trends 2025: A Transformed World*. Its executive summary opened: "The international system—as constructed following the Second World War—will be almost unrecognizable by 2025." The United States, it judged, was "likely to remain the single most powerful actor," but its "relative strength—even in the military realm—will decline." Now that 2025 has passed, the forecast can be checked.

## How did a crisis made in America leave the dollar more central?

A [[term:recession|recession]] began in December 2007, home prices fell by more than a fifth, and in September 2008 Lehman Brothers failed. The rescue split both parties. On September 29, 2008, the House rejected the first bill, 205 to 228, with 133 Republicans and 95 Democrats voting no; four days later it passed a revised one, which authorized $700 billion for the Troubled Asset Relief Program, or TARP, to prop up banks. In February 2009 President Obama's stimulus, estimated at about $787 billion, passed without a single House Republican vote.

The Federal Reserve cut its target interest rate nearly to zero and began buying bonds on a vast scale, which came to be called [[term:quantitative-easing|quantitative easing]]. It also set up dollar swap lines with 14 foreign central banks so that they could supply dollars to their own banks. A crisis that began in American housing ended with the world queuing for American money. The council had expected the dollar to become "something of a first among equals in a basket of currencies by 2025"; in mid-2025 it still made up 56.3 percent of the world's reported reserves, against 21.1 percent for the euro.

The losses fell unevenly. Unemployment more than doubled, to 10 percent, and from 2005 to 2009, Pew Research Center found, median wealth fell 66 percent among Hispanic households and 53 percent among Black households, against 16 percent among white ones, mostly because house values plunged. A country that prizes self-reliance had rescued its banks with public money. Treasury puts TARP's net cost at $31.1 billion, as of 2023; defenders call that a rescue that worked, and opponents in both parties argued that it protected the firms that caused the crisis.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why did a crisis that began in American housing leave the dollar more central to world finance, not less?">
  Banks around the world owed and traded in dollars, so the panic became a scramble for dollars, which only the Federal Reserve could create. Its swap lines made it the world's backstop.
</Checkpoint>

An imagined case shows why the currency matters.

<Checkpoint question="A panic starts in a large economy whose currency few foreign banks borrow in, and its central bank offers swap lines to the world, as the Fed did in 2008. Why would the offer calm markets far less?">
  Banks abroad would still need dollars, the currency their debts are written in, and no central bank can print another country's money.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did both parties rethink China?

The council expected China to have the world's second-largest economy by 2025; at market exchange rates it passed Japan in 2010. Its share of world output at market rates rose from 7.2 percent in 2008 to 16.6 percent in 2025, and in 2024 it made 28.1 percent of the world's manufacturing value added, against America's 17.2 percent.

For two decades presidents of both parties had bet that trade would change China, the bet lesson 9 followed to China's entry into the World Trade Organization in 2001. After 2008 they called it off. President Obama declared in 2011 that "the United States is a Pacific power, and we are here to stay." President Trump's 2017 National Security Strategy called for rethinking "policies based on the assumption that engagement with rivals and their inclusion in international institutions and global commerce would turn them into benign actors and trustworthy partners," and in 2018 his administration put [[term:tariff|tariffs]] on Chinese imports. President Biden kept them and raised the rate on Chinese electric vehicles to 100 percent in 2024. His 2022 strategy called China the only competitor with both the intent and, increasingly, the power to reshape the international order, and that October he restricted China's access to advanced chips. Congress passed the CHIPS and Science Act, which subsidizes chipmaking at home, with 17 Republican votes in the Senate and 24 in the House.

Voters moved as well. Pew found that 42 percent of Americans viewed China unfavorably in 2008, 83 percent in spring 2023 and 71 percent in March 2026, though its early phone surveys and later online panel are not strictly comparable. In January 2026, 44 percent of Republicans but 14 percent of Democrats called China an enemy.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what belief about China did both parties give up after 2008, and what replaced it?">
  Both had believed that drawing China into world trade would make it a benign partner. Both replaced that with competition: Trump with tariffs, Biden by keeping them and adding chip controls, and Congress with chip subsidies.
</Checkpoint>

A policy can turn while its old voters stay in office.

## Where did the new strengths come from?

The council expected oil and gas production to be "concentrated in unstable areas." It did not foresee the boom under way in Texas, where firms were pairing horizontal drilling with hydraulic fracturing, which cracks shale with fluid pumped in at high pressure. By 2023 the United States had been the world's largest crude oil producer for six straight years, at 12.9 million barrels a day. In 2019 it exported more energy than it imported for the first time since 1952, and in 2023 it was the largest exporter of liquefied natural gas, sending 66 percent to a Europe replacing Russian pipeline gas. Yet in 2024 it still imported more crude oil than it exported.

Was the boom geography or policy? The economists Zhongmin Wang and Alan Krupnick of Resources for the Future found in 2013 that federal research and tax credits from the late 1970s planted the "seed," but that "it was the private entrepreneurship from Mitchell Energy & Development" that "played the primary role" in Texas's Barnett shale. Mitchell profited by leasing land and mineral rights cheaply and later selling at a much higher price, a mechanism "made possible by the private land and mineral rights ownership system in the United States." Geology and high gas prices helped; a 2012 Breakthrough Institute report they cite stresses government's role. Policy kept steering both ways: Congress repealed the ban on crude oil exports in 2015; Biden paused reviews of new gas-export applications in January 2024, and Trump ended the pause on January 20, 2025.

OpenAI released ChatGPT on November 30, 2022, less than two months after those chip controls. Stanford's AI Index counted $285.9 billion of private AI investment in the United States in 2025, 23.1 times China's $12.4 billion, though private totals likely understate China's spending; in March 2026 the best American model led the best Chinese one by just 2.7 percent. Biden followed with a 2023 order on safe and trustworthy AI; Trump rescinded it, in a running fight over [[issue:ai-regulation|how to regulate AI]], and his 2025 action plan declared that "the United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence."

Both booms ran on people too. The foreign-born share rose from 12.9 percent in 2010 to 14.8 percent in 2024, a level last seen in 1890, and in the year to June 2025 net immigration added 1.3 million people, down from 2.7 million the year before, while births outnumbered deaths by about 519,000. Yet the AI Index found that the number of AI scholars moving to the United States had dropped 89 percent since 2017.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why is it contested whether the shale boom was an advantage of geography or of policy?">
  The rock was there, but it paid only after federal research and tax credits, a private firm's risk-taking, and a law of private mineral rights that let the firm profit. Scholars weigh those parts differently.
</Checkpoint>

Now try a country with the rock but not the rest.

## Declining, enduring, or divided?

In 1994, Pew found, 17 percent of Republicans and 16 percent of Democrats viewed the other party very unfavorably; in 2022, 62 and 54 percent did. The parties in Congress were "farther apart ideologically today than at any time in the past 50 years," Pew reported in 2022. All three major rating agencies cut America's credit rating: S&P in 2011, blaming [[term:partisan|partisan]] divisions; Fitch in 2023, citing "repeated debt limit standoffs"; and Moody's in 2025, citing successive governments' failure to stop rising debt. Shutdowns came under presidents of both parties: 16 days in 2013 under Obama, and 34 full days in 2018 and 2019 and 42 in 2025 under Trump.

Immigration showed the pattern most sharply. A Senate overhaul passed 68 to 32 in 2013, with 14 Republican votes, and never reached a House vote. After Border Patrol encounters at the Mexican border hit a record of more than 2.2 million in fiscal 2022, a bipartisan Senate border deal failed, 49 to 50, in February 2024, and that June Biden suspended entry whenever encounters averaged 2,500 a day, limiting [[term:asylum|asylum]] claims. On January 20, 2025, Trump suspended migrants' entry at the southern border by proclamation; in fiscal 2025 encounters fell to 237,538, the lowest since 1970. In Gallup's polls, 55 percent of Americans wanted immigration decreased in June 2024 and 29 percent in June 2026, when 48 percent of Republicans and 19 percent of Democrats did.

## What the 250-year arc does and does not tell us

Sort this era's advantages as the series has sorted every other. Geography and timing: shale under Texas, and an AI boom that arrived where the leading firms and capital already were. Choices and policy: the Fed's lending to the world; federal gas research and tax credits, and an old legal choice to leave mineral rights with landowners; the 2015 export repeal; chip controls and subsidies; immigration rules that swung between administrations. The troubles of other nations: China's population, falling since 2021; Japan's economy, about $5.2 trillion in 2008 and $4.5 trillion in 2025 in dollars by the IMF's count; and Europe's loss of Russian gas. The joints are contested. Shale was rock, policy and private risk at once; immigration is a policy choice that also answers the pull of a large, rich economy; and the strong dollar may reflect American choices, other countries' weakness, or both.

The long arc is a record, not a forecast. By the Maddison Project's estimates, the United States produced about 2 percent of the world's output in 1820, about 10 percent in 1870, about 17 percent in 1900, about 27 percent in 1950 and about 15 percent in 2022. Every rise mixed all three kinds of advantage; the 1950 peak was partly the ruin of every rival, as lesson 6 showed; and the alarms of the 1970s in lesson 8 were followed by three decades in which the share held at about 21 to 22 percent. The arc cannot say whether that pattern will repeat, whether a falling share means American failure or other nations' rise, or who will share in what comes next, though the losses of 2008 fell hardest on Hispanic and Black households. The 2008 forecast warns both ways: it saw China's rise, if fifteen years slow, and missed the shale boom entirely.

The series began by asking what made the United States what it became, and how the rest of the world shaped it. After 250 years the answer still has three parts, geography and timing, choices, and the fortunes of others, and the three readings of today disagree about which of them will matter most next.
