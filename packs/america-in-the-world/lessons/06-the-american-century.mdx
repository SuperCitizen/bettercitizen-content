# How did America come to lead the postwar world?

On February 17, 1941, less than ten months before Japan's attack on [[term:pearl-harbor]], *Life* magazine printed an editorial by its publisher, Henry Luce, titled "The American Century." Luce urged Americans "to accept wholeheartedly our duty and our opportunity as the most powerful and vital nation in the world," and "to exert upon the world the full impact of our influence, for such purposes as we see fit and by such means as we see fit." Within ten years the United States had won the war, tied its allies' currencies to the dollar, paid for much of Western Europe's recovery and promised to defend it. How much of that was American achievement, and how much was the wreckage of everyone else?

## Why did the war leave one great power standing?

In mid-1940 the United States had about 458,000 people on active military duty; by mid-1945, about 12 million. Federal spending rose from 9.6 percent of gross domestic product (GDP, the value of everything the economy produces in a year) in fiscal 1940 to 41.0 percent in fiscal 1945, and national defense alone took 36.6 percent. Washington set targets and paid the bills; private companies built the ships and planes. Whatever the story of self-reliance, the war economy was directed from Washington on a scale never seen before.

The war also decided who could work, and who was pushed aside. In June 1941, facing the labor leader A. Philip Randolph's threat of a march on Washington, Roosevelt barred discrimination in defense industries; the armed forces stayed segregated until Truman ordered an end to it in 1948. In 1942, Executive Order 9066 was used to force about 122,000 people of Japanese ancestry into camps, nearly 70,000 of them American citizens. And the Bracero Program brought more than four million Mexican farmworkers north on temporary contracts between 1942 and 1964, often for meager wages.

Abroad, the scale was different. By the National WWII Museum's estimates, which it warns vary widely, the war killed about 418,500 Americans, about 24 million people in the Soviet Union and about 20 million in China. The forty-eight states saw no invasion or bombing campaign. By the Maddison Project's estimates, America's output per person rose about 27 percent between 1940 and 1950, while Western Europe's barely moved.

  The fighting happened on other nations' soil. America's factories came through intact and grew on government orders while its rivals lost millions of people and much of their industry, so its 1950 lead was partly its own growth and partly their loss.
</Checkpoint>

People forget the second half of that answer.

</Checkpoint>

## Why did the postwar world run on dollars?

In July 1944, delegates from 44 nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the peace's money system from rival plans by Harry Dexter White of the U.S. Treasury and John Maynard Keynes of Britain. They created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and a system of fixed exchange rates (the price of one currency in another) centered on the dollar: members fixed their currencies against it, and the dollar was fixed in gold at $35 an ounce. Think of a wheel: every currency a spoke bolted to the dollar, and the hub bolted to gold. The image breaks in one place. This hub had interests of its own: it issued the money everyone needed, and promised a gold backing that lesson 8 shows it could not keep.

Europe's problem was a shortage of dollars. In 1946 and 1947 its deficit in trade and payments ran at about $9 billion a year, and private lenders could not close the gap (Crafts 2011). On June 5, 1947, Secretary of State George Marshall offered help at Harvard: "Our policy is directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos." Truman signed the Economic Cooperation Act on April 3, 1948, and Congress appropriated $13.3 billion over four years. The Soviet Union kept Eastern Europe out, from concern "over potential U.S. economic domination," in the State Department historians' words. Because the aid came as American goods, exporters and farmers backed it.

The aid came to a little over 1 percent of America's national income a year and around 2 percent of the recipients' output (Crafts 2011). Scholars agree on that, and disagree about what it did.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Generosity and strategy">
    Defenders argue that its timing and terms mattered more than its size. The economist Barry Eichengreen and his collaborators argue that it eased the fights over stabilizing budgets, notably in France and Italy, and pushed Europe toward freer trade and markets. J. Bradford De Long and Eichengreen (1991) call it "history's most successful structural adjustment program," one that "did play a major role in setting the stage" for Western Europe's rapid growth. The historian Michael Hogan (1987) adds the strategy: officials hoped to remake Western Europe as a smaller version of America's integrated, mixed capitalist economy.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="An overstated effect">
    The critics argue that the plan's economic effect has been overstated. The economic historian Alan Milward, in *The Reconstruction of Western Europe, 1945–51* (1984), argues that the aid was too small to do much for growth by financing investment, and that Washington could not rebuild Europe to its own blueprint: the payments union Europeans built fell short of the customs union and early convertibility Americans wanted. Europe's own governments, on this reading, did much of the work. The economist Nicholas Crafts's survey (2011) concludes that "the direct effects were positive but modest."
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how did Bretton Woods and the Marshall Plan put the United States at the center of the postwar economy?">
  Bretton Woods fixed other currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold, so members' trade ran through American money. The Marshall Plan then supplied the dollars Europe lacked, on American conditions, tying Western Europe's recovery to Washington.
</Checkpoint>

Now try a government on the other side.

<Checkpoint question="A European government in 1948 needs American coal and machinery, holds few dollars, and has turned Marshall aid down. What are its options, and what does its position show?">
  It must earn dollars, borrow them or go without, since its own currency cannot buy American goods. That is the hub's leverage: whoever controls the scarce currency sets the terms, the domination Moscow said it feared.
</Checkpoint>

## Why did the wartime allies become enemies?

On March 12, 1947, President Truman asked Congress for $400 million for Greece and Turkey, declaring: "I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." That July the diplomat George Kennan, writing anonymously in *Foreign Affairs*, called for "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies." Containment meant holding Soviet power within the lines it had reached, neither rolling it back nor accepting its spread, and it became the organizing idea of the [[term:cold-war]].

On April 4, 1949, the United States, Canada and ten Western European countries founded [[term:nato]], whose Article 5 says "an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all." It was, in the State Department historians' words, "the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered into outside of the Western Hemisphere." Kennan saw the threat as mainly political, but NSC 68 called for a major military buildup in 1950, and the Korean War settled the argument: defense rose from 4.9 percent of GDP in 1950 to 13.8 percent in 1953. Leaving office in January 1961, President Eisenhower warned against the "unwarranted influence" of what he called the [[term:military-industrial-complex]].

Why did it happen? Historians have argued along three lines.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Orthodox">
    The first account, the Truman administration's own, defended by historians such as Arthur Schlesinger Jr. ("Origins of the Cold War," 1967), argues that the Cold War was the free world's response to Communist expansion. It points to Soviet conduct: governments imposed across Eastern Europe, pressure on Greece and Turkey, the 1948 coup in Czechoslovakia and the Berlin crisis. On this view American policy was defensive and necessary. The historian John Lewis Gaddis's *We Now Know* (1997), drawing on newly available communist sources, argues that Stalin bore primary responsibility.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Revisionist">
    The revisionist case begins with the historian William Appleman Williams's *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959). Williams argues that American policy had long been driven by the "Open Door," the conviction that American prosperity required open markets abroad. Washington's push for open economies in Europe, Marshall Plan included, looked to Moscow like a threat to its security zone, and the far stronger United States bore more of the responsibility. It points to American planning about markets and to Moscow's reason for refusing the Marshall Plan. The tragedy was genuine ideals undercut by an informal empire.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Post-revisionist">
    Post-revisionists argue that neither older school captured how the conflict was made. Gaddis's *The United States and the Origins of the Cold War, 1941–1947* (1972) traced American policy to domestic politics, bureaucratic habit, personalities and perceptions of Soviet intentions; in 1983 he named an "emerging post-revisionist synthesis." The historian Melvyn Leffler's *A Preponderance of Power* (1992) argues that officials feared less the Soviet army than the disorder of a ruined Europe and Asia, and worked to bring Western Europe, Germany and Japan into the American orbit. It points to archives showing fear and misreading on both sides.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The schools still move: Gaddis, once the leading post-revisionist, moved toward the orthodox view as Soviet sources opened.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what was containment, and what made NATO a break with America's past?">
  Containment meant holding Soviet power within the lines it had reached, with aid, alliances and, after 1950, a large military. NATO made it a treaty promise, America's first peacetime alliance outside its own hemisphere.
</Checkpoint>

The schools are easiest to tell apart by what each treats as the first move.

## Who shared in the boom?

The Servicemen's Readjustment Act, signed on June 22, 1944, and soon called the GI Bill, paid veterans' tuition and living costs, offered unemployment pay and helped them buy homes and businesses. Within seven years about 8 million veterans had used its education benefits, 2.3 million of them at colleges, and by 1955 it had helped finance 4.3 million home loans. Degrees awarded by American colleges more than doubled between 1940 and 1950. The suburb is remembered as the work of self-made families, and they did work hard; but the degree and the mortgage often rested on a federal benefit.

The law's text made no distinction by race, but its delivery ran through local banks, colleges and offices. Black veterans often could not get bank loans for homes in Black neighborhoods, and discrimination kept many out of white suburbs. The economists Sarah Turner and John Bound (2003) found that the GI Bill raised college attainment for white men and Black men born outside the South, but did little for Black veterans limited to the South's segregated colleges. The political scientist Ira Katznelson's *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005) argues that the law was built to accommodate [[term:jim-crow]] by leaving its administration to local officials.

Immigration ran at a low ebb. The national-origins quotas of 1924 still governed who could come, and the share of residents born abroad fell from 8.8 percent in 1940 to 6.9 percent in 1950, the lowest since the census began asking in 1850. The war opened it only for strategy's sake: repealing Chinese exclusion in 1943 was "almost wholly grounded in the exigencies of World War II," and China got about 105 visas a year. In 1952 the McCarran–Walter Act ended Asian exclusion but kept the national-origins quotas, over Truman's veto.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: who shared in the postwar boom, and how could a law that never mentioned race still leave people out?">
  Millions of veterans gained degrees and homes through federal benefits. But the benefits flowed through local gatekeepers, so where those were segregated Black veterans got far less, and quotas kept most would-be immigrants out.
</Checkpoint>

That point about delivery travels beyond 1944.

<Checkpoint question="Congress creates a home-buying grant for every veteran, paid through whichever local lenders choose to take part. Using the GI Bill's record, what would you check first to learn who will really benefit?">
  Check who controls delivery: which lenders take part and where they lend. The GI Bill was equal on paper, but local gatekeepers decided who got loans and admissions.
</Checkpoint>

## What was earned, what was inherited, and what is still argued

Sorted plainly, America's position came from three kinds of advantage.

**Geography and timing.** Two oceans kept invasion and bombing away. The country entered the war more than two years after it began in Europe, and it was big enough to turn being spared into a lead.

**Choices and policy.** Washington mobilized on a scale it had never tried, then chose to stay engaged: Bretton Woods, the Marshall Plan, NATO and the GI Bill could each have gone another way. So could the choices that left people out, from the camps of 1942 to the quotas of 1952.

**The troubles of other nations.** Every other great power ended the war invaded, bombed or exhausted, and Western Europe's output per person was barely higher in 1950 than in 1940. Part of America's 1950 share was simply the absence of rivals.

The numbers and the war's toll are settled; the sorting is argued. Revisionists read the choices as an informal empire, defenders as generosity joined to self-interest, and Milward's readers credit Europeans with much of the success. One fact cuts both ways. As Europe and Japan rebuilt, the American share of world output fell back to about 22 percent by 1970 (Maddison). Read one way, 1950's peak was other nations' ruin; read another, the policies worked, since a rebuilt Western Europe was what Washington had paid for.

Luce's American Century had arrived, and so had a contradiction at its center: a country that told the world it stood for free peoples still segregated its own citizens by law across much of the South. The next lesson follows the movement that forced it into the open, from *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954 to the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and asks how far the Cold War pushed the country to face it.
