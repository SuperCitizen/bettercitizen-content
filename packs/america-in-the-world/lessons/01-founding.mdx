# How did thirteen colonies become a country in a world at war?

On June 7, 1776, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia rose in the Continental Congress in Philadelphia with a resolution of three sentences. The first declared that "these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States." The second said it was "expedient forthwith to take the most effectual measures for forming foreign Alliances." The third called for a plan of confederation. Independence and the hunt for allies entered American history in the same breath: four days later, Congress named one committee to draft a declaration and another to plan the alliances.

That resolution opens the question this series follows across 250 years: how did the United States become what it became, and how much of that did the rest of the world decide? By the Maddison Project's estimates, the country produced about 2 percent of the world's output in 1820 and about 27 percent in 1950. Each lesson sorts the advantages behind that climb into three kinds: **geography and timing**, what the land and the moment handed Americans; **choices and policy**, what Americans and their governments decided; and **the troubles of other nations**, what other peoples' wars, debts and revolutions made possible. Each also asks who shared in what was built, and who was left out.

## What did thirteen colonies produce, and for whom?

In 1775 the thirteen colonies were farms, forests and fishing ports on the western edge of the British Empire. The Chesapeake grew tobacco, the Carolina low country rice and indigo, the middle colonies wheat. New England sold fish, timber and ships, and carried cargoes to the sugar islands of the West Indies. British trade laws, the Navigation Acts, steered that commerce through British ports and ships.

It was a prosperous place for those who owned it. Much of the work behind the most valuable crops was done by people who owned nothing, not even themselves. And the land that made farms cheap was Native land. When settlers poured over the Appalachians after 1763, Native nations fought back, and a war led by the Ottawa leader Pontiac took all but two of Britain's western forts. London's Proclamation of 1763 forbade settlement west of the mountains, angering settlers and the Virginia gentry who had invested in western land. The crisis that broke the empire came out of a victory. In the Seven Years' War, which ended in 1763, Britain beat France and took all French territory east of the Mississippi. The war had been enormously expensive, and Parliament decided the colonists should help pay. It enforced duties on sugar and molasses and passed the Stamp Act of 1765, a tax on paper goods. Colonists answered with riots, boycotts and the slogan "No taxation without representation." The economy explains why the quarrel mattered to merchants and planters. It does not explain why so many would die over it.

## What made breaking away thinkable?

Plenty of people paid taxes they disliked without founding a country. What turned a tax quarrel into a revolution was a set of ideas from the Enlightenment, the movement that tested inherited authority against reason and natural rights. From the English philosopher John Locke, colonists took the claim that government rests on consent and may be replaced when it attacks the rights it exists to protect. From the French writer Montesquieu they took the warning that liberty survives only where power is divided.

The Declaration of Independence, adopted on July 4, 1776, put the ideas in a sentence: "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal." Governments, it went on, derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed," the principle called [[term:popular-sovereignty]]. It also faced outward, ending with a claim to the "full Power to levy War, conclude Peace, contract Alliances, establish Commerce" that independent states hold. French officials would not consider an alliance until the colonies declared themselves a country.

Who was in "all men"? The founding's own documents answer plainly. Thomas Jefferson, who drafted the Declaration, was a Virginia slaveholder, and in 1790 nearly one American in five was Black, almost all of them enslaved. The Declaration accused the king of turning "the merciless Indian Savages" against the frontier. In March 1776 Abigail Adams asked her husband John, then in Congress, to "remember the ladies" in the new laws; with one partial exception, the new states did not give women the vote. [[term:suffrage]], the right to vote, followed property as well as sex: most states limited it to men who owned property or paid taxes, so many free men were shut out too.

## Why did France fight Britain's war?

France had its own reasons. It had lost Canada and its lands east of the Mississippi in 1763, and its ministers saw the rebellion as a chance to profit from British troubles. From late 1775 it secretly shipped gunpowder and arms through dummy companies and private traders.

A battle turned secret help into an alliance. When news of a British surrender at Saratoga reached Paris in December 1777, the foreign minister, the comte de Vergennes, offered an alliance. On February 6, 1778, Benjamin Franklin and his fellow commissioners signed a commercial [[term:treaty]] and a treaty of alliance. The alliance's stated end was "to maintain effectually the liberty, Sovereignty, and independance absolute and unlimited of the said united States," and neither side could make "Truce or Peace with Great Britain, without the formal consent of the other."

The alliance turned a rebellion into a world war. In 1780, 5,500 French troops landed at Newport, Rhode Island. In 1781 a French fleet drove off a British fleet in the Battle of the Capes on September 5 and blockaded the Chesapeake, trapping General Cornwallis at Yorktown. About 17,600 American and French soldiers besieged 8,300 British troops, and on October 19, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered. Britain was also fighting in India, Gibraltar, the West Indies and Ireland, and in March 1782 Parliament voted against carrying on the American war. French loans to the Americans passed two million dollars.

The peace showed American choices at work. With Vergennes's consent, Franklin, John Adams and John Jay negotiated directly with Britain and signed preliminary articles on November 30, 1782, without France at the table. The Treaty of Paris of September 3, 1783, acknowledged the United States "to be free sovereign and independent states" and set its western boundary at the Mississippi. No Native nation signed it, though Britain signed away land that Native nations held; most had sided with Britain as the lesser danger to their lands. And Article 7 required British forces to leave "without causing any destruction, or carrying away any Negroes or other property of the American inhabitants."

</Checkpoint>

## How did a weak republic survive Europe's wars?

Under the Articles of Confederation, in force from 1781, Congress could not tax or regulate trade. States ignored the treaty's promise that British creditors could collect prewar debts, so Britain kept its Great Lakes forts. Spain closed the Mississippi to American shipping, and in 1785 the United States stopped paying interest on its French loans.

The Constitution, written in 1787 and in force from 1789, answered with power. Congress could now "lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises" and "regulate Commerce with foreign Nations," and treaties became "the supreme Law of the Land." The new government resumed payments to France in 1790 and settled those debts in 1795. It lived on the [[term:tariff]]: taxes on imports supplied 80 to 90 percent of national revenue in the nineteenth century, so its income rose and fell with foreign trade.

The Constitution also protected slavery without naming it. It counted "three fifths of all other Persons" toward each state's seats in the House, barred Congress from stopping "the Migration or Importation of such Persons" before 1808, and required that a person "held to Service or Labour" who escaped "shall be delivered up." It left "Indians not taxed" out of the count. At the Convention, James Madison "thought it wrong to admit in the Constitution the idea that there could be property in men," and the word slave appears nowhere in the text.

Then Europe went to war. Revolutionary France executed its king in January 1793 and was soon fighting Britain and Spain. The 1778 treaty had guaranteed France's American islands "forever," and France wanted help. On April 22, 1793, Washington proclaimed instead "a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerant Powers." Britain seized American ships and sailors anyway, and John Jay's treaty, approved by the Senate 20 to 10 in June 1795, got the British out of the Great Lakes forts but settled little else. Spain, beaten in Europe and afraid of an Anglo-American alliance, gave Thomas Pinckney a treaty on October 27, 1795, opening the Mississippi and New Orleans to American trade, without the alliance Spain asked for. France, angry at Jay's treaty, seized American ships, and from 1798 the old allies fought an undeclared naval war. Congress also lengthened the wait for citizenship, which a 1790 law had opened only to a "free white person." The Convention of 1800 ended the war and the alliance; the United States would not sign another formal alliance for nearly a century and a half.

Washington's Farewell Address of September 1796 turned the decade into advice: "The great rule of conduct for us in regard to foreign nations is in extending our commercial relations, to have with them as little political connection as possible." He grounded it in geography: "Our detached and distant situation invites and enables us to pursue a different course." Later generations took the address as an inspiration for [[term:isolationism]].

## Geography, choices or others' troubles? What is still argued

Historians agree on these events far more than on what drove them.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="The ideological reading">
    Bernard Bailyn's *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (1967) rests on the pamphlets colonists published before independence. In them he found a sincere, coherent fear that British ministers were plotting against American liberty. Taxes mattered as evidence of the plot. In a chapter titled "The Contagion of Liberty," Bailyn traced how the same arguments spread to slavery, established churches and social rank. On this reading, ideas made the Revolution, and then outran the men who wrote them.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The economic reading">
    Charles Beard's *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution of the United States* (1913) called the Constitution "essentially an economic document." Its framers, he argued, were men of money, public securities, trade and shipping who wanted a government strong enough to pay debts, enforce contracts and restrain debtor-friendly state legislatures. Robert E. Brown (1956) and Forrest McDonald (1958) concluded that the records did not bear Beard out. Robert McGuire's statistical study (2003) revived a narrower claim: delegates' economic interests help predict their votes on particular clauses, alongside their ideas.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Slavery at the center">
    Edmund Morgan's *American Slavery, American Freedom* (1975) argued that in Virginia freedom for white men and slavery for Black people grew up together, each making the other possible. David Waldstreicher's *Slavery's Constitution* (2009) argues that slavery sat at the heart of the 1787 text, protected though never named. On this reading, the exclusions were part of the design: a republic of free men built on enslaved labor and on Native land.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The evidence settles part of this: the clauses are in the text, the pamphlets survive, the delegates' finances can be counted. It cannot settle weight. Sean Wilentz's *No Property in Man* (2018) grants that the Convention protected slaveholders, but argues that keeping property in man out of the text gave later antislavery politicians ground to stand on.

Sorting the advantages raises the same problem.

<Panel title="Geography and timing" tone="blue">

- An ocean between the republic and Europe's armies
- Abundant land, which was Native land
- Independence just after France lost its North American empire

</Panel>
<Panel title="Choices and policy" tone="gold">

- Seeking allies in the same resolution as independence
- A Constitution with power to tax trade and keep treaties
- Neutrality in 1793, and no alliance after 1800

</Panel>
<Panel title="The troubles of other nations" tone="green">

- France's wish to undo 1763
- Britain's wars in India, Gibraltar and the West Indies
- Spain's defeats in Europe, which bought Pinckney's treaty

</Panel>

The sorting is itself argued. Samuel Flagg Bemis subtitled his 1926 study of Pinckney's treaty *A Study of America's Advantage from Europe's Distress*; the State Department's history of the same treaty dwells on Pinckney's threat to walk out. Land is harder still: Americans counted it a gift of geography, but it became American by war and treaty. And the economic historian Douglass North argued in 1961 that the country prospered as a neutral trader while Europe fought, a claim still tested.

Europe's distress paid its largest dividend just after this lesson ends. In 1803 Napoleon, his army in Saint-Domingue decimated by yellow fever and war with Britain looming, sold the whole Louisiana territory to the United States for $15 million. The next lesson follows the country across that land from 1800 to 1860, and asks who paid for the expansion.
