# How did America become the world's industrial giant?

At 12:47 p.m. on Monday, May 10, 1869, a Western Union telegrapher at Promontory, Utah, sent one word down the wire: "D-O-N-E." The last spike had joined two railroads across the continent. The Union Pacific had employed more than 8,000 Irish, German and Italian immigrants; the Central Pacific's workforce included over 10,000 Chinese laborers. Thirteen years later, Congress barred Chinese laborers from entering the country. Immigrant hands joining a continent, and a door shut on some of them: both run through the years in which the United States became the world's largest industrial economy.

## How a continent became one market

The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated that the United States made 7.2 percent of the world's manufactured goods in 1860, when Britain made 19.9 percent. By 1913 the American share was 32.0 percent, more than Britain's 13.6 and Germany's 14.8 combined. Estimates of total output agree, more gently.

Output per person shows how close the race was. By the Maddison estimates the average American produced about 82 percent as much as the average Briton in 1870 and about 23 percent more by 1913. There was no single day of overtaking: the United States first edged ahead in 1880, fell behind in several years between 1888 and 1898, and stayed ahead from 1899.

Why here? Part of the answer is the ground: between 1870 and 1910, Paul David and Gavin Wright write, the United States became the world's leading producer of minerals. Part was size. The population grew from 38.6 million in 1870 to about 97 million in 1913, all inside one market, and that was a choice made in 1787. The Constitution gave Congress the power "To regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States," the [[term:commerce-clause]], and said no state could "lay any Imposts or Duties on Imports or Exports" without Congress's consent. A Pittsburgh mill met no customs house at the Ohio line.

And part was federal help that the story of self-made men leaves out. The Pacific Railway Act of 1862 gave the builders public land along the track and federal bonds of $16,000 a mile, triple that in the mountains. In all, Congress granted 174 million acres of public land for four transcontinental railroads. The historian Richard White argues in *Railroaded* (2011) that this dependence on public largess drew the railroads into new forms of corruption.

The other great policy was the protective [[term:tariff]], a tax on imports meant to shelter home industry. Around 1890, by one calculation the economist Douglas Irwin reports, duties on imported capital goods averaged around 40 percent. Whether that wall built American industry is disputed.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="The tariff as builder">
    **Who holds it:** the economist Ha-Joon Chang, in *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002), heir to Alexander Hamilton's "infant industry" argument. **The case:** a young industry needs time behind a wall to learn what its foreign rivals already know. Citing Bairoch, Chang writes that from the Civil War to the Second World War the United States "was literally the most heavily protected economy in the world," and it became the richest. **The evidence:** Kevin O'Rourke found tariffs positively correlated with growth across ten countries from 1875 to 1914. Irwin's own study found that the McKinley tariff of 1890 brought tinplate making about a decade sooner, and Keith Head found strong learning behind the steel rail tariff.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The tariff as burden">
    **Who holds it:** the Dartmouth economist Douglas Irwin, a historian of trade policy, and economists who share his doubts. **The case:** tariffs and growth arriving together does not prove that one caused the other. Growth hinged more on population and investment than on productivity, and by raising the price of imported machinery the tariff may have slowed investment. **The evidence:** productivity grew fastest in transportation, utilities, distribution and other services, which faced little foreign competition. Speed-ups had a price: Irwin's tinplate study concludes that protection "does not pass a cost-benefit test," and Head found that the rail tariff hurt the railroads that bought the rails.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Both tabs cite the same studies. The quarrel is over how to weigh an industry's head start against the higher prices everyone else paid for it.

## Who did the work, and who was shut out

The foreign-born were 14.4 percent of the population in 1870, peaked at 14.8 percent in 1890, and were still 14.7 percent in 1910: about one resident in seven. In 1892 a federal immigration station opened on Ellis Island in New York harbor, and Annie Moore of Ireland was the first person processed there. More than 12 million immigrants passed through it.

The economists Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson count about 55 million Europeans who left for the New World between 1850 and 1914. American wages and land pulled them. Hardship at home pushed, and some of it was made in America: falling transport costs let cheap grain from the prairies and elsewhere flood Europe, and Kevin O'Rourke found that it lowered land rents throughout Europe and real wages outside Britain.

Law decided who could come. For Europeans the door stood open; Congress set no quotas on them until the 1920s, the next lesson's story. For the Chinese it closed. The Chinese Exclusion Act of May 6, 1882, banned Chinese laborers for ten years and barred courts from naturalizing Chinese immigrants; it was extended in 1892, made permanent in 1902 and repealed only in 1943. "For the first time," the National Archives notes, "federal law proscribed entry of an ethnic working group."

Others were inside and still left out. Under the Dawes Act of 1887, reservation land held in common was broken into individual allotments, and as allotments passed to non-Native buyers, Native communities lost vast acreage. In *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) the Supreme Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored races," leaving the South's [[term:jim-crow]] laws in place for more than half a century.

## Giants, strikers and a government that took sides

Andrew Carnegie came from Dunfermline, Scotland, in 1848 and worked as a bobbin boy for $1.20 a week. He built a steel plant near Pittsburgh in 1875 and in 1901 sold his company to J. P. Morgan for $480 million. John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil grew so large that the federal government's suit charged it with "controlling 90 per cent of the business of producing, shipping, refining, and selling petroleum and its products." What should a government do about giants like these, the giants of the [[term:gilded-age]]?

Washington's answers were new and weak. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 made railroads the first industry under federal regulation, but "In practice," says the National Archives, "the law was not very effective." [[term:antitrust]] law began with the Sherman Act of 1890, which outlawed "Every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade" but defined none of its terms. In 1895 the Supreme Court held that a company refining about 98 percent of the country's sugar had not broken it.

In the great strikes, government sided with the owners. In 1877 a 10 percent wage cut on the Baltimore and Ohio set off a railroad strike that by some estimates drew 100,000 workers; federal troops helped end it, and dozens died. At Homestead in 1892, Carnegie's manager Henry Frick locked out 3,800 workers and brought in about 300 Pinkerton guards; nearly a dozen people died, the governor sent 8,500 troops, and the union was broken. In 1894 the American Railway Union refused to handle Pullman cars until a federal injunction and thousands of marshals and Army troops ended the boycott, leaving dozens dead. Upholding the jailing of its leaders, the Supreme Court declared in *In re Debs* (1895) that "The entire strength of the nation may be used to enforce in any part of the land the full and free exercise of all national powers."

Germany, meanwhile, insured workers against sickness in 1883, accidents in 1884, and old age and disability in 1889. Washington offered industrial workers nothing of the kind; federal spending was, by rough estimates, about 2.5 percent of the economy in 1900. Historians read these giants two ways.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Robber barons">
    **Who holds it:** the writer Matthew Josephson, whose *The Robber Barons* (1934) gave the era its lasting label, and historians since who stress power and corruption. **The case:** the great fortunes were taken more than made, through bribery, monopoly and the crushing of rivals and workers, and they bent government to private ends. **The evidence:** in 1867 Oakes Ames gave shares in Crédit Mobilier, the company building the Union Pacific, to two senators and nine representatives. Standard Oil's grip was such that the Supreme Court upheld its breakup in 1911. And the defeat at Homestead brought 12-hour days, wage cuts and 500 lost jobs.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Industrial statesmen">
    **Who holds it:** the historian Allan Nevins, whose *John D. Rockefeller* (1940) was part of a revival of the business leader as industrial statesman, and the business historian Alfred Chandler, whose *The Visible Hand* (1977) won the Pulitzer Prize. **The case:** the giant firm was a new tool. Chandler traced how big business, beginning with the railroads, built hierarchies of professional managers, and argued that modern enterprise took over wherever such coordination did better than the market. **The evidence:** the output itself, nearly a third of the world's manufacturing by 1913. And Carnegie rose from bobbin boy to steel master, then gave away some $350 million.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## Joining the empires: the turn outward

Europe's empires were at their height. By 1914 European powers ruled almost all of Africa, Ethiopia and Liberia excepted, and in China the European powers and Japan guarded their spheres of influence and trading privileges. Americans began to look outward. In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner quoted the Census Bureau's finding that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line," and called it "the closing of a great historic movement." Three years earlier the naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan had argued, in *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890), that the economy would soon produce more than the country could absorb, so the United States should seek markets abroad, guarded by a battleship navy and bases.

In 1898 the country acted. After the battleship *Maine* sank in Havana harbor on February 15, the United States went to war with Spain in April and had a cease-fire by August 12, the day a joint resolution made Hawaii a U.S. territory. The Treaty of Paris that December guaranteed Cuba's independence, and Spain ceded Guam and Puerto Rico and sold the Philippines for $20 million. Filipinos who wanted independence, not a new ruler, fought on. The Philippine-American War of 1899 to 1902 killed over 4,200 American and over 20,000 Filipino combatants, and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died from violence, famine and disease.

The American empire stayed smaller than Europe's. Secretary of State John Hay's Open Door notes of 1899 and 1900 asked only for equal trading opportunity in China, where the United States had little clout and no territory. And the new world power kept a small army: 154,914 Americans were on active duty in 1913, and defense took roughly 0.7 percent of the economy, close to the normal peacetime level before World War II that the economic historian John Joseph Wallis finds, about 1 percent of national output.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how did America's turn outward after 1890 resemble Europe's empires, and how did it differ?">
  Like Europe, it took colonies and fought a colonial war in the Philippines. Unlike Europe, it took few, leaned on trade access and naval bases, and kept an army of about 155,000.
</Checkpoint>

## What the era settled, and what is still argued

Some things are settled. By 1913 the United States made nearly a third of the world's manufactured goods, and its people produced more per head than the British. The gains were real, and so were the costs, which fell hardest on those kept out or held down: Chinese laborers barred, Native nations stripped of land, Black Southerners segregated by law, and strikers who met troops.

What is still argued is how to sort the advantages.

Resources look like geography, but David and Wright argue that American mineral abundance was "socially constructed," the product of legal, institutional and technological adaptation, which makes it a choice as much as a gift. The tariff was a policy, but whether it helped at all is the first section's argument. And in Richard White's reading, the railroads were public subsidy and private corruption as much as enterprise.
