# What did America gain from opening to the world, and who paid?

On March 8, 2000, at the Paul H. Nitze School of Advanced International Studies, President Bill Clinton asked Congress to make China’s normal trade status permanent. “Economically, this agreement is the equivalent of a one-way street,” he said: China would cut its tariffs, while “all we do is to agree to maintain the present access which China enjoys.” For the first time, he promised, “we’ll be able to export products without exporting jobs.” Congress agreed, China joined the World Trade Organization in December 2001, and by 2007 American factories employed about 3.4 million fewer people than in 2000. What did a deal that changed none of America’s tariffs have to do with that?

## Why did the 1980s begin with a recession?

In March 1980, consumer prices were rising at close to 15 percent a year. Paul Volcker, chairman of the [[term:federal-reserve]] since August 1979, set out to break the [[term:inflation]] Americans had come to expect, and by early 1981 the Fed had let the rate banks charge one another overnight approach 20 percent. The recession of 1981 and 1982 pushed unemployment to nearly 11 percent and hit hardest those who built things: by the end of 1982, unemployment was 22 percent in home building and 24 percent in auto manufacturing. Inflation fell under 5 percent and averaged 3.5 percent in the late 1980s.

Think of a thermostat everyone has learned to cheat: when workers and firms expect prices to rise 10 percent a year, they write contracts that make it so, and only a large shock breaks the loop. The image breaks in one place: a thermostat throws no one out of work.

The second remaking crossed party lines. Since 1940 a federal board had set airline fares and routes. Jimmy Carter, a Democrat, signed the Airline Deregulation Act in 1978 and the Motor Carrier Act in 1980, loosening rules that had “insulated the trucking industry from competition since 1935.” Ronald Reagan, a Republican, signed the Garn-St Germain Act in 1982, letting savings and loans make commercial loans: “All in all, I think we hit the jackpot.” Bill Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act in 1999, repealing large parts of the 1933 Glass-Steagall Act’s wall between commercial and investment banking.

The results split by industry. The economist Clifford Winston’s 1998 review found real airfares roughly 33 percent lower and truckload rates at least 75 percent lower, though “consumers have not shared equally” and truckers lost significant wages. In finance, weak thrifts used their new powers “to gamble unsuccessfully for recovery,” as a Federal Reserve history puts it, and the cleanup cost taxpayers as much as an estimated $124 billion. Deregulation was government stepping back; the cleanup was government stepping back in.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did the Volcker disinflation and the deregulation of transportation and finance each buy, and who paid?">
  Volcker bought an end to high inflation with a deep recession, hardest on construction and autos. Deregulation bought cheaper travel and shipping at the cost of some workers’ wages, and in finance, risks that taxpayers ended up covering.
</Checkpoint>

## What did America win when the Cold War ended?

The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, and on December 25, 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as president of a Soviet Union that was breaking into independent republics. The [[term:cold-war]] rival that had organized American strategy for four decades was gone, leaving the United States in a position many scholars call [[term:hegemony]].

The country cashed part of the peace. Defense spending fell from 5.1 percent of GDP in 1990 to 2.9 percent in 2000, active-duty forces from about 2.04 million to 1.38 million, and federal outlays from 21.2 to 17.7 percent of GDP.

What the victory meant was argued from the start, most famously by the political scientists [[person:francis-fukuyama]] and Samuel Huntington.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="The end of history">
    In “The End of History?” (1989), Fukuyama argued that the contest of ideas was over, with fascism defeated and communism losing its believers: “What we may be witnessing is not just the end of the Cold War … but the end of history as such: that is, the end point of mankind’s ideological evolution and the universalization of Western liberal democracy as the final form of human government.” Big wars, he argued, needed “large states still caught in the grip of history,” and those seemed to be passing. It was no happy ending: “The end of history will be a very sad time.”
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The clash of civilizations">
    In “The Clash of Civilizations?” (1993), Huntington answered that ideology had been one phase of conflict, not its end: “The great divisions among humankind and the dominating source of conflict will be cultural.” Western ideas such as democracy and free markets “often have little resonance” elsewhere, and many states would try “to modernize but not to Westernize.” On this view 1991 was a moment of Western power, not a universal civilization’s arrival.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Historians disagree, too, about why the Soviet Union fell. Peter Schweizer’s *Victory* (1994) credits Reagan-era pressure; Stephen Kotkin’s *Armageddon Averted* (2001) argues it collapsed from within.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what did the end of the Cold War give the United States, and what did Fukuyama and Huntington disagree about?">
  It removed the rival that had organized American strategy and let defense spending fall from 5.1 to 2.9 percent of GDP. Fukuyama saw liberal democracy’s victory as an idea; Huntington foresaw conflict along lines of culture.
</Checkpoint>

Test the two readings on a new case.

<Checkpoint question="From 1978 on, China’s economy grew by an average of more than 9 percent a year, while its Communist Party kept its monopoly on power. Which perspective does that record trouble more, and how would its defenders answer?">
  It troubles Fukuyama’s more: he expected “mounting pressure for change in the political system” as China’s economy opened. His defenders answer that he claimed a victory of ideas, which would “govern the material world in the long run.” Huntington’s “to modernize but not to Westernize” fits, though China grew inside Western-built trade rules.
</Checkpoint>

America’s share of world output fell because others, above all China, grew faster. At market exchange rates, a different yardstick, China rose from 1.7 to 7.2 percent of world output between 1990 and 2008, and in 2010 it passed the United States in manufacturing.

George H. W. Bush signed the North American Free Trade Agreement in 1992; in 1993 the House approved it 234 to 200, with 132 Republicans and 102 Democrats for and 156 Democrats against. Lorenzo Caliendo and Fernando Parro find it raised Mexico’s welfare by 1.31 percent and America’s by 0.08 percent; Shushanik Hakobyan and John McLaren find it “dramatically lowering wage growth for blue-collar workers” in the most exposed industries and places.

China was the bigger shock. Since 1980 Chinese goods had paid the low [[term:tariff]] rates most partners paid, about 4 percent on average in 1999, but only while Congress renewed that status each year; without renewal, rates would have jumped to levels first set in 1930, about 36 percent. Permanent status changed no rate anyone paid. Picture land leased by the year, at a rent that might jump ninefold at any renewal: a permanent lease changes nothing on the bill and everything about whether anyone builds a factory. Justin Pierce and Peter Schott find that the industries most exposed to the removed threat lost the most jobs. The image breaks in one place: China’s own reforms, which moved over 150 million workers from farm to city, were pushing exports up regardless.

Three economists, David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson, measured the result. Chinese goods rose from 0.6 percent of American spending on goods in 1991 to 4.6 percent in 2007, and in their main estimate this competition explains one-quarter of the fall in manufacturing jobs from 1990 to 2007; with two coauthors they put net job losses at 2.0 to 2.4 million from 1999 to 2011. The losses were local: Tennessee, with its furniture makers, was far more exposed than Alabama, with its heavy industry, and in exposed places wages stayed depressed “for at least a full decade.” There the rise in disability payments was over thirty times the rise in Trade Adjustment Assistance, which paid about $2 a person in 2007.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Gains from trade">
    Most trade economists hold this view, which protects the income and choice open markets create: gains are large but spread thin, and losses are visible. Gary Hufbauer and Zhiyao Lu put the payoff to the United States from trade expansion between 1950 and 2016 at $2.1 trillion, about $18,000 per household. “Trade typically favors the poor, who concentrate spending in more traded sectors,” find Pablo Fajgelbaum and Amit Khandelwal. Abroad, China’s growth lifted almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty. The remedy is to cushion the displaced, not to close markets.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Concentrated costs">
    Economists who study trade’s local effects hold this view, which protects the workers and towns that bore the adjustment: gains spread across a nation of shoppers never reached the town whose plant closed. Dani Rodrik warned in 1997 that open markets divided people with the skills and mobility to flourish from people without them. The evidence came later: losses lasting a decade, and trade assistance so small that disability rolls did the cushioning. The winners never compensated the losers.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Strengthened rival">
    Security strategists hold this view, which protects America’s position against a peer, because trade policy is also power policy. Clinton promised that China would “import one of democracy’s most cherished values, economic freedom”; critics answered, as he quoted them, “China is a growing threat to Taiwan and its neighbors. We shouldn’t strengthen it.” By 2008 China made 14.5 percent of the world’s manufacturing value added, to America’s 17.6. In 2018 President Trump’s trade office told Congress that “the United States erred in supporting China’s entry into the WTO,” and two former Obama officials, Kurt Campbell and Ely Ratner, wrote that “diplomatic and commercial engagement have not brought political and economic openness.”
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how could trade with China raise the nation’s income and still leave particular places worse off for a decade?">
  The gains spread thinly across the country, while the losses landed on towns whose industries met Chinese imports. Workers there did not move or retrain quickly, and the program meant to help them was tiny.
</Checkpoint>

Now try all three views on a new case.

<Checkpoint question="A new trade deal ends tariffs on imported bicycles. Bike prices fall nationwide, and a county whose two bicycle plants employ most of its workers loses both. What would each perspective stress, and what does the China-shock evidence predict for the county ten years on?">
  The gains view stresses the many buyers who pay less, especially poorer families. The costs view stresses the county, which the evidence says will still be depressed a decade later. The rival view asks who now makes the bicycles.
</Checkpoint>

## Who shared in the new economy?

The era’s other revolution began inside government. The internet started as a Defense Department experiment in the late 1960s; the National Science Foundation’s network linked about 2,000 computers in 1986 and more than 2 million by 1993. The World Wide Web came from Europe: Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist at CERN, invented it in 1989. Households with internet access rose from 18 percent in 1997 to 42 percent in 2000, and Federal Reserve economists credited information technology with about two-thirds of the late-1990s step-up in productivity growth. The foundations were public, some of them foreign; the companies were private.

The country also changed who lived in it. The foreign-born share rose from 4.7 percent in 1970 to 7.9 percent in 1990 and 12.9 percent in 2010, the highest since 1920. Reagan’s Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 paired penalties on employers of unauthorized workers with a path to legal status, which nearly 2.7 million people took. A National Academies panel found in 2016 that over ten years or more the effect on native-born workers’ wages overall “is very small,” with any losses falling mostly on earlier immigrants and native-born high-school dropouts.

The gains were large and lopsided. Between 1979 and 2012 the top 1 percent’s share of household income rose from 10.0 to 22.5 percent, and the earnings gap between college-educated and high-school-educated men working full time doubled, from $17,411 to $34,969 in 2012 dollars. From 1980 to 2012, real hourly earnings of college-educated men rose 20 to 56 percent, while those of men with only a high-school diploma fell 11 percent. Autor, who assembled these figures, points to computers taking over routine tasks and to trade with low-wage countries. And Raj Chetty’s team found that the share of children out-earning their parents fell from about 90 percent for those born in 1940 to 50 percent for those born in the 1980s.

Places pulled apart too. Peter Ganong and Daniel Shoag document “a dramatic decline” over thirty years in the rate at which poorer states caught up with richer ones, and trace much of it to housing: high prices in rich places kept less-skilled workers from moving there.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: who gained most from the new economy of 1980 to 2008, and who gained least?">
  The richest and the best-educated gained most: the top 1 percent’s share of income more than doubled and the college earnings gap doubled. Men without degrees, and places whose industries met Chinese imports, gained least or lost ground.
</Checkpoint>

Apply that to one worker.

<Checkpoint question="A home health aide in a town that lost its furniture plant hears that pay is higher in a booming coastal metro. Using this section, what is likely to keep her where she is, and what does that predict for the gap between the two places?">
  Housing: Ganong and Shoag find that high prices in rich places eat up the wage gain for less-skilled workers, so fewer move. When workers cannot follow the jobs, the gap between places stops closing.
</Checkpoint>

## What this era settled, and what it left open

Some of the record is settled: Volcker’s recession ended high inflation, transportation deregulation lowered prices, the China shock cost particular places jobs and wages for a decade, and the internet rose on public research. What stays contested is how to sort the era’s advantages.

Two items sit on the borders. If American pressure hastened the Soviet collapse, as Schweizer argues, 1991 was partly a choice; if the system fell from within, as Kotkin argues, it was another nation’s trouble. If the China shock came from permanent trade status, as Pierce and Schott find, it was a choice; if mainly from China’s own reforms, as Autor, Dorn and Hanson emphasize, it happened to America. Whether engagement with China was a mistake, and what the winners owed the losers, are value questions.

The era ended in the financial crisis of 2008. Lesson 10 takes up that crisis, China’s rise, the politics of the places left behind, and what 250 years do and do not tell us about what comes next.
