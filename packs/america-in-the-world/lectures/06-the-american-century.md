February 17, 1941. The United States is not yet at war.

In Life magazine, its publisher, Henry Luce, prints an editorial. He calls it The American Century.

Americans, he writes, should accept their duty as "the most powerful and vital nation in the world." [beat] And they should use their influence "for such purposes as we see fit and by such means as we see fit."

[pause]

Less than ten months later, Japan attacks Pearl Harbor. Within ten years, the United States has won the war. It has tied its allies' currencies to the dollar. It has paid for much of Western Europe's recovery, and promised to defend it.

So here is the question. How much of that was American achievement? [beat] And how much was the wreckage of everyone else?

By the end, you will be able to sort America's postwar advantages into three kinds. Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] And the troubles of other nations. Hold on to those three. I will ask for them later.

We will take it in four stops. First, the war, and what it did to America and to everyone else. Second, the dollar and the Marshall Plan. Third, how wartime allies became enemies. And last, who shared in the boom, and who was kept out.

## One power left standing

First, America itself. In mid-1940, about 458,000 Americans were on active military duty. By mid-1945, about 12 million were.

In 1940, federal spending came to 9.6 percent of the economy. By 1945 it was more than two fifths. Defense alone took more than a third.

[beat]

Washington set the targets and paid the bills. Private companies built the ships and the planes. Whatever the story of self-reliance, the war economy was directed from Washington, on a scale never seen before.

The war also decided who could work, and who was pushed aside.

In June 1941, the labor leader A. Philip Randolph threatened a march on Washington. Roosevelt answered by barring discrimination in defense industries. The armed forces themselves stayed segregated until Truman ordered an end to it in 1948.

In 1942, an executive order was used to force about 122,000 people of Japanese ancestry into camps. Nearly 70,000 of them were American citizens.

And from 1942 to 1964, the Bracero Program brought Mexican farmworkers north on temporary contracts. More than four million of them came, often for meager wages.

[pause]

Now look abroad, where the scale was different. The National WWII Museum warns that its estimates vary widely. By its count, the war killed about 418,500 Americans. It killed about 24 million people in the Soviet Union, and about 20 million in China.

The forty-eight states saw no invasion, and no bombing campaign. Every other great power ended the war invaded, bombed or exhausted.

By the Maddison Project's estimates, America's output per person rose about 27 percent across the 1940s. [beat] Western Europe's barely moved.

So the American share of the world's output grew. It was about 21 percent in 1940. It was about 27 percent in 1950. And Americans were only about 6 percent of the world's people.

> By 1950, Americans made more than a quarter of the world's output.

One caution. You may have heard that in 1945, America made half the world's output. No source this lesson could verify supports that. The Maddison Project has no world total for 1945 at all.

[pause]

Put another way, the American edge was built two ways at once. America grew. [beat] And its rivals were ruined.

Let's test that. Imagine a small neutral country. Its factories came through the war as untouched as America's. Why would it not have become a superpower in 1945? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because being spared only turned into dominance at America's scale. America had about 6 percent of the world's people, and a continent's resources. A small country spared the same damage still has a small economy.

## A world built around the dollar

That is the war. Now the money.

In July 1944, delegates from 44 nations met at Bretton Woods, in New Hampshire, to design the money system for the peace. They worked from rival plans by Harry Dexter White of the United States Treasury and John Maynard Keynes of Britain.

They created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. And they created a system of fixed exchange rates. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. Under Bretton Woods, members fixed their currencies against the dollar. And the dollar was fixed in gold, at 35 dollars an ounce.

> Bretton Woods tied members' currencies to the dollar, and the dollar to gold.

Think of a wheel. Every currency is a spoke, bolted to the dollar. The dollar is the hub, and the hub is bolted to gold.

[beat]

The picture breaks in one place. A hub has no interests of its own. This one did. America issued the money everyone needed, and it promised a gold backing. A later lecture in this series shows that it could not keep that promise.

Europe's problem was a shortage of dollars. In 1946 and 1947, its deficit in trade and payments ran at about 9 billion dollars a year. Private lenders could not fill the gap.

On June 5, 1947, Secretary of State George Marshall offered help, in a speech at Harvard. Listen for what he says the policy is against. [beat] "Our policy is directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos."

[pause]

Hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos.

Truman signed the law on April 3, 1948. Over the next four years, Congress appropriated 13.3 billion dollars for European recovery.

The Soviet Union kept itself and Eastern Europe out. The State Department's historians trace that refusal to Soviet concern over potential American economic domination.

The aid came as American goods, so exporters and farmers backed it.

How big was it? The aid came to a little over 1 percent of America's national income each year. For the countries receiving it, it came to around 2 percent of their output.

Scholars agree on that size. [beat] They disagree about what the money did. There are two main views.

The first is the generosity-and-strategy view. Its holders include the economist Barry Eichengreen, and the historian Michael Hogan. It guards a claim about design: that timing and terms can matter more than size.

Their strongest argument is that the aid's conditions pushed Europe toward freer trade and markets. Their evidence includes France and Italy, where the aid eased political fights over stabilizing budgets. Eichengreen and J. Bradford De Long call it "history's most successful structural adjustment program." And Hogan adds the strategy. American officials hoped to remake Western Europe as a smaller version of America's own mixed economy.

> The generosity-and-strategy view: the aid set the stage for Europe's growth.

The second is the overstated-effect view. Its leading voice is the economic historian Alan Milward, writing in 1984. It guards the credit owed to Europe's own governments and institutions.

Their strongest argument is that the aid was too small to do much for growth by paying for investment. And Washington could not rebuild Europe to its own blueprint. The payments union Europeans built fell short of what Americans wanted. Their evidence includes the economist Nicholas Crafts's survey of the research. He concludes that "the direct effects were positive but modest."

> The overstated-effect view: the aid was too small to drive Europe's recovery.

Both sides accept the size. The argument is over what a small sum, with conditions attached, could change. And that is a contested question, not a settled one.

## Allies into rivals

That is the money. Now the turn from allies to enemies.

On March 12, 1947, President Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey. It is a long sentence, so listen for who he promises to support.

"I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures."

[pause]

Free peoples, resisting.

That July, the diplomat George Kennan wrote anonymously in the journal Foreign Affairs. He called for "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies."

Containment meant holding Soviet power inside the lines it had already reached. Not rolling it back. Not accepting its spread. Holding it where it stood.

Put another way, containment was a fence, not a war. And it became the organizing idea of the Cold War.

On April 4, 1949, the United States, Canada and ten Western European countries signed the North Atlantic Treaty, founding NATO. Its Article 5 says that "an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all."

An attack on one is an attack on all. The State Department's historians call NATO the first peacetime military alliance America entered outside its own hemisphere.

[beat]

Kennan saw the Soviet threat as mainly political. But in 1950, a planning paper called NSC 68 asked for a major military buildup. And the Korean War settled the argument.

National defense had taken 4.9 percent of the economy in 1950. By 1953 it took 13.8 percent.

Leaving office in January 1961, President Eisenhower warned of the "unwarranted influence" of what he called the military-industrial complex.

[pause]

Why did the Cold War happen at all? Historians have argued along three lines. Here is the ground they share. The wartime alliance broke apart within a few years of victory. What they dispute is who moved first, and why.

The first is the orthodox view. It was the Truman administration's own account. Historians such as Arthur Schlesinger Junior defended it in 1967.

Its holders argue that the Cold War was the free world's response to Communist expansion. It guards the claim that American policy was defensive, and necessary. Their evidence is Soviet conduct. Governments imposed across Eastern Europe. Pressure on Greece and Turkey. A coup in Czechoslovakia in 1948, and the Berlin crisis.

And when communist sources became available, the historian John Lewis Gaddis drew on them in 1997. He argued that Stalin bore primary responsibility.

> The orthodox view: Soviet expansion started the Cold War, and America responded.

The second is the revisionist view. It begins with the historian William Appleman Williams, in his 1959 book, The Tragedy of American Diplomacy.

Its holders argue that American policy had long been driven by the Open Door. That is the conviction that American prosperity required open markets abroad. It guards the claim that the stronger power carries the larger share of the blame.

On this view, Washington's push for open economies in Europe looked to Moscow like a threat. Their evidence is America's own planning about markets, and Moscow's stated reason for refusing the Marshall Plan. The tragedy, for Williams, was genuine ideals undercut by an informal empire.

> The revisionist view: America, chasing open markets, bore more of the blame.

The third is the post-revisionist view. Its holders include Gaddis himself, in his earlier work from 1972, and the historian Melvyn Leffler, writing in 1992.

Its holders argue that neither older school captured how the conflict was made. It guards the claim that the causes were many. Gaddis traced American policy to domestic politics, bureaucratic habit, personalities, and perceptions of Soviet intentions. Leffler argues that officials feared the disorder of a ruined Europe and Asia more than the Soviet army.

Their evidence is the archives. They show fear and misreading on both sides.

> The post-revisionist view: fear and misreading on both sides made the Cold War.

[beat]

And the schools still move. Gaddis, once the leading post-revisionist, moved toward the orthodox view as Soviet sources opened.

Let's practice telling them apart. A historian writes this. Neither Truman nor Stalin wanted war. Each took the other's moves to secure his own side as a bid for more. And each answer confirmed the other's fear. Which school is that closest to?

[long pause]

The post-revisionist school. It finds the cause in both sides' fears, rather than in Soviet expansion or in American markets.

One more question, and this one I will leave with you. Of the three accounts, take the one you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## Who shared in the boom

The last stop is home.

On June 22, 1944, the Servicemen's Readjustment Act became law. People soon called it the GI Bill. It paid veterans' tuition and living costs. It offered unemployment pay. And it helped them buy homes and businesses.

Within seven years, about 8 million veterans had used its education benefits. About 2.3 million of them went to colleges. And by 1955, it had helped finance 4.3 million home loans.

The suburb is remembered as the work of self-made families, and they did work hard. But the degree and the mortgage often rested on a federal benefit.

[beat]

The law's text made no distinction by race. But its delivery ran through local banks, local colleges and local offices.

Black veterans often could not get bank loans for homes in Black neighborhoods. And discrimination kept many out of white suburbs.

The economists Sarah Turner and John Bound studied the results in 2003. The GI Bill raised college attainment for white men, and for Black men born outside the South. It did little for Black veterans limited to the South's segregated colleges.

The political scientist Ira Katznelson, in a 2005 book, argues that the law was built to accommodate Jim Crow, by leaving it to local officials.

> The GI Bill's words were equal. Its delivery was local.

In plainer words: equal on paper, unequal in practice.

Immigration ran at a low ebb. The national-origins quotas of 1924 still decided who could come.

In 1940, 8.8 percent of residents had been born abroad. By 1950 it was 6.9 percent. That was the lowest share since the census began asking, in 1850.

The war opened the door only a crack, and for strategy's sake. In 1943 Congress repealed Chinese exclusion. The State Department's historians call that a decision driven almost wholly by the war. China got about 105 visas a year.

Then in 1952, the McCarran-Walter Act ended Asian exclusion. But it kept the national-origins quotas, and Congress passed it over Truman's veto.

## What to keep

At the start, I gave you three kinds of advantage. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] And the troubles of other nations.

Geography and timing: two oceans, a late entry into the war, and a country big enough to turn being spared into an advantage.

Choices and policy: Bretton Woods, the Marshall Plan, NATO and the GI Bill. Each could have gone another way. So could the choices that left people out, from the camps of 1942 to the quotas of 1952.

The troubles of other nations: part of America's share in 1950 was the absence of rivals.

[pause]

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. By 1950, Americans made more than a quarter of the world's output. Part of that was American growth, and part was everyone else's ruin.

[beat]

Two. Bretton Woods tied members' currencies to the dollar, and the dollar to gold. The Marshall Plan then supplied the dollars Europe lacked. Whether that aid set the stage for Europe's growth, or was too small to drive it, is still argued.

[beat]

Three. Containment became the organizing idea of the Cold War, and NATO made it a treaty. Orthodox, revisionist, post-revisionist. [beat] Three answers to who moved first.

[beat]

And four. The GI Bill's words were equal. Its delivery was local. Prosperity was shared widely, but not with everyone.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. The numbers are settled, and so is the war's unequal toll. The sorting is argued.

Revisionists see the choices as an informal empire. Defenders see generosity joined to self-interest. And Milward's readers credit Europeans with much of the success.

One fact cuts both ways. As Europe and Japan rebuilt, the American share of world output fell back, to about 22 percent by 1970. Taken one way, the 1950 peak was other nations' ruin. Taken another, the policies worked. A rebuilt Western Europe was what Washington had paid for.

[pause]

Luce's American Century had arrived. And so had a contradiction at its center. A country that told the world it stood for free peoples still segregated its own citizens by law, across much of the South.

The next lecture follows the movement that forced that contradiction into the open. It runs from Brown versus Board of Education, in 1954, to the Civil Rights Act of 1964. And it asks how far the Cold War pushed the country to face it.

[pause]

Go back to February 1941. A magazine publisher tells a country still at peace to use its power for purposes it sees fit. [beat] Within ten years, it has that power. Partly by its own choices. And partly because every rival was invaded, bombed or exhausted.
