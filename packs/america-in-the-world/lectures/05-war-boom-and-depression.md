July 31, 1914. New York. [beat] The New York Stock Exchange does not open.

War has just broken out in Europe. Foreign investors are rushing to sell American stocks and bonds, and to turn the money into gold.

So the Treasury Secretary, William McAdoo, keeps the exchange shut. It stays shut until December 12.

[pause]

Hold on to that picture. In the summer of 1914, the United States owed the rest of the world more than the world owed it.

Five years later, Europe's governments owed the United States Treasury more than ten billion dollars.

> In five years, America went from the world's debtor to its creditor.

This lecture follows that reversal to 1941. By the end, you will be able to explain how a war made America the creditor, why its boom collapsed, and what the New Deal built and left out.

We will take it in four stops. First, the war that turned the money around. Second, the boom, and who was let in to share it. Third, the crash, and why it went so deep. And last, the New Deal, and the road back out into the world.

## The war that reversed the flow

Start in mid-1914. Foreigners held about 7.2 billion dollars of investments in the United States. Americans owned about 3.5 billion dollars privately abroad. [beat] Roughly half as much.

So the country was a net debtor: it owed the world more than it was owed.

Then the war turned the flow around. For more than two years, the United States stayed neutral. In the words of the State Department's historians, it was "supplying munitions and financial assistance to the Allies."

In April 1917, Germany's renewed submarine attacks brought America into the war. Woodrow Wilson told Congress, "The world must be made safe for democracy."

In 1913 the country had about 155,000 people on active military duty. In 1918 it had nearly 2.9 million. And its Treasury lent the Allies more than ten billion dollars.

By 1919, the private numbers had flipped. Americans' investments abroad had doubled, to about 7.0 billion dollars. Foreign holdings in the United States had fallen to about 3.3 billion. The figures from before 1919 are rough, but the reversal is not in doubt.

[pause]

Now here is the turn. The peace did not follow Wilson's design. The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, in November 1919 and again in March 1920. The United States never joined the League of Nations.

But it could not step out of Europe's money. Washington refused to cancel the war debts. Then, in 1922, the Fordney-McCumber Tariff Act raised tariffs. That made it harder for Europeans to sell in America, and so to earn the dollars they needed to pay.

What kept the system running was a circle. American banks lent to Germany. [beat] Germany paid reparations to Britain and France. [beat] And Britain and France paid the United States.

When the lending stopped, the circle broke. By mid-1933, every European debtor except Finland had defaulted.

Let's test that with two imaginary countries. Country A lends heavily to Country B. B can repay only by selling its goods to A. Then A raises its tariffs on B's goods. What happens next? Take a moment.

[long pause]

B struggles to repay, unless A keeps lending it more. [beat] To pay in A's currency, B has to earn it by selling to A, or borrow it from A. The tariff narrows the first road, so repayment comes to depend on fresh loans. That is the 1920s in one picture.

## Who was let in

That is the money. Now the boom, and the people.

By the Maddison Project's estimates, the average American in 1929 produced about 1.36 times as much as the average Briton.

The economic historian Paul Bairoch estimated that the United States made 32.0 percent of the world's manufactured goods in 1913. These are scholarly estimates, not official counts.

The boom had a creed. In October 1928, campaigning for president, Herbert Hoover praised "the American system of rugged individualism." He set it against European "doctrines of paternalism and state socialism."

But not everyone was let in to share the boom. The Immigration Act of 1924 set a quota for each nationality. Each could send 2 percent of its foreign-born population, as counted in the 1890 census.

Why 1890? [beat] Because that census was taken before the largest arrivals from southern and eastern Europe. So counting from 1890 limited what the State Department's historians call "newer immigration."

> In the 1924 law, choosing the base year was the policy.

Put another way, for Europe the arithmetic did the work. No country had to be named to shrink its quota.

The act also barred anyone "ineligible for citizenship" by race or nationality. That shut out immigrants from Asia. People from the Western Hemisphere were left outside the quotas. In the State Department historians' words, the law's "most basic purpose" was "to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity."

Now listen to the door closing. In fiscal 1914, about 1.2 million people became permanent residents. In fiscal 1925, about 294,000. And in fiscal 1933, with the Depression deterring them too, about 23,000.

The share of residents born abroad fell, from 13.2 percent in 1920 to 8.8 percent in 1940.

## Why the crash went so deep

So that is the boom. Now the fall.

The stock market crash of October 1929 began the slump. It does not explain how deep the slump went.

Between 1929 and 1933, real output fell by about 26 percent. Unemployment rose from 3.2 percent of the labor force to 24.9 percent. [beat] Roughly one worker in four.

About 9,000 banks suspended operations from 1930 to 1933, with no deposit insurance behind them. And from late 1930 to early 1933, the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent.

Prices fell with it. A general fall in prices is called deflation. Deflation made every debt heavier, because incomes fell while the sums owed did not.

[pause]

Here is a number that should not be true. By the Maddison estimates, output per American fell by a third from 1929 to 1933. In 1933 and 1934, the average Briton was ahead again. [beat] The country America had overtaken briefly overtook it back.

The world was turning inward at the same time. A thousand economists asked President Hoover to veto a new tariff, called Smoot-Hawley. He signed it on June 17, 1930. Scholars disagree about how much harm it did. But the State Department's historians conclude that it "did nothing to foster cooperation among nations." World trade fell by some 66 percent from 1929 to 1934.

Underneath ran the gold standard. A dollar was a promise of a fixed weight of gold, and the system tied countries' interest rates together. When Britain left gold on September 21, 1931, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York raised its discount rate to stop gold leaving, in the middle of a banking panic. The United States suspended gold in April 1933.

[pause]

Now the question, set on ground nobody owns. Economists agree on most of these facts. What they argue about is which facts mattered most. There are three main answers.

The first is monetary contraction. It comes from the economists Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, in A Monetary History of the United States, from 1963. It warns against a central bank that stands aside in a panic. They argued that the Federal Reserve turned a recession into a catastrophe, by letting bank failures shrink the money supply. Their evidence is the size and timing of the monetary collapse.

That argument won over the Fed itself. In 2002, Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor, told Friedman, "Regarding the Great Depression. You're right, we did it. We're very sorry."

> The monetary-contraction case: the Fed let the money supply collapse.

The second is the gold standard. It comes from the economist Barry Eichengreen, in a book called Golden Fetters, from 1992. It warns against a fixed system that ties every country's hands. He argued that the gold standard spread the slump worldwide. To protect their gold, central banks raised interest rates in a depression.

His evidence is a natural experiment. Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs found, in 1985, that countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner. And Charles Kindleberger, writing in 1973, argued that the system needed a leader to keep markets open and lend in a crisis. Britain no longer could. [beat] The United States would not.

> The gold-standard case: gold spread the slump and tied central banks' hands.

The third is spending and debt. It comes from the economist Peter Temin, writing in 1976, in the tradition of John Maynard Keynes. It warns against blaming money alone. Temin argued that the first blow was a fall in spending, not in money. Irving Fisher argued in 1933 that debts and falling prices fed each other. And Bernanke's own study of 1983 argued that bank failures also cut off lending to small firms and farmers. Their evidence is that spending and credit collapsed in ways the money supply alone does not explain.

> The spending-and-debt case: spending and credit collapsed, not only money.

What can the evidence settle? Most of the facts, but not their weights. The argument is over the first push, and how much each cause mattered. [beat] That is a contested question, not a settled one.

Let's test the gold-standard case. Two countries fall into a slump in 1930. One leaves gold in 1931. The other holds on until 1936. On Eichengreen's argument, which one recovers first? Think it through.

[long pause]

The one that left gold. [beat] Leaving gold freed its central bank to let money and credit grow, instead of defending its reserves. That is what Eichengreen and Sachs found: the countries that left earlier recovered sooner.

## What the New Deal built, and who it left out

So that is the fall. Now the response.

Franklin Roosevelt was inaugurated in March 1933, and he closed the banks for a national holiday. In June, the Banking Act of 1933 created federal deposit insurance.

The New Deal added work relief and farm programs. It added the National Industrial Recovery Act, which let industries set prices and wages together, until the Supreme Court struck it down. In August 1935 came the Social Security Act, with old-age insurance funded by payroll taxes, and unemployment insurance. The Wagner Act, the same year, protected workers' right to organize.

Federal spending rose from 3.4 percent of the economy in 1930 to 9.6 percent in 1940. [beat] It roughly tripled.

Did it work? The results look mixed. Real output passed its 1929 level in 1936. Unemployment came down to 14.3 percent in 1937. But it rose to 19.0 percent in the relapse of 1938.

Here again there are two sides.

The first is recovery and reform. Its defenders want to protect the case for a government that acts in a slump. They point to fast growth after 1933, the year Roosevelt took the dollar off gold. The economist Christina Romer found that nearly all the recovery before 1942 came from monetary expansion. It was fed by gold flowing in after the devaluation, and by capital fleeing Europe after 1935.

The economist Price Fishback found that relief and public works spending raised state incomes about one for one. And the economist Michael Darby argued that counting relief workers as employed cuts the official unemployment rates, from 1934 to 1941, by 4 to 7 points.

> The case for recovery and reform: leaving gold and relief brought recovery.

The second is that the New Deal prolonged the slump. Its critics want to protect the case for markets that set their own wages and prices. They point to how slowly jobs came back. The economists Harold Cole and Lee Ohanian argued, in 2004, that New Deal policies toward industry and labor held real wages well above trend. So firms hired fewer workers. In their model, that was an important reason the economy failed to return to its normal path. Critics also point to Fishback's finding that farm programs mostly helped large owners, at the expense of tenants and farm workers.

> The case that it prolonged the slump: wage and price rules held jobs back.

And one criticism comes from the other side. The economist E. Cary Brown argued in 1956 that deficit spending was never tried at full scale.

[pause]

You have now heard both sides at their strongest. One of them convinced you less. Take that one, and state its best case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

Now the edges of the protections. The Social Security Act's old-age insurance excluded "agricultural labor" and "domestic service in a private home." The Wagner Act left out the same two groups.

> Farm and domestic workers were left out of the first protections.

Who were they? By the 1930 census, about 65 percent of Black workers were in those jobs. For white workers, the figure was 27 percent. Yet about 74 percent of all the workers excluded were white.

Why was the line drawn there? Scholars disagree. Robert Lieberman, Ira Katznelson and Mary Poole argue that race shaped it. In Lieberman's and Katznelson's accounts, Southern Democrats would support a national program only if the South's segregated farm and household labor stayed outside it.

Gareth Davies and Martha Derthick argue that Southern Democrats were not the source. Larry DeWitt, a historian at the Social Security Administration, traces the exclusions to the Treasury Secretary, Henry Morgenthau. In February 1935, Morgenthau told Congress that payroll taxes could not practically be collected from farms and households. Most of these workers were covered in 1950, and the rest in 1954.

Both sides agree on the result. [beat] The workers with the least to fall back on were the last to be covered.

## The road back out, and what to keep

Abroad, the 1930s were the high tide of isolationism. Critics blamed bankers and arms traders for drawing the country into the last war, and Congress listened. In 1935 it banned arms sales to nations at war. In 1936 it banned loans to them.

Then war broke out in Europe in 1939, and the rules bent, step by step. Arms sales for cash, in November 1939. More than 50 old destroyers, traded for British bases in September 1940. And on December 29, 1940, Roosevelt told the country, "We must be the great arsenal of democracy."

On March 11, 1941, he signed a House bill numbered 1776, "An Act to Promote the Defense of the United States." [beat] We know it as Lend-Lease. Over the war, it sent some 50 billion dollars in aid to more than 30 countries.

[pause]

Now sort the era's advantages into three kinds.

Geography and timing: an ocean between America and the fighting, a late entry into the first war, and a home market of about 122 million people by 1929.

[beat]

Choices and policy: demanding repayment while raising tariffs, closing the door in 1924, defending gold and then leaving it, and Lend-Lease.

[beat]

And the troubles of other nations: Europe's war made America the creditor, Britain could no longer steady the world economy, and capital fleeing Europe sent gold to America.

Some items fit more than one box. The economist William Silber argues that McAdoo's choices in 1914 began the dollar's rise toward a world currency. On that view, the creditor position was partly a policy achievement. And Romer's evidence makes the recovery partly a product of Europe's troubles.

Before we gather it up, pull something back. Earlier I gave you three explanations for why the Depression went so deep. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Money. [beat] Gold. [beat] And spending and debt.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. In five years, America went from the world's debtor to its creditor. And the war debts became a trap, because high tariffs blocked the road to repayment.

[beat]

Two. In the 1924 law, choosing the base year was the policy. Counting from 1890 cut newer immigration, and the law shut out Asia entirely.

[beat]

Three. The crash began the slump. Banks, money, gold and debt made it a depression, and the three explanations still compete over the weights.

[beat]

Four. The New Deal made Washington an insurer of bank deposits and of old age. Whether it sped recovery or prolonged the slump is still argued. And farm and domestic workers were left out of the first protections.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. What made the Depression so deep. Whether the New Deal sped recovery or slowed it. Why its protections stopped where they did. And how much of America's position was earned, and how much was handed to it by others' misfortune.

In December 1941, the attack on Pearl Harbor brought the United States into the war it had been arming. The next lecture asks how that war, and the ruin of every rival, made an American century.

For now, go back to that morning in 1914. A stock exchange that would not open, because America owed the world. [beat] Twenty-seven years later, America was lending the world the means to fight.
