Paris. April 11, 1803.

The American minister, Robert Livingston, is trying to buy one city. [beat] New Orleans. He and James Monroe have authority to pay up to 10 million dollars, for the city and the Floridas.

That day, the French foreign minister, Talleyrand, changes the question. He asks whether the Americans wish to have the whole of Louisiana.

[pause]

The whole of Louisiana. Less than three weeks later, the Americans signed for all of it. About 828,000 square miles, for 15 million dollars. That is roughly 4 cents an acre.

The United States doubled in size in one treaty. [beat] And the reason lay on an island in the Caribbean.

Its census counted 9.6 million people in 1820. By 1860 it counted 31.4 million, and the country reached the Pacific.

By the end, you will be able to say where that growth came from, and who paid for it.

This series sorts each era's advantages into three kinds. [beat] Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] And the troubles of other nations. I will ask for those three later.

We will take it in four stops. First, why Napoleon sold. Second, whose land the West was. Third, how much the economy rested on slavery. And last, the canals, railroads and newcomers of the market revolution.

## Why Napoleon sold

In 1800, France acquired Louisiana from Spain, and Napoleon Bonaparte had a plan for it.

He wanted to rebuild a French empire in the Americas. Its prize was Saint-Domingue, a sugar colony in the Caribbean. In 1791, the enslaved people there had risen against slavery. Napoleon meant to retake the colony, and to use Louisiana as the granary that fed it.

Americans watched with alarm. The produce of the West reached the ocean through New Orleans. Jefferson put it this way. "There is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural and habitual enemy." [beat] "It is New Orleans."

[pause]

Then the plan died, in Saint-Domingue.

Napoleon sent an army under his brother-in-law, General Leclerc. It fought a revolution, led first by Toussaint Louverture, and then by Jean-Jacques Dessalines. And it fought yellow fever. At one point, the fever was killing 100 to 120 men a day.

With his army dying, and a new war with Britain threatening, Napoleon gave up on Louisiana.

> Louisiana was for sale because of a revolution in the Caribbean.

Put another way, the American bargain was made possible by other people's struggle. France's plan failed, and in 1804 the colony became independent Haiti.

Here is a hard fact to hold beside that one. The United States was a slaveholding republic, and it had just profited from Haiti's victory. [beat] It did not recognize Haiti until 1862.

So the Purchase is the purest case of the third kind of advantage: the troubles of other nations. But it took choices too. Jefferson read the Constitution strictly, and he could find no clause that let him buy territory. He set his doubts aside.

And there was one thing France could not hand over. What it sold was a claim. France claimed the land under a European legal theory called the Doctrine of Discovery. Native nations had lived there for millennia. The Purchase left the United States to deal with those nations directly, for their land.

Let's test that claim about cause. Suppose we learned that Napoleon had decided to sell before his army failed. What would that do to the story? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It would break it. [beat] A cause has to come before its effect. If Napoleon had already chosen to sell, the disaster in Saint-Domingue could not explain the sale.

## Whose land was the West?

That was the claim on paper. Now the people on the land.

East of the Mississippi, much of the Southeast's best land for cotton belonged to five nations. The Cherokee. [beat] The Chickasaw. [beat] The Choctaw. [beat] The Muscogee, or Creek. [beat] And the Seminole.

In 1830, Congress passed the Indian Removal Act, and President Andrew Jackson signed it. It authorized the president to negotiate removal treaties with the nations east of the river, and to send them to land in the west.

It was not a foregone conclusion. The House passed it 102 to 97, with northern members almost all opposed.

> Indian removal was a choice. It passed the House by five votes.

The removals followed. Between 1831 and 1838, all five nations were forced from their homelands, toward Indian Territory, in today's Oklahoma.

In May 1838, the army began rounding up about 19,000 Cherokees. A missionary doctor, Elizur Butler, estimated that more than 4,000 of them died along the way. That was a fifth of the Cherokee people. The road they walked became known as the Trail of Tears.

[pause]

And the Cherokee were one nation among many. The historian Jeffrey Ostler counts all the nations moved west in the 1830s and 1840s. He estimates that about 88,000 people were forced out. [beat] And he estimates that 12,000 to 17,000 of them died.

Further west came Texas, annexed in 1845. Then came war with Mexico, declared in May 1846. In the treaty of 1848, Mexico ceded about 525,000 square miles. That was 55 percent of its territory.

The phrase for all of it came from an unsigned magazine essay of 1845. It is usually credited to the editor, John L. O'Sullivan. Listen for the word Providence. He wrote of "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions."

[pause]

Manifest destiny. Was it a national vision, or a name for conquest? That is one of the oldest arguments in American history. Both sides accept the record of removal and war. They disagree about what it means.

The first reading begins with the historian Frederick Jackson Turner, in an address of 1893. Listen to how much he claims. "The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development."

[beat]

Turner argued that cheap land on a moving frontier let ordinary newcomers own farms of their own. And he argued that this gave Americans a distinct character, and a more democratic politics.

This reading protects what its holders see as a real achievement: a republic of landowning farmers. Its holders also point to the world around it. The historian Sam Haynes has shown how fear of Britain shaped the drive to annex Texas. In a continent still ringed by empires, this reading argues, a continental republic was a matter of security too.

> The national-vision reading: cheap land made American politics more democratic.

The second reading is the New Western History. Its landmark is a book from 1987, The Legacy of Conquest, by the historian Patricia Nelson Limerick. Limerick argues that the West was never free land. It was other people's country, taken by treaties signed under pressure, by removal and by war. And she argues that the contest over its land did not end when Turner declared the frontier closed.

This reading protects the people the national story left out. Its holders point to the record. A removal act that passed by five votes. Tens of thousands driven from their homes. A war that took more than half of Mexico. Ostler goes further. He argues that removal meets the definition of genocide, because the government kept pursuing it after its deadly effects were known.

> The conquest reading: the West was other people's country, taken, not found.

[pause]

The two readings collide on one word: free. The evidence settles the removals and the deaths. It cannot settle which word fits. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## Cotton, and the people who picked it

The land taken from Native nations became cotton land. And enslaved people worked it.

The census counted about 1.5 million enslaved people in 1820. By 1860 it counted nearly four million. That was about one American in eight.

Many were sold from the older states to the new cotton country. They passed through markets like New Orleans, the largest slave market in the Southwest.

Their cotton fed the mills of Britain, the heart of the Industrial Revolution. By Paul Bairoch's estimates, Britain made about a fifth of the world's manufactured goods by 1860. Southern planters captured a dominant share of the Atlantic cotton trade. And cotton was the key American export.

One more fact, because both sides will need it. The amount an enslaved worker picked in a day rose three to four times between 1800 and 1860.

[pause]

Everyone in this debate accepts those facts. Everyone accepts that slavery was profitable for slaveholders, and that it rested on violence. The argument is over how central slavery was to American growth.

The first side is the new history of capitalism. Its historians include Edward Baptist and Sven Beckert, who both published books in 2014.

Baptist argues that slavery's expansion was the driving force of American history, from the Constitution to the Civil War. He argues that picking rates rose because of an escalating system of torture. And counting cotton's direct and indirect roles, he puts it at almost half of American economic activity in 1836.

Beckert calls the system war capitalism. He means the violent seizure of land and labor, combined with new machines and wage workers. And he notes that enslaved people, counted as property, were worth more than all the North's industrial capital.

This side protects the place of the enslaved at the center of the story of American wealth. Its holders point to the testimony of formerly enslaved people, to slave-market records, and to the credit that planters raised on human collateral.

> The new historians of capitalism: slave-grown cotton was an engine of growth.

The second side is the economic historians, led in this debate by Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode. They argue that the new history gets the numbers wrong.

Baptist's own figure puts the cotton crop around 1836 at about 77 million dollars. That is some 5 percent of the economy's output. To reach almost half, the critics argue, he added sales of land and of enslaved people, which are not output. And he added inputs already counted in cotton's price.

The economist Peter Rousseau puts cotton at 5 to 6 percent of output.

On productivity, Olmstead and Rhode draw on more than 600,000 daily picking records. They argue that the gains came mainly from new cotton varieties, with larger bolls that were easier to pick.

This side protects the standard of evidence. Olmstead reports a consensus in his field that slavery was detrimental to economic development. And its holders point out that capitalism survived emancipation, and that cotton output soared after the war.

> The economic historians: cotton was about a twentieth of output, not half.

Here is a test that can favor one side. Sea Island cotton was a different variety, grown under the same system of slavery and violence. Suppose its pickers' daily rates barely rose between 1800 and 1860, while the rates of upland pickers tripled. Which reading does that help? Think it through.

[long pause]

The economic historians'. [beat] If escalating torture drove the gains, they should appear wherever the same system ruled, Sea Island plantations included. Gains confined to upland cotton point to the new upland seed. Olmstead and Rhode made exactly this comparison, and found very little increase on Sea Island plantations.

So what does the evidence settle? It settles that millions were enslaved, and that their labor was profitable and brutal. It cannot settle, on its own, how much of a nation's growth one institution caused.

[pause]

You have heard two arguments now, and four readings. Take the one you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way its own holders would.

[pause 5s]

## Canals, wires and newcomers

That is the South. Now the networks that tied the country together.

Before the Erie Canal opened, in October 1825, moving a ton of wheat from New York's Genesee Valley to Albany took 20 days by wagon. [beat] It cost 100 dollars. After the canal, a ton could reach New York City in 10 days. [beat] It cost 5 dollars.

Here is the part the legend leaves out. It was a state-owned waterway, built by New York.

The same pattern ran through the era's other networks. Railroads grew from about 3,000 miles of track in 1840, to about 30,000 by 1860.

In 1843, Congress appropriated 30,000 dollars for Samuel Morse's experimental telegraph line. On May 24, 1844, Morse sent his message from the Capitol to Baltimore. [beat] "What hath God wrought."

And in 1850, Congress gave three states public land along the route of a railroad from Chicago to Mobile. That was a subsidy, paid in land. Much of that land, the government had acquired from Native nations.

> Public money and public land helped build the market revolution.

Put another way, the self-reliant farmer was real. [beat] But he shipped his wheat on a public canal.

Now the people who came to do the work. A potato blight reached Ireland in the fall of 1845. The famine that followed killed about one million people. That was an eighth of Ireland's population. Hard times in the German states sent others.

In 1860, about seven of every ten residents born abroad had been born in Ireland or in Germany. And people born abroad made up 13.2 percent of the whole population.

The reaction was fierce, and largely anti-Catholic. In Philadelphia in 1844, a riot lasting four days burned two Catholic churches. At least 14 people died.

Then came a secret society whose members answered questions with "I know nothing." It grew into the American Party. In the Congress of 1855 to 1857, it held 51 of the House's 234 seats. Then the fight over slavery broke it apart.

[pause]

So where did this leave the United States? Two numbers carry most of it, and both are scholarly estimates.

The first is its share of the world's output. By the Maddison Project's estimates, it rose from 2.3 percent in 1820 to 5.5 percent in 1850. That is more than double, in thirty years.

The second is output per person. In 1860, the average American produced about 87 percent as much as the average Briton. So the country was rising fast, and it had not yet caught Britain.

One more number, because it can mislead. In 1860, the federal government spent only about 1.4 percent of the economy's output. A friend concludes that government played little part in the era's growth. What is your friend missing? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Spending is only one channel. [beat] Government acted through land and force more than through its budget. It took land from Native nations, and sold it or gave it to railroads. It won more than half of Mexico's territory by war.

A small budget can sit beside a very large role.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull something back. At the start, I gave you three kinds of advantage. Can you still name them?

[pause]

Geography and timing. [beat] Choices and policy. [beat] The troubles of other nations.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Louisiana was for sale because of a revolution in the Caribbean. The troubles of other nations opened the door. A choice to buy walked through it.

[beat]

Two. Indian removal was a choice. It passed the House by five votes. Whether the West was a national vision or other people's country is still argued.

[beat]

Three. Enslaved people grew the cotton that fed Britain's mills. The new historians of capitalism call it an engine of growth. The economic historians measure it at about a twentieth of output.

[beat]

Four. Public money and public land helped build the market revolution. Irish and German newcomers came to work in it, and met a fierce reaction.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Historians argue about the sorting itself. Turner's free land treats the West as geography. Limerick's conquest treats it as a choice.

The costs are not in dispute. Native nations lost their homelands and thousands of lives. Mexico lost more than half its territory. And nearly four million people lived enslaved in 1860.

The war with Mexico also reopened the most dangerous question in American politics. Could slavery spread west? In 1846, the Wilmot Proviso proposed that slavery never exist in land taken from Mexico. Southern senators blocked it. The question stayed open until the Confederacy's defeat, in 1865.

The next lecture takes up that war. It asks what the war settled, and what it did not. And it asks why Britain and France chose not to intervene, though Britain's mills ran on Southern cotton.

For now, go back to that room in Paris, in April 1803. A minister came to buy one city, and he was offered half a continent. [beat] You know now why the offer came. And you know who was never asked.
