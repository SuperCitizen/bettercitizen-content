November 2008. Two months earlier, the investment bank Lehman Brothers failed.

Now the National Intelligence Council, the intelligence community's long-range forecaster, publishes a report. It is called Global Trends 2025. [beat] And its first sentence reads like a farewell to an era.

"The international system—as constructed following the Second World War—will be almost unrecognizable by 2025."

[pause]

The United States, the council judged, was "likely to remain the single most powerful actor." But its "relative strength—even in the military realm—will decline."

[beat]

The year 2025 has now passed. So we can check.

By the end, you will be able to say what changed for America after 2008, and to give three serious readings of where it stands now.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the crash of 2008, and why it left the dollar more central, not less.

Second, China, and why both parties changed their minds about it.

Third, the new strengths: rock, code and people.

And last, the argument over what it all means, and what 250 years can and cannot tell us.

## The crash and the dollar

A recession began in December 2007. Home prices fell by more than a fifth. And in September 2008, Lehman Brothers failed.

The rescue split both parties. On September 29, 2008, the House rejected the first rescue bill, 205 to 228. 133 Republicans voted no. [beat] So did 95 Democrats.

Four days later, a revised bill passed, setting aside 700 billion dollars to prop up the banks. It was called TARP. In February 2009, President Obama's stimulus passed, at about 787 billion dollars, without a single House Republican vote.

[pause]

The Federal Reserve went further. It cut its target interest rate nearly to zero, and it began buying bonds on a vast scale. That bond buying came to be called quantitative easing.

And it did one more thing. Banks abroad needed dollars, because their debts were written in dollars. So the Fed set up swap lines with 14 foreign central banks, so that each could hand dollars to its own banks.

> A crisis made in America ended with the world queuing for American money.

The forecast had expected the dollar to become "something of a first among equals in a basket of currencies by 2025." In mid-2025, the dollar still made up 56.3 percent of the world's reported reserves. [beat] The euro made up 21.1 percent.

At home, the losses fell unevenly. Unemployment more than doubled, to 10 percent. Between 2005 and 2009, Pew Research Center found, median wealth fell 66 percent among Hispanic households, and 53 percent among Black households. [beat] Among white households, it fell 16 percent. The main cause was plunging house values.

And a country that prizes self-reliance had rescued its banks with public money. The Treasury puts the net cost of TARP at 31.1 billion dollars. Defenders call that a rescue that worked. Opponents in both parties argued that it protected the firms that caused the crisis.

Let's test the idea on a case that never happened. A panic starts in a large economy whose currency few foreign banks borrow in. Its central bank offers swap lines to the world, as the Fed did in 2008. Why would that offer calm the markets far less? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because frightened banks abroad would still need dollars. Their debts are written in dollars, and no central bank can print another country's money.

## China, and the bet that ended

That is the crash. Now the rival.

The forecast expected China to have the world's second-largest economy by 2025. Measured at market exchange rates, China passed Japan in 2010. [beat] Fifteen years early.

China's share of world output, at market rates, rose from 7.2 percent in 2008 to 16.6 percent in 2025. And in 2024, China made 28.1 percent of the world's manufacturing. America made 17.2 percent.

For two decades, presidents of both parties had bet that trade would change China. The last lecture followed that bet to China's entry into the World Trade Organization, in 2001. After 2008, both parties called it off.

[pause]

In 2011, in Canberra, President Obama declared that "the United States is a Pacific power, and we are here to stay."

In 2017, President Trump's National Security Strategy called for rethinking the old approach. Listen for the assumption it names. It meant "policies based on the assumption that engagement with rivals and their inclusion in international institutions and global commerce would turn them into benign actors and trustworthy partners."

[beat]

Benign actors and trustworthy partners. That was the bet, named and dropped. In 2018, Trump's administration put tariffs on Chinese imports.

President Biden kept those tariffs, and in 2024 he raised the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles to 100 percent. In 2022, he restricted China's access to advanced chips.

And Congress passed the CHIPS and Science Act, which pays to make chips at home. It won 17 Republican votes in the Senate, [beat] and 24 in the House.

> Both parties gave up the bet that trade would change China.

Put another way, the leaders converged. Their voters did not, quite. In January 2026, Pew found, 44 percent of Republicans called China an enemy. [beat] Among Democrats, 14 percent did.

Let's test it. A senator voted to normalize trade with China in 2000. In 2022, she voted for the CHIPS and Science Act. Which belief has she most plausibly given up? Think it through.

[long pause]

The belief that bringing China into world commerce would turn it into a benign partner. That is the assumption the 2017 strategy named. She has not given up trading with China. She has given up the bet.

## Rock, code and people

That is the rival. Now the new strengths, and where they came from.

The forecast expected oil and gas production to be "concentrated in unstable areas." It did not foresee the boom already under way in Texas, where firms were pairing horizontal drilling with hydraulic fracturing, which cracks shale with fluid pumped in at high pressure.

By 2023, the United States had been the world's largest producer of crude oil for six straight years, at 12.9 million barrels a day. In 2019, it exported more energy than it imported, for the first time since 1952. And in 2023, it was the world's largest exporter of liquefied natural gas. Europe, replacing Russian pipeline gas, took 66 percent of it.

[beat]

Yet in 2024, America still imported more crude oil than it exported.

[pause]

So was the boom geography, or policy? The answer is contested.

The economists Zhongmin Wang and Alan Krupnick, of Resources for the Future, studied its history in 2013. They found that federal research and tax credits, starting in the late 1970s, planted the "seed."

But they also found that a private firm, Mitchell Energy, "played the primary role" in Texas's Barnett shale. Mitchell got paid for its risk by leasing land and mineral rights cheaply. Later it sold the land, its know-how and itself, for much more.

That mechanism, they wrote, was "made possible by the private land and mineral rights ownership system in the United States." A 2012 report by the Breakthrough Institute, which they cite, stresses the role of government.

> Wang and Krupnick: the shale boom needed rock, policy and private risk.

Put another way, a country with the same rock but state-owned minerals would lack the reward Mitchell counted on.

And policy kept steering, in both directions. Congress repealed the ban on crude oil exports in 2015. In January 2024, the Biden administration paused reviews of new applications to export gas, and on January 20, 2025, the Trump administration ended the pause.

Now the code. On November 30, 2022, less than two months after those chip controls, OpenAI released ChatGPT.

Stanford's AI Index counted 285.9 billion dollars of private investment in American AI in 2025, 23.1 times China's, though the index warns that private totals likely understate what China spends. And in March 2026, the best American model led the best Chinese one by just 2.7 percent.

In 2023, President Biden issued an order on safe and trustworthy AI. President Trump rescinded it. His 2025 action plan declared that "the United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence."

[pause]

And both booms ran on people. The share of Americans born abroad rose from 12.9 percent in 2010 to 14.8 percent in 2024, a level last seen in 1890.

In the year to June 2025, net immigration added 1.3 million people, down from 2.7 million the year before. Births outnumbered deaths by about 519,000.

And yet the AI Index found that the number of AI scholars moving to the United States had dropped 89 percent since 2017.

## Declining, enduring, or divided

That is the evidence. Now the argument over what it means, starting on ground nobody owns.

In 1994, Pew found, 17 percent of Republicans viewed the Democrats very unfavorably. Among Democrats, the figure for Republicans was 16 percent. By 2022, those figures were 62 percent and 54 percent. And the parties in Congress, Pew reported, were "farther apart ideologically today than at any time in the past 50 years."

[pause]

The divisions show up abroad. All three major rating agencies cut America's credit rating. S&P in 2011, blaming partisan divisions. Fitch in 2023, citing "repeated debt limit standoffs." And Moody's in 2025, citing a failure to stop rising debt.

The government shut down under presidents of both parties: for 16 days in 2013, under Obama, and for 42 full days in 2025, under Trump.

Immigration showed the pattern most sharply. In 2013, an overhaul passed the Senate with 14 Republican votes, and never reached a vote in the House. Border Patrol encounters at the Mexican border hit a record of more than 2.2 million in fiscal 2022.

In 2024, a bipartisan Senate border deal failed, and President Biden then suspended entry whenever crossings ran high. On January 20, 2025, President Trump suspended migrants' entry at the southern border. In fiscal 2025, encounters fell to 237,538, [beat] the lowest since 1970.

In June 2026, Gallup found, 48 percent of Republicans wanted immigration decreased. [beat] Among Democrats, 19 percent did.

[pause]

Now the measures of America's position. One thing in the lesson's table matters most.

There are two ways to measure a country's share of world output. One, purchasing-power parity, values output at one set of prices everywhere. The other uses market exchange rates.

At purchasing-power parity, America's share fell, from 17.8 percent in 2008 to 14.6 percent in 2025. At market exchange rates, it rose, from 22.8 percent to 26.0 percent.

> Two yardsticks, two directions. Never compare one with the other.

The Federal Reserve's broad index of the dollar averaged about 91 in 2008 and about 123 in 2025. So part of that rise at market rates is a stronger dollar.

Here are three serious readings of the same numbers. Each gets the same four things: who holds it, what it protects, its argument, and its evidence.

The first is the relative-decline reading. It is held by the council that wrote the 2008 forecast, and by the journalist Fareed Zakaria, whose book The Post-American World called the shift "the rise of the rest." It protects a clear view of a world with more centers of power. Its argument is that the change is in everyone else.

It points to the purchasing-power share, falling. To China's share at market rates, more than doubled. To China's share of manufacturing, far above America's. And to an AI lead of only 2.7 percent.

> The relative-decline reading: the change is in everyone else.

The second is the enduring-advantages reading. It is held by the political scientist Michael Beckley, in a book called Unrivaled, and by Stephen Brooks and William Wohlforth. They grant that America is less dominant than twenty years ago, but argue it remains on top. This reading protects strengths built over generations. Its argument is that gross size flatters rivals. Power is what remains after the costs of feeding, policing and defending a population.

It points to the market-rate share, rising. To output per person, up from 1.39 to 1.52 times Britain's. To energy exports, and AI investment 23 times China's. To a growing population while China's falls. And to Finland and Sweden joining NATO.

> The enduring-advantages reading: power is what remains after the costs.

The third is the divided-at-home reading. It is held by Richard Haass, then president of the Council on Foreign Relations. His book Foreign Policy Begins at Home argues that the biggest threat to the United States comes not from abroad, but from within.

The political scientists Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue a related case from eroding norms of mutual toleration and forbearance. So does the conservative scholar Yuval Levin, from institutions used as platforms for being seen. This reading protects the capacity to govern. Its argument is that rock, code and newcomers are means, and using them takes a system that can make and keep commitments.

It points to the hostility between the parties, the downgrades, the shutdowns, and policies that reverse with each election.

> The divided-at-home reading: the biggest threat comes from within.

[pause]

Take the reading that convinced you least. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

The evidence settles that the yardsticks point different ways, and that the parties are farther apart. It cannot settle which of those facts will matter most. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## What 250 years can tell us

Before we gather it up, pull something back. Near the start, I gave you a line about the crash of 2008. Can you still say how that crisis ended?

[long pause]

With the world queuing for American money.

Now sort this era the way the series has sorted every other, into three kinds of advantage.

Geography and timing: shale under Texas, and an AI boom that arrived where the leading firms and capital already were.

[beat]

Choices and policy: the Fed's lending to the world. Federal gas research, and an old legal choice to leave mineral rights with landowners. Chip controls and subsidies. And immigration rules that swung between administrations.

[beat]

The troubles of other nations: China's population, falling since 2021. Japan's economy, about 5.2 trillion dollars in 2008, and about 4.5 trillion in 2025. And Europe's loss of Russian gas.

[pause]

The joints between those kinds are contested. Shale was rock, policy and private risk at once. Immigration is a policy choice that also answers the pull of a large, rich economy. And the strong dollar may reflect American choices, other countries' weakness, or both.

By the Maddison Project's estimates, America produced about 2 percent of the world's output in 1820. About 10 percent in 1870. About 17 percent in 1900. About 27 percent in 1950. [beat] And about 15 percent in 2022.

> The long arc is a record, not a forecast.

Every rise mixed all three kinds of advantage. The peak of 1950 was partly the ruin of every rival. And the alarms of the 1970s were followed by three decades in which America's share held at about 21 to 22 percent.

But the arc cannot say whether that pattern will repeat, or whether a falling share means American failure or other nations' rise. And it cannot say who will share in what comes next, though the losses of 2008 fell hardest on Hispanic and Black households.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A crisis made in America ended with the world queuing for American money. The Fed's swap lines made it the world's backstop.

[beat]

Two. Both parties gave up the bet that trade would change China. Obama turned to the Pacific, Trump imposed tariffs, and Biden kept them and added chip controls.

[beat]

Three. Wang and Krupnick: the shale boom needed rock, policy and private risk. And policy on AI and immigration swung with each change of president.

[beat]

And four. Two yardsticks, two directions. The relative-decline reading: the change is in everyone else. The enduring-advantages reading: power is what remains after the costs. The divided-at-home reading: the biggest threat comes from within.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: which reading is right, and whether the coming decades will repeat the last ones.

This was the last stop, so the argument goes back to the question the series began with. What made the United States what it became, and how did the rest of the world shape it? After 250 years, the answer still has three parts. Geography and timing. Choices. And the fortunes of others. The three readings disagree about which will matter most next.

[pause]

Go back, one last time, to that forecast from November 2008. It saw China's rise, if fifteen years slow. It missed the shale boom entirely.

[beat]

The forecast warns both ways. And so, after 250 years, does the record.
