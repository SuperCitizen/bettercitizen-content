Picture a school boardroom, packed with parents. The board has approved the closing of a school, and the room has come to object.

[beat]

Now go back a few weeks.

Before the meeting where that closure was approved, a document appeared on the district's website. Almost nobody opened it. It is called the agenda packet, and it runs to dozens or hundreds of pages. Staff reports. Budget transfers. The superintendent's recommendations. Contracts to ratify. Policies up for a first reading.

Every consequential decision the board is about to make is previewed there. In writing. Days in advance. By law.

In California, the law is exact about it. The Brown Act, the open-meetings law on the books since 1953, now requires the agenda 72 hours before a regular meeting. To the minute.

So the parents in that packed room are reacting to a decision that was legible, in a document, weeks earlier. Usually twice, since most policies get two readings.

[pause]

> Open-government law gives the public a standing head start on its school board.

And the first skill of engagement is simply to take it.

[pause]

Last time, we looked at how a school board works. This lecture is the other half: how to work with one. By the end, you will know when to show up, which door to knock on, and where a small group's effort counts most.

We will take it in four steps.

First, your district, and its calendar.

Second, the meeting, and the law that shapes it.

Third, the right door, in the right order.

And last, the ballot, and what to expect, honestly.

## Your district and its calendar

Start with three look-ups. Ten minutes, all told.

Which district are you in? Your address determines it. And the boundaries may not match your city, or what your neighbor assumes. Metropolitan areas layer elementary districts, high-school districts, and unified districts. If your state's tool confuses you, there is an official district locator, run by NCES.

Who sits on the board? The district's site lists the members and their terms. Some districts elect by trustee area, or sub-district, and there the site shows which member is yours. In an at-large district, all of them are.

And when and where does the board meet? Regular monthly meetings are the norm, with committee meetings and special meetings between them. If you subscribe to the agenda list, the packet arrives in your inbox, on the legal schedule.

[pause]

One more piece of orientation pays for itself. The board keeps a calendar of its own, and its decisions run on it.

Budgets are adopted on a cycle set by statute: spring into early summer, in most states. Curriculum adoptions follow the state's review windows. And in a number of states, collective-bargaining proposals must be made public before negotiations begin. The word for that is "sunshined."

So if your issue is money, the moment is the winter budget workshops, not the June adoption. If your issue is a textbook, the moment is the adoption committee, not the final vote.

Think of the board's year as a river. It has rapids, and it has flats. Engagement timed to the rapids moves things. [beat] Engagement timed to the flats is testimony for the record.

Let's follow one decision down that river: the budget. It moves in five stages.

First, in winter, the staff draft. The superintendent's budget office builds a draft, around enrollment projections and the state's funding estimates. Advisory committees see pieces of it first, and a seat on one of those is the earliest input there is.

Second, from winter into spring, the board's study sessions. These are workshops where the board weighs trade-offs in public, and line items are genuinely in play. These are the rapids. A comment here, or an email to a member, can still move money.

Third, the public hearing. Most states require at least one noticed public hearing on the proposed budget, before any vote to adopt it.

Fourth, adoption. The board votes by a statutory deadline, and sets the local levy within state caps. By now the text is set. [beat] Speeches are for the record.

And fifth, implementation. The superintendent executes the budget, and mid-year revisions return to the board as the state updates its numbers.

[pause]

Notice where the openings were. Every point where the public can actually bend the outcome comes before the adoption vote. And the adoption vote is the one that draws the crowd.

Curriculum runs on the same shape. An adoption committee reviews materials against state standards. The board takes a public first reading. Then comes the final vote. And the rule generalizes.

> The earlier the stage, the more a single prepared voice weighs.

The earlier the stage, the smaller the audience. And the smaller the audience, the more one prepared person counts.

## The meeting and its legal skeleton

Now the meeting itself. Every state has an open-meetings law, and school boards are its classic subject. The details vary. The architecture recurs, and it has four parts.

One. Agendas are posted in advance. California's Brown Act, the model statute, requires 72 hours for a regular meeting. Other states range from 24 hours to a week.

Two. Business is limited to what the posted agenda noticed.

Three. Deliberation happens in public, with minutes, and increasingly with video.

And four. Public comment is guaranteed: typically one to three minutes for each speaker, on agenda items, and usually in a general slot too.

[beat]

There is one exception, and it confuses newcomers. It is called closed session, or executive session.

A board may lawfully retreat behind doors, but only for enumerated topics: individual personnel matters, pending litigation, strategy for labor negotiations, certain student discipline cases, real-estate deals.

Why allow it at all? Consider the alternative. The board would be discussing a named employee's evaluation, or its settlement posture in a lawsuit, on YouTube.

But the law imposes a discipline on the closed door. The topics must be cited on the agenda. Final actions generally must be reported out in public. And policy debates may not be smuggled into the back room.

When boards are sued under open-meetings laws, it is usually for exactly that smuggling. Or for its cousin: a quorum's worth of members "deciding by email" before the meeting.

Let's test that line. Tonight's agenda lists two closed sessions. One is titled "Public Employee Performance Evaluation: Superintendent." The other is titled "Conference with Labor Negotiators."

A parent group is furious. The board has a proposed cell-phone ban before it, and it will not take the ban into closed session too. The parents want it there, in their words, "to speak freely."

Who has the law right? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The board does. [beat] On both halves.

Personnel evaluations and bargaining strategy are classic, lawful topics for closed session. A cell-phone ban is something else. It is a policy that applies generally, and that is exactly what open-meetings laws force into public, however much franker the private conversation would be.

Suppose the board did debate the ban privately, in closed session or in an email thread among a quorum. It would be courting an open-meetings violation, and the unwinding of the vote.

> Open-meetings law draws its line by topic, not by comfort.

## The right door, in the right order

The law gets you into the room. But is the boardroom the right room? Often it is not.

The school system has a deeper operational ladder than city government, and the board sits at the top of it. Which means most concerns should not start there.

The principle underneath comes from the last lecture. The board governs policy, and it employs exactly one person: the superintendent. It does not reach around the superintendent to manage classrooms. In well-run districts, it may not.

So sort a concern by its level. There are three.

The first level is one child, or one school. A grade, a conflict with a teacher, a bullying incident: start with the teacher, then the principal. A school's schedule, its traffic, a problem with its facilities: start with the principal, then the relevant department in the central office. Special-education services start with the case manager, then the district's special-education office. The board's part, at this level, is the policy and the budgets behind the problem.

The second level is districtwide policy: the discipline code, library policy, calendars, the choice of curriculum. Now you have reached the board, through comment, through email, and through the policy committee. This is the board's actual job.

The third level is state law: state standards, graduation requirements, testing mandates. Here the board is the wrong door again. These belong to your state legislator, and to the state board of education. The local board can pass a resolution that lobbies the state. [beat] Nothing more.

[pause]

Two venues outrank the lectern for sustained influence. Both are chronically short of volunteers.

The first is the school site council, along with the district's advisory committees: budget advisory, facilities bond oversight, curriculum adoption, special-education parent advisory. That is where draft policy is shaped, months before a board vote.

The second is the PTA or PTO, along with the education foundations. They give you standing, allies, and a flow of information.

The political science here is unsentimental, as it is for city government.

> Organized and early beats eloquent and late.

A seat on the bond-oversight committee is worth a year of three-minute comments.

[pause]

Let's sort one. A parent wants three things. She wants her son moved out of a chaotic algebra class. She wants the district's middle-school math sequence redesigned. And she wants the state's mandate of algebra in eighth grade repealed.

Three wants. Where does each one go? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

The first is operational, and individual. It goes to the teacher, then the principal. The board cannot reassign one student, and should not.

The second is district policy. It goes to the curriculum committee and the superintendent's instructional staff, and then to the board, which would vote on a redesigned sequence. The high-leverage version is a seat on a site council, or on an adoption committee.

The third is state law. It goes to legislators, and to the state board of education. The local board can pass a lobbying resolution. It cannot repeal a mandate.

> One concern, three governments. The skill is the sorting.

## The ballot

In the end, board engagement runs through elections, and almost no one votes in them. The National School Boards Association estimates that turnout typically lands between 5 and 10 percent, especially in off-cycle districts. That is Sarah Anzia's math about organized groups: a tiny electorate works in favor of whoever shows up.

That cuts two ways for you. First, a few hundred mobilized voters can decide a seat. At this scale, a weekend of canvassing genuinely matters. Second, the turnout problem is itself a target for engagement.

Districts also come to the voters directly. Bond measures, for construction, and tax overrides and levies, for operations, need approval at the polls in most states. And the campaigns, for and against, are run by volunteers.

The heavy machinery exists here too. In many states, board members are subject to recall. But the recent wave of school-board recalls carries a realistic note: [beat] most fail to qualify.

Let's check that with arithmetic. Your group has 40 volunteers. It wants to replace a board member it considers unresponsive.

Last cycle's off-year election drew about 1,900 ballots. The winner took 1,000 votes. A recall would need 4,500 valid signatures, just to reach the ballot.

Where does the arithmetic say to spend the energy? And why?

[pause 5s]

On the next regular election.

Compare the two bars. The recall needs more than four times as many signatures as it took votes to win the seat. That is why most school-board recalls die at the petition stage.

Now the other bar. In an electorate this small, 40 volunteers, canvassing for a few months, can realistically find and turn out the few hundred net votes that decide the race.

The recall has one advantage: speed. [beat] The regular election's advantage is its bar.

> A plurality of a tiny electorate: a bar a small organized group can clear.

That is Anzia's math. [beat] Working for you, this time.

And then there is the seat itself. School board is the most accessible elected office in America. Filing requirements are modest. Incumbents regularly run unopposed. And candidates have won with vote totals in the hundreds.

## What to expect, honestly

Boards move at the speed of their constraints. There are state mandates, and funding formulas. There are federal obligations, under IDEA and civil-rights law. There are union contracts that run for years. And there are budgets where compensation consumes the overwhelming majority of spending.

What is left to fight over? Perhaps a few discretionary percentage points. Which is why decisions about closures and programs get so hot.

[pause]

So expect slowness. New policy takes months, through two readings. New programs take budget cycles.

And expect that the room is not the district. The loudest meeting in memory still gathers a tiny, unrepresentative slice of families. Board members know it. And since 2020, they have also absorbed a level of vitriol that drives resignations.

So the engaged citizen's edge is the opposite of volume.

> The citizen's edge: reading the packet, knowing the calendar, picking the right door, returning.

Let's see whether one piece has stuck. Early on, I said the board's year has rapids and flats. Suppose your issue is money, and the board adopts its budget in June. When was your moment?

[long pause]

In the winter. At the budget workshops, the study sessions, when line items were still in play. By the June adoption, the text is set, and speeches are for the record.

What does a first month look like? Four small things: running your address through the district locator, subscribing to the agenda list, going through one full packet, and attending or streaming one meeting. At that meeting, the thing to note is what was actually decided, against what the comment line was about. And when you are back at a screen, the Coach can help you draft a letter to your board member.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Open-government law gives the public a standing head start on its school board. The packet previews every consequential decision, in writing, in advance.

[beat]

Two. The earlier the stage, the more a single prepared voice weighs. For the budget, that means the winter workshops, not the June vote.

[beat]

Three. Meetings are public, and closed session is for listed topics only. Open-meetings law draws its line by topic, not by comfort.

[beat]

Four. The board sits at the top of the ladder, so most concerns start lower. One concern, three governments. The skill is the sorting. And organized and early beats eloquent and late.

[beat]

And five. The ballot. A plurality of a tiny electorate is a bar a small organized group can clear. So the next regular election usually beats the recall.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not a tactics manual for winning any particular school fight. And it took no side in the open ones: phones, books, curricula, closures.

It also leaves a question open, one the last lecture raised and no one has settled. What is the cure for an institution with 5 percent turnout? It could be moving its elections. It could be mobilizing its absentees. Or it could be accepting that intense minorities, governing offices few people watch, is what local democracy is.

What the lecture does claim is narrower. The board is the most reachable government you have. Its law forces its work into the open, in advance. And the distance between reading the packet and shaping the outcome is shorter here than anywhere else in American politics.

[pause]

Before your school board's next regular meeting, a packet will appear on the district's website. Almost nobody will open it. [beat] You know what is in it. And you know what it is worth.
