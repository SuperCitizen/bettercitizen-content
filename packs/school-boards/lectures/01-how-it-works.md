On November 5, 2024, the country was watching the presidential returns. In Chicago, voters were also doing something their city had never done.

[beat]

Chicago's Board of Education was created 187 years earlier. In all that time, no Chicagoan had ever voted for a member of it. [beat] That night, they elected ten, from ten new districts.

For three decades before that, the system had been one-person rule. The mayor appointed the board, and the board ran the fourth-largest school system in the nation.

The change was ordered by the state, and it arrives in installments. The new board, seated in January 2025, had 21 members, and it was a hybrid: ten elected, alongside eleven the mayor still appointed. The timetable then has Chicago voting on the whole board, president included, in November 2026. The fully elected board takes its seats in January 2027.

[pause]

Sit with the strangeness of that. One of America's biggest cities is only now adopting an institution that some 13,300 other communities have had all along. And millions of the people in those communities could not name a single member of theirs.

> The school board: America's most common elected government, and its least examined.

So let's examine it. By the end, you will be able to say who really controls a public school, who pays for it, and who chooses the people in charge.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the unit: what a school district is.

Second, the layers: what the state controls, what Washington controls, and where the money comes from.

Third, the board, and the one person it employs.

And last, the voters: who actually elects a school board.

## Thirteen thousand governments

Start with the unit. Most American public schools are not run by city hall. They are run by something called an independent school district.

An independent school district is a government of its own, separate, and built for one purpose. It has its own elected board and its own budget. Often it has its own power to tax. And its boundaries may or may not match the line of any city or county.

How many are there? The National Center for Education Statistics counted 13,318 regular school districts, in the school year ending in 2023.

That number is what remains of one of the great quiet centralizations in American history. In the school year that began in 1939, there were more than 117,000 districts, most of them rural fiefdoms with a single schoolhouse. Then came the consolidation movement, pushed by administrative reformers. Over a generation, it merged districts by the tens of thousands.

[pause]

The map that remains is wildly uneven, and nobody designed it. Hawaii runs a single statewide district. Texas has more than a thousand. Florida and Maryland organize by county, which is why their districts rank among the nation's largest.

Each district is governed by a board. Typically it has five to nine members, though big cities run larger. Typically they serve part-time, and typically they are paid little or nothing.

Add them up, and the national corps runs to roughly 90,000 board members. That makes school board member one of the largest classes of elected officials in the country. And elected is the right word, because more than 90 percent of boards are elected. The exceptions are appointed, and high-profile, and we will come to them.

> Most public schools are run by a separate government: the independent school district.

## The layer cake

So a district is a government of its own. But how much does it actually control? Less than it looks. Think of a cake with three layers: federal, state, and local.

Begin with a silence. Education appears nowhere in the federal Constitution. Under the logic of the Tenth Amendment, the duty falls to the states, and every state constitution obligates the state to provide free public education.

So the state legislature is the sovereign of K-12. It sets the academic standards, the graduation requirements, and the rules for teacher certification. It sets the testing regimes, and the funding formula. And it can redraw districts, merge them, or take them over.

[beat]

That has a consequence for the local layer.

> A school board exercises power the state lent it.

Local control is real. [beat] But it is delegated, leashed, and revocable. If you know Dillon's Rule, the rule for cities, this is the same grammar. And the leash can be pulled taut. States have taken over struggling districts: Newark, for over two decades. Philadelphia. Houston, in 2023.

Now the federal layer. It is thinner than most people assume. Washington has never run schools, and has never set course content. It works almost entirely through two things: money, and civil-rights law.

Its levers date from 1965, and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. There are Title One dollars, for high-poverty schools. There is IDEA funding, since 1975, which obligates districts to educate students with disabilities. There are nutrition programs. And there is a civil-rights floor, covering race, sex, and disability. That floor is enforced by the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights, and by the courts.

> Washington works through money and civil-rights law, not curriculum.

[pause]

Now follow the money. These figures come from the most recent national accounting, for fiscal year 2023.

Public schools took in about 982 billion dollars. [beat] Just under a trillion.

Where did it come from? States supplied about 45 percent. Local sources supplied about 42 percent, overwhelmingly through the property tax. And the federal government supplied about 13 percent.

Treat that federal share with care. It is a peak, inflated by the pandemic, and not a baseline. Before COVID-19, it hovered around 8 percent. It swelled with roughly 190 billion dollars in relief funds, and it has been receding since.

And where does the money go? Current spending ran to about 16,500 dollars for each pupil, nationally. Instruction takes about 59 cents of every one of those dollars.

One more thing about that money. Every stream arrives with strings. State aid carries standards and testing mandates. Federal dollars carry program rules and civil-rights conditions. And local levies and bonds often require direct approval from voters. Which is why districts campaign at you.

[pause]

Let's test the layers. Two candidates are running for your school board.

The first promises to abolish the state's standardized tests, and to rewrite the graduation requirements. The second promises to redirect 5 percent of the budget from central administration to classroom aides.

Which promise can a board majority actually keep? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the second.

Testing regimes and graduation requirements are state law. A local board can lobby the state capital. It cannot opt out. The budget's internal allocation is another matter. That is the board's core power: it adopts the budget, and it can shift discretionary dollars among priorities.

So judging board candidates starts with sorting their promises into the right layer of the cake. And the sorting yields a rule worth keeping.

> Much of what gets shouted about at board meetings is decided somewhere else.

## The board and its one employee

We have the layers. Now look inside the local one. What does a board itself do?

A school board is a legislature, plus an employer. Here are its irreducible jobs.

It hires, evaluates and, when it comes to that, fires the superintendent. It adopts the budget, and where state law allows, sets the local levy within caps. It approves the collective-bargaining agreements with the unions of teachers and staff. It sets district policy: attendance zones, discipline codes, calendars, the closing and opening of schools. It chooses curricula and textbooks, within state standards. And it puts bond issues and tax overrides before the voters.

The superintendent runs the system day to day. The superintendent hires and supervises the principals, manages operations, drafts the budget the board votes on, and implements the policies the board adopts.

> The board governs. The superintendent manages.

That is the textbook theory. [beat] And its breakdown is the textbook dysfunction.

Scholars of governance keep finding the same thing, from a research synthesis by Deborah Land, in 2002, to the board surveys of Frederick Hess. Effective boards focus on goals, policy, and oversight. Troubled boards micromanage operations: individual personnel cases, decisions about a single building, grudges against vendors. They keep at it until no one can say who is accountable for results.

The relationship is also fragile. In a national study by AASA, sitting superintendents average 5.4 years in their current post. In cities, the average has historically run closer to three. And churn at the top correlates with strategy whiplash below. That is one reason the single most consequential act of a board is the hire.

[pause]

Let's put that division to work. A district must fill four needs this summer. A new middle-school principal. A new superintendent. Next year's adopted budget. And a ratified teachers' contract.

Which of the four belong to the board itself? Think it through.

[long pause]

Three of them: the superintendent hire, the budget adoption, and the contract ratification. The principal is the superintendent's hire.

Here is the reason. When it comes to hiring, the board's list is short.

> The board employs exactly one person: the superintendent.

Its other powers it exercises by vote. It adopts the budget that the superintendent's staff drafts, and it approves the collective-bargaining agreements. Principals are hired and supervised by the superintendent.

And a board that reaches around its one employee to staff the buildings? That is micromanagement, the very failure Land and Hess document.

## Who votes for all this

So who chooses the board? More than 90 percent of board members are elected. Now look at how.

Mostly, the races are formally nonpartisan. And very often they are off-cycle: held on dates with nothing federal on the ballot.

The result is turnout that is hard to believe. By the National School Boards Association's own estimate, turnout in board elections typically runs from 5 to 10 percent.

[pause]

Five to ten percent. What does a number that small do?

Sarah Anzia, in a book called Timing and Turnout, supplies the mechanism. Off-cycle timing shrinks the electorate to the organized. And in school politics, the best-organized participants are the adults employed by the system.

Terry Moe presses the point, in a 2011 book called Special Interest. The members of teachers' unions have the most concentrated stake in what a board decides, and they reliably mobilize for low-turnout races. So those unions, in Moe's account, long enjoyed outsized influence over the very boards that sit across the bargaining table.

And Anzia's data show it concretely. Districts that vote off-cycle pay experienced teachers more than districts that vote on-cycle. [beat] The electoral calendar, converted into contract terms.

Whatever you think a board should do, this is who has been choosing it.

> By the school boards' own association, turnout typically runs 5 to 10 percent.

Then there are the appointed exceptions. They are the country's institutional laboratory, and there are three to know.

New York City's schools have answered to the mayor since 2002, subject to periodic renewal fights in the state capital.

Boston's school committee has been appointed since 1992. In 2021, in an advisory question, 79 percent of voters backed a return to elections. [beat] The committee stayed appointed anyway, because the change requires state legislation, and that legislation has not come.

And Chicago, where we began, is in the middle of its transition, from the appointed model to a fully elected board.

What is the argument for appointment? Coherence, and accountability through the mayor. And the argument against? It removes the one government most families touch from direct democratic reach. The experiments are still running, which means the evidence is still arriving.

[pause]

One more test. A district holds its board elections in the spring of odd-numbered years.

Two questions. Who is structurally favored in those races? And what single change of procedure would most directly alter that, without changing a word of education policy?

[pause 5s]

The favored are the organized stakeholders. Above all, the district's own employee unions. Their members have concentrated stakes, and they turn out whatever the calendar says. So they loom large in an electorate of 5 to 10 percent.

And the lever is the calendar itself: moving the election on-cycle, onto the November ballot of an even-numbered year. That multiplies turnout, and it dilutes the share of any organized bloc.

There is a standard rejoinder. On-cycle voters, it runs, know nothing about board candidates, and simply vote their party. So the trade-off is representativeness against informedness. In plainer words: a bigger electorate, or a better-informed one.

## After 2020

One last turn, and a brief one. After 2020, school-board politics got nationally loud. First came pandemic closures and mask rules. Then fights over how race, gender, and American history are taught. Then library collections.

The institution shows the strain and the energy at once. Attempts to recall board members multiplied. The marquee event was in San Francisco, in February 2022, when voters removed three members. National groups, on the right and on the left, began recruiting and funding candidates. Turnout rose in places. And surveys of board members reported harassment driving resignations.

Now use what you have learned. Those fights concentrate on the slice of authority that boards actually hold: library policy, curriculum choice within state standards, personnel. And they routinely spill onto turf that boards do not hold: state standards, and federal civil-rights law.

And the side that understands the layer cake tends to win the litigation, [beat] even when it loses the shouting match.

Before we gather it up, let's see what has stuck from the very start. A friend says to you that the mayor and the city council run the local public schools. "It's all the city."

In most of the country, what is wrong with that picture? And what kind of government actually runs the schools?

[long pause]

Most districts are independent, special-purpose governments. They are legally separate from the city, with their own elected board, their own budget, and frequently their own property-tax levy. The city council usually has no authority over them at all. The district's boundaries may not even match the city's.

The big-city appointed systems are the exceptions that make headlines. They are not the rule.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Most public schools are run by a separate government: the independent school district. There are some 13,300 of them.

[beat]

Two. The layers. The state legislature is the sovereign, and a school board exercises power the state lent it. Washington works through money and civil-rights law, not curriculum.

[beat]

Three. The money. States supply about 45 percent, local sources about 42, and Washington the rest. Every stream arrives with strings.

[beat]

Four. The board governs. The superintendent manages. The board employs exactly one person, and its textbook failure is micromanagement.

[beat]

And five. More than 90 percent of boards are elected, very often off-cycle, and turnout typically runs 5 to 10 percent. That favors the organized. A few big cities appoint their boards instead.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not done. It has not refereed the open arguments. Do elected or appointed boards produce better schools? Would on-cycle elections cure board politics, or merely make it partisan again? Is the mobilization since 2020 a democratic renewal, or an institutional degradation? On each, serious people disagree, and the evidence is contested.

[pause]

What you now hold is the machine. Thirteen thousand special-purpose governments. A state sovereign above them. A federal funder beside them. A hired executive below them. [beat] And an electorate of single digits, choosing the whole thing.

The next lecture is the user's manual: finding your district, working a meeting, and the ladder of engagement, from public comment to the ballot.

For now, go back to Chicago. That city went 187 years without electing a single school board member. [beat] Most of the country has been able to all along. And typically, 5 to 10 percent of its voters do.
