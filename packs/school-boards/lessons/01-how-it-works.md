# How U.S. school boards actually work

K-12 education is one of the most structurally complex policy areas in the U.S. Authority is divided across three levels — federal, state, local — with significant variation by state. Most actual policy is set locally by elected school boards, but they operate within state and federal frameworks.

## The three-level structure

**Federal level:**
- Limited direct authority. The U.S. Department of Education does not run schools.
- Provides funding (Title I for low-income students, IDEA for special education, school nutrition).
- Sets civil-rights enforcement (Title VI race; Title IX sex; Section 504 disability).
- Enforces certain accountability requirements (testing under No Child Left Behind/ESSA).

**State level:**
- Substantial authority. State constitutions establish state responsibility for public education.
- Set curriculum standards (state standards; some states adopt Common Core, others don't).
- Set graduation requirements.
- Set teacher certification standards.
- Provide ~45-50% of K-12 funding (varies by state).
- Set school-funding formulas distributing state aid to districts.

**Local level:**
- School districts are the primary governance unit. There are ~13,000 school districts in the U.S.
- Most are governed by elected school boards.
- Set local policy: budgets, hiring, curriculum implementation, school calendars, school-closing decisions.
- Provide ~40-45% of K-12 funding (mostly through property taxes).

This layered structure means that what a school board can actually decide depends on what the state has delegated and what federal law requires. Tensions among levels are common — a school board may want to do something the state won't allow, or be required to do something it would rather not.

## Elected vs. appointed school boards

Most school boards (~90%) are elected. The election structure varies:
- **At-large** vs. **by district**: similar to city councils, with similar trade-offs around representation.
- **Nonpartisan vs. partisan**: most school-board elections are formally nonpartisan, though partisan dynamics often appear in campaigns.
- **Off-cycle elections**: many school-board elections happen separately from general elections, producing low turnout (often 5-10%).

A small number of large urban districts have appointed school boards (sometimes by mayor, sometimes by city council). New York City, Chicago, Boston (until 2024 — recent transition to elected), and a few others use this model. The argument for: professional accountability, alignment with city priorities. The argument against: democratic representation, especially of historically-marginalized communities.

## Board size and composition

School board size varies:
- Small rural districts: often 3-5 members.
- Medium districts: typically 5-9 members.
- Large urban districts: 9-15 members.

Membership varies by state:
- Some states require certain demographic representation (rare).
- Some states have term limits; most don't.
- Most board members serve part-time; compensation ranges from nothing to modest stipends ($5,000-15,000/year typical).

## The superintendent

The school board hires and oversees the superintendent (sometimes called district administrator or chief executive). The superintendent:

- Manages day-to-day district operations.
- Hires and supervises principals and senior staff.
- Implements board policy.
- Drafts the budget for board approval.
- Represents the district publicly.

The board's relationship with the superintendent is the most-important governance relationship in most districts. A common dysfunction: boards that try to manage operations themselves rather than setting policy and overseeing the superintendent. Effective boards focus on goals, policy, and oversight; they leave operations to professional staff.

Superintendent tenure has been declining nationally. The average tenure of urban superintendents is ~3 years; small-district superintendents stay longer. High turnover correlates with district instability and worse student outcomes.

## What school boards actually control

Within state and federal constraints:

- **Annual budget.** The largest single decision; typically ~$50K-$5K per pupil per year × number of students.
- **Capital projects.** School construction, renovation, technology investments.
- **Curriculum implementation.** State sets standards; the board chooses textbooks, programs, supplemental materials.
- **Hiring senior staff.** The superintendent and senior administrators.
- **District policy.** Discipline policies, grading policies, school-day schedules, transportation, attendance zones.
- **Property tax rates.** In states that allow it, the board can set local property tax rates within state caps.
- **Bond issuances.** Major capital expenditures often require bond issuance, which requires board approval and (typically) voter approval.

What school boards don't control:
- **Curriculum standards** (state-set in most cases).
- **Graduation requirements** (state-set).
- **Teacher certification** (state-set).
- **Compulsory-education laws** (state-set).
- **Special education** (federal IDEA framework).
- **Civil rights** (federal enforcement).

## Recent shifts

Two recent trends are worth noting:

**Increased partisan attention.** School-board races in recent years have attracted unprecedented attention to issues like curriculum content (history, race, sexuality), book selection, and pandemic-era policies. Outside groups (both progressive and conservative) have increased involvement in formerly-quiet school-board races. Turnout has risen in some places.

**Charter-school competition.** Many states allow charter schools, which operate under different governance (typically chartered by the state or by an independent authorizer, not the local school board). The result is competition for students and funding between traditional districts and charters. The empirical record on charter performance is mixed and politically contested.

These shifts mean school-board politics in 2025 looks different from school-board politics in 2010. The institutional structure remains similar; the political intensity has changed.

## What's at stake

Despite the layered constraints, school boards control:
- The budget (the largest local-government expenditure in most jurisdictions).
- Hiring decisions that shape the educational environment.
- Implementation choices that determine how state policy actually plays out.
- Local-policy decisions on discipline, scheduling, transportation, programs.

Compounded over years, these decisions shape educational outcomes for generations of students. School-board engagement is one of the most concrete forms of civic participation available.
