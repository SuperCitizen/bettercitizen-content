# How U.S. school boards actually work

On November 5, 2024, while the country watched the presidential returns, Chicagoans did something their city had never done in the 187 years since its Board of Education was created: they elected school board members. Ten of them, from ten new districts — the first installment of a state-mandated transition that ended three decades of one-person rule, in which the mayor appointed the board that ran the nation's fourth-largest school system. The new 21-member board seated in January 2025 was a hybrid, ten elected members alongside eleven the mayor still appointed; in November 2026 Chicago votes for all of it, president included, with the fully elected board seated in January 2027. Sit with the strangeness: one of America's biggest cities is only now adopting an institution that some 13,300 other communities have had all along — and that millions of their residents could not name a single member of. The school board is simultaneously the most common elected government in America and the least examined. This lesson examines it.

## Thirteen thousand governments nobody designed

Start with the unit. Most American public schools are run not by city hall but by an **independent school district** — a separate, special-purpose government with its own elected board, its own budget, often its own taxing power, and boundaries that may or may not match any city or county line. The National Center for Education Statistics counted **13,318** regular school districts in 2022-23. That number is the residue of one of the great quiet centralizations in American history: in 1939-40 there were more than 117,000 districts, most of them one-schoolhouse rural fiefdoms, and the consolidation movement — pushed by the administrative reformers historian David Tyack chronicled in *The One Best System* (1974) — merged them by the tens of thousands over a generation. The map that remains is wildly uneven by design of no one: Hawaii runs a single statewide district; Texas has more than a thousand; New England keeps tiny town-based districts; Florida and Maryland organize by county, which is why their districts rank among the nation's largest.

Governing each district is a board — typically five to nine members (big cities run larger), typically part-time, typically paid little or nothing. The National School Boards Association puts the national corps at roughly **90,000 board members**, which makes "school board member" one of the largest classes of elected officials in the country. More than 90 percent of boards are elected; the high-profile exceptions are appointed, and we will come to them.

## The layer cake: who actually controls what

Here is the constitutional architecture, and it will feel familiar if you've worked through the city-councils pack — local control is real but delegated, leashed, and revocable. Education appears nowhere in the U.S. Constitution; under the Tenth Amendment's logic, every state constitution instead obligates the *state* to provide free public education. The state legislature is therefore the sovereign of K-12: it sets academic standards, graduation requirements, teacher certification, compulsory-attendance ages, testing regimes, and the funding formula — and it can redraw, merge, or take over districts. A school board exercises power the state lent it, the same grammar as Dillon's Rule for cities, and state takeovers of struggling districts (Newark for over two decades, Philadelphia, Houston in 2023) are the leash pulled taut within [[term:federalism]]'s bottom tier.

The federal layer is thinner than most people assume — and it works almost entirely through money and [[term:civil-rights]] law, not curriculum. Washington has never run schools or set course content. Its levers since the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965: **Title I** dollars for high-poverty schools, **IDEA** funding (since 1975) that obligates districts to educate students with disabilities, nutrition programs, and the civil-rights floor — Title VI (race), Title IX (sex), Section 504 (disability) — enforced by the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights and the courts. The Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015 kept annual testing but handed accountability design back to the states, retreating from No Child Left Behind's federal prescriptions.

Now the money, from the most recent national accounting (NCES, fiscal year 2023): public K-12 took in **$981.8 billion**, of which states supplied about 45 percent, local sources about 42 percent — overwhelmingly the property tax — and the federal government 12.9 percent. Treat that federal share as a pandemic-inflated peak, not the baseline: it hovered around 8 percent before COVID-19, swelled with the roughly $190 billion in ESSER relief funds, and has been receding since the last of that money had to be obligated by late 2024. Current spending ran **$16,560 per pupil** nationally — ranging from $10,265 in Utah to $31,037 in the District of Columbia — with instruction taking about 59 cents of every current-expenditure dollar.

The budget's internal allocation, by contrast, is the board's core power: it adopts the budget and can shift discretionary dollars among priorities. Evaluating board candidates starts with sorting their promises into the right layer of the cake — much of what gets shouted about at board meetings is decided somewhere else.</p>

## The board and the superintendent: policy versus administration

A school board is a legislature-plus-employer. Its irreducible jobs: hire, evaluate, and (when it comes to that) fire the **superintendent**; adopt the budget and (where state law allows) set the local levy within caps; approve collective-bargaining agreements with teachers' and staff unions; set district policy — attendance zones, discipline codes, grading and homework policy, calendars, school closures and openings; choose curricula and textbooks *within* state standards; and put [[term:bond]] issues and tax overrides before the voters for capital projects. The superintendent runs the system day to day: hires and supervises principals, manages operations, drafts the budget the board votes on, implements the policies the board adopts.

That division — the board governs, the superintendent manages — is the textbook theory, and its breakdown is the textbook dysfunction. Governance scholars' consistent finding, from Deborah Land's research synthesis (2002) to Frederick Hess's board surveys, is that effective boards focus on goals, policy, and oversight, while troubled ones micromanage operations — individual personnel cases, building-level decisions, vendor grudges — until no one can say who is accountable for results. The relationship is also fragile: sitting superintendents in AASA's 2025-26 national study average 5.4 years in their current post, and the urban average has historically run closer to three. Churn at the top correlates with strategy whiplash below, which is one reason the board's single most consequential act is the hire.

## Who votes for all this

More than 90 percent of board members are elected — mostly in formally nonpartisan races, and very often **off-cycle**, on dates with nothing federal on the ballot. The result is the most extreme version of the turnout pattern you met in the city-councils pack: the National School Boards Association's own estimate is that board-election [[term:voter-turnout]] typically runs **5 to 10 percent**. Sarah Anzia's *Timing and Turnout* (2014) supplies the mechanism and the stakes: off-cycle timing shrinks the electorate to the organized, and in school politics the best-organized participants are the adults employed by the system. Terry Moe's *Special Interest* (2011) presses the point — teachers' unions, whose members have the most concentrated stake in board decisions and reliably mobilize for low-turnout races, long enjoyed outsized influence over the very boards that sit across the bargaining table. Anzia's data show concretely that off-cycle districts pay experienced teachers more than on-cycle ones, the electoral calendar converted into contract terms. Whatever you think the board *should* do, this is who has been choosing it.

The appointed exceptions are the country's institutional laboratory. New York City's schools have answered to the mayor since 2002, subject to periodic renewal fights in Albany. Boston's committee has been appointed since 1992 — and stayed appointed even after 79 percent of voters backed a return to elections in a 2021 advisory question, because the change requires state legislation that has not come. Chicago, as the cold open described, is mid-transition from the appointed model to a fully elected 21-member board in January 2027. The argument for appointment is coherence and accountability-through-the-mayor; the argument against is that it removes the one government most families touch from direct democratic reach. The live experiments mean the evidence is still arriving.

<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> A district holds its board elections in odd-year springs. Per Anzia and Moe, who is structurally favored in those races — and what single procedural change would most directly alter that, without changing a word of education policy?</summary>
<p>Organized stakeholders — above all the district's own employee unions, whose members have concentrated stakes and turn out regardless of the calendar, and who therefore loom large in a 5-10 percent electorate. Moving elections <strong>on-cycle</strong> (to even-November ballots) is the procedural lever: it multiplies turnout and dilutes any organized bloc's share. The standard rejoinder — that on-cycle voters know nothing about board candidates and just vote party heuristics — is the same trade-off you saw with city councils: representativeness versus informedness, now with a bargaining-table twist Anzia actually measured in teacher pay.</p>
</details>

## The intensification, briefly

School-board politics got nationally loud after 2020 — pandemic closures and mask rules, then fights over how race, gender, and American history are taught, then library collections — and the institution shows the strain and the energy at once. [[term:recall]] attempts against board members multiplied (San Francisco's February 2022 recall, in which voters removed three members with more than 70 percent voting yes in each case, was the marquee event); national groups on the right and left began recruiting and funding candidates for offices that used to cost four figures; turnout rose in places; and surveys of members reported harassment driving resignations. Read this era with this lesson's framework in hand. The [[term:culture-war]] fights concentrate on the slice of authority boards actually hold (library policy, curriculum choice within standards, personnel) and routinely spill onto turf they don't (state standards, federal civil-rights law) — and the side that understands the layer cake tends to win the litigation even when it loses the shouting match.

## What this lesson is not

It is not a guide to *doing* anything yet, and it deliberately did not referee the live arguments: whether elected or appointed boards produce better schools, whether on-cycle elections would cure or merely repartisan-ize board politics, whether the post-2020 mobilization is democratic renewal or institutional degradation — the honest answer to each is that serious people disagree and the evidence is contested. What you now hold is the machine: 13,318 special-purpose governments, a state sovereign above them, a federal funder beside them, a hired executive below them, and an electorate of single digits choosing the whole thing. Lesson 2 is the user's manual — finding your district, working a meeting, and the engagement ladder from public comment to the ballot.
