Ask most Americans to name the big ideas behind their government, and two phrases come first. Separation of powers. Checks and balances.

Now search the Constitution for those two phrases.

[beat]

You will come up empty. Neither phrase is there.

[pause]

So where are the two ideas?

> The Constitution never announces the principle. It builds it.

It hands one kind of power to Congress, another to a president, and a third to the courts.

Why build a government that way? By the end of this lecture, you will be able to answer in the words of James Madison, who did as much as anyone to shape the design. We will get there in four steps. First, the problem the Framers were trying to solve. Second, the text: three sentences that divide the power. Third, the argument, in two of Madison's essays. And last, a near miss: a tempting mistake about what the word separate means.

## The problem: power that checks its own work

Begin with a case that has nothing political riding on it.

Imagine a school where the principal writes the dress code, patrols the hallways looking for violations, and hears every appeal from a student who believes a punishment was unfair.

Suppose the code bans "distracting" clothing. Who decides what counts as distracting? The principal. Who hands out the punishment? The principal. And who rules on whether that punishment was fair? [beat] The principal.

This principal may be a perfectly decent person, but that is beside the point. What matters is that nothing in the arrangement would catch a mistake, or an abuse, if one occurred. A vague rule can be stretched at the moment it is enforced. And the person who stretched it is also the person who decides whether the stretch was acceptable.

> The problem is power that checks its own work.

The delegates who gathered in Philadelphia in 1787 had met that problem from two directions.

They had fought a war against a king whose power they considered unchecked. Then they had lived for several years under the Articles of Confederation, a national government that consisted of a Congress and little else. It had no separate executive and almost no courts, and it could not compel the states to pay the money it requested from them.

Meanwhile, elected legislatures in the states were demonstrating that an assembly could overreach as easily as a monarch. Madison later wrote that the legislative branch was "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex."

[pause]

So the Framers wanted a government strong enough to act. But they were unwilling to trust any single part of it with the whole job.

## The text: three vesting clauses

That is the problem. Now the text that answers it.

Each of the first three articles opens with a sentence that vests a power, which means it places that power in a particular institution. Those three sentences are the vesting clauses. As you hear each one, listen for what kind of power it is, and who receives it.

Article One. "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States." [beat] Congress makes the law.

Article Two. "The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America." [beat] The president carries out the law.

Article Three. "The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court," and in the lower courts Congress creates. [beat] The courts decide cases under the law.

> Legislative power to Congress. Executive power to a president. Judicial power to the courts.

But the Framers did more than divide the jobs. They had the three institutions chosen in three different ways, and they set them running on three different clocks.

House members face the voters every two years. Senators serve for six years, with only about a third of the seats coming up at any one election.

A president serves for four years.

Federal judges never appear on a ballot. The president nominates them, the Senate confirms them, and they hold office "during good Behaviour," which in practice means for life.

The clocks are staggered on purpose, and the effect shows on any election day. Voters can replace the whole House. They can replace about a third of the Senate. And how many federal judges can they replace? [beat] None.

So a wave of public anger or enthusiasm can sweep through the House in a single November. To remake the Senate, the same wave has to last through several elections. And it reaches the courts only gradually, as judges retire and are replaced.

> No single election can change every branch.

Now put those clocks to work. A movement wins the presidency, and a majority of the House, in the same election. Why might it still fail to pass its program? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because the Senate and the courts run on other clocks. Only about a third of the Senate was on the ballot, so most senators were chosen in earlier elections, and no judge was on the ballot at all. A bill has to pass both chambers, and a law can later be challenged in court.

Put another way, the design requires a winning coalition to hold together across several elections before it controls every institution.

## The argument: two essays by Madison

Why go to this much trouble?

Madison supplied the answer in the Federalist essays, which he wrote with Alexander Hamilton and John Jay to persuade New York to ratify the Constitution. Two of those essays matter here. One defines the danger. The other offers the remedy.

The danger comes first, in Federalist 47. It is one long sentence, so listen for how it ends.

"The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective, [beat] may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."

[pause]

The very definition of tyranny.

Notice what that definition leaves out. It says nothing about whether the rulers are wise, or popular, or even elected.

> For Madison, tyranny is a fact about structure: all powers in the same hands.

A government that holds all three powers in one set of hands meets the definition, [beat] even on its best day.

Madison credited the idea to the French writer Montesquieu, who had argued in 1748 that liberty depends on keeping the three powers apart.

[pause]

Now think back to the school we began with. Its principal may be a perfectly decent person. So by Madison's way of thinking, what is wrong with that school?

[long pause]

The structure is wrong. One set of hands writes the rule, enforces the rule, and judges the appeal. A school is not a government, but the shape is the same. And Madison's definition never asks whether the person in charge is decent.

But a definition is not a remedy. Writing "the branches shall remain separate" on paper would do nothing to stop an ambitious official, and Madison knew it.

[pause]

His remedy appears in Federalist 51, from 1788. The passage opens with two short sentences, and the whole design is inside them.

"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place."

[pause]

Notice that Madison does not ask officials to be virtuous. Instead, he connects two things. "The interest of the man" is each official's self-interest. "The constitutional rights of the place" are the powers of the office that the official holds. Connect them, and a senator who wants to matter has a personal reason to defend the Senate, just as a judge has a personal reason to defend the courts.

> The design does not try to abolish ambition. It puts ambition to work.

The passage goes on. "If men were angels, no government would be necessary."

[pause]

Men are not angels. So Madison names the difficulty, in the sentence to carry with you. It sets two tasks, in order.

[beat]

"In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: [beat] you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself."

[pause]

First, control the governed. Then, control itself.

Now a question that only that sentence can answer. The Framers had known two governments: the Articles of Confederation, and the rule of a king. Which of Madison's two tasks did the Articles fail at? And which was the complaint against the king?

[pause 5s]

The Articles failed at the first. Their Congress could neither make the states pay nor carry out its own decisions.

The complaint against the king concerned the second. His government was able to act, but nothing obliged it to control itself.

The Constitution attempts both at once. It gives the government enough power to govern, and then it divides that power, so that the government has to check itself.

## The near miss: separate does not mean sealed off

Here is the tempting mistake: to picture the branches as three sealed rooms.

Madison rejected that reading, in the same essay that defined tyranny. Montesquieu, he wrote, "did not mean that these departments ought to have no partial agency in, or no control over, the acts of each other."

[pause]

Partial agency. In plainer words, a limited part in another branch's work.

> Separate does not mean sealed off.

What the principle forbids is one branch holding the whole power of another. Not a part of it. [beat] The whole.

When a president vetoes a bill, the executive is taking a part in lawmaking. When the Senate votes on a judge, the legislature is taking a part in staffing the courts. Those overlaps are the design, not a flaw in it.

Let's test that on four cases. For each one, ask yourself whether it is a part of another branch's power, or the whole.

One. The president vetoes a bill that Congress has passed.

[beat]

Two. The Senate rejects a nominee for a federal judgeship.

[beat]

Three. One official writes a criminal law, and is the only judge under it.

[beat]

Four. The Supreme Court strikes down part of a federal law.

Which of the four would violate the separation of powers, as Madison defined it?

[long pause]

The third. That official holds the whole of two powers, making the law and judging under it, in the same hands. In the other three, one branch takes a limited part in another's work. That is partial agency.

[pause]

Two terms are often treated as one, and now you can tell them apart. Separation of powers is the division of the three jobs among three institutions. [beat] Checks and balances are the shares that each branch holds in the others' jobs.

In 1960, the political scientist Richard Neustadt joined the two ideas in a single line. The Convention, he wrote, had created not a government of separated powers but "a government of separated institutions sharing powers."

> Neustadt's line: separated institutions, sharing powers.

## Where the school breaks down

Return to the school. It suggests a tidy fix: give the three jobs to three different people. The real design is less tidy, in two ways.

You have just heard the first. The three branches share powers instead of holding them in isolation.

The second is that the Framers never expected the branches to be equal. "In republican government," Madison wrote in Federalist 51, "the legislative authority necessarily predominates."

[pause]

That expectation explains why Congress alone is divided against itself, into two chambers that have different terms and different voters, so that each can slow the other. The word for a legislature built that way is bicameral.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Constitution never announces the principle. It builds it, in three vesting clauses. Legislative power to Congress. Executive power to a president. Judicial power to the courts.

[beat]

Two. The three institutions run on three clocks. Voters can replace the whole House at once, but only a third of the Senate, and no judges at all. So no single election can change every branch.

[beat]

Three. Madison's definition, in Federalist 47. All powers in the same hands is the very definition of tyranny. That is a fact about structure.

[beat]

Four. Madison's remedy, in Federalist 51. Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The design puts ambition to work.

[beat]

And five. Separate does not mean sealed off. The principle forbids one branch holding the whole power of another, not a part. Separation of powers is the division. Checks and balances are the shares.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Has the balance among the branches shifted since the Framers' day? That remains an open argument.

Critics in both parties argue that the presidency now holds far more power than the Framers anticipated. Others answer that Congress gave much of that power away by passing laws, and can take it back in the same manner.

What you have now is the blueprint that the argument is about.

And a blueprint is not a working machine. The next lecture turns to the moving parts: what each branch can actually do to stop another, and how often it succeeds.

For now, go back to the search we began with. The phrase "separation of powers" is still not in the Constitution. [beat] But you know now where the idea is. Three opening sentences. Three clocks. [beat] And ambition, made to counteract ambition.
