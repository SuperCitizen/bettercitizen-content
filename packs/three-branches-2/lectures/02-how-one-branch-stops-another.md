Here are two stories about two presidents. Listen for what they share.

On September 23, 2016, President Barack Obama, a Democrat, vetoed a bill. It allowed the families of September 11 victims to sue foreign governments in American courts. Five days later, Congress overrode him. The Senate vote was 97 to 1. It turned out to be the only override of his eight years in office.

On December 23, 2020, President Donald Trump, a Republican, vetoed the annual defense policy bill. The House overrode him on December 28, and the Senate followed on New Year's Day, by 81 to 13. That was the only override of his first term.

[pause]

So what do the two stories share? [beat] In both fights, members of the president's own party voted against him. And the totals could not have been reached without them.

Hold on to that detail. It will come back more than once.

Last time, we had a blueprint: three institutions that share powers. Now come the moving parts. By the end of this lecture, you will be able to name the tools each branch holds against the others, and to say how often they are used, and what their use depends on.

We will take the tools in four steps. First, the veto and its arithmetic. Second, two tools that Congress holds: the purse, and the Senate's consent. Third, the courts' tool, which the text of the Constitution never mentions. And last, the heaviest tool of all: impeachment.

## The veto and its arithmetic

The rule is in Article One, section seven. A bill that passes both chambers goes to the president, who may sign it or return it with objections. If the president returns it, each chamber votes again. And a bill "approved by two thirds of that House," in both chambers, becomes law without the president's signature.

> It takes two-thirds of each chamber to override a veto.

Two details are easy to overlook.

The first is what "two thirds" counts. In 1919, the Supreme Court read it to mean two-thirds of the members voting, provided that a quorum is present, and not two-thirds of every seat in the chamber.

The second is that one kind of veto cannot be overridden at all. When Congress has adjourned, the president can decline to sign the bill. That maneuver is known as a pocket veto.

Now try the arithmetic, for a Senate in which all 100 members vote. A majority is 51, and a majority is enough to pass a bill. But to force a bill into law over a veto, the Senate needs 67.

So test yourself. Sixty senators vote to override. Does the veto stand?

[long pause]

It stands. Sixty is a majority that could pass the bill, but it cannot force the bill into law. The veto falls only at 67, and only if the House reaches two-thirds as well.

Now recall our two stories. The defense bill got 81 votes in the Senate, and the lawsuit bill got 97. Both cleared the line. The lesson page has a simulator where you can move that count yourself.

What matters here is the distance between 51 and 67. A simple majority is enough to pass a bill. Forcing one into law over a veto takes a supermajority, and a supermajority almost always requires votes from the president's own party.

> An override almost always needs votes from the president's own party.

That requirement explains why overrides are so rare. Here is the Senate's own count, from 1789 to November 2026. Presidents have cast about 1,500 regular vetoes. [beat] Congress has overridden 112 of them. That is about 7 percent. And more than a thousand pocket vetoes could not be overridden at all.

Even so, those counts understate the veto's real force. Everyone in Congress can count to 67. So a believable threat of a veto shapes a bill long before it reaches the president's desk. The political scientist Charles Cameron gave that process a name: veto bargaining.

## The purse, and the Senate's consent

The veto is a weapon the president holds against Congress. Now turn it around. What is the strongest weapon pointing the other way? [beat] Money.

Article One, section nine, says it in one sentence. "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law."

[pause]

In plainer words, a president can command the executive branch, but cannot fund any part of it without an act of Congress.

> The president commands the executive branch. Only Congress can fund it.

James Madison regarded this as the legislature's decisive advantage. In Federalist 58, he called the power over the purse "the most complete and effectual weapon with which any constitution can arm the immediate representatives of the people."

And notice the timing. A veto fight or an impeachment is a rare event. The budget returns every year, and each return gives Congress another opportunity to attach conditions to what the executive branch may do.

Congress holds a second tool, and this one sits in the Senate. Article Two, section two, gives the president the power to nominate judges and senior officials. But only, in the Constitution's words, "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate."

[pause]

Advice and consent. That is the name the power goes by. Confirming a judge takes a majority of the Senate. And the power has been used in more than one way.

Ronald Reagan, a Republican president, had nominated Robert Bork. In 1987, a Senate with a Democratic majority voted Bork down, by 42 to 58.

Barack Obama, a Democratic president, nominated Merrick Garland in March of 2016. A Senate with a Republican majority took no action at all. The seat remained open until 2017, when the Senate confirmed a new president's nominee, by 54 to 45.

One Senate said no. [beat] The other said nothing. And each side insisted that its own case was principled, and that the other was not.

So go back to the text, and listen to the words once more. The president appoints judges "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate." Does that text require the Senate to vote on every nominee? Answer from the words alone.

[long pause]

The text does not say. It makes the Senate's consent a condition of appointment. But it says nothing about hearings, deadlines or reasons.

That is why 1987, with its vote and its rejection, and 2016, with no vote at all, were both uses of the same power. Whether declining to act is a fair use of that power is a political argument. The words of the Constitution do not resolve it.

> The Constitution requires the Senate's consent. It sets no deadline and no procedure.

## Judicial review: the check the text never mentions

Now the courts. Where does the Constitution give courts the power to strike down a law?

[beat]

Nowhere, in so many words. Article Three vests "the judicial Power," and stops there.

The reasoning came from Chief Justice John Marshall, in a case called Marbury v. Madison, in 1803. His argument runs in three steps. The Constitution is law. A statute that conflicts with it cannot also be law. So deciding which one governs a case is the ordinary work of a court.

"It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department," Marshall wrote, "to say what the law is."

[pause]

To say what the law is. That power is called judicial review.

> Judicial review: a court measures a law or an official act against the Constitution.

Alexander Hamilton had made the same case fifteen years earlier, in Federalist 78, and he had added a caution. The courts, he wrote, would be the "least dangerous" branch, because they have "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."

[pause]

A court commands no army and controls no budget. So its rulings bind only because the other branches, and the public, treat them as binding.

> Hamilton's caution: the courts have "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."

The record shows this check operating against presidents of both parties.

In 1952, President Harry Truman, a Democrat, ordered the government to seize the nation's steel mills, in order to head off a strike during the Korean War. The Supreme Court ruled, 6 to 3, that he had no authority to do so.

In 1974, the Court ruled, 8 to 0, that President Richard Nixon, a Republican, had to surrender White House tape recordings for use in a criminal trial.

[pause]

Now remember Hamilton's caution. The Court commanded no army. [beat] Truman returned the mills. Nixon released the tapes, and sixteen days after the ruling, he resigned.

[pause]

One distinction before we move on, because not everything a court does is judicial review. Here are four scenes. Only one of them is judicial review.

One. An appeals court reverses a trial judge who allowed improper evidence.

[beat]

Two. The Supreme Court rules that a federal law violates the Constitution.

[beat]

Three. A Senate committee questions a nominee for the Supreme Court.

[beat]

Four. The Supreme Court agrees to hear a case from a state court.

Which one is judicial review?

[long pause]

The second scene. The Supreme Court rules that a federal law violates the Constitution. That is a court measuring a law against the Constitution, and that is judicial review.

The appeals court reversing a trial judge is appellate review, in which a higher court corrects a lower one. The Senate committee is advice and consent. And agreeing to hear a case is a court choosing its cases.

## Impeachment: an accusation, then a trial

The last tool is the heaviest.

Impeachment begins in the House, which may accuse a president, a judge or another civil officer by a majority vote. That accusation is the impeachment. Removal is a separate step. The Senate holds a trial, and a conviction requires two-thirds of the senators present.

> To impeach means to accuse, not to remove.

The Senate has tried a president four times. Andrew Johnson, in 1868. Bill Clinton, in 1999. And Donald Trump, in 2020, and again in 2021. [beat] Every one of those trials ended in acquittal.

In its entire history, the Senate has convicted only eight officials. [beat] All eight were federal judges.

You already know the explanation. It is the same two-thirds arithmetic that governs the veto. Unless one party holds 67 seats in the Senate, removing a president requires votes from members of the president's own party.

[pause]

Before we gather this up, check yourself. Here are four actions, and each takes a certain vote. Overriding a veto. Confirming a judge. Impeaching an official. Convicting and removing one. Which take a majority, and which take two-thirds?

[pause 5s]

Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber. Confirming a judge takes a majority of the Senate. Impeaching an official takes a majority of the House. And convicting and removing one takes two-thirds of the senators present.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five tools, and who holds each.

One. The veto belongs to the president. It takes two-thirds of each chamber to override it, and that almost always needs votes from the president's own party. So overrides are rare.

[beat]

Two. The purse belongs to Congress. The president commands the executive branch. Only Congress can fund it.

[beat]

Three. Advice and consent belongs to the Senate. The Constitution requires the Senate's consent. It sets no deadline and no procedure.

[beat]

Four. Judicial review belongs to the courts. A court measures a law or an official act against the Constitution. And the courts have neither force nor will, but merely judgment.

[beat]

Five. Impeachment belongs to Congress, in two steps. The House accuses, and the Senate tries. To impeach means to accuse, not to remove.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture does not settle.

Madison's design assumes that officeholders will defend their own branch. In his words, "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

In 2006, the legal scholars Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes argued that this assumption fails once political parties are strong. Members of Congress, they wrote, tend to act as allies or opponents of the president according to party. The real contest then becomes, in their phrase, a "separation of parties, not powers."

On their account, the checks operate vigorously when different parties hold different branches, and weakly when a single party holds them all.

The two overrides we began with point in the other direction. In each of them, members crossed a president of their own party. But a handful of cases cannot settle the question, and the record contains examples on both sides.

[pause]

What you can carry away is a set of questions, to ask about any clash between the branches.

Who holds the tool?

[beat]

How many votes does it take to use it?

[beat]

And what would make the people who hold it willing to use it?

[pause]

Think once more of those two vetoes. A Democrat in 2016. A Republican in 2020. The tool was the override, and the count was two-thirds. [beat] And both times, enough members of the president's own party were willing.
