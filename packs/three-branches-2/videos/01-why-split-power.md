## The phrase that is not there

Search the Constitution for the phrase "separation of powers" and you will come up empty. "Checks and balances" is not there either.

[beat]

The document never announces the principle. It simply builds it.

> The Constitution never names the separation of powers. It builds it.

## Three vesting clauses

Each of the first three articles opens with a sentence that vests a power, which means that it places that power in a particular institution. Article One gives the legislative powers to Congress. Article Two begins: "The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America." Article Three vests the judicial power in one supreme Court, and in the lower courts Congress creates.

- Article I: Congress makes the law
- Article II: the President carries it out
- Article III: the courts decide cases

## The very definition of tyranny

Imagine a school where the principal writes the dress code, patrols the hallways, and hears every appeal. The principal may be a perfectly decent person. What matters is that nothing in the arrangement would catch a mistake or an abuse.

- One person writes, enforces and judges

James Madison defined the danger in Federalist 47. The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, he wrote, "may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny." The definition says nothing about whether the rulers are wise, popular or even elected.

> For Madison, tyranny is a fact about structure.

## Ambition against ambition

A definition is not a remedy. Madison's appears in Federalist 51: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place."

- Federalist No. 51

He does not ask officials to be virtuous. A senator who wants to matter has a personal reason to defend the Senate.

> The design does not abolish ambition. It puts ambition to work.

## Separate does not mean sealed off

Madison rejected the picture of the branches as three sealed rooms. When a President vetoes a bill, the executive is taking a part in lawmaking. When the Senate votes on a judge, the legislature is taking a part in staffing the courts.

- The veto: a part in lawmaking
- Confirming judges: a part in the courts

> Checks and balances: each branch holds a share in the others' jobs.

## What stays open

Whether the balance has shifted since then remains an open argument. Critics in both parties contend that the presidency now holds far more power than the Framers anticipated. Others reply that Congress gave much of that power away by passing laws, and can take it back in the same manner.
