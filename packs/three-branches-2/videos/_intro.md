## A principle with no name

Search the Constitution for the phrase "separation of powers" and you will come up empty. "Checks and balances" is not there either. The two ideas that most Americans would name first about their government appear nowhere in its founding text.

> The Constitution never announces the principle. It builds it.

## Two lessons

The first lesson asks why the Framers divided federal power among Congress, the President and the courts. It works from the Constitution's own text and from James Madison's argument in the Federalist essays.

- 1. Why split power three ways?

The second asks how one branch stops another: the veto and the override, the purse, the Senate's consent, judicial review and impeachment. Its examples are paired across party lines.

- 2. How one branch stops another

Neither lesson says whether the balance among the branches is the right one today. They give the blueprint, and the moving parts, that the argument is about.

- The blueprint, then the moving parts

## Where to start

Start with the first lesson, Why did the Framers split power three ways? It begins with those two missing phrases.
