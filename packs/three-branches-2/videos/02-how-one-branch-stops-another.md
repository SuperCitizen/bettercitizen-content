## Two overrides

In 2016, President Barack Obama, a Democrat, vetoed a bill that allowed the families of September 11 victims to sue foreign governments. Congress overrode him, and the Senate vote was 97 to 1.

In 2020, President Donald Trump, a Republican, vetoed the annual defense policy bill. Congress overrode him too, and the Senate vote was 81 to 13.

- 2016: Senate 97 to 1
- 2021: Senate 81 to 13

In both fights, members of the President's own party voted against him.

> Neither override could have passed without the President's own party.

## From 51 to 67

A simple majority is enough to pass a bill. Forcing one into law over a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber. In a Senate where all 100 members vote, that is 67, and a supermajority almost always requires votes from the President's own party.

- Pass a bill: a simple majority
- Override a veto: two-thirds of each chamber

That is why overrides are so rare. Congress has overridden about 7 percent of regular vetoes.

- Overridden: about 7% of regular vetoes

## A check with no army

The courts hold judicial review, the power to measure a law or an official act against the Constitution. Alexander Hamilton wrote that the courts have "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment."

- Hamilton, Federalist No. 78

A court commands no army and controls no budget. Its rulings bind only because the other branches, and the public, treat them as binding. In 1952 the Supreme Court ruled against President Harry Truman, a Democrat, and in 1974 against President Richard Nixon, a Republican. Truman returned the steel mills he had seized, and Nixon released his tape recordings.

> A court's rulings bind because the other branches treat them as binding.

## An accusation, then a trial

Impeachment begins in the House, which may accuse an official by a majority vote. To impeach means to accuse, not to remove. The Senate then holds a trial, and a conviction requires two-thirds of the senators present. It is the same two-thirds arithmetic that governs the veto.

- Impeach: a majority of the House
- Convict: two-thirds of senators present

## Parties, not powers?

Madison's design assumes that officeholders will defend their own branch. In 2006 the legal scholars Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes argued that this assumption fails once political parties are strong. On their account, the checks operate vigorously when different parties hold different branches, and weakly when a single party holds them all.

- Levinson and Pildes, 2006

The two overrides point in the other direction.

> "Separation of parties, not powers": an argument the record does not settle.
