# Why did the Framers split power three ways?

Search the Constitution for the phrase "separation of powers" and you will come up empty, and "checks and balances" is not there either. The two ideas that most Americans would name first about their government appear nowhere in its founding text.

The document never announces the principle; it simply builds it. Article I begins by handing one kind of power to Congress, Article II hands another to a President, and Article III hands a third to the courts. To understand why the Framers constructed the government that way, it helps to begin with the problem they were trying to solve.

## The problem: power that checks its own work

Consider a case with nothing political riding on it. Imagine a school where the principal writes the dress code, patrols the hallways looking for violations, and hears every appeal from students who believe they were punished unfairly. Suppose the code bans "distracting" clothing. The principal decides what counts as distracting, hands out the punishment, and then rules on whether that punishment was fair.

This principal may be a perfectly decent person, but that is beside the point. What matters is that nothing in the arrangement would catch a mistake or an abuse if one occurred. A vague rule can be stretched at the moment it is enforced, and the person who stretched it is also the person who decides whether the stretch was acceptable.

The delegates who gathered in Philadelphia in 1787 had encountered this problem from two directions. They had fought a war against a king whose power they considered unchecked, and they had then lived for several years under the Articles of Confederation, a national government that consisted of a Congress and little else. It had no separate executive and almost no courts, and it could not compel the states to pay the money it requested from them. Meanwhile, elected legislatures in the states were demonstrating that an assembly could overreach as easily as a monarch. James Madison, who did as much as anyone to shape the new design, later wrote that the legislative branch was "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex."

The Framers therefore wanted a government strong enough to act, but they were unwilling to trust any single part of it with the whole job.

## The text: three vesting clauses

Each of the first three articles opens with a sentence that *vests* a power, which means that it places that power in a particular institution.

The three institutions are chosen in three different ways, and they run on three different clocks. House members face the voters every two years. Senators serve for six years, with only about a third of the seats coming up at any one election, and until the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913 they were chosen by state legislatures instead of by voters. A President serves for four years. Federal judges never appear on a ballot at all: the President nominates them, the Senate confirms them, and they hold office "during good Behaviour," which in practice means for life.

That staggering is deliberate, and its effect is visible on any election day.

A wave of public anger or enthusiasm can sweep through the House in a single November. To remake the Senate, the same wave has to last through several elections, and it reaches the courts only gradually, as judges retire and are replaced.

Two numbers in this picture are not in the Constitution, although most people assume that they are. Congress fixed the House at 435 members by an ordinary law in 1929, and it set the Supreme Court at nine justices in the same way in 1869.

<Checkpoint question="A movement wins the presidency and a majority of the House in the same election. Why might it still fail to pass its program?">

Because the Senate and the courts run on other clocks. Only about a third of the Senate was on the ballot, which means that most senators were chosen in earlier elections, and no judge was on the ballot at all. A bill has to pass both chambers, and a law can later be challenged in court. The design therefore requires a winning coalition to hold together across several elections before it controls every institution.

</Checkpoint>

## The argument: two essays by Madison

Why go to this much trouble? Madison supplied the answer in the *Federalist* essays, which he wrote with Alexander Hamilton and John Jay in order to persuade New York to ratify the Constitution. In No. 47 he defined the danger:

> **In the primary sources:** "The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective, may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."

Notice what the definition leaves out. It says nothing about whether the rulers are wise, popular or even elected, because tyranny, for Madison, is a fact about structure. A government that holds all three powers in one set of hands meets the definition even on its best day. Madison credited the idea to the French writer Montesquieu, who had argued in 1748 that liberty depends on keeping the three powers apart.

A definition is not a remedy, however. Simply writing "the branches shall remain separate" on paper would do nothing to stop an ambitious official, and Madison knew it. His remedy appears in No. 51.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Federalist No. 51 (1788)">

"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place. … If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary. In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself."

</Callout>

Read the first two sentences slowly. Madison does not ask officials to be virtuous. Instead, he connects each official's self-interest ("the interest of the man") to the powers of the office that the official holds ("the constitutional rights of the place"). A senator who wants to matter therefore has a personal reason to defend the Senate, just as a judge has a personal reason to defend the courts. The design does not attempt to abolish ambition; it puts ambition to work.

<Checkpoint question="Madison says a government must first control the governed and then control itself. Which of the two did the Articles of Confederation fail at? Which was the complaint against the king?">

The Articles failed at the first, because Congress could neither make the states pay nor carry out its own decisions. The complaint against the king concerned the second: his government was able to act, but nothing obliged it to control itself. The Constitution attempts both at once, by giving the government enough power to govern and then dividing that power so that the government has to check itself.

</Checkpoint>

## The near miss: separate does not mean sealed off

It is tempting to picture the branches as three sealed rooms, but Madison rejected that reading in the same essay that defined tyranny. Montesquieu, he wrote, "did not mean that these departments ought to have no partial agency in, or no control over, the acts of each other."

What the principle forbids is one branch holding the *whole* power of another. When a President vetoes a bill, the executive is taking a part in lawmaking, and when the Senate votes on a judge, the legislature is taking a part in staffing the courts. Those overlaps are the design, not a flaw in it.

This is also the difference between two terms that are often used as though they meant the same thing. [[term:separation-of-powers]] is the division of the three jobs among three institutions. [[term:checks-and-balances]] are the shares that each branch holds in the others' jobs. In 1960 the political scientist Richard Neustadt joined the two ideas in a single line, writing that the Convention had created not a government of separated powers but "a government of separated institutions sharing powers."

## Where the school breaks down, and what stays open

The school example suggests a tidy fix, which is to give the three jobs to three different people. The real design is less tidy in two ways.

First, as the previous section showed, the three branches share powers instead of holding them in isolation. Second, the Framers never expected the branches to be equal. "In republican government," Madison wrote in No. 51, "the legislative authority necessarily predominates." That expectation explains why Congress alone is divided against itself, into two chambers that have different terms and different voters, so that each can slow the other. [[term:bicameral]] is the word for a legislature built that way.

Whether the balance has shifted since then remains an open argument. Critics in both parties contend that the presidency now holds far more power than the Framers anticipated, while others reply that Congress gave much of that power away by passing laws and can take it back in the same manner. This lesson does not settle that argument; it gives you the blueprint that the argument is about.

A blueprint is not a working machine, however. The next lesson turns to the moving parts: what each branch can actually do to stop another, and how often it succeeds.
