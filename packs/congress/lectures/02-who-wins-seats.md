October 2024. The Gallup poll asks Americans whether they approve of the way Congress is doing its job.

Sixteen percent say yes. [beat] Sixteen.

That same year, 387 members of the House ask voters for another term. And 371 of them win it.

[pause]

So here is the puzzle. People think poorly of Congress. And they keep sending the same members back.

The political scientist Richard Fenno made that puzzle the title of an essay in 1975. If, as Ralph Nader says, Congress is "the broken branch," how come we love our congressmen so much?

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to answer him. You will know why the people already in Congress usually win again. You will know where the real contest for most seats takes place. And you will know who ends up in Congress as a result.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the number itself: what it hides, and what it shows.

Second, why the election that matters is often not the one in November.

Third, who serves.

And last, whether that matters.

## What the number hides, and what it shows

Start with a word. An incumbent is the person who holds an office right now. When a sitting member runs again, they run as the incumbent.

In 2024, House incumbents who ran again won 95.9 percent of the time. That counts the four who lost in their own party's primaries earlier in the year. In the House, the rate has been above 90 percent in every election from 2014 through 2024.

The Senate swings more. In 2022, every senator who ran again won. In 2024, 22 of 25 did. And in 1980, only about 55 percent did.

So the tempting conclusion is that holding a seat makes a member nearly unbeatable. [beat] Hold on. That is the near miss.

> A reelection rate measures more than incumbency.

Here is what hides inside it. Three things.

The first is who runs. The rate counts only members who sought another term. In 2024, 46 House members left instead. And members who expect a hard race have a reason to be among them.

The second is the district. Many districts lean so heavily toward one party that almost any nominee of that party would win there.

The third is the challengers. Strong challengers tend to wait for an open seat, rather than take on someone already in office.

[pause]

Here is a number that shows the third one at work. At the start of 2025, the House had 435 members. Two hundred fifty-eight of them first got there by succeeding a retiring member of their own party. Fewer than one in five first got there by beating an incumbent.

Put another way, most members did not knock anyone out. They inherited a seat their party already held.

[pause]

That is what the number hides. Now, what it does show. Incumbents do have real advantages.

The most influential account comes from the political scientist David Mayhew, in a 1974 book called Congress: The Electoral Connection. Mayhew asked a simple question. What would Congress look like if members were driven, above all, by the wish to be reelected?

His answer was that it would look a great deal like the Congress we have. And he named three things such members do.

Advertising. [beat] Building a familiar, favorable name.

Credit claiming. [beat] Tying themselves personally to things the government did for the district. A new bridge. A grant. A problem solved with a federal agency.

And position taking. [beat] Putting themselves on record on the issues voters care about.

> Advertising, credit claiming, position taking: three ways members chase reelection.

The office supplies the tools for all three. Each House member runs an office paid for by an allowance that averages about $1.93 million a year. Most of it pays for staff.

Those staff answer the mail. They handle casework: a constituent's lost veterans' benefits, a stuck passport application. And every case solved is credit claimed.

Then there is money. In the 2024 election cycle, House incumbents raised about $3.35 million each, on average. Challengers raised about $510,000. That challengers' figure includes candidates who lost their primaries. But the gap is large either way.

One protection incumbents do not have is a term limit. And a state cannot impose one. In 1995, in a case called U.S. Term Limits versus Thornton, the Supreme Court held, five to four, that states may not add qualifications for Congress beyond the ones in the Constitution.

Changing that would take a constitutional amendment.

Let's test what we have so far. Here is a scene.

A House member wins reelection with 70 percent of the vote. A political scientist looks at the result and says it does not show how much incumbency helped.

What does she mean? Take a moment.

[long pause]

She means the district might have voted that way for anyone. If the district leans that hard toward the member's party, almost any nominee from that party would have won a similar share.

So the real value of incumbency is a difference. It is what the incumbent got, minus what a newcomer from the same party would have gotten in the same place. And you cannot read that difference off the winning share.

## The election that matters

That brings us to the districts themselves. Let's move to ground nobody owns.

Picture an invented state with two parties, the Lantern Party and the Harbor Party. Its Third District votes about 70 to 30 for Lantern, in every election.

Ask yourself: when is that seat decided?

[beat]

Not in November. The November race there is settled before it starts.

The only election that can change who holds the seat is the Lantern Party's primary. A primary is the election in which a party's own voters choose its nominee.

And primaries are different. Turnout is usually lower than in November. The voters who do show up tend to be the party's most committed.

So the member from the Third District has more to fear from a challenger in her own party than from any Harbor candidate.

> In a safe seat, the primary is the real election.

Think of a tournament where the semifinal decides the champion. The final is a formality.

Here is where that picture breaks. In a tournament, the same crowd watches both rounds. In a safe district, the semifinal is decided by a smaller crowd than the final, and often a different one.

Real districts look a lot like the Third. In 2024, 38 of the 435 House races had no candidate at all from one of the two major parties. Two out of three House incumbents on the November ballot won at least 60 percent of the two-party vote.

A seat like that, one that reliably goes one way, is called a safe seat.

[pause]

And the primaries do end careers, in both parties. In 2024 they ended four, two from each side.

Republican Bob Good of Virginia lost his primary by fewer than 400 votes, out of about 63,000.

Democrat Jamaal Bowman of New York lost his, about 59 percent to 41.

Democrat Cori Bush of Missouri lost hers, about 51 percent to 46.

And Republican Jerry Carl of Alabama lost to another sitting Republican, after redistricting put them in the same district.

In 2026, 11 House members lost their primaries. Seven were Democrats and four were Republicans.

The Senate is different, because a senator's district is a whole state, and no map-drawer can reshape a state. Senate incumbents lose more often, and the losses can fall on either party.

In 2024, all three senators who lost were Democrats. In 2026, eight senators lost, and all eight were Republicans. Two of them lost in primaries. They were the first senators to lose a primary since 2012.

Those same 2026 elections gave Democrats control of both chambers, for the Congress that begins in January 2027.

So why are so many House seats safe? Two explanations compete. One is how district lines are drawn. The other is where Americans choose to live. How much each one matters is a live argument, and the Gerrymandering pack takes it up.

## Who serves

So far, the question has been who wins. Now, who are the winners?

Winning a seat takes money, time, connections, and usually a prior office. So Congress is not a random sample of the country. Here are the numbers, as of 2025.

Women hold about 29 percent of the seats. Women are about 51 percent of the country.

Non-Hispanic White Americans are 74 percent of Congress, and 58 percent of the population.

In the House, the share of Black members is 14 percent, the same as the population. The Hispanic share is 11 percent, against 20 percent of the population.

[pause]

Education shows the widest gap of all. Ninety-six percent of House members hold a bachelor's degree. Among American adults 25 and older, it is about 39 percent.

> Congress differs from the country most in education, occupation, and wealth.

And most members have held office before. Seventy percent of House members did, before they arrived. Fewer than one in five has served in the military now, against 73 percent in the early 1970s.

Age and money are harder to compare fairly. The Constitution sets minimum ages, 25 for the House and 30 for the Senate. So Congress would skew older even if seats were filled at random. At the start of 2025, the average representative was about 58, and the average senator about 64.

Wealth has no official count, because members report their assets in broad ranges. One news outlet, NOTUS, estimated in March 2026 that at least 73 of the 100 senators are millionaires. The Federal Reserve put the median American family's net worth at $215,900.

And class shows up in careers. The political scientist Nicholas Carnes found that the average member of Congress has spent less than two percent of their adult life in a working-class job.

Now a recall question. Earlier, I gave you three things that hide inside a reelection rate. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Who runs. [beat] How the district leans. [beat] And which challengers wait for an open seat.

Hold on to the first one. Who runs shapes who serves, too.

## Does it matter who serves?

That is what Congress looks like. Now the harder question: does it matter?

Political theorists have a name for a legislature that mirrors its population in traits like race, gender, and class. They call it descriptive representation. Whether Congress should look more like the country is a value question. And both sides have serious advocates.

The case that it matters comes from the political theorist Jane Mansbridge, in a 1999 article. Her title asks whether Black Americans should represent Black Americans, and women represent women. Her answer was a contingent yes.

Shared experience matters in particular situations, she argued, not always. Where a group distrusts the system, a member who shares its experience communicates better. Where an issue is not yet on the agenda, people who live with it are likelier to raise it. And a legislature that includes people once kept out tells everyone that those groups are fit to rule.

Carnes adds evidence about class. On his account, members' working lives shape how they see economic issues, and how they vote on them.

> The case for: shared experience changes what members notice and raise.

The case that accountability matters more comes from Hanna Pitkin, whose 1967 book is the classic work on representation. Voters, she argued, should judge representatives by what they do, not by what they are.

A focus on resemblance, she wrote, leaves "no room for representation as accountability."

On this view the Constitution has it right. It sets a minimum age, a period of citizenship, and living in the state. It leaves everything else to the voters, who can choose anyone they believe will act for them.

And the political theorist Suzanne Dovi adds a caution, even for those who value resemblance. Not every member who shares a group's traits represents that group well.

> The case against: judge members by what they do, not who they are.

Here is a question to sit with, and I will not answer it.

Pick the side you find less convincing. Now state its best case, in its own strongest terms.

[pause 5s]

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A reelection rate measures more than incumbency. It also measures who chose to run, how the district leans, and which challengers waited.

[beat]

Two. Incumbents still have real advantages: a staffed office, casework, and far more money. Mayhew's three words describe what they do with them. Advertising, credit claiming, position taking.

[beat]

Three. In a safe seat, the primary is the real election. And primaries have ended careers in both parties.

[beat]

Four. Congress differs from the country most in education, occupation, and wealth. Whether that is a problem is a question of values, with Mansbridge on one side and Pitkin on the other.

[pause]

What is not settled is what, if anything, to do about it. Term limits would take a constitutional amendment. More competitive districts depend on who draws the lines. And no rule can make voters choose people who look like them. Whether any rule should is the open question.

The next lecture asks what happens once members arrive. Who runs Congress, and how do members decide how to vote?

[pause]

So, back to Fenno's puzzle. Sixteen percent approve of Congress. Ninety-six percent of House members who run again win.

Part of the answer is the office itself: the staff, the casework, the money. Part of it is who runs, and where. And part of it is that, in most districts, November was never really the contest.

> The question is often settled before November, in the primary.
