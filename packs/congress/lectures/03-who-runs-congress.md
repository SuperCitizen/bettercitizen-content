January 3, 2023. The newly elected House of Representatives meets to choose its Speaker. And it cannot.

Kevin McCarthy, the leader of the new Republican majority, needs a majority of the votes. On the first ballot, 19 Republicans vote for someone else.

So the House votes again. And again. And again.

At 12:34 in the morning on January 7, on the fifteenth ballot, McCarthy finally wins. [beat] With 216 votes.

[pause]

No Speaker election had needed more than one ballot since 1923.

Nine months later, eight Republicans joined 208 Democrats and removed him, 216 to 210. No Speaker had ever been removed before.

That episode shows something an organization chart does not. The people who run Congress keep their jobs only as long as enough of their own members back them.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to say who holds power inside Congress, and how they got it. And you will be able to explain how members decide how to vote, and what the evidence says about it.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the committees, where Congress divides its work.

Second, the leaders, who set the agenda.

Third, three answers to the question of how a member should vote.

And last, the evidence: what members actually do.

## Committees

Start with the volume of work. In the Congress that met in 2023 and 2024, members introduced more than 19,000 measures.

Of those, about 16,500 were bills and joint resolutions, the kinds that can become law. And 274 became law. That is under 2 percent.

So something has to choose. That something is the committee system.

Each chamber divides its work among standing committees. A standing committee is a permanent panel that covers a set of subjects. Today the House has 20 of them, and the Senate has 16.

Taxes go to House Ways and Means and to Senate Finance. Farm programs go to the two Agriculture committees. The military goes to Armed Services. Committees hold hearings, rewrite bills line by line, and oversee the federal agencies in their area.

> Committees divide the work, and the majority party runs every one.

Here is a picture to hold. Think of a hospital, with its departments. Cardiology sees heart patients, and its doctors know hearts best.

Now here is where that picture breaks. A hospital does not let one faction pick every department head. In Congress, the majority party holds most of the seats on every committee, and every chair.

[pause]

So how does a member get a seat? Through the party.

In the House, each party's steering committee nominates its members for committees. The party's caucus or conference approves the list. And then the full House adopts it.

After every election, the two parties' leaders negotiate how many seats each party gets on each committee. Only the Ethics Committee is split evenly, five members to five.

Chairs are nominated by the majority party, and then elected by the House. Seniority no longer guarantees a gavel. House Republicans limit their chairs to three terms in a row. Senate Republicans limit theirs to six years.

Let's test that. Here is a scene.

A newly elected representative wants to shape farm policy. She wants a seat on the Agriculture Committee. Who actually decides whether she gets it?

Take a moment.

[long pause]

Her party decides, before the House as a whole has any say. Her party's steering committee nominates her, and her party's caucus approves. Only then does the full House adopt the list.

Even the number of Agriculture seats her party can hand out was settled earlier, by the two parties' leaders. So the people she most needs to persuade are her own party's leaders and colleagues.

## Leaders

That is the committees. Now the people at the top.

The Constitution names only one House leader, in a single sentence. "The House of Representatives shall chuse their Speaker and other Officers."

That is all it says. It does not even say the Speaker must be a member of the House. Every Speaker so far has been one. But the Constitution does not require it.

The Speaker presides over the House. And through the Rules Committee, the Speaker decides which bills reach the floor, and on what terms.

The whole House elects the Speaker. In practice, the majority party picks its candidate, and then has to hold its members together.

> The Speaker controls the House floor, but only with the party's votes.

Now the math of that vote, because it explains January 2023. A Speaker needs a majority of the votes cast for a person by name. A member who votes "present" does not count toward that total. So a present vote lowers the bar.

Here is a puzzle. On that fifteenth ballot, McCarthy got 216 votes, and Hakeem Jeffries got 212. Six Republican holdouts voted present.

So how was 216 enough? Work it through.

[pause 5s]

Add the votes for named candidates. 216 plus 212 is 428. Half of 428 is 214. So 215 was a majority.

[beat]

McCarthy's 216 cleared it, because six of his opponents chose present instead of naming someone else.

And Democratic leaders have faced their own holdouts. In January 2019, 15 Democrats did not vote for Nancy Pelosi. She still won, with 220 votes. In 2021, five did not, and she won 216 to 209.

[pause]

Below the Speaker, each party in each chamber elects a floor leader and a whip. The majority leader runs the floor day to day. The minority leader leads the other side.

And the whips count votes in advance, and bring wavering members along. The word comes from fox hunting. The "whipper-in" was the rider who kept the hounds from straying from the pack.

The Senate is built differently. Under the Constitution, the Vice President presides, and votes only to break a tie. A president pro tempore stands in when the Vice President is away.

Neither one runs the Senate. [beat] The majority leader does.

The parties created that post in the 1920s. And its main tool comes from a single day: August 13, 1937.

That day, Vice President John Nance Garner announced that when several senators seek recognition at once, the chair will call on the majority leader first. Speaking first lets the leader make the first motion. And that shapes what the Senate takes up.

> The Senate majority leader's power rests on being recognized first.

As of late November 2026, the Speaker is Mike Johnson, a Republican. The Senate majority leader is John Thune, also a Republican. The Democratic leaders are Hakeem Jeffries in the House and Chuck Schumer in the Senate.

That is about to change. Democrats won both chambers in November 2026. So when the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, the majority jobs switch parties.

House Democrats have nominated Jeffries for Speaker. House Republicans have chosen Jim Jordan to lead them in the minority. Senate Republicans reelected Thune as their leader. And Senate Democrats choose their leaders on December 1.

## Three answers to one question

That is who runs Congress. Now the harder question. Once a member has a seat, how should she vote?

Let's move to ground nobody owns. Picture Representative Dana Reyes. Her invented district mixes farm towns with one college city.

A bill comes to the floor. It would end a crop subsidy her district's farmers rely on, and spend the money on research grants.

Polls in her district run about two to one against it. She has studied the bill, and she thinks it is good policy. And her party's leaders have made it a priority. Their whip has asked for her vote.

How should she vote?

[beat]

Political scientists describe three classic answers.

The first is the delegate. A representative is the voters' agent, there to vote as the district wants. Reyes votes no.

The second is the trustee. Voters chose her to think, and they can judge her at the next election. Reyes votes yes.

The third is the partisan. Voters chose a party's program, and breaking from it blurs what the label means. Reyes votes yes, with her party.

> Delegate, trustee, partisan: three answers to how a member should vote.

Each answer has a history.

The trustee's most famous defense is a speech Edmund Burke gave in 1774, to the voters of Bristol, England, who had sent him to Parliament. Listen for one word: judgment.

"Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion."

[pause]

His judgment. [beat] And the ending matters. Six years later, after disagreements with his constituents, Burke withdrew from the Bristol election.

The delegate has deep American roots. Founding-era towns sometimes sent their representatives off with written instructions. In August 1789, a member of the First Congress, Thomas Tucker, moved to add the people's right "to instruct their Representatives" to the amendments that became the Bill of Rights.

It did not make it in. The First Amendment protects the right to petition the government. Not to instruct it.

The partisan has its champions too. In 1950, a committee of the American Political Science Association called for "a more responsible two-party system." Parties with clear programs, and members who carry them out, so that voters know what they are choosing.

And a Speaker put it more bluntly. In 2003, Dennis Hastert set out his principles for the job. The fifth was "to please the majority of your majority." A Speaker, he said, should not move bills that run against the wishes of most of his own party.

Most members mix all three. Political scientists call the blend the politico. A delegate on the issues the district watches closely. A trustee on the ones it does not. And a partisan when the leaders need the vote.

Let's test it. Reyes votes yes, against her district's polls. From the roll call alone, which model was she following?

Take a moment.

[long pause]

You cannot tell. A yes vote fits the trustee, who thought the bill was right. It fits the partisan, whose party wanted it. And it fits a politico's reasoning too.

> A roll call records what a member did, not why.

Political scientists argue over exactly that gap. Hold on to it. We need it in a minute.

## What members actually do

So much for what members should do. Here is what they do.

A party-unity vote is a roll call in which most of one party votes against most of the other.

In 2025, according to CQ Roll Call's annual study, 85.3 percent of roll calls were party-unity votes. That is the highest share since the study began. And on those votes, the average member of each party sided with their party between 92 and 96 percent of the time.

The study's authors note one thing that inflates the share somewhat: it counts two votes on each nomination.

[pause]

Scales that place members from liberal to conservative by their votes tell the same story. Since 2003 in the House, and 2005 in the Senate, the most conservative Democrat has been more liberal than the most liberal Republican.

Put another way, the two parties no longer overlap at all.

Yet members of both parties still break ranks, often on the biggest votes.

In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act passed the House 218 to 214. Two Republicans voted no: Brian Fitzpatrick and Thomas Massie. In the Senate, three Republicans voted no: Susan Collins, Rand Paul, and Thom Tillis. The bill passed only when Vice President JD Vance broke a 50 to 50 tie.

On the Democratic side, there was March 2025. Nine Democratic senators, including their leader, Chuck Schumer, joined the independent Angus King. They voted to end debate on a government funding bill most of their party opposed. It became law the next day.

And in January 2025, 46 House Democrats and 12 Democratic senators voted for the Laken Riley Act, an immigration-enforcement bill, against most of their party.

[pause]

So why is party voting so high? A minute ago I gave you a gap. Can you name it?

[long pause]

A roll call records what a member did, not why. And here, the same numbers support two different stories.

The first comes from the political scientist Keith Krehbiel, in a 1993 article called "Where's the Party?" What looks like party discipline, he argued, may just be members voting their own preferences, which happen to line up with their party's. Parties sorted into like-minded teams would vote together with no pressure at all.

The second comes from Gary Cox and Mathew McCubbins, in a 2005 book called Setting the Agenda. The majority party's real power, they argued, is choosing which questions come to a vote at all. So unity on the floor is partly a product of what leaders keep off the floor.

> The same party-line votes fit two stories: shared views, or agenda control.

Both accounts fit the roll calls. They disagree about what happens before the roll is called.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Committees divide the work, and the majority party holds most of their seats and every chair. Members get their committee seats through their party.

[beat]

Two. The Speaker controls the House floor, but only with the party's votes. And a present vote lowers the bar. In the Senate, the majority leader's power rests on being recognized first.

[beat]

Three. There are three answers to how a member should vote. Delegate, trustee, partisan. Most members blend them, and a roll call cannot tell you which one was at work.

[beat]

Four. Members vote with their parties more than at any time in modern records, and members of both parties still break ranks on major votes.

[pause]

What is not settled is whether all that party voting is good for representation. Advocates of responsible parties argue that disciplined parties give voters a clear choice, and someone to hold accountable. The delegate tradition answers that a member who votes the party line every time has stopped representing a district, and started representing a caucus.

That argument continues in the Political Parties pack. And the How a Bill Becomes Law pack follows a bill through the gates these leaders control.

[pause]

So come back to that night in January 2023. Fifteen ballots. A Speaker who won only because six holdouts voted present. And who, nine months later, lost the job when eight Republicans voted with the other side.

The rules give the leaders their tools. The members decide how long they keep them.

> Leaders hold power in Congress only as long as their members let them.
