Philadelphia, the middle of July, 1787. The Constitutional Convention is on the edge of falling apart.

For weeks, the delegates have been stuck on a single question. In the new national legislature, should a state's voice depend on how many people it has? Or should every state count the same?

On July 16, they vote on a way out. North Carolina switches sides. The delegation from Massachusetts splits down the middle, so it casts no vote at all.

And the proposal passes. [beat] Five states to four.

[pause]

That one-state margin is why the United States has, in effect, two legislatures stacked inside one Congress. Every federal law since has had to win over two bodies, elected on different schedules, from different maps, by different arithmetic.

By the end, you will be able to say what that design does, and what it costs. You will also know the strongest case on each side of the argument it left behind.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the deal itself: two rival plans, and the compromise between them.

Second, the two machines the deal built.

Third, an image to carry with you: two locks on one door.

And last, the price of an equal Senate, in today's numbers.

## Two plans and a split

The delegates arrived with two rival blueprints.

The populous states favored the Virginia Plan. It proposed a bicameral legislature, a legislature of two chambers, with both chambers apportioned by population. More people, more votes, in both houses.

The small states favored the New Jersey Plan. It proposed to amend the existing Articles of Confederation. And under the Articles, every state had an equal vote in Congress, no matter its size.

Votes by population, or an equal vote for every state. [beat] That was the choice.

In June, the Convention set the New Jersey Plan aside and kept working from Virginia's. The small states did not go quietly. Gunning Bedford of Delaware warned that if the large states broke up the Confederation, the small ones would "find some foreign ally."

[pause]

On July 2, a motion to give each state one vote in the second chamber tied, five states to five.

Roger Sherman of Connecticut had been pushing a way through. Keep two chambers, but give them different foundations. Population in the first. One equal voice for each state in the second.

A committee with one delegate from each state recommended a version of that idea, and on July 16 it passed. History calls it the Great Compromise, or the Connecticut Compromise, after Sherman's state.

> Population in the House. Equal states in the Senate. That is the Great Compromise.

Put another way, each plan got one chamber. The House follows Virginia's principle. The Senate follows New Jersey's.

Not everyone celebrated. James Madison, the chief architect of the Virginia Plan, opposed it. To him, and to other nationalists, a Senate with equal votes looked too much like the weak Confederation Congress they had come to replace.

And the settlement set up a darker bargain. Once House seats were to follow population, the delegates had to decide who counted as population.

Their answer is called the Three-Fifths Compromise. It counted three of every five enslaved people toward a state's share of House seats. That inflated the representation of the slaveholding states, which were counting people they denied every political right.

[pause]

The rule held until 1868, when the Fourteenth Amendment based apportionment on "the whole number of persons in each State."

One more fact about the deal, and it will matter at the end. The Senate's equal vote is the most entrenched rule in the Constitution. Article Five allows almost anything to be amended, with one exception: "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate."

[pause]

Without its consent. Hold on to that phrase.

## One job, two machines

That is the deal. Now look at what it built.

The two chambers share one essential job. A bill becomes law only if both pass the identical text, and then the president signs it, or Congress overrides a veto.

Beyond that, the Constitution built them to be different, on purpose. Think of each chamber as a machine with a set of dials. There are four.

Size. The House has 435 voting members, a number Congress fixed by law in 1929. The Senate has 100: two for each state.

Term. A representative serves two years, and every House seat is on the ballot at every election. A senator serves six, and the terms are staggered, so that only about a third of the seats are up every two years.

Voters. A representative is elected by one district, and districts hold roughly equal populations. A senator is elected by a whole state. Until 1913, the state legislatures did that choosing.

And qualifications. A representative must be at least 25, and a citizen for seven years. A senator must be at least 30, and a citizen for nine.

[pause]

Now look at which way the dials point. In the House, toward responsiveness: short terms, small districts, every seat exposed at once. In the Senate, toward endurance: long terms, statewide constituencies, slightly older members, and a calendar that never lets the whole body turn over at once.

> The House is built to respond. The Senate is built to endure.

Each chamber also holds powers the other does not.

The House holds two. Revenue bills must start there. And the House impeaches, which means it brings the charges.

The Senate holds three. It tries impeachments, with two-thirds needed to convict. It approves treaties. And it confirms appointments.

[beat]

Trials, treaties, confirmations. Keep those three. I will ask for them later.

The logic is the logic of the dials. The Framers gave the slower, steadier body the final say on decisions they expected to outlast the passions of a single season.

Let's test the dials. A sudden swing in the national mood hits in one November election. What is the most it can change, in each chamber, that year?

[long pause]

In the House, potentially everything. All 435 seats are on the ballot.

In the Senate, only about a third: the 33 or 34 seats whose terms are ending, plus any special election to fill a vacancy. The other two-thirds of the senators were elected in earlier years, and they keep their seats.

So a mood has to last through at least two elections to reach most of the Senate. That delay is the staggered terms doing exactly what they were designed to do.

## Two locks on one door

Now the image. Picture a door with two locks, cut by two different locksmiths.

A burglar who copies one key still cannot get in. [beat] And a resident who has lost one key cannot get in either.

That is the bargain of a two-chamber legislature.

> Two chambers: harder to do something bad, and harder to do anything at all.

The image breaks in one place. Real locks do not negotiate, and the chambers do. Each can amend the other's bill and send it back. So the key that finally turns is often one that neither chamber started with.

Madison had lost the fight over how to build the Senate. He defended it anyway, once the Constitution went to the states for ratification.

In Federalist 62, he argued that a second chamber, distinct from the first and sharing its power, doubles the people's protection. A scheme that betrays the public has to win over two separate bodies, and not just one.

He added a second argument, one that is easy to miss. A body that changes slowly guards against laws that change faster than citizens, or businesses, can keep up with.

So the case for a second chamber is double protection, and steadier law. Its cost is the other half of the image. [beat] Every law gets harder to pass.

[pause]

The second lock is not hypothetical, and it does not belong to either party. Two cases, one from each.

In 2017, a Republican House passed a bill to repeal and replace much of the Affordable Care Act, 217 to 213. In the Senate, a much narrower version, known as "skinny repeal," needed only a simple majority. It failed, 49 to 51, when three Republicans joined the entire Democratic caucus in voting no.

In 2021, a Democratic House passed a sweeping bill on elections and ethics, 220 to 210. Senate Democrats brought up their own version. On June 22, every senator in their caucus voted to begin debate on it, and every Republican voted no. [beat] The result was 50 to 50.

That tie was not a win. The vote was to end a filibuster against even starting debate, and that takes 60 votes, not a majority.

[pause]

In both cases, one party controlled both chambers, and the House majority still could not move the Senate. In 2017, the Senate's majority split. In 2021, a Senate rule set the bar above a majority.

A Senate rule. So here is a question. What would it take to move that bar: an amendment to the Constitution, an act of Congress signed by the president, or something less?

[long pause]

Something less. The Constitution asks only for a majority in each chamber to pass a bill. The 60-vote hurdle comes from the Senate's own rules, which the Senate itself adopted and can change. It is Rule 22, and it sets the bar at three-fifths of the senators, a level fixed in 1975.

Think of it as a third lock, one the Senate added to its own door.

> The 60-vote rule is the Senate's own, not the Constitution's.

Which is why arguments about the filibuster are arguments about Senate rules, and not about amending the Constitution.

And none of this is a law of nature. Forty-nine states copy the two-chamber model in their own legislatures. [beat] Nebraska does not. Since 1937, it has run a legislature with a single chamber, officially nonpartisan.

Bicameralism is a choice with trade-offs. It is not a requirement of democracy.

## The price of an equal Senate

Now the price. The Great Compromise was a deal between states of different sizes. And the differences in size have grown enormously since 1787.

In the 2020 census, California counted about 39 and a half million residents. Wyoming counted about 577,000.

That is roughly 68 Californians for every Wyomingite. [beat] And both states get two senators.

[pause]

In the House, the gap mostly closes, which was the point of the compromise. California has 52 representatives. Wyoming has one.

So what does the equal Senate give the small states? Let's test it.

Senators from the 26 least populous states could form a majority of the Senate. Does that mean those states can pass laws over the objections of the most populous states?

[long pause]

No. Not alone. A bill still needs a majority of the House, where seats track population. And it needs the president's signature, or a veto override.

What equal representation does give the smaller states is two things. A blocking position in the Senate. And a bigger voice in the powers the Senate holds alone. I asked you to keep three of those. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Trials, treaties, confirmations. In those, the House plays no role, except to bring the impeachment charges.

> Equal representation gives small states the power to block, not to legislate alone.

Is that trade-off worth it? The argument is open, and both sides have serious advocates. Here is each case in turn.

First, the case for the equal Senate. It starts from the states. The United States is a federal system: a union of states, as well as a nation of individuals. And the Senate is where the states are represented as states.

It makes three further points. Equal representation keeps less populous, often rural regions from being outvoted on everything by a few large metropolitan states. It forces any lasting national majority to be geographically broad, and not just numerous. And it was the price of the union itself. Without it, the small states would not have signed, which is why Article Five protects it more strongly than any other clause.

> The case for: the Senate is where the states are represented as states.

Now the case against. It starts from the citizen. The political scientist Robert Dahl made it in 2001, in a book called How Democratic Is the American Constitution? Equal representation in the Senate, Dahl argued, is among the Constitution's least democratic features.

It, too, makes three further points. The vote of a citizen in a small state counts for far more, in Senate elections, than the vote of a citizen in a large one. Senators who represent a minority of the population can form a majority. And the minorities this protects are defined by state lines, not by any shared interest that needs protecting. Many residents of large states are rural, and many residents of small states are not.

> The case against: equal votes for states mean unequal votes for citizens.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The Great Compromise. The Virginia Plan wanted both chambers apportioned by population. The New Jersey Plan kept an equal vote for every state. The deal: population in the House, equal states in the Senate. It passed by one state.

[beat]

Two. The House is built to respond, and the Senate is built to endure. Two-year terms, with every seat up at once. Six-year terms, with about a third up at a time. And the Senate alone holds trials, treaties and confirmations.

[beat]

Three. Two locks on one door. Two chambers make it harder to do something bad, and harder to do anything at all. The first half is Madison's case, in Federalist 62. The second half is the cost. And the 60-vote rule is a third lock: the Senate's own, not the Constitution's.

[beat]

And four. The price. Roughly 68 Californians for every Wyomingite, and two senators for each state. That gives small states the power to block, not to legislate alone. The case for: the states are represented as states. The case against: equal votes for states mean unequal votes for citizens.

[pause]

Here is what is settled, and what is not. The mechanics are settled. Two chambers, built on two principles of representation, must agree on the identical text of every law.

What is not settled is whether the balance is right, for a country whose states now differ in population by more than sixty to one.

And here the phrase from Article Five comes back. Without its consent. Most proposals to change the Senate would need a constitutional amendment. A change to equal representation itself would need something no other amendment requires: the consent of every state that would lose its equal vote.

That is why most reform debates today are about the Senate's rules, above all the filibuster, and not about its structure.

From here, the argument turns to the people who fill these seats: who actually wins elections to the House and the Senate, and why incumbents so rarely lose. To watch the two chambers work together on a bill, there is the pack called How a Bill Becomes Law. To see how House districts are drawn, there is the pack on gerrymandering.

For now, go back to Philadelphia. July 16, 1787. Five states to four.

[beat]

One state's margin. And every federal law since has needed both keys.
