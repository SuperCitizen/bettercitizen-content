# Why does Congress have two chambers?

By the middle of July 1787, the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia was close to falling apart. For weeks the delegates had been stuck on a single question: in the new national legislature, should a state's voice depend on how many people live there, or should every state count the same? On July 16 they voted on a way out. North Carolina switched sides. Massachusetts's delegation split down the middle, so it cast no vote at all. The proposal passed five states to four.

That one-state margin is why the United States has, in effect, two legislatures stacked inside one Congress. Every federal law since has had to win over two bodies that are elected on different schedules, from different maps, by different arithmetic. This lesson is about what that design does, and what it costs.

## A big-state plan, a small-state plan, and a split

The delegates arrived with two rival blueprints. The **Virginia Plan**, favored by the populous states, proposed a [[term:bicameral]] legislature with *both* chambers apportioned by population: more people, more votes, in both houses. The **New Jersey Plan**, favored by the small states, proposed amending the existing Articles of Confederation, under which every state had an equal vote in Congress no matter its size.

On June 19 the Convention set the New Jersey Plan aside and kept working from Virginia's. The small states did not go quietly. On June 30 Gunning Bedford of Delaware warned that if the large states broke up the Confederation, the small ones would "find some foreign ally." On July 2 a motion to give each state one vote in the second chamber tied, five states to five. Roger Sherman of Connecticut had been pushing a way through: keep two chambers, but give them different foundations, with population in the first and one equal voice per state in the second. A committee with one delegate from each state recommended a version of that idea on July 5, and it passed on July 16. History calls it the **Great Compromise**, or the Connecticut Compromise, after Sherman's state.

Not everyone celebrated. James Madison, the Virginia Plan's chief architect, opposed it. So did other nationalists who wanted a strong central government, such as Rufus King and Gouverneur Morris. To them an equal-vote Senate looked too much like the weak Confederation Congress they had come to replace.

The settlement also set up a darker bargain. Once House seats were to follow population, the delegates had to decide who counted as population. The **Three-Fifths Compromise** counted three of every five enslaved people toward a state's share of House seats. That inflated the representation of slaveholding states, which counted people they denied every political right. The rule held until the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) based apportionment on "the whole number of persons in each State."

> **Why this matters:** The Senate's equal vote is the most entrenched rule in the Constitution. Article V allows almost anything to be amended, but it carves out one exception: "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate."

## Same job, two different machines

The chambers share one essential job: a bill becomes law only if both pass the *identical* text and then the President signs it, or Congress overrides a veto (Art. I, §7). Beyond that, the Constitution built them to be different on purpose.

| | House of Representatives | Senate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Members | 435 voting members, a number Congress fixed by law in 1929 | 100: two per state |
| Term | 2 years; every seat is on the ballot at every election | 6 years, staggered so about a third of seats are up every two years |
| Minimum age and citizenship | 25 years old; a citizen for 7 years | 30 years old; a citizen for 9 years |
| Who elects them | Voters in a district of roughly equal population | Voters of the whole state (state legislatures did this until 1913) |
| Powers it holds alone | Revenue bills must start here; it impeaches, meaning it brings the charges | It tries impeachments (two-thirds to convict), approves treaties (two-thirds of senators present), and confirms appointments |

Read the rows as a set of dials. The House's dials are turned toward **responsiveness**: short terms, small districts, every seat exposed at once. The Senate's are turned toward **endurance**: long terms, statewide constituencies, slightly older and longer-naturalized members, and a staggered calendar that never lets it turn over all at once. The Senate's role in [[term:advice-and-consent]] and in trying [[term:impeachment]] cases follows the same logic. The Framers gave the slower, steadier body the final say on decisions they expected to outlast the passions of a single season.

<Checkpoint question="A sudden swing in the national mood hits in one November election. What is the most it can change in each chamber that year?">
  In the House, potentially everything: all 435 seats are on the ballot. In the Senate, only the roughly one-third of seats (33 or 34) whose six-year terms are ending, plus any special election to fill a vacancy. The other two-thirds of senators were elected in earlier years and keep their seats. A mood has to last through at least two elections to reach most of the Senate. That delay is the staggered terms doing exactly what they were designed to do.
</Checkpoint>

## Two locks on one door

Picture a door with two locks, cut by two different locksmiths. A burglar who copies one key still can't get in. A resident who has lost one key can't either. That's the bargain of a two-chamber legislature in one image: it's harder to do something bad, and harder to do anything at all. The image breaks in one place. Real locks don't negotiate, but the chambers do. Each can amend the other's bill and send it back, so the key that finally turns is often one that neither chamber started with.

Madison, who had lost the fight over how to build the Senate, defended it anyway once the Constitution went to the states for ratification. In *Federalist* No. 62 he argued that a second chamber, distinct from the first and sharing its power, doubles the people's protection. A scheme that betrays the public has to win over two separate bodies rather than one. He added a second argument that's easy to miss: a body that changes slowly guards against laws that change faster than citizens, or businesses, can keep up with.

The second lock is not hypothetical, and it doesn't belong to either party. In 2017 a Republican House passed a bill to repeal and replace much of the Affordable Care Act, 217–213. In the Senate, which needed only a simple majority on that vote, a much narrower "skinny repeal" failed 49–51 when three Republicans joined the entire Democratic caucus in voting no. In 2021 a Democratic House passed a sweeping elections-and-ethics bill, H.R. 1, by 220–210. Senate Democrats brought up their own version, and on June 22 every senator in their caucus voted to begin debate on it while every Republican voted no: 50–50. That tie wasn't a win. The vote was to end a filibuster against even starting debate, and that takes 60, not a majority. In both cases the same party controlled both chambers, and the House majority still couldn't move the Senate. In 2017 the Senate's majority split. In 2021 a Senate rule set the bar above a majority.

That third lock, the [[term:filibuster]], is why the *How a bill becomes law* pack counts the hurdles as 218, 51, 60, and 67: House majority, Senate majority, cloture, veto override. Only the first two come from the Constitution's majority rule. The 60 is the Senate's own creation, and the 67 is the Constitution's two-thirds requirement for overriding a veto.

None of this is a law of nature. Forty-nine states copy the two-chamber model in their own legislatures. Nebraska doesn't. After a 1934 ballot measure championed by U.S. Senator George Norris, it has run a single-chamber, officially nonpartisan legislature since 1937. Its existence is a standing reminder that bicameralism is a choice with trade-offs, not a requirement of democracy.

## The price of an equal Senate, in today's numbers

The Great Compromise was a deal between states of different sizes, and the size differences have grown enormously since 1787. In the 2020 census, California counted 39,538,223 residents and Wyoming 576,851, roughly 68 Californians for every Wyomingite. Both states get two senators.

In the House the gap mostly closes, which was the point of the compromise. After the 2020 census, California has 52 representatives, while six states have exactly one: Alaska, Delaware, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, and Wyoming. Even here the fit isn't perfect, because a state can't have a fraction of a seat. The average district after 2020 holds about 761,000 people. Wyoming, Vermont, and Alaska each have fewer than that, yet each is guaranteed a whole seat.

<Checkpoint question="Senators from the 26 least-populous states could form a Senate majority. Does that mean those states can pass laws over the objections of the most populous states?">
  No. Not alone. A bill still needs a House majority, where seats track population, plus the President's signature or a veto override. What equal representation *does* give the smaller states is a blocking position in the Senate, and a bigger voice in its exclusive powers: confirmations, treaties, and impeachment trials, where the House plays no role (it only brings the impeachment charges). The trade-off is real, but it shows up in what the Senate can stop and what it alone decides, not in a power to legislate solo.
</Checkpoint>

Whether that trade-off is worth it is a live argument, and both sides have serious advocates.

**The case for the equal Senate.** The United States is a [[term:federalism]] system, a union of states as well as a nation of individuals, and the Senate is where the states are represented as states. Equal representation keeps less populous, often rural regions from being outvoted on everything by a few large metropolitan states. It also forces any lasting national majority to be geographically broad, not just numerous. And it was the price of the union itself: without it the small states would not have signed, which is why Article V protects it more strongly than any other clause.

**The case against.** Political scientist Robert Dahl, in *How Democratic Is the American Constitution?* (2001), argued that equal representation in the Senate is among the Constitution's least democratic features. The vote of a citizen in a small state counts for far more in Senate elections than the vote of a citizen in a large one. Senators representing a minority of the population can form a majority. And the "minorities" this protects are defined by state lines, not by any shared interest that needs protecting. Many residents of large states are rural, and many residents of small states are not.

## What this lesson settles — and what it doesn't

The mechanics are settled. Two chambers, built on two principles of representation, must agree on the identical text of every law. The House is designed to respond quickly and the Senate to endure. The second chamber makes bad laws harder to pass and every law harder to pass, by design. The [[term:separation-of-powers]] system adds the President and the courts on top of that.

What isn't settled is whether the balance is right for a country whose states now differ in population by more than sixty to one. Most proposals to change the Senate would need a constitutional amendment. A change to equal representation itself would need something no other amendment requires: the consent of every state that would lose its equal vote. That's why most live reform debates are about the Senate's *rules*, above all the filibuster, rather than its *structure*.
