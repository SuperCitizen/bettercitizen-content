# Who wins seats in Congress, and why do incumbents rarely lose?

In the Gallup poll taken at the start of October 2024, 16 percent of Americans said they approved of the way Congress was doing its job. That same year, 387 members of the House sought another term, and 371 of them won it. Even counting the four who lost in their own party's primaries, 95.9 percent of the House members who ran again kept their seats.

The political scientist Richard Fenno turned that puzzle into the title of a 1975 essay: "If, as Ralph Nader Says, Congress Is 'the Broken Branch,' How Come We Love Our Congressmen So Much?" This lesson is about the answer. It covers who wins seats in Congress, why the people already holding them usually win again, and who ends up serving as a result.

## Ninety-five percent, and what the number hides

An **incumbent** is the person who currently holds an office. In the House, incumbents who sought reelection won more than 90 percent of the time in every election from 2014 through 2024, according to the Brookings Institution's *Vital Statistics on Congress*. The House rate was 94.0 percent in 2022 and 95.9 percent in 2024. Senate rates swing more widely. In 2022 every senator who ran again won. In 2024, 22 of 25 did (88.0 percent). In 1980 only 55.2 percent did.

The tempting conclusion is that holding office makes someone nearly unbeatable. That's the near miss. A reelection rate measures something real, but at least three other things hide inside it.

First, the rate counts only members who ran. Forty-six House members left in 2024 rather than seek another House term, and members who expect a hard race have a reason to be among them. Second, many districts lean so heavily toward one party that any candidate from that party would probably win. Third, strong challengers tend to wait for an open seat rather than take on a sitting member. The figure below shows how members of today's House first got there.

Almost six in ten House members began by inheriting a seat their own party already held. Fewer than one in five began by defeating someone already in office. Some of the reelection rate is incumbency, and some of it is who decides to run, where.

## Why incumbents win

The most influential account of incumbents' behavior is David Mayhew's *Congress: The Electoral Connection* (1974). Mayhew asked what Congress would look like if members were driven above all by the wish to be reelected, and found that the assumption explained a great deal. He named three activities that serve the goal. **Advertising** builds a familiar, favorable name. **Credit claiming** ties the member personally to things the government did for the district, such as a new bridge, a grant, or a solved problem with a federal agency. **Position taking** puts the member on record on issues voters care about.

The office itself supplies the tools. Each House member runs an office paid for by a Members' Representational Allowance. It averages $1,928,107 a year per member, unchanged since 2023, and most of it pays for staff. Those staff answer mail and handle **casework**: a constituent's lost veterans' benefits or a stuck passport application. Every case solved is credit claimed. Incumbents also raise far more money. In the 2023–24 cycle, House incumbents raised about $3.35 million each on average, compared with about $510,000 for challengers. The challengers' average includes candidates who lost their primaries, but the gap is large either way.

One protection incumbents do not have is a term limit, and states cannot impose one. In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton* (1995), the Supreme Court held 5–4 that states may not add qualifications for members of Congress beyond those in the Constitution. Changing that would take a constitutional amendment.

Incumbency's real value is the *difference* between what the incumbent got and what a newcomer from the same party would have gotten in the same district. That difference can't be read off the winning share, which is why a raw reelection rate overstates it.

## Safe seats move the election to the primary

Try this on neutral ground. Imagine an invented state with two parties, the Lantern Party and the Harbor Party. Its 3rd District votes about 70 to 30 for Lantern candidates in every election. The November race there is settled before it starts. The only election that can change who holds the seat is the Lantern Party's **primary**, the election in which a party's voters choose its nominee. Primary turnout is usually lower than in November, and the voters who do turn out tend to be the party's most committed. So the member from the 3rd District has more to fear from a challenger in her own party than from any Harbor candidate.

Think of a tournament in which the semifinal decides the champion and the final is a formality. The comparison breaks in one place: the semifinal of a safe district is decided by a smaller crowd than the final, and often a different one.

Real districts look a lot like the 3rd. In 2024, 38 of the 435 House races (8.7 percent) had no candidate from one of the two major parties. Among House incumbents on the November ballot, 67.1 percent won at least 60 percent of the two-party vote. Only 69 members won with 55 percent or less. A seat that reliably goes one way is called a **safe seat**.

In 2024 the primaries ended four House careers, two in each party. Republican Bob Good of Virginia lost his June primary to John McGuire by fewer than 400 votes out of about 63,000. Democrat Jamaal Bowman of New York lost to George Latimer, 58.6 percent to 41.4. Democrat Cori Bush of Missouri lost to Wesley Bell, 51.1 percent to 45.6. Republican Jerry Carl of Alabama lost to Barry Moore, another sitting Republican, after redistricting put them in the same district. In 2026, 11 House members lost their primaries, seven Democrats and four Republicans. Pew Research Center counted that as the fourth-most since 1992.

The Senate works differently, because a senator's district is a whole state, which no map-drawer can reshape. Senate incumbents lose more often, and the losses can fall on either party. All three senators who lost in 2024 were Democrats. In 2026 eight senators lost and all eight were Republicans, two of them in primaries: John Cornyn of Texas and Bill Cassidy of Louisiana. They were the first senators to lose a primary since Richard Lugar of Indiana in 2012. In the same elections, Democrats won control of both chambers for the 120th Congress, which begins on January 3, 2027.

Why are so many House seats safe? Two explanations compete: how district lines are drawn, and where Americans choose to live. How much each contributes is a live argument, taken up in the *Gerrymandering* pack.

## Who ends up in Congress

Winning a seat takes money, time, connections and usually a prior office. So the people who win are not a random sample of the country. Before you read the numbers, guess.

The gaps run in different directions. The House's Black membership matches the population, the Hispanic share is about half the population's, and women hold under a third of the seats. Education shows the widest gap: almost every member has a college degree, against about 39 percent of adults 25 and older.

Age and money are harder to compare fairly. The Constitution sets minimum ages of 25 for the House and 30 for the Senate, so Congress would skew older than the population even if seats were filled at random. At the start of 2025 the average representative was 57.9 and the average senator 63.9. For wealth there is no official count, because members report their assets in broad ranges. NOTUS estimated in March 2026 that at least 73 of the 100 senators are millionaires, with a median of nearly $4.4 million, not counting their homes. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey put the median American family's net worth at $215,900. Class shows up in careers, too. The political scientist Nicholas Carnes found in *White-Collar Government* (2013) that the average member of Congress has spent less than two percent of their adult life in a working-class job.

## Does it matter who serves?

[[term:descriptive-representation]] is the name political theorists give to a legislature that mirrors its population in traits like race, gender and class. Whether Congress *should* look more like the country is a value question, and both sides have serious advocates.

**The case that it matters.** The political theorist Jane Mansbridge, in a 1999 article titled "Should Blacks Represent Blacks and Women Represent Women? A Contingent 'Yes'," argued that shared experience is valuable in particular situations, not always. Where a group distrusts the system, a member who shares its experience communicates better. Where an issue isn't yet on the agenda, people who live with it are likelier to raise it. And a legislature that includes people once kept out tells everyone that those groups are fit to rule. Carnes adds evidence for class. On his account, members' working lives shape how they see economic issues and how they vote on them, so a Congress with almost no working-class members makes different economic policy.

**The case that accountability matters more.** Hanna Pitkin's *The Concept of Representation* (1967), the classic work on the subject, argued that voters should judge representatives by what they do, not by what they are. A focus on resemblance, she wrote, leaves "no room for representation as accountability." On this view the Constitution has it right. It sets only a minimum age, a period of citizenship and living in the state, and leaves the rest to voters, who can choose anyone they believe will act for them. The political theorist Suzanne Dovi adds a caution even for those who value resemblance: not every member who shares a group's traits represents that group well.

## What this lesson settles — and what it doesn't

The numbers are settled. Incumbents usually win, though part of that is who chooses to run and where, not incumbency alone. Most House seats are safe enough that the primary, not November, is the election that can change who holds them. And Congress differs from the country in measurable ways, most of all in education, occupation and wealth.

What isn't settled is whether those patterns are a problem and, if so, which fix is worth its cost. Term limits would take a constitutional amendment. More competitive districts depend on who draws the lines. And no rule can make voters choose people who look like them, or should.
