# Who runs Congress, and how do members decide how to vote?

On January 3, 2023, the newly elected House met to choose its Speaker, and couldn't. Kevin McCarthy, the leader of the new Republican majority, needed a majority of the votes, but 19 Republicans voted for someone else on the first ballot. At 12:34 a.m. on January 7, on the 15th ballot, he finally won with 216 votes. No Speaker election had needed more than one ballot since 1923. Nine months later, on October 3, eight Republicans joined 208 Democrats to remove him, 216–210. No Speaker had ever been removed before.

The episode showed something an organization chart doesn't: the people who run Congress keep their jobs only as long as enough of their own members back them. This lesson is about those people and the committees they work through. It also asks how members decide how to vote.

## Committees: Congress divides the work

In the 118th Congress (2023–24), members introduced 19,306 measures. Of those, 16,554 were bills and joint resolutions, the kinds that can become law. Committees in one chamber or the other reported 1,596 measures to the floor, and 274 became public laws, under 2 percent of the bills and joint resolutions. The *How a bill becomes law* pack follows a bill through the gates where it can die. This section is about the committees themselves.

Each chamber divides its work among **standing committees**, permanent panels that each cover a set of subjects. In the 119th Congress the House has 20 and the Senate 16, plus a few select and joint committees. Taxes go to House Ways and Means and Senate Finance, farm programs to the two Agriculture committees, the military to Armed Services. Committees hold hearings, rewrite bills line by line, and oversee the federal agencies in their area.

Think of a hospital's departments. Cardiology sees heart patients, and its doctors know hearts best. The comparison breaks on one point: a hospital doesn't let one faction pick every department head. In Congress the majority party holds most committee seats and every chair.

Members get their seats through their parties. In the House, each party's steering committee nominates members for committees, the party's caucus or conference approves the list, and the full House adopts it. After every election, party leaders negotiate how many seats each party gets on each committee. Only the Ethics Committee is split evenly, five to five. The majority party nominates every chair, and the Senate works in a similar party-first way. Seniority no longer guarantees a gavel: House Republicans limit their chairs to three consecutive terms, and Senate Republicans to six years.

<Checkpoint question="A newly elected representative wants to shape farm policy, so she wants a seat on the Agriculture Committee. Who actually decides whether she gets it?">
  Her party, before the House as a whole has any say. Her party's steering committee nominates members for seats, her party's caucus or conference approves the list, and only then does the full House adopt it. Even the number of Agriculture seats her party can hand out was settled earlier, by the two parties' leaders. So the people she most needs to persuade are her own party's leaders and colleagues.
</Checkpoint>

## Leaders: who sets the agenda

[[term:speaker-of-the-house]] is the only House office the Constitution names, and it does so in one sentence: "The House of Representatives shall chuse their Speaker and other Officers" (Art. I, §2). The Speaker presides over the House, and through the Rules Committee decides which bills reach the floor and on what terms. The whole House elects the Speaker, but in practice the majority party picks its candidate and must then hold its members together.

The math of that floor vote explains January 2023. A Speaker needs a majority of the votes cast *for a person by name*. A member who votes "present" lowers the bar. On the 15th ballot, six Republican holdouts voted present instead of voting for someone else. That left 428 votes for named candidates, so 215 was enough, and McCarthy's 216 cleared it. Democratic leaders have faced their own holdouts. In January 2019, 15 Democrats voted for someone other than Nancy Pelosi or voted present, and she still won with 220 votes. In 2021 five did, and she won 216 to 209.

Below the Speaker, each party in each chamber elects a floor leader and a **whip**. The majority leader runs the floor day to day, and the minority leader leads the other side. Whips count votes in advance and bring wavering members along. The word comes from fox hunting: the "whipper-in" was the rider who kept the hounds from straying from the pack.

The Senate's leadership is built differently. Under the Constitution the Vice President presides, voting only to break a tie, and a president pro tempore stands in when the Vice President is away. Neither runs the Senate. The real power belongs to the majority leader, a post the parties created in the 1920s. Its main tool is a precedent from August 13, 1937. That day Vice President John Nance Garner announced that when several senators seek recognition at once, the chair will call on the majority leader first. Whoever speaks first can make the first motion, so the majority leader shapes what the Senate takes up.

## How members decide: three answers to one question

Start on neutral ground. Imagine Representative Dana Reyes, whose invented district mixes farm towns with one college city. A bill comes to the floor that would end a crop subsidy her district's farmers rely on and spend the money on research grants. Polls in her district run about two to one against it. She has studied the bill and thinks it's good policy. Her party's leaders have made it a priority, and their whip has asked for her vote. How should she vote?

Political scientists describe three classic answers.

<Panels title="Three models of representation">
<Panel title="Delegate" tone="blue">

**Vote as the district wants.** The representative is the voters' agent. Reyes votes no.

</Panel>
<Panel title="Trustee" tone="gold">

**Vote your own best judgment.** Voters chose her to think, and she answers to them at the next election. Reyes votes yes.

</Panel>
<Panel title="Partisan" tone="green">

**Vote with your party.** Voters chose a party's program, and breaking from it blurs what the label means. Reyes votes yes.

</Panel>
</Panels>

The trustee model's most famous defense is a speech Edmund Burke gave in 1774 to the voters of Bristol, England, who had sent him to Parliament: "Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion." Six years later, after disagreements with his constituents, Burke withdrew from the Bristol election.

The delegate model has deep American roots: founding-era towns sometimes sent their representatives off with written instructions. In August 1789 a member of the First Congress, Thomas Tucker, moved to add the people's right "to instruct their Representatives" to the amendments that became the Bill of Rights. It didn't make it in. The First Amendment protects the right to petition the government, not to instruct it.

The partisan model has scholarly champions too. In 1950 a committee of the American Political Science Association called for "a more responsible two-party system," with parties that offer clear programs and members who carry them out, so voters know what they are choosing. Practicing politicians put it more bluntly. In 2003 Speaker Dennis Hastert set out his principles for the job. The fifth was "to please the majority of your majority": a Speaker, he said, should not move bills that run against the wishes of most of his own party.

Most members mix the three, a blend political scientists call the **politico** model: a delegate on the issues the district watches closely, a trustee on those it doesn't, and a partisan when the leaders need the vote.

<Checkpoint question="Representative Reyes votes yes, against her district's polls. From the roll call alone, which model was she following?">
  You can't tell. A yes vote fits the trustee model (she thought the bill was right) and the partisan model (her party wanted it) equally well, and a politico's reasoning too. A roll call records what a member did, not why. Political scientists argue over exactly that gap.
</Checkpoint>

## The evidence: a party-line Congress, with exceptions

A **party-unity vote** is a roll call in which most of one party votes against most of the other. In CQ Roll Call's annual study, 85.3 percent of roll calls in 2025 were party-unity votes, the highest share since the study began. On those votes the average member of each party, in each chamber, sided with their party between 92 and 96 percent of the time. (The authors note that counting two votes on each nomination inflates the share somewhat.) Scales that place members from liberal to conservative by their votes tell the same story. Since 2003–04 in the House and 2005–06 in the Senate, the most conservative Democrat has been more liberal than the most liberal Republican. The parties no longer overlap at all.

Members of both parties still break ranks, often on the biggest votes. In July 2025 two House Republicans, Brian Fitzpatrick and Thomas Massie, voted against their party's 2025 budget reconciliation bill, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which passed 218–214. In the Senate three Republicans, Susan Collins, Rand Paul and Thom Tillis, voted no, and the bill passed only when Vice President JD Vance broke a 50–50 tie. On the Democratic side, in March 2025 nine Democratic senators, including their leader, Chuck Schumer, and the independent Angus King voted to end debate on a government funding bill most of their party opposed. That let it come to a final vote, and it became law the next day. In January 2025, 46 House Democrats and 12 Democratic senators voted for the Laken Riley Act, an immigration-enforcement bill, against most of their party.

Why is party voting so high? The same numbers support two stories. In "Where's the Party?" (1993), the political scientist Keith Krehbiel argued that what looks like party discipline may be members voting their own preferences, which happen to line up with their party's. Parties sorted into like-minded teams would then vote together with no pressure at all. Gary Cox and Mathew McCubbins argued in *Setting the Agenda* (2005) that the majority party's real power is choosing which questions come to a vote at all, so floor unity is partly a product of what leaders keep off the floor. Both accounts fit the roll calls. They disagree about what happens before the roll is called.

## What this lesson settles — and what it doesn't

The structure is settled. Committees divide the work, and the majority party holds most of their seats and every chair. Leaders are chosen by their parties and keep the job only while their members back them. Members vote with their parties more than at any time in modern records, and members of both parties still break ranks on major votes.

What isn't settled is whether that is good for representation. Advocates of responsible parties argue that disciplined parties give voters a clear choice and someone to hold accountable. The delegate tradition answers that a member who votes the party line every time has stopped representing a district and started representing a caucus. The *Political Parties* pack takes up that argument, with the strongest case for stronger parties and for weaker ones.
