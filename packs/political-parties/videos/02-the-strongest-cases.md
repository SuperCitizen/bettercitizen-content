## A manifesto, 1950

In 1950, the American Political Science Association published a manifesto. It argued that America's two parties were too alike and too incoherent to offer voters a real choice. They should become more programmatic and more disciplined.

- 1950: parties too alike, too incoherent

Seventy-five years later, the parties are more sorted and more unified than at any point in living memory. And a record 45 percent of American adults refuse to identify with either one.

> The wish was granted, and a record 45 percent refuse both parties.

## Two dials

Party strength is about the organization: can it control who runs under its label, and fund its campaigns? Partisanship is about voters: how firmly are they attached? Today the organizations are weak and the loyalty is intense. Three camps dispute what that means.

- Party strength: the organization
- Partisanship: the voters

## The responsible-party advocate

The responsible-party advocate argues that only parties can bundle thousands of issues into a governing program, and stand accountable for the results. You can fire the team. The advocate points to comparative research: a strong party moderates, because its brand must win majorities for decades. The prescription is to let parties control more nomination and more money.

- More party control of nominations and money

> The responsible-party case: with strong parties, voters can fire the team.

## The anti-party reformer

The anti-party reformer argues that the two parties act as a cartel, colluding more than they compete. The reformer points to the rules: the two parties write the ballot-access laws their competitors must satisfy, draw the districts they run in, and for a generation set the polling threshold for presidential debates. The prescription is to open the primaries, and let the two earn their dominance under fair rules.

- Open primaries, fair rules

> The anti-party case: a regulated industry whose two incumbents wrote the regulations.

## The diagnostician

The weak-parties, strong-partisanship diagnostician argues that the first two are arguing about the wrong dial. The formal parties have become shells, while voters' partisanship has hardened into identity. The diagnostician points to research on negative partisanship: voters driven less by enthusiasm for their own side than by fear and loathing of the other. The prescription is to rebuild the organizations and drain the hostility, both at once.

- Negative partisanship: voting against the other side

> The diagnosis: weak parties, strong partisanship, each disabling the other's cure.

## What each must swallow

Each camp concedes something. The advocate must accept less internal democracy. The reformer must explain why the weakest-party era coincided with the angriest politics. The diagnostician has a direction, not a mechanism.

- Less internal democracy
- Weakest parties, angriest politics
- A direction, not a mechanism

The lesson gives each case at full length, with its scholars and its evidence, and asks what would move you.
