## A warning, 1796

In September 1796, George Washington told the country he would not seek a third term, and left a warning behind. He cautioned against "the baneful effects of the spirit of party."

[beat]

The man who helped him draft it, Alexander Hamilton, was the effective leader of one of the two factions Washington was warning about. That December, the election to replace him was the first contested party election for president.

- Adams, Federalist: 71 electoral votes
- Jefferson, Republican: 68

## A Constitution with no parties

Search the Constitution for political parties and you find nothing. That was not an oversight. James Madison called them factions, and thought them dangerous. But he argued that you could abolish faction only by abolishing liberty. The Constitution set out to control its effects instead.

- Madison: control faction's effects, not its causes

So why did parties arrive so fast? The political scientist John Aldrich gives an answer. The Constitution created offices worth winning, and decisions that needed a majority again and again. A standing coalition beat improvising one every time.

In 1800, party-line voting by electors produced an exact tie. The Twelfth Amendment, in 1804, rewrote presidential balloting to fit.

> The Constitution never mentions parties, but it has been amended because of them.

## One word, three things

The political scientist V. O. Key taught that the word "party" bundles three different things.

The party in the electorate is the voters who identify with it. There is no membership card, and there are no dues.

- In the electorate: the voters

The party as organization is the machinery: committees, chairs, staff, rules and money.

- As organization: the machinery

The party in government is the officeholders elected under the label, such as the caucuses in Congress that choose the Speaker.

- In government: the officeholders

> The three faces rise and fall independently.

## The money around the machine

The organization turns out to be the weakest face. No national chair can fire a governor, or compel a single vote in Congress. And across the 2023 to 2024 cycle, every party committee in America combined reported 2.7 billion dollars in federal receipts. Outside groups spent a record 4.5 billion on the same elections.

- All party committees: $2.7 billion
- Outside groups: $4.5 billion
