## A party sues to act like one

On December 9, 2025, the Supreme Court heard a case about how much a party committee may spend in coordination with its own candidates. The Republican Party's Senate committee challenged the federal limits. The Democratic Party's national committees intervened to defend them.

- NRSC v. FEC: pending as of mid-2026

> Both parties showed up, on opposite sides, to argue how strong parties should be.

## Every reform is a bet

There are five live families of party reform: nomination rules, fusion voting, proportional representation, party finance, and the parties' own internal rules. Each one is a bet on a diagnosis of what is wrong.

- Nomination rules
- Fusion and proportional representation
- Party finance
- The parties' own rules

Reforms that weaken party organizations are championed by people who think parties have too much power. Reforms that strengthen them come from people who think the organizations have too little. Reforms that add parties come from people who think the problem is the duopoly itself.

> The label tells you nothing. The bet tells you everything.

## Fusion: one candidate, two lines

Take fusion. Two parties nominate the same candidate. The candidate appears on each party's ballot line, and the votes are added together. So a minor party can deliver its votes instead of splitting them.

- Same candidate, two lines, votes added

In the 19th century this was ordinary practice. Most states then banned it. In 1997 the Supreme Court held, six to three, that the federal Constitution permits those bans. Full fusion survives in New York and Connecticut. A century of it in New York produced influential pressure groups, not a multiparty system.

- Legal in full: New York, Connecticut

## Party money: two predictions

Now the case in court. The challengers' side argues from research comparing the states. Legislatures in states that restrict party money are more polarized, not less. Parties are pragmatists that fund electable moderates, and the donors who fill the vacuum are purists.

- For striking the limits: party money moderates

The defenders' side argues that the limits stop circumvention. Without them, a donor who has given the maximum to a candidate simply gives to the party, and candidate contribution limits die.

- For keeping them: they stop circumvention

> Both predictions cannot be fully right, and one is about to be tested.

## The reforms conflict

Which reform is right depends on which diagnosis you accept, and the reforms conflict. Finance deregulation strengthens the two parties' organizations. Proportional representation dilutes the duopoly itself. Open primaries weaken every party's definition.

- The reforms pull against each other
