September 19, 1796. A Philadelphia newspaper prints a long letter from George Washington, addressed to "Friends and Fellow-Citizens."

He will not seek a third term. And he wants to leave a warning behind.

He cautions the country against "the baneful effects of the spirit of party." He calls party passion "a fire not to be quenched."

[pause]

A fire not to be quenched.

Now the punchlines. The address was never delivered as a speech. And the man who helped Washington draft it, Alexander Hamilton, was the effective leader of one of the two parties-in-the-making that Washington was warning about.

The cabinet had already split. Hamilton's faction at the Treasury and Thomas Jefferson's faction at the State Department were funding rival newspapers to attack each other.

That December, the election to replace Washington became the first contested party election in presidential history. John Adams, the Federalist, won 71 electoral votes. Thomas Jefferson, the Republican, won 68.

[pause]

The Framers had written a Constitution with no political parties in it. Politics filled it with parties before the founding generation left the stage.

Was that a failure of the design, or the design working as intended, or something no design could prevent? This whole series circles that question. To take it up, we first need to know what a party actually is.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to say what a party is, who holds power inside one, and why America has exactly two.

We will take it in four steps.

First, how a Constitution written against parties filled up with them.

Second, what the word "party" names. It names three things, not one.

Third, the organization chart, and the money that routes around it.

And last, why there are exactly two parties, and why the two keep changing.

## A Constitution against parties

Search the Constitution for political parties, and you find nothing. No nominations. No primaries. No majority or minority leaders. The only "Party" in the text is a party to a lawsuit, in Article Three.

That silence was not an oversight. The Framers knew parties well, from English politics and from their own state legislatures. They called them factions. And they regarded faction roughly the way physicians regarded fever.

The classic diagnosis is James Madison's, in Federalist 10. And his striking move was to reject the obvious cure.

You could abolish faction, he reasoned, only by abolishing liberty. "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire."

[pause]

In plainer words, faction feeds on liberty the way fire feeds on air. You cannot remove the one and keep the other.

The causes of faction, Madison wrote, are "sown in the nature of man." So there are only two ways to cure its mischiefs. You remove its causes, or you control its effects.

The Constitution chose the second. "Extend the sphere," Madison wrote, "and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests." Make the republic large enough, and no single passion can assemble a durable national majority. The machine was designed so that factions would check, fragment, and exhaust each other.

The historian Richard Hofstadter called that design "a Constitution against parties." And he documented the irony in it. The machine built to frustrate organized faction was, almost immediately, operated by two organized factions. [beat] Madison himself co-founded one of them.

And Jefferson? In 1789, he had written this. "If I could not go to heaven but with a party, I would not go there at all."

[beat]

He went on to build the most successful party of the era, and to ride it to the presidency.

Let's test what we have so far. Madison thought faction was dangerous. The Framers controlled the Constitutional Convention. So why didn't they write a ban on political parties into the document? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because Madison's own analysis ruled it out. The causes of faction are sown in the nature of man. To suppress parties, you would have to suppress the liberty to organize, to speak, and to associate. That would be a cure "worse than the disease." So the Constitution tries instead to control the effects of faction, through size and fragmentation.

Now the harder question. Why did a design against parties fail so fast?

Hold on to the answer the political scientist John Aldrich gives, because it organizes everything that follows.

The Constitution created offices worth winning. And it created decisions that required assembling a majority, again and again: on the national bank, on the debts of the states, on Britain against France. Ambitious politicians kept facing the same fights, and they found it easier to keep one coalition standing than to build a new one for every vote.

> Aldrich's answer: a standing coalition beats improvising one every time.

On his account, parties were not a betrayal of the constitutional system. They were a rational response to it.

The consequences came quickly. By 1800, electors voting on strict party lines had produced an exact tie in the Electoral College, and a constitutional crisis. In 1804, the Twelfth Amendment quietly rewrote presidential balloting to make room for parties.

> The Constitution never mentions parties. It has been amended because of them.

## One word, three things

That is how the parties arrived. Now, what is one?

Take the Democratic Party. Is it a voter registered in New Jersey? Is it a building on South Capitol Street, with a chair and a budget? Is it two hundred-odd members of Congress, voting together?

[beat]

The political scientist V. O. Key taught generations of students that the word "party" bundles three different things. Most confusion about parties, he taught, comes from sliding between them in the middle of a sentence.

Here are his three faces of a party.

The first is the party in the electorate: the voters who identify with it. There is no membership card, and there are no dues. In most states, joining a party means checking a box on a voter-registration form.

So this face is measured by what people call themselves. In Gallup's averages for 2025, 27 percent of American adults called themselves Republicans, and the same share called themselves Democrats. A record 45 percent called themselves independents. But most of those independents lean toward one side, and reliably vote with it.

[beat]

The second face is the party as organization: the formal machinery. Committees at every level, chairs, staff, rules, conventions, and money. This is the face people picture when they say "the party decided." And on inspection, it turns out to be the weakest of the three.

[beat]

The third face is the party in government: the officeholders elected under the label. The caucuses in Congress, the delegations in fifty state legislatures, and the president as the party's leader in practice. This is the face that writes the laws.

> One word, three things: the loyalty, the machinery, and the officeholders.

The framework earns its keep because the three faces move independently, and American history offers every combination. The machines of the 1880s were mighty organizations with loosely attached voters. The reverse, feeble organizations with ferociously attached voters, is a strong claim about the present. The next lecture examines it.

So when you hear, in the same week, that "the parties have never been weaker" and that "the parties have never been stronger," listen for the face. The two speakers are usually talking about different ones. And both may be right.

Let's put the three faces to work. A state party chair spends the spring recruiting a popular sheriff to run for an open seat in Congress, and connects her to trained field staff.

Which face of the party is acting? Think it through.

[long pause]

The party as organization. Recruiting, staffing, and campaign services are the machinery. The sheriff joins the party in government only if she wins. And the voters who pull the lever for her are the party in the electorate.

## The organization chart, and the money around it

Now take the middle face apart: the machinery.

On paper, each major party is a federation. At the base sit thousands of county and precinct committees, staffed mostly by volunteers. Above them sit fifty state parties. And at the top sit six national committees: the Democratic and Republican National Committees, and four hill committees, run by the parties in Congress to elect their own chambers' partisans.

[pause]

Here is what the chart does not show: command authority. [beat] Because there is none.

No national chair can fire a governor, deny a senator renomination, or compel a single vote in Congress.

Political scientists call the modern committees service parties. A service party raises money, builds voter files, trains staff, and transfers funds to the races that need them. It serves. It does not command.

Real authority over who carries the label belongs to the voters in primaries. And real control of a campaign belongs to the candidate's own organization.

Then there is the money. It is the quickest way to see how far the formal organizations have been eclipsed.

Take the election cycle that ended in 2024. Every party committee in America combined, national, state, and local, reported 2.7 billion dollars in federal receipts.

[beat]

Outside groups spent a record 4.5 billion dollars on the same elections. Most of them were super PACs, funded without contribution limits.

> The formal party system now commands less money than the groups around it.

It was not always so. The party of the nineteenth century was an employer. Under the patronage system, it traded government jobs for loyalty. Then the Pendleton Act, in 1883, began strangling patronage, and civil-service reform finished the job.

Today's party cannot give you a job. So what still binds tens of millions of people to a label that pays them nothing? That puzzle sits at the center of the next lecture.

[pause]

One more test. A first-term senator routinely votes against her party's leadership. Who can remove her from office, or take away her party label?

You have four candidates: the national committee chair, the president, her state party, and her party's caucus in the Senate. Take a moment.

[long pause]

None of them.

The chair commands a service bureaucracy. The president commands attention, but not ballots. The caucus can strip her committee assignments, but not the label.

That leaves one group: the primary voters of her own state.

> Only primary voters can deny an officeholder the party's nomination.

That is why party discipline today runs through the threat of a primary challenge. And that answer used two of Key's three faces. Can you still name all three?

[long pause]

The party in the electorate. The party as organization. The party in government.

Discipline now runs through the first, and not through the second. Weak machinery, strong loyalty. Keep that asymmetry in mind, because the next lecture is built on it.

## Why exactly two

Most democracies have parties. Almost none have had the same two parties dominating every level of office since 1856.

The classic explanation is called Duverger's law, after the French political scientist Maurice Duverger. In his words, "the simple-majority single-ballot system favours the two-party system."

[pause]

In plainer words: elect one winner per district, on a single ballot, and two parties tend to dominate. Two engines drive it.

The first is the mechanical effect. Picture a party that wins 15 percent of the vote in every district in the country. How many seats does it hold? [beat] None. Support that is spread out converts to zero seats.

> In a winner-take-all district, 15 percent everywhere wins nothing anywhere.

The second is the psychological effect. Voters see that result coming. So they desert third candidates in advance, and vote for the lesser evil that can win. Donors and ambitious politicians make the same calculation, and third parties are emptied of talent before any ballots are cast.

[pause]

American rules then reinforce the pattern, beyond what Duverger requires.

Single-member House districts are not in the Constitution. Congress has required them by statute, without exception, since a law of 1967.

Ballot-access laws are written by two-party legislatures, and minor parties must clear their thresholds again, cycle after cycle.

And the presidency is a single prize that cannot be divided, awarded through state-by-state contests that are winner-take-all.

## Six party systems, and a fight about the present

So the number two is fixed. What the two parties contain is not.

> The two-party system is permanent. The two coalitions are not.

Political scientists organize American history into party systems, separated by realignments: elections, or short eras, in which the coalitions durably re-sort. The textbook counts six. Here they are, in order.

First, Hamilton's Federalists against the Republicans of Jefferson and Madison. The Federalists opposed the War of 1812, never recovered, and dissolved.

Second, from 1828, Democrats against Whigs. Martin Van Buren built the first mass party, around Andrew Jackson. And Van Buren argued, against the Founders, that permanent organized competition was good for the republic. Parties, he held, would discipline ambition and give voters real choices.

Third, the Whigs shattered over slavery, and a new antislavery Republican Party won the presidency with Lincoln, in 1860. Every party system since has been Republicans against Democrats.

Fourth, from 1896, McKinley's industrial Republicans defeated Bryan's alliance of farmers and labor, and a generation of Republican dominance followed.

Fifth, the New Deal coalition, from 1932. The Depression made Democrats the majority party, until a contradiction inside the coalition, over civil rights, eventually broke it.

Sixth, from 1968, a long re-sorting. The South migrated to the Republicans over a generation. And the parties slowly sorted themselves, by ideology, by geography, and eventually by education.

[pause]

And a seventh? [beat] Here the textbook stops, and the argument starts.

Some scholars argue that the re-sorting around 2016, by education and by populism, began a new system. Others date the break earlier. And others doubt that numbered party systems were ever the right way to carve up history.

That doubt has a source. The realignment framework is the most famous periodization in American political science, and one of the most battered. In 2002, the political scientist David Mayhew tested its claims, and found most of them wanting.

So the defensible version is modest. Coalitions re-sort, sometimes quickly, and usually visibly only in hindsight. Three dates really were hinges. 1860. [beat] 1896. [beat] 1932.

Whether 2016 was a fourth is exactly the kind of question the framework cannot answer in real time.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Constitution never mentions parties. It was built to frustrate faction, and it filled with parties anyway, because a standing coalition beats improvising one every time. It has been amended because of them.

[beat]

Two. One word, three things: the loyalty, the machinery, and the officeholders. The party in the electorate, the party as organization, the party in government. They rise and fall independently.

[beat]

Three. The machinery serves. It does not command. The formal party system now commands less money than the groups around it. And only primary voters can deny an officeholder the party's nomination.

[beat]

Four. Duverger's law. In a winner-take-all district, 15 percent everywhere wins nothing anywhere. So voters, donors, and candidates leave third parties in advance, and American rules reinforce the pattern.

[beat]

And five. The two-party system is permanent. The two coalitions are not. The textbook counts six party systems, and the label for the present one is unsettled.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We have the mechanics. We do not have the evaluation. Are parties a disease the republic manages, or the organs it thinks with?

[pause]

The Founders thought they had answered that. You have now heard two answers. Washington held that party passion is a fire not to be quenched. Van Buren held that permanent organized competition is good for the republic.

Choose the one you find less convincing, and state its best case, the way its author would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

The next lecture takes up three answers that are argued today, each at full strength. One: American parties are too weak, and should be strengthened. Two: they are entrenched cartels, and should be opened up. Three: the real pathology is hollow organizations bolted to ferocious loyalties.

For now, go back to Washington's letter. Every February, a senator reads the Farewell Address aloud to the Senate. That has happened every year since 1896. [beat] And the chamber that listens is organized, seated, staffed, and scheduled by the two parties the address warns against.

American politics has never resolved that tension. [beat] It has institutionalized it.
