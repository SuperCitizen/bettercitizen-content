December 9, 2025. The Supreme Court hears argument in a case where the Republican Party, in effect, is suing for the right to act like a party.

The case is NRSC v. FEC. The National Republican Senatorial Committee is challenging federal limits, in place since the 1970s, on how much a party committee may spend in coordination with its own candidates.

For many House races in 2026, that limit is about 65 thousand dollars. For a Senate race in the largest state, it is about 4 million.

Then the case turned. The Justice Department took the rare step of siding with the challengers. So the Democratic Party's national committees stepped in to defend the law.

[beat]

Both parties showed up, on opposite sides, to argue about how strong parties should be.

[pause]

As of mid-2026, the decision is pending. But hold the oddity in mind. The strongest case for letting parties spend freely does not come from people who like money in politics. It comes from scholars who watched the money go somewhere worse.

Every reform in this lecture has that shape. It is a bet on a diagnosis. And it usually has surprising friends, and surprising enemies.

Last time, we built three diagnoses of what is wrong with the parties. This time, the remedies. By the end, you should be able to take a party reform and say who is pushing it, what it would do, and what it costs.

We will take it in four steps. First, the rule that sorts every reform, with nomination rules as the example. Second, two reforms that would add parties: fusion voting and proportional representation. Third, the reform that would strengthen the parties we have, through their money. And last, the rules that parties can rewrite for themselves.

## Every reform is a bet

Sort the reforms by what they do to the parties. There are three kinds, and each kind belongs to one of the three camps from last time.

Some reforms weaken the party organizations: open primaries, nonpartisan ballots. They are championed by people who think the parties have too much power. Those are the anti-party reformers.

[beat]

Some reforms strengthen the organizations: deregulating party finance, restoring gatekeeping. They are championed by people who think the organizations have too little power. Those are the responsible-party advocates.

[beat]

And some reforms add parties: fusion voting, proportional representation. They come from people who think the problem is the two-party duopoly itself, and not the strength dial. Those are the diagnosticians, and with them the structural reformers.

[pause]

Weaken. Strengthen. Add. [beat] And all three camps call themselves reformers.

> The label tells you nothing. The bet tells you everything.

Take the oldest and most active family first: nomination rules. Who may vote in a primary, and whether the primary is partisan at all. That fight is running state by state. One point about it belongs here, because it concerns the parties themselves.

Picture a line. At one end is the closed primary. At the other is the top-four primary, open to all candidates. Every move along that line makes the same trade. The more outsiders help choose a party's nominee, the less the label means.

> The anti-party bet: open the nomination, trading party definition for voter access.

That trade is exactly the outcome anti-party reformers want. And it is exactly the outcome responsible-party advocates dread.

The constitutional boundary runs the same way. A state may design an all-candidate system around the parties. It may not force a party's own nomination open to nonmembers.

Let's test that. A state replaces its partisan primaries with a top-four primary, open to all candidates. A responsible-party advocate objects. What is the strongest version of the objection? Think it through.

[long pause]

It is an objection about the label. The responsible-party case rests on legible team accountability. The label must reliably signal a program, and that requires the organization to control who carries it. An all-candidate primary deliberately dissolves that control.

And notice what the objection is not. It is not that such a primary is unconstitutional. In 2008, in a case called Washington State Grange, the Supreme Court upheld the top-two structure, because it nominates no one on the party's behalf.

## Fusion voting

Now the reforms that add parties. The first is fusion voting: the nineteenth century's reform, back in court.

Fusion means cross-nomination. Two parties nominate the same candidate. The candidate appears on each party's ballot line, and the votes are added together.

So a voter can support the Democratic nominee as a Working Families candidate, or the Republican nominee as a Conservative. That sends a countable signal about why the candidate won.

In the nineteenth century, this was ordinary American practice. Minor parties like the Greenbackers and the Populists bargained their ballot lines for influence, and major-party candidates courted them.

[beat]

That is exactly why it died. In the 1890s, fusion tickets nearly broke Republican control of several states. The losing side's legislatures banned the practice, and most states followed.

A century later, the Supreme Court locked the bans in place. The case, in 1997, was called Timmons. By six to three, the Court held that Minnesota could forbid a minor party from nominating a willing major-party candidate. States, it said, may enact election rules "that may, in practice, favor the traditional two-party system."

[pause]

Favor the traditional two-party system.

But be exact about what Timmons did. It spoke only to the federal Constitution. Under it, a state may ban fusion. [beat] No state must. And the state constitutions were left open.

> Timmons permitted fusion bans. It did not require them.

So the revival is being fought in that open lane, one state constitution at a time. New Jersey's courts, the first asked, said no. Wisconsin is next. A group called United Wisconsin sued in 2025 to void that state's ban, which dates to 1897. As of mid-2026, the case is pending.

Whose bet is this? The diagnosticians'.

> The diagnosticians' bet: build new parties without spoilers.

Fusion's appeal is that it creates real, countable minor parties without the spoiler problem. A third party can matter by delivering the votes on its line, and not by splitting them.

The honest assessment cuts both ways. Full fusion survives in New York and Connecticut, where it was never banned, and New York shows that the model is durable. It also shows the model's modesty. A century of fusion there produced influential pressure groups attached to the duopoly. [beat] It did not produce a multiparty system.

Put it to work. A centrist organization wants to influence which major-party candidates win, without ever running a spoiler. Why does fusion serve that goal better than founding a conventional third party? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Under fusion, the organization nominates the major-party candidate it prefers. Its ballot line then shows exactly how many votes it delivered. That is leverage it can spend in policy negotiations, with no risk of splitting its own side's vote.

A conventional third party, in a plurality system, mostly threatens the major party nearest to it. And the first legal obstacle is the one we have just met: anti-fusion statutes, roughly a century old, in most states.

## Proportional representation

The second reform that adds parties is the most structural on the menu. It attacks the two-party pattern at its root.

It is a bill called the Fair Representation Act, reintroduced in Congress in July 2025. It would end single-member House districts. States would elect their representatives from districts of three to five members each, by ranked-choice voting, with the lines drawn under nonpartisan criteria.

What does that change? The arithmetic.

> In a five-member district, roughly 17 percent elects a member.

So a party with 20 percent support across a state stops winning nothing, and starts winning seats.

The first lecture gave you Duverger's law, and its two effects. Now the mechanical effect runs in reverse. And the psychological effect follows it: votes for a third party stop being wasted, so people cast them.

And how could it happen? Congress could do this by ordinary statute. It is the same power Congress used in 1967 to require single-member districts in the first place.

The fullest argument is Lee Drutman's, in a 2020 book called Breaking the Two-Party Doom Loop. Here it is, in its own terms. When two fully sorted national parties face each other in winner-take-all contests, every election becomes existential. Compromise becomes betrayal. And the system spirals. That spiral is his "doom loop."

America in the middle of the twentieth century escaped it, Drutman argues, because the formal two-party system concealed a working four-party one: a liberal and a conservative wing of each. Proportional rules would restore that fluidity honestly.

[pause]

The trade-offs deserve equal candor. Here are four.

In multiparty systems, governing coalitions form after the election, in negotiations that voters do not control.

[beat]

Representatives in a five-member district share a constituency, and no longer own one.

[beat]

Small parties can become kingmakers, out of proportion to their vote.

[beat]

And the American versions are projections, not observations. The United States has no modern experience with proportional House elections.

As of mid-2026, the bill is an agenda-setting exercise, with no path to passage. Its sponsors' theory is that doom-loop politics will eventually create its own demand.

## Party money

Now the reform that would strengthen the parties we have. This is the responsible-party bet, and it is the one in court.

Campaign-finance law has whipsawed the parties for fifty years. In the 1970s, the law capped what parties could give to their candidates, and spend with them. The parties found a workaround, called soft money: unlimited funds for what was called party-building. In 2002, the McCain-Feingold Act banned soft money for the national parties outright. And then, in 2010, Citizens United and the cases after it unleashed unlimited outside spending.

[pause]

What was the result? Squeeze the accountable channel, leave the unaccountable one open, and you get the picture from the first lecture: a super PAC economy that outspends the entire formal party system.

The scholarly counterattack came in 2015, from Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner. They used the states as a laboratory. Some states restrict party money severely, and some barely restrict it at all. And they found that legislatures in the party-restricting states are more polarized, [beat] not less.

[pause]

The mechanism, they argue, is this. Parties are pragmatists, and they fund moderates who can win. The donors who fill the vacuum are ideological purists.

So their conclusion is deliberately heretical for reformers. If you must have money in politics, route it through the parties. Parties face voters, file disclosures, and care about winning majorities.

> The responsible-party bet: route the money through parties, which face voters.

That is the scaffolding under NRSC v. FEC. Strike the coordination limits, or raise them, and money flows back through party committees.

The defenders of the limits answer with a prediction of their own. They argue that coordination limits are armor against circumvention. Without them, a donor who has given the maximum to a candidate then gives to the party, with a wink. And the limits on contributions to candidates die.

> The defenders' case: without coordination limits, candidate contribution limits die.

That argument carried a 2001 case called FEC v. Colorado Republican, and the full Sixth Circuit in 2024. Both predictions cannot be fully right. And one of them is about to be tested.

[pause]

Let's test it ourselves. Suppose the Court strikes the limits on coordinated spending. On La Raja and Schaffner's evidence from the states, what first effect should you predict? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Money and operational control shift from super PACs back toward party committees, with a moderating pull on nominees.

Notice what the prediction is not. Super PACs would not disappear, because their independence is constitutionally protected. And total spending would not shrink. What changes is the routing. The defenders' worry is the honest cost: the same routing makes parties a conduit around the limits on contributions to candidates.

## The parties' own rules

The last family needs no statute and no court. Parties can rewrite themselves.

Before we take it up, pull the map back. Four families so far, and each was somebody's bet. Can you say whose? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Nomination rules: the anti-party reformers. Fusion and proportional representation: the diagnosticians. Party money: the responsible-party advocates. The fifth family is contested among all three camps.

The modern case is the Democratic fight over superdelegates: party leaders and elected officials, seated at the convention automatically, and unpledged. They were created for 1984, as a professional check on primary electorates.

One misconception first. People say, "Superdelegates decide Democratic nominations." [beat] They never have. Every nominee since 1984 also led among pledged delegates.

But the 2016 cycle made their potential to decide one radioactive. So in 2018, the Democratic National Committee, the DNC, barred automatic delegates from voting on a first convention ballot, unless the outcome is already determined.

> The 2018 reform traded peer review for legitimacy.

The serious argument was always the threat against the taint. The threat: does an insider backstop deter capture by an outsider? The taint: does its mere existence delegitimize the winner? The 2018 reform answered that the taint costs more.

That is exactly the trade one school of research warns about. It found that the "invisible primary" among party insiders historically did the real vetting.

The Republican National Committee runs the opposite model, with no unpledged class at all. Its automatic delegates are bound by state results, like everyone else. So the organization has even less discretion to stop a nominee its leadership opposes, as 2016 demonstrated.

As of mid-2026, the DNC's rules for 2028 are still being deliberated. Whatever emerges will be the cleanest available reading of which diagnosis the party itself believes.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Every reform is a bet on a diagnosis. Some weaken the party organizations, some strengthen them, and some add parties. The label tells you nothing. The bet tells you everything.

[beat]

Two. Nomination rules are the anti-party bet: open the nomination, trading party definition for voter access.

[beat]

Three. The diagnosticians' bet is to build new parties without spoilers. Fusion does it with a second ballot line. Timmons permitted fusion bans. It did not require them. Proportional representation does it with arithmetic. In a five-member district, roughly 17 percent elects a member.

[beat]

Four. Party money is the responsible-party bet: route the money through parties, which face voters. The defenders' case: without coordination limits, candidate contribution limits die.

[beat]

And five. The parties' own rules. The 2018 reform traded peer review for legitimacy. And superdelegates have never decided a nomination.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: which reform is right. That depends on which diagnosis you accept. And the reforms conflict. Deregulating party finance strengthens the duopoly's organizations. Proportional representation dilutes the duopoly itself. Open primaries weaken every party's definition. Fusion strengthens minor parties, and leaves the major ones intact.

[pause]

So choose the reform you would be least inclined to support. State the best case for it, the way its advocates would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

This series ends here, but the argument does not. Watch three things from mid-2026 forward.

The decision in NRSC v. FEC, which will either re-arm the formal parties, or confirm the super PAC status quo.

[beat]

The Wisconsin fusion ruling, which will show whether state constitutions offer the revival a path that Timmons closed federally.

[beat]

And the DNC's rules for 2028: the purest measure of how a party that feels hollow grades its own condition.

For now, go back to that courtroom, in December 2025. Two parties, on opposite sides, arguing over how strong parties should be. [beat] You can now say what each reform is betting on, and what it costs.

A defensible position names its diagnosis. It picks the reform that addresses it. It states the trade-off it is accepting. And it identifies the result that would prove the bet wrong.
