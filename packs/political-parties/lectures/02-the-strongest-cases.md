September 1950. The American Political Science Association does something professional associations almost never do. [beat] It publishes a manifesto.

It runs 99 pages. It was drafted by a committee of the discipline's leading scholars, under E. E. Schattschneider. And its title is its demand: Toward a More Responsible Two-Party System.

The committee's complaint was that America's two parties were failing democracy, by being too alike and too incoherent. They could not offer voters a real choice, or deliver on a mandate. So elections decided everything, and settled nothing.

The prescription: make the parties more programmatic, more disciplined, more distinct.

[pause]

Seventy-five years later, the wish has been granted, beyond the committee's dreams. The parties are more ideologically sorted, and more unified as legislators, than at any point in living memory.

And the public's verdict is in. A record 45 percent of American adults now refuse to identify with either one.

[pause]

So either the best political scientists of 1950 misdiagnosed the disease. [beat] Or the operation succeeded, and the patient got sicker anyway.

Sorting out which is the argument of this lecture. And it has three sides, not two.

Last time, how the parties work. This time, what is wrong with them. By the end, you should be able to state each of three positions the way its smartest advocates state it, and to say what each one has to concede.

We will take it in three steps. First, we untangle two things that travel under one name. Second, the three cases, each at full strength. And third, where the cases collide.

Each case gets the same treatment: who holds it, what it protects, what it argues, what it points to, and what it concedes.

## Two dials, one word

Every position in this debate trades on the word "strong." So pin it down, with the three faces of a party from last time: the voters, the organization, and the officeholders.

Party strength, properly, is about the organization. Can the party control who runs under its label? Can it fund its campaigns, staff its operations, and discipline its officeholders?

Partisanship is about the electorate: how firmly voters are attached to a party identity, and how reliably that identity drives what they do.

> Party strength is the organization. Partisanship is the voters' attachment.

Those are not the same dial. And American history has visited every combination of the two settings.

In the machine era of the Gilded Age, both dials were turned up: armies of patronage workers, ballots printed by the parties, and presidential turnout near 80 percent.

In the middle of the twentieth century, the organizations were still substantial, but the voters were flexible. They split their tickets, and there was a bipartisan center. That is the world the 1950 report was complaining about.

By the 1970s and 1980s, both dials were down.

And today? [beat] Hollowed committees, and campaigns centered on candidates. But historically intense party loyalty in the electorate.

[pause]

Weak organizations. Strong partisanship. That is the setting we are in now.

How did we arrive here? Reformers spent the half-century after 1950 weakening the organizations, on the theory that voters would supply the judgment that parties no longer imposed. Instead, voters re-polarized, while the organizations kept shriveling.

[pause]

Whether that setting is a crisis, an opportunity, or a misreading is exactly what the three camps dispute.

Test the two dials. A columnist writes: "American parties are stronger than ever. Just look at the lockstep party-line votes in Congress."

Which confusion is the columnist most plausibly making? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The columnist is mistaking two things for organizational strength: a cohesive party in government, and the partisanship of voters. Lockstep voting shows the first. Today it is driven largely by the second: sorted, loyal primary electorates. And neither one says whether the organization can recruit, fund, vet, or discipline.

A party whose officeholders vote together, because each one fears their own primary voters, can be organizationally feeble. That picture is the heart of the third camp's diagnosis.

## The responsible-party advocate

Now the three cases, each as its smartest advocates argue it. None is a straw man. All three are held by serious scholars and reformers today.

Begin with the responsible-party advocate. Who holds this position? It runs from Schattschneider and the 1950 report to a 2018 book called Responsible Parties, by Frances Rosenbluth and Ian Shapiro.

What does it protect? Accountability: the power of the whole electorate to judge the whole team.

It starts from Schattschneider's dictum, on page one of his 1942 book, Party Government. "The political parties created democracy and modern democracy is unthinkable save in terms of the parties."

[pause]

Unthinkable save in terms of the parties.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. A country of 340 million people cannot govern itself by individual deliberation. Someone must bundle thousands of issues into a small number of governing programs. Someone must recruit and vet the people to carry them out. And someone must stand publicly accountable for the results. Only parties, this position argues, do all of that.

And when parties are strong, accountability is legible. You may know nothing about your own representative. But you know whether the team in power delivered. [beat] And you can fire the team. That is retrospective voting, the workhorse of democratic control.

> The responsible-party case: strong parties let voters fire the whole team.

The second argument surprises people. [beat] Strong parties also moderate.

Rosenbluth and Shapiro argue it by comparing countries. A strong party is a long-lived institution, and its brand must win majorities for decades. So it has every incentive to sand down the obsessions of its factions, and to resist a charismatic populism that might win one cycle and poison the brand for a generation. Weak parties cannot resist. They get captured.

On this view, the great American error of the last half-century was dismantling the gatekeepers. Nominations were handed to primary electorates: a fraction of the electorate, and more ideological than the party's own voters. Party money was capped so tightly that campaign funding migrated to unaccountable outside groups and dark-money networks. And every step was celebrated as democratization.

What does this position point to? Celebrity outsiders capturing nominations, over the organization's objections. Members of Congress more afraid of primary challengers than of general electorates. And governing programs assembled by donor networks, not by party councils.

So the prescription follows. Let parties control more of the nomination, and more of the money. Then judge them by results.

And what does it concede? Strong parties are less internally democratic. Its advocates answer that internal democracy was never the point. Accountability of the whole team to the whole electorate is. Even so, this camp must defend party elites to a public that despises them.

## The anti-party reformer

Now the anti-party reformer, built the same way.

Who holds this position? It is a reform tradition that runs from the Progressive era to today's open-primary and independent movements.

What does it protect? Fair rules of competition, and the voters who decline both brands.

Its strongest modern statement borrows a word from antitrust. [beat] Cartel.

In an article of 1995, Richard Katz and Peter Mair argued that established parties in mature democracies collude more than they compete. Such parties, they wrote, "become agents of the state," and use its resources "to ensure their own collective survival."

[pause]

Their own collective survival.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The American duopoly fits that description uncomfortably well. The two parties write the ballot-access laws that their competitors must satisfy. They draw the districts they run in. They sit on the bodies that administer their own elections. For a generation, they set a polling threshold of 15 percent for the presidential debates, and no outsider ever cleared it under the rule. Taxpayers even fund the parties' nomination contests.

This is not a market, this position argues. [beat] It is a regulated industry, whose two incumbents wrote the regulations.

What does this position point to? First, a democratic cost. Forty-five percent of American adults decline both brands. That is a record, and far more than identify with either party. Yet nearly every route to public office still runs through one of them.

Second, evidence that parties are not indispensable, hiding in plain sight. Over three-quarters of American municipalities elect their councils and mayors on nonpartisan ballots. And Nebraska has run a nonpartisan legislature since the 1930s.

Nobody serious proposes abolishing parties. Madison's logic still holds. But the parties' privileges are another matter.

> The anti-party case: the parties' privileges are a policy choice.

So the prescription follows. Open the primaries. Neutralize the gerrymander. Lower the walls around the ballot. Take election administration out of partisan hands. Then let the duopoly earn its dominance, under fair rules.

And what does it concede? Its own standing. It is the most popular position with the public, and the least popular with political scientists. Its advocates answer that the discipline is mistaking the parties' interests for democracy's. Even so, this camp must explain why the weakest-party era in American history coincided with the angriest politics, and not the calmest.

## The diagnostician

Third, the weak-parties, strong-partisanship diagnostician. Same treatment.

Who holds this position? The political scientist Julia Azari named the condition in 2016, days before that year's election. The defining feature of our era, she wrote, is "weak parties and strong partisanship."

[pause]

Weak parties and strong partisanship.

This camp says the first two are arguing about the wrong dial. So pull the two dials back, from the start of this lecture. Can you still say what each one measures?

[long pause]

Party strength is the organization. Partisanship is the voters' attachment. And the combination we have now is toxic, this position argues, precisely because each half disables the cure for the other.

> The diagnostician's case: weak parties and strong partisanship each disable the other's cure.

What does it protect? Accountability again, at both ends: organizations that answer to voters, and voters who are free to fire their own side.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Take the weak half first.

In a 2024 book called The Hollow Parties, Daniel Schlozman and Sam Rosenfeld supply the autopsy of the organizations. The formal parties have become shells: fundraising portals and legal containers. The real work of defining what a party stands for has been outsourced, to donor networks, to ideological media, and to aligned groups that face no voters.

A hollow party cannot do what the responsible-party camp wants, because it gatekeeps nothing. In 2016, a hostile outsider captured a major-party nomination, over the visible objections of virtually its entire organizational establishment. And weakening a hollow party further, as the anti-party camp proposes, mostly transfers power to the even less accountable groups around it.

Now the strong half. The electorate's partisanship has hardened into something more like identity than ideology. Research by Alan Abramowitz and Steven Webster, in 2016, documented the mechanism. It is called negative partisanship. Growing numbers of voters are driven less by enthusiasm for their own side than by fear and loathing of the other.

What does this position point to? Record straight-ticket voting. The near-extinction of split outcomes. And elections nationalized into a single existential contest.

Negative partisanship, this position argues, inverts accountability. You cannot fire your team, however it performs, when the other team is an enemy.

[pause]

And it explains the great puzzle of the era. Record numbers call themselves independent, while voting grows more rigidly partisan than at any point in the survey era. Most independents lean. [beat] And leaners vote like loyalists.

So the prescription follows. Rebuild the organizations, and drain the hostility that identity fuels. Both are required. Neither is sufficient. Anyone selling a single-lever fix, this camp argues, has misdiagnosed the disease.

And what does it concede? That its prescription is the least satisfying of the three. Its members answer that it is also the most honest. Even so, this camp must concede that "rebuild the organizations and soften the identities" is a direction, not a mechanism. It is easy to prescribe, and it has no proven dosage.

## Where the cases collide

Three cases, each at full strength. Now choose the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

Underneath all three cases sits one scholar's lens. In a 1995 book called Why Parties?, John Aldrich argued that parties are not expressions of public sentiment. They are institutions that politicians build, to solve their own recurring problems: regulating competition for office among the ambitious, stabilizing majorities in a legislature, and mobilizing electorates at scale.

> Aldrich's lens: the form of a party changes. The functions do not.

That cuts in every direction at once. Parties cannot be abolished, only redesigned, which is a problem for the strongest anti-party hopes. They will be rebuilt by self-interested politicians, not by civic architects, which is a problem for responsible-party blueprints. And if the formal organization goes hollow, something else will perform its functions, less accountably. That is the diagnosticians' core point.

Now strip the rhetoric. Each camp is making a different bet, and each bet can be tested against evidence.

The responsible-party camp bets that where parties control more money and more of the nomination, politics is more moderate and more accountable. The record that Rosenbluth and Shapiro assemble supports that bet across countries. The sharpest American test is party campaign finance, which the next lecture examines.

[beat]

The anti-party camp bets that opening party processes produces better representation. The American evidence so far shows effects that are real, but modest. They are nowhere near the transformation that either advocates or opponents predicted.

[beat]

The diagnosticians bet that reforms aimed at either dial will underdeliver, as long as negative partisanship supplies the motive force of politics. The 2020s keep testing that claim.

Let's put all three to work at once. Here is a ballot initiative. It would abolish taxpayer-funded party primaries. In their place, each party would choose its nominees at a convention of dues-paying members.

Which camp supports it? Which opposes it? And which is torn? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

The responsible-party advocate supports it. It restores the organization's core power, which is nomination, and it makes the party a membership institution again.

The anti-party reformer opposes it. It hands nominations to an even smaller, more insular group, and it strengthens the cartel's grip on access to the ballot.

And the diagnostician is torn. The initiative does rebuild organizational muscle, and on this view that is good. But it does nothing about negative partisanship. And the convention of a hollow party might be captured by its loudest faction, which is the very failure the plan is meant to prevent.

If you can reproduce all three reactions unprompted, you understand this lecture.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Two dials. Party strength is the organization. Partisanship is the voters' attachment. Today the organizations are weak, and the partisanship is strong.

[beat]

Two. The responsible-party case, from Schattschneider to Rosenbluth and Shapiro: strong parties let voters fire the whole team. It concedes less internal democracy.

[beat]

Three. The anti-party case, with Katz and Mair's word, cartel: the parties' privileges are a policy choice. It must explain why the weakest-party era was also the angriest.

[beat]

Four. The diagnostician's case, in Azari's phrase: weak parties and strong partisanship each disable the other's cure. It offers a direction, not a mechanism.

[beat]

And five. Aldrich's lens, under all three. Politicians build parties to solve their own problems. The form of a party changes. The functions do not. So parties cannot be abolished, only redesigned.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not a verdict. The three cases are not equally fashionable. The responsible-party view dominates among political scientists. The anti-party view dominates among voters. And the diagnosis dominates among the people who write about the other two. But fashion is not evidence, and each camp's bet is still open.

[pause]

Each diagnosis implies a different prescription, and the prescriptions conflict. So the next lecture walks the actual menu of reforms. Of each one, it asks which diagnosis the reform is betting on, and what the evidence says about the bet.

For now, go back to 1950, and to the choice we started with. Either the committee misdiagnosed the disease, or the operation succeeded and the patient got sicker anyway. [beat] You have now heard three answers to that, each at full strength. A defensible position takes one of them seriously.

> A defensible position states what it concedes, and what evidence would change it.
