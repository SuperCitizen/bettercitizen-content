# The strongest cases: stronger parties, weaker parties, or hollow parties

In September 1950, the American Political Science Association did something professional associations almost never do: it published a manifesto. *Toward a More Responsible Two-Party System* — 99 pages, drafted by a committee of the discipline's leading scholars under E. E. Schattschneider — argued that America's two parties were failing democracy by being too *alike* and too *incoherent*. Loose coalitions that agreed on little and enforced nothing, they could not offer voters a real choice or deliver on a mandate, so elections decided everything and settled nothing. The committee's prescription: make the parties more programmatic, more internally disciplined, more *distinct*. Seventy-five years later the wish has been granted beyond the committee's dreams — the parties are more ideologically sorted and legislatively unified than at any point in living memory — and the public's verdict is in: a record 45 percent of American adults now refuse to identify with either one. Either the best political scientists of 1950 misdiagnosed the disease, or the operation succeeded and the patient got sicker anyway. Sorting out which is the argument of this lesson — and it has three sides, not two.

## First, untangle two things that travel under one name

Every position in this debate trades on the word "strong," so pin it down using Lesson 1's three faces. **Party strength** properly refers to the organization: can the party control who runs under its label, fund its campaigns, staff its operations, and discipline its officeholders? **Partisanship** refers to the electorate: how firmly are voters attached to a party identity, and how reliably does it drive their behavior? The two are not the same dial, and American history has visited every combination of the two settings:

| | Strong partisanship (loyal voters) | Weak partisanship (flexible voters) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strong organizations** | Gilded Age machine era (1870s–1890s): armies of patronage workers, presidential turnout near 80%, party-printed ballots | Mid-20th century (1940s–1960s): substantial organizations, but split-ticket voting and a [[term:bipartisan]] center — the world the APSA report complained about |
| **Weak organizations** | **Today** (1990s–): hollowed committees and candidate-centered campaigns, but historically intense party loyalty in the electorate | The 1970s–1980s "dealignment": journalist David Broder's 1972 book was titled The Party's Over |

The table is the lesson in miniature. When the APSA committee wrote, organizations were reasonably strong and attachments loose. Reformers spent the next half-century weakening the organizations — nomination by [[term:primary]], campaign-finance limits, the collapse of patronage — on the theory that voters would supply the judgment parties no longer imposed. Instead, voters re-polarized while the organizations kept shriveling. Whether that lower-left cell is a crisis, an opportunity, or a misreading is precisely what the three camps dispute.

## Three cases, each at full strength

Each position below is presented as its smartest advocates argue it, with their evidence. None of them is a straw man; all three are held by serious scholars and reformers today.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Responsible-party advocate">
    Begin with Schattschneider's dictum, on page one of *Party Government* (1942): "The political parties created democracy and modern democracy is unthinkable save in terms of the parties." A country of 340 million people cannot govern itself by individual deliberation; someone must bundle thousands of issues into a small number of governing programs, recruit and vet the people to carry them out, and stand publicly accountable for the results. Only parties do this. When they are strong, accountability is *legible*: you may know nothing about your representative, but you know whether the team in power delivered, and you can fire the team — retrospective voting, the workhorse of democratic control.

    Strong parties also *moderate*, which surprises people. Frances Rosenbluth and Ian Shapiro's *Responsible Parties* (2018) makes the case comparatively: a strong party is a long-lived institution whose brand must win majorities for decades, so it has every incentive to sand down its factions' obsessions and resist a charismatic [[term:populism]] that might win one cycle and poison the brand for a generation. Weak parties cannot resist; they get captured. On this view the great American error of the last half-century was dismantling the gatekeepers — handing nominations to primary electorates (a fraction of the electorate, more ideological than the party's own voters), capping party money so tightly that campaign funding migrated to unaccountable outside groups and [[term:dark-money]] networks, and celebrating every step as democratization. The results are in: celebrity outsiders capturing nominations over the organization's objections, members of Congress more afraid of primary challengers than of general electorates, and governing programs assembled by donor networks rather than party councils. The prescription follows: let parties control more nomination and more money, and judge them by results. Note what this case concedes: strong parties are *less* internally democratic. Its advocates answer that internal democracy was never the point — accountability of the whole team to the whole electorate is.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Anti-party reformer">
    The reform tradition runs from the Progressive era ([[ideology:american-progressivism]]) to today's open-primary and independent movements, and its strongest modern statement borrows a word from antitrust: *cartel*. Richard Katz and Peter Mair's landmark 1995 article argued that established parties in mature democracies collude more than they compete — they "become agents of the state" and use its resources "to ensure their own collective survival." The American duopoly fits uncomfortably well: the two parties write the ballot-access laws their competitors must satisfy, draw the districts they run in, sit on the bodies that administer their own elections, and for a generation set a 15 percent polling threshold for presidential-debate access that no outsider ever cleared under the rule. Taxpayers even fund the parties' nomination contests. This is not a market; it is a regulated industry whose two incumbents wrote the regulations.

    The democratic cost is now measurable. Forty-five percent of American adults — a record, and far more than identify with either party — decline both brands, yet nearly every route to public office still runs through one of them. And the reformers' counter-evidence to party indispensability is hiding in plain sight: over three-quarters of American municipalities elect their councils and mayors on nonpartisan ballots, Nebraska has run a nonpartisan legislature since the 1930s, and the republic's courts and city managers function without party labels. Nobody serious proposes abolishing parties — Madison's logic still holds — but their *privileges* are a policy choice. Open the primaries, neutralize the gerrymander, lower ballot-access walls, take election administration out of partisan hands, and let the duopoly earn its dominance under fair rules. This camp is candid about its standing: it is the most popular position with the public and the least popular with political scientists — which its advocates read as the discipline mistaking the parties' interests for democracy's.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Weak-parties / strong-partisanship diagnostician">
    The third camp says the first two are arguing about the wrong dial. The political scientist Julia Azari named the condition in 2016, days before that year's election: the defining feature of our era is "weak parties and strong partisanship," and the combination is toxic precisely because each half disables the cure for the other. Daniel Schlozman and Sam Rosenfeld's *The Hollow Parties* (2024) supplies the organizational autopsy: the formal parties have become shells — fundraising portals and legal containers — while the actual work of defining what the party stands for has been outsourced to donor networks, ideological media, and aligned groups that face no voters. A hollow party cannot perform the function the responsible-party camp wants (it gatekeeps nothing — a hostile outsider captured a major-party nomination in 2016 over the visible objections of virtually its entire organizational establishment) — and weakening it further, as the anti-party camp proposes, mostly transfers power to the even less accountable penumbra around it.

    Meanwhile the electorate's partisanship has hardened into something closer to identity than ideology. Alan Abramowitz and Steven Webster's research on **negative partisanship** (2016) documented the mechanism: growing numbers of voters are driven less by enthusiasm for their own side than by fear and loathing of the other, producing record straight-ticket voting, the near-extinction of split outcomes, and elections nationalized into a single existential contest. Negative partisanship inverts accountability — you cannot fire your team, however it performs, when the other team is an enemy — and it explains the great puzzle of the era: record numbers calling themselves independent while voting behavior grows more rigidly partisan than at any point in the survey era. Most "independents" lean, and leaners vote like loyalists. The camp's prescription is the least satisfying and, its members argue, the most honest: rebuilding organizations *and* draining identity-fueled hostility are both required, neither is sufficient, and anyone selling a single-lever fix has misdiagnosed the disease.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

> **The scholar's lens:** John Aldrich's *Why Parties?* (1995) is the foundation under all three tabs. Parties, Aldrich argued, are not expressions of public sentiment but institutions *politicians* build to solve their own recurring problems: regulating competition among the ambitious for office, stabilizing legislative majorities that would otherwise cycle endlessly among alternatives, and mobilizing electorates at scale. The form changes — Van Buren's mass party, the machine, the modern party "in service" to its candidates — but the functions do not. The implication cuts every direction at once: parties cannot be abolished, only redesigned (a problem for the strongest anti-party hopes), they will be rebuilt by self-interested politicians rather than civic architects (a problem for responsible-party blueprints), and if the formal organization goes hollow, *something* — donors, media, movements — will perform its functions less accountably (the diagnosticians' core point).

## Where the cases collide — and what evidence would move you

Strip the rhetoric and the three camps make different falsifiable bets. The responsible-party camp bets that where parties control more money and more nomination, politics is more moderate and accountable; the comparative record Rosenbluth and Shapiro assemble supports it across countries, and Lesson 3 examines the sharpest American test (party campaign finance). The anti-party camp bets that opening party processes produces better representation; the American evidence so far — reviewed honestly in the Primaries & Caucuses pack — shows effects that are real but modest, nowhere near the transformation either advocates or opponents predicted. The diagnosticians bet that reforms aimed at either *strength* dial will underdeliver as long as negative partisanship supplies the motive force of politics — a claim the 2020s keep testing.

Notice also what each camp must swallow. Responsible-party advocates must accept less internal democracy and defend party elites to a public that despises them. Anti-party reformers must explain why the weakest-party era in American history coincided with the angriest politics, not the calmest. And the diagnosticians must concede that "rebuild the organizations and soften the identities" is a direction, not a mechanism — easy to prescribe, with no proven dosage.

<Checkpoint question="A ballot initiative would abolish taxpayer-funded party primaries and let each party choose nominees at a convention of dues-paying members. Which camp supports it, which opposes it, and which is genuinely torn — and why?">
  The responsible-party advocate supports it: it restores the organization's core power, nomination, and makes the party a membership institution again. The anti-party reformer opposes it: it hands nominations to an even smaller, more insular group and strengthens the cartel's grip on access to the ballot. The diagnostician is torn — it genuinely rebuilds organizational muscle (good, on their view), but it does nothing about negative partisanship, and a hollow party's convention might simply be captured by the loudest faction (the very failure it is meant to prevent). If you can reproduce all three reactions unprompted, you understand this lesson.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson is not

It is not a verdict. The three cases are not equally fashionable — the responsible-party view dominates among political scientists, the anti-party view among voters, the diagnosis among people who write about the other two — but fashion is not evidence, and each camp's bet remains live. It is also not yet a lesson about *doing* anything: each diagnosis implies a different prescription, and the prescriptions conflict. Lesson 3 walks the actual reform menu — primary rules, fusion voting, proportional representation, party finance, internal party democracy — and asks of each: which diagnosis is this reform betting on, and what does the evidence say about the bet? A defensible position on parties takes one of these three cases seriously, states what it concedes, and names the evidence that would change its holder's mind.
