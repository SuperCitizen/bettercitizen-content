# The reform landscape: what would actually change the parties

On December 9, 2025, the Supreme Court heard argument in a case where the Republican Party, in effect, sued for the right to act like a party. *National Republican Senatorial Committee v. FEC* challenges the federal limits — in place since the 1970s — on how much party committees may spend *in coordination with* their own candidates (for 2026: as little as $65,300 for many House races, up to about $4.07 million for a Senate race in the largest state). The Justice Department took the rare step of siding with the challengers, so the Democratic Party's national committees intervened to defend the law — meaning both parties showed up, on opposite sides, to argue about how strong parties should be. As of mid-2026 the decision is pending. Hold the oddity in mind: the strongest case *for* letting parties spend freely comes not from people who like money in politics but from scholars who watched the money go somewhere worse. Every reform in this lesson has that shape — it is a bet on one of Lesson 2's three diagnoses, and it usually has surprising friends and enemies. This lesson walks the five live families of party reform: who is pushing each, what it would actually do, and what it costs.

## Every reform is a bet on a diagnosis

| Reform family | What it would change | Whose bet it is | Status, mid-2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nomination rules (open / top-two / top-four primaries) | Who picks the people on the November ballot | Anti-party reformers | Active state-by-state trench warfare — see the Primaries & Caucuses pack |
| Fusion voting | Lets minor parties cross-nominate major-party candidates | Diagnosticians (build new parties without spoilers) | Legal in full form in N.Y. and Conn.; revival litigation lost in N.J. (2025), pending in Wisconsin |
| Proportional representation (Fair Representation Act) | Multi-member House districts elected by ranked ballot | Diagnosticians and structural reformers | Reintroduced July 2025 (H.R. 4632); no path to passage |
| Party finance (raise or strike coordination limits) | How much money flows through parties vs. outside groups | Responsible-party advocates | NRSC v. FEC argued Dec. 2025; decision pending |
| Internal party democracy (superdelegates, binding rules) | Who controls the parties' own nominations | Contested among all three camps | DNC's 2028 rules not yet set; RNC binds all delegates |

Read the third column closely, because it upends the usual political map. The reforms that *weaken* party organizations (open primaries, nonpartisan ballots) are championed by people who think parties have too much power; the reforms that *strengthen* them (finance deregulation, restored gatekeeping) are championed by people who think the organizations have too little; and the reforms that *add* parties (fusion, proportional representation) come from people who think the problem is the duopoly itself, not the strength dial. All three camps call themselves reformers. The label tells you nothing; the bet tells you everything.

## Nomination rules — the lever already in motion

The oldest and most active reform family — who may vote in a [[term:primary]], and whether the primary is partisan at all — belongs to the Primaries & Caucuses pack, which tracks the state-by-state scoreboard. One point belongs here, because it is about the parties rather than the elections. Every move along the spectrum from closed primary to top-four trades *party definition* for *voter access*: the more outsiders help choose a party's nominee, the less the label means, which is exactly the outcome anti-party reformers want and responsible-party advocates dread. The constitutional boundary runs the same way: states may design all-candidate systems *around* the parties, but may not force a party's own nomination open to nonmembers. And parties retain an underused unilateral option — running their own party-administered contests under their own rules, no statute required — which is why the deepest fights over nomination keep ending up *inside* the parties, in the rules fights of the final section.

## Fusion voting — the 19th century's reform, relitigated

**Fusion** (cross-nomination) lets two parties nominate the *same* candidate, with the candidate appearing on each party's ballot line and the votes added together. A voter can support the Democratic nominee *as* a Working Families candidate, or the Republican nominee *as* a Conservative — sending a countable signal about *why* the candidate won. In the 19th century this was ordinary American practice: minor parties — Greenbackers, Populists — bargained their lines for influence, and major-party candidates courted them. That is precisely why it died. After fusion tickets nearly broke Republican control of several states in the 1890s, the losing side's legislatures banned the practice; Wisconsin's 1897 ban, by its own legislative history, targeted the Democratic-Populist alliances of the decade. Most states followed, and the bans were locked in place a century later by *Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party* (1997), where the Supreme Court held 6–3 that Minnesota could forbid a minor party from nominating a willing major-party candidate: states may enact election rules "that may, in practice, favor the traditional two-party system," because a state may decide "that political stability is best served through a healthy two-party system." Note what *Timmons* did and did not do — it permitted fusion bans under the *federal* Constitution; it did not require them, and it left state constitutions open.

Full fusion survives where it was never banned — New York, whose Conservative Party (founded 1962) and Working Families Party (1998) are durable players in major-party politics, and Connecticut — and the open lane in state constitutions is where the revival is being fought:

The honest assessment cuts both ways. Fusion's appeal is that it creates *real, countable* minor parties without the spoiler problem that [[term:plurality]] voting otherwise imposes — a third party can matter by delivering its line's votes rather than by splitting them. New York's experience shows the model is durable; it also shows its modesty — a century of fusion there produced influential pressure groups attached to the duopoly, not a multiparty system. And the revival currently depends entirely on state judges reading state constitutions more generously than *Timmons* read the federal one; New Jersey's courts, the first asked, said no.

<Checkpoint question="A centrist organization wants to influence which major-party candidates win — without ever running spoiler candidates. Why does fusion serve that goal better than founding a conventional third party, and what is the first legal obstacle in most states?">
  Under fusion, the organization nominates the major-party candidate it prefers, and its ballot line shows exactly how many votes it delivered — leverage it can spend in policy negotiations, with no risk of splitting its side's vote. A conventional third party in a plurality system mostly threatens the major party *closest* to it (Duverger's logic from Lesson 1). The obstacle: roughly a century-old anti-fusion statutes in most states, which Timmons (1997) says the federal Constitution tolerates — so the fight runs state by state, under state constitutions, where New Jersey has said no and Wisconsin is pending.

## Proportional representation — repealing Duverger

The most structural proposal on the menu attacks the two-party equilibrium at its root. The **Fair Representation Act** (H.R. 4632, reintroduced July 23, 2025 by Rep. Don Beyer with a handful of co-sponsors) would end single-member House districts: states would elect representatives from districts of three to five members by [[term:ranked-choice-voting]], drawn under nonpartisan criteria, with mid-decade redistricting banned. In a five-member district, a bloc of roughly 17 percent elects a member — so a party with 20 percent support statewide stops winning *nothing* and starts winning *seats*. Duverger's mechanical effect runs in reverse, and the psychological effect follows it: votes for a third party stop being wasted, so people cast them. Congress could do this by ordinary statute under the Elections Clause — the same power it used to *mandate* single-member districts in 1967.

The fullest argument is Lee Drutman's *Breaking the Two-Party Doom Loop* (2020): when two fully sorted national parties face each other in winner-take-all contests, every election becomes existential, compromise becomes betrayal, and the system spirals — his "doom loop." Mid-century America escaped it, Drutman argues, because the formal two-party system concealed a working four-party one (liberal and conservative wings of each), which made shifting coalitions possible. Multiparty democracy via proportional rules would restore that fluidity honestly. The trade-offs deserve equal candor: in multiparty systems, governing coalitions form *after* the election in negotiations voters don't control; representatives in five-member districts share a constituency rather than owning one; small parties can become kingmakers out of proportion to their vote; and the American versions are projections, not observations — the U.S. has no modern experience with proportional House elections. (A side effect worth noticing: multi-member districts make [[term:gerrymandering]] vastly harder, since packing and cracking lose their power when seats divide proportionally — which is why the FRA is marketed as a redistricting reform as much as a party reform.) As of mid-2026 the bill is an agenda-setting exercise, not a live vote; its sponsors' theory is that doom-loop politics will eventually create its own demand.

## Party money — the strongest-parties bet, in court now

Campaign-finance law has whipsawed the parties for fifty years ([[term:campaign-finance]]). The 1970s FECA regime capped what parties could give to and spend with their candidates; the parties found the **soft money** workaround (unlimited "party-building" funds); the McCain-Feingold Act (2002) banned national-party soft money outright; and then [[term:citizens-united]] (2010) and its progeny unleashed unlimited *outside* spending. Squeeze the accountable channel, leave the unaccountable one open, and the result was Lesson 1's chart: a [[term:super-pac]] economy that outspends the entire formal party system.

The scholarly counterattack is Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner's *Campaign Finance and Political Polarization: When Purists Prevail* (2015). Using the states as a laboratory — some restrict party money severely, some barely at all — they found that legislatures in party-restricting states are *more* polarized, not less. The mechanism: parties are pragmatists that fund electable moderates, while the donors who fill the vacuum are ideological purists. Their conclusion, deliberately heretical for reformers: if you must have money in politics, route it through the parties, which face voters, file disclosures, and care about winning majorities — not through groups that face no one ([[term:dark-money]] included). That is the intellectual scaffolding under *NRSC v. FEC*: strike (or raise) the coordination limits and money flows back through party committees. The defenders' counter, which carried *FEC v. Colorado Republican* (2001) and the en banc Sixth Circuit in 2024: coordination limits are anti-circumvention armor — without them, a donor who has maxed out to a candidate simply gives to the party with a wink, and candidate contribution limits die. Both predictions cannot be fully right, and one of them is about to be tested live.

## The parties' own rules — democracy inside the cathedral

The final lever needs no statute and no court: parties can rewrite themselves. The modern case study is the Democratic **superdelegate** fight. Created for 1984 after the Hunt Commission — party leaders and elected officials seated automatically, unpledged — superdelegates were meant as a professional check on primary electorates. They never actually reversed a pledged-delegate leader, but after the 2016 cycle made their *potential* to do so radioactive, the DNC in 2018 barred automatic delegates from voting on a first convention ballot unless the outcome is already determined. The reform traded a peer-review mechanism for legitimacy — exactly the trade *The Party Decides* school warns about, since its research found that the "invisible primary" of party insiders historically did the real vetting. The RNC runs the opposite model: no unpledged class at all — its automatic delegates (three per state) are bound by state results like everyone else, which means the organization has even *less* discretion to stop a nominee its leadership opposes, a fact 2016 demonstrated vividly. As of mid-2026, the DNC's delegate-selection and calendar rules for 2028 are still being deliberated; whatever emerges will be the cleanest available reading of which diagnosis the party itself believes.

> **Common misconception:** "Superdelegates decide Democratic nominations." They never have — every nominee since their 1984 creation also led among pledged delegates. The serious argument was always about the *threat* (does an insider backstop deter outsider capture?) versus the *taint* (does its mere existence delegitimize the winner?). The 2018 reform answered: the taint costs more. Whether that answer survives a genuinely contested 2028 convention is an open question.

## What this lesson did not settle

It did not settle which reform is right, because that depends on which Lesson 2 diagnosis you accept — and the reforms genuinely conflict: finance deregulation strengthens the duopoly's organizations; proportional representation dilutes the duopoly itself; open primaries weaken every party's definition; fusion strengthens *minor* parties while leaving the majors intact. Watch three things from mid-2026 forward: the *NRSC v. FEC* decision, which will either re-arm the formal parties or confirm the super-PAC status quo; the Wisconsin fusion ruling, which will tell us whether state constitutions offer the revival a path *Timmons* closed federally; and the DNC's 2028 rules, the purest measure of how a hollow-feeling party grades its own condition. A defensible position here names its diagnosis, picks the reform that actually addresses it, states the trade-off it is accepting — and identifies the result that would prove the bet wrong.
