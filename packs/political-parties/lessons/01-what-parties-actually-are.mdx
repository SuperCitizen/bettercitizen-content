# What parties actually are

On September 19, 1796, Philadelphia's *American Daily Advertiser* published a long letter from George Washington to "Friends and Fellow-Citizens." He would not seek a third term — and he wanted to leave a warning behind. He cautioned the country in "the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party," called party passion "a fire not to be quenched," and warned that "the alternate domination of one faction over another, sharpened by the spirit of revenge," was "itself a frightful despotism" in the making. Now the punchlines. The address was never delivered as a speech — and the man who helped Washington draft it, Alexander Hamilton, was the effective leader of one of the two proto-parties Washington was warning about. The cabinet had already split: Hamilton's Treasury faction and Thomas Jefferson's State Department faction were funding rival newspapers to attack each other. That December, the election to replace Washington became the first contested party election in presidential history — Federalist John Adams, 71 electoral votes; Republican Thomas Jefferson, 68. The Framers had written a Constitution with no political parties in it. Politics filled it with parties before the founding generation left the stage. Whether that was a design failure, the design working as intended, or something no design could prevent is the question this whole pack circles. Start with what a party actually *is*.

## A Constitution against parties

Search the Constitution for political parties and you find nothing. The only "Party" in the text is a litigant — "Controversies to which the United States shall be a Party," in Article III. No nominations, no primaries, no majority and minority leaders, no party-line anything. This was not an oversight. The Framers knew parties intimately from English politics and the state legislatures of the 1780s; they called them **factions**, and they regarded faction roughly the way physicians regarded fever.

James Madison's [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 10 is the canonical diagnosis. A faction, he wrote, is a group of citizens "united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest," adverse to the rights of others or to "the permanent and aggregate interests of the community." But Madison's striking move was to reject the obvious cure. You could abolish faction only by abolishing liberty — and "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires." Since "the latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man," there are only "two methods of curing the mischiefs of faction: the one, by removing its causes; the other, by controlling its effects." The Constitution chose the second: "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests," so that no single passion could assemble a durable national majority. The machine was designed so factions would check, fragment, and exhaust each other.

The historian Richard Hofstadter called this "a Constitution against parties" — and documented the irony: the machine built to frustrate organized faction was, almost immediately, operated *by* two organized factions, and Madison himself co-founded one of them. By 1791 he and Jefferson were organizing congressional opposition to Hamilton's financial program and recruiting a newspaper to carry the fight. Jefferson had written, just two years earlier, "If I could not go to heaven but with a party, I would not go there at all." He went on to build the most successful party of the era and ride it to the presidency.

Why did the anti-party design fail so fast? Hold onto the answer the political scientist John Aldrich gives in *Why Parties?*, because it organizes everything that follows: the Constitution created offices worth winning and decisions that required assembling majorities again and again — on the national bank, on assumption of state debts, on Britain versus France. Ambitious politicians facing the same fights repeatedly found that a standing coalition beat improvising one every time. Parties were not a betrayal of the constitutional system; they were a rational response to it. By 1800, party-line elector discipline produced an exact Electoral College tie and a constitutional crisis, and the 12th Amendment (1804) quietly rewrote presidential balloting to accommodate the existence of parties — the story told in detail in the Electoral College pack. The Constitution never mentions parties, but it has been amended *because of* them.

> **Why this matters:** Every February since 1896, a senator has read Washington's Farewell Address aloud to the chamber — a body organized, seated, staffed, and scheduled by the two parties the address warns against. American politics has never resolved this tension; it has institutionalized it.

<Checkpoint question="Madison thought faction was dangerous. The Framers controlled the Constitutional Convention. So why didn't they simply write a ban on political parties into the document?">
  Because Madison's own analysis ruled it out. The causes of faction are "sown in the nature of man," so suppressing parties would require suppressing the liberty to organize, speak, and associate — a cure "worse than the disease." The Constitution instead tries to control faction's *effects* through size and fragmentation. And within a decade, the men who built that machine — Madison included — discovered that operating it required exactly the durable coalitions they had feared.
</Checkpoint>

## One word, three things: the three faces of a party

What is the Democratic Party? A voter registered in New Jersey? A building on South Capitol Street with a chair and a budget? Two hundred-odd members of Congress voting together? The political scientist V. O. Key Jr. taught generations of students that the word "party" bundles three different things, and most confusion about parties comes from sliding between them mid-sentence. His framework, canonical since *Politics, Parties, and Pressure Groups*:

- **The party-in-the-electorate** — the voters who identify with the party. There is no membership card and there are no dues; in most states, "joining" a party means checking a box on a voter-registration form, and in the rest it means nothing more than telling a pollster. This face is measured in self-identification: in Gallup's 2025 averages, 27 percent of American adults called themselves Republicans, 27 percent Democrats — and a record 45 percent independents, though most of those independents lean toward and reliably vote with one side, a wrinkle Lesson 2 takes seriously.
- **The party-as-organization** — the formal machinery: national committees, state committees, county and precinct committees, chairs, staff, rules, conventions, and money. This is the face people picture when they say "the party decided," and it is the face that turns out, on inspection, to be weakest.
- **The party-in-government** — the officeholders elected under the label: the congressional caucuses that choose the Speaker and committee chairs, the [[term:partisan]] delegations in fifty state legislatures, the president as de facto party leader. This face writes the laws, and its cohesion is what makes a midterm verdict or a presidential coattail effect legible as a *party* outcome rather than 535 individual ones.

The framework earns its keep because the three faces move independently. American history offers every combination: mighty organizations with loosely attached voters (the 1880s machines), feeble organizations with ferociously attached voters (a strong claim about the present that Lesson 2 will examine), and disciplined officeholders atop both. When you hear "the parties have never been weaker" and "the parties have never been stronger" in the same week — and you will — the speakers are usually each talking about a different face, and both may be right.

## The org chart — and the money that routes around it

On paper, each major party is a federation. At the base sit thousands of county and precinct committees staffed mostly by volunteers. Above them, fifty state parties, each a creature of *state* law as much as of party rules. At the top, six national committees: the Democratic National Committee (dating to 1848) and the Republican National Committee (1856), plus four "hill committees" run by the congressional parties themselves — the DCCC and NRCC for the House, the DSCC and NRSC for the Senate, each existing to elect its chamber's partisans. Every four years a national convention formally outranks them all to nominate a presidential ticket — though, as the Primaries & Caucuses pack shows, the nomination is settled by [[term:primary]] voters and delegate math long before the gavel falls.

What the chart does not show is command authority, because there is none. No national chair can fire a governor, deny a senator renomination, or compel a single vote in Congress. Political scientists describe the modern committees as **service parties**: they raise money, build voter files, train staff, and transfer funds to the races that need them. Real authority over who carries the label belongs to primary electorates, and real campaign control belongs to the candidates' own organizations.

Then there is the money, which is the quickest way to see how far the formal organizations have been eclipsed. Across the 2023–24 cycle, every party committee in America combined — national, state, and local — reported $2.7 billion in federal receipts. Outside groups, mostly [[term:super-pac]]s funded without contribution limits, spent a record $4.5 billion on the same elections. A single pro-Harris super PAC, Future Forward, spent about $517 million; Trump's MAGA Inc. spent about $377 million — each one organization, accountable to no voter and no convention, operating at the scale of a national committee.

Read the bars carefully: the entire formal party system, top to bottom, now commands *less* money than the unaccountable penumbra around it. It was not always so. The 19th-century party was an employer and a welfare state — the [[term:patronage-system]] traded government jobs for loyalty, and machines like [[term:tammany-hall]] converted immigrants' votes into coal, jobs, and bail. The Pendleton Act (1883) began strangling patronage, civil-service reform finished the job, and television and consultants gave candidates a way to reach voters without precinct captains. Today's party cannot give you a job. What still binds tens of millions of people to a label that pays them nothing is the puzzle at the center of Lesson 2.

<Checkpoint question="A first-term senator routinely votes against her party's leadership. Who can actually remove her from office or take away her party label — the national committee chair, the president, her state party, or the Senate caucus?">
  None of them. The formal organizations have no such power: the chair commands a service bureaucracy, the president commands attention but not ballots, and the caucus can strip committee assignments but not the label. Only *primary voters* in her state can deny her renomination — which is why modern party discipline runs through the party-in-the-electorate (the threat of a primary challenge), not the party-as-organization. Weak machinery, strong loyalty: keep that asymmetry in mind for Lesson 2.
</Checkpoint>

## Why exactly two

Most democracies have parties. Almost none have had the same two parties dominating every level of office since 1856. The classic explanation is **Duverger's law**, after the French political scientist Maurice Duverger, who observed that "the simple-majority single-ballot system favours the two-party system" — about as close, he ventured, as political science gets to a true sociological law. Two engines drive it. The *mechanical effect*: in a [[term:plurality]], winner-take-all district, a party winning 15 percent everywhere wins nothing anywhere; dispersed support converts to zero seats. The *psychological effect*: voters see that coming and desert third candidates in advance, voting for the lesser evil that can actually win — and donors and ambitious politicians make the same calculation, emptying third parties of talent before any ballots are cast. The Voting Systems pack develops both effects and the international evidence.

American institutions then reinforce the equilibrium beyond what Duverger requires. Single-member House districts are not in the Constitution — they have been required by statute, with interruptions, since 1842, and without exception since a 1967 law (2 U.S.C. §2c), so no state may experiment with multi-member House elections that could give a third party a foothold. Ballot-access laws, written by two-party legislatures, set signature and vote thresholds that minor parties must re-clear cycle after cycle. The presidency is a single, indivisible prize awarded through state-by-state winner-take-all contests. And from 2000 until its sidelining, the Commission on Presidential Debates required 15 percent in national polls for a podium — a bar no candidate outside the two parties ever cleared under the rule. Third parties in America have mattered — but historically by *threat and absorption*: the major parties co-opt any third-party issue that starts winning votes, which is a kind of influence that leaves no seats behind.

## Six party systems — and a fight about the present

The two-party *system* is permanent; the two *coalitions* are not. Political scientists organize American history into successive **party systems**, separated by realignments — elections or short eras in which the coalitions durably re-sort ([[term:electoral-realignment]]). The dates below are the textbook ones; treat the last entry as a live controversy, not a fact.

A caution before you memorize the dates. The realignment framework — V. O. Key again, systematized by Walter Dean Burnham, who saw realignments arriving on a roughly 30-to-40-year cycle — is the most famous periodization in American political science, and also one of the most battered. David Mayhew's *Electoral Realignments* (2002) tested the genre's claims (periodicity, sudden durable change, turnout surges) and found most of them wanting — the realignment the cycle predicted for the 1990s never arrived in any form the theory could recognize. The defensible version is modest: coalitions re-sort, sometimes quickly, usually visibly only in hindsight — 1860, 1896, and 1932 really were hinges. Whether 2016 was one is exactly the kind of question the framework cannot answer in real time.

> **Think it through:** If realignments are typically visible only decades later, what evidence — turnout, registration, county-level swings, generational replacement — would persuade you, *now*, that the coalitions of 2026 are a new party system rather than the old one mid-argument?

## What this lesson did not settle

You now have the mechanics: a Constitution that never mentions parties and was amended to accommodate them; a three-faced definition precise enough to keep arguments honest; an org chart whose formal pyramid is dwarfed by the money around it; Duverger's engines plus American reinforcements; and a party-systems history whose present chapter has no agreed title. What none of this settles is the *evaluative* question the Founders thought they had answered: are parties a disease the republic manages, or the organs it thinks with? Lesson 2 steel-mans the three live answers — that American parties are too weak and should be strengthened, that they are entrenched cartels and should be opened up, and that the real pathology is hollow organizations bolted to ferocious loyalties. Lesson 3 then walks the actual reform menu, from fusion voting to proportional representation, that would rewrite this lesson's mechanics.
