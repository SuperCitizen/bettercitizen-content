# From railroads to Silicon Valley

In the lesson on a nation of inventors, the story was about individuals and the institutions that protected and trained them. The twentieth-century story is increasingly about organizations — corporations, universities, and the federal government — and about a recurring pattern in which public investment and private enterprise advanced together.

## Knitting a continent together

After the Civil War, two technologies stitched the country into a single economy. The **railroads**, subsidized by federal land grants and capped by the transcontinental line completed in 1869, collapsed travel times and freight costs. The **telegraph** ran alongside the tracks, letting prices, schedules, and orders move faster than any train. Together they created a **continental market**: a manufacturer in the East could now sell to the West, and raw materials could flow back the other way. National-scale companies, standardized time zones, and modern management practices all grew out of this networked economy.

## Industrializing invention

Edison's "invention factory" had shown that research could be organized. In the early twentieth century, large corporations made it permanent. **General Electric** established an industrial research laboratory in 1900; **AT&T** founded **Bell Telephone Laboratories** in 1925. Bell Labs in particular became one of the most productive research organizations in history, its scientists later credited with the transistor, the laser, information theory, and foundational work in computing — several earning Nobel Prizes. Corporate **research and development (R&D)** turned invention from a gamble into a budgeted, ongoing activity, staffed by salaried scientists and engineers.

## The federal turn to big science

World War II changed the scale of American science. The federal government, needing weapons and technology quickly, mobilized universities and industry as never before. The **Manhattan Project** — a vast, secret effort drawing on national laboratories, university physicists, and industrial contractors — produced the first atomic weapons and demonstrated that the government could marshal scientific talent toward enormous, urgent goals. Wartime work also accelerated radar, the proximity fuze, penicillin production, and early electronic computing.

The architect of much of this mobilization was the engineer **Vannevar Bush**, who directed the wartime Office of Scientific Research and Development. In 1945 he delivered to the President a report titled **"Science, the Endless Frontier."** Bush argued that basic research — the open-ended pursuit of knowledge — was "the pacemaker of technological progress," and that because private firms underinvest in it, the federal government should fund basic science in universities while leaving researchers free to follow their curiosity. The report's most lasting result was the creation, in 1950, of the **National Science Foundation (NSF)**, establishing a permanent peacetime role for federal support of basic research.

## The Cold War and the digital age

The Cold War sustained this commitment. After the Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, the United States responded with new institutions. **NASA** was created in 1958, and within a decade the **Apollo** program landed astronauts on the Moon in 1969 — a demonstration of what concentrated public investment could achieve. Also in 1958, the Defense Department created the agency now known as **DARPA** (the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency) to fund high-risk, high-payoff research.

DARPA's most consequential project was the **ARPANET**, a computer network first connected in 1969 that pioneered packet switching and the protocols from which the **internet** later grew. The network was built by university and corporate researchers on federal contracts, then opened to wider use, before private companies and millions of individuals built on top of it.

Meanwhile, the **semiconductor** revolution unfolded. The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, gave way to the integrated circuit in the late 1950s. Early demand from the military and the space program helped a young industry mature. A cluster of firms south of San Francisco — descended from Shockley Semiconductor and Fairchild, and including Intel, founded in 1968 — gave the region its nickname, **Silicon Valley**. As chips grew cheaper and more powerful, the **personal computer** became possible in the 1970s, and companies such as Apple and others put computing into homes and offices.

## A recurring pattern

Walter Isaacson, chronicling this digital revolution, stressed that it was rarely the work of lone geniuses. It emerged from collaboration — among researchers, between disciplines, and especially between sectors. That is the thread running through this entire history: a **public-private partnership** in which government funded the risky early research and infrastructure (the telegraph subsidy, land-grant universities, the NSF, NASA, DARPA), and private enterprise commercialized, scaled, and improved the results. American innovation has rarely been purely public or purely private. Its distinctive strength has been the handoff between the two — from railroads and research labs to the chips and networks that define modern life.
