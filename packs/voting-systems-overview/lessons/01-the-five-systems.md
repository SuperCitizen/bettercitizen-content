# The five voting systems most people argue about

Most American jurisdictions use **plurality** (also called first-past-the-post) for federal and most state offices. The systems below are alternatives that are either already in use somewhere in the U.S. or actively debated for adoption.

This lesson explains how each system works *mechanically*. The next lesson covers what each does well, what each does poorly, and where the empirical evidence lands. The point of doing it in this order: you can argue about tradeoffs intelligently only after you can describe each system in one paragraph without looking it up.

## 1. Plurality voting (status quo in most U.S. elections)

- **Ballot:** vote for one candidate.
- **Winner:** the candidate with the most votes — even if that's less than 50%.
- **Where used:** the President (within each state), the U.S. House and Senate, governors, and most state legislative seats.

The defining feature is that you cannot express any preference beyond your single first choice. If your favorite is unlikely to win, "wasting your vote" on them can effectively help your least-preferred candidate — the **spoiler effect**.

## 2. Ranked-choice voting (instant-runoff form)

- **Ballot:** rank candidates from most-preferred to least.
- **Winner:** if any candidate has a majority of first-choice votes, they win. If not, the lowest-ranked candidate is eliminated, and ballots that had them first transfer to those voters' next choice. Repeat until one candidate has a majority of continuing ballots.
- **Where used:** Maine and Alaska (statewide), New York City, San Francisco, Minneapolis, Cambridge MA (city offices), and ~50 other U.S. jurisdictions for various offices.

A multi-winner variant (Single Transferable Vote, STV) exists for legislative elections but is rare in the U.S.

## 3. Approval voting

- **Ballot:** check the name of every candidate you find acceptable. No ranking, no scores.
- **Winner:** whoever gets the most check marks.
- **Where used:** Fargo ND (city offices, since 2020), St. Louis MO (with a separate runoff round, since 2021).

Approval is the simplest reform mechanically — it asks a single yes/no per candidate.

## 4. Score voting (also called range voting)

- **Ballot:** give each candidate a numeric score, e.g., 0–10 or 0–5. You may give the same score to multiple candidates.
- **Winner:** whoever has the highest total score across all ballots.
- **Where used:** No U.S. governmental elections at present. Used internally by many private organizations (boards, conferences) and by the United Nations Population Fund's executive board for some purposes.

Score voting is the most expressive system in this list — it lets you say not just *who* you prefer, but *how much*.

## 5. STAR voting (Score Then Automatic Runoff)

- **Ballot:** score candidates 0–5, like score voting.
- **Winner:** the two candidates with the highest total scores advance to an "automatic runoff" — within those two, the candidate that more ballots scored higher wins.
- **Where used:** Used in some private organizations and primaries; on the ballot in Eugene OR, 2020 (failed to pass).

STAR is a hybrid: scoring captures intensity, the runoff captures preference between the two finalists.

## What you'll notice

- All four reform systems let you express something plurality cannot.
- Each does so differently, and *those differences are where the real argument lives*.
- "Which is best" is not the right question. The right questions are: best at what? Under what conditions? At what cost in voter cognitive load and counting complexity?
