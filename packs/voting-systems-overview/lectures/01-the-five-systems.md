On March 3, 2009, the voters of Burlington, Vermont, elected a mayor. [beat] Then they spent a year arguing about who had actually won.

The voters had ranked the candidates on their ballots. And that one stack of ballots could be counted three ways.

Count only first choices, and the Republican, Kurt Wright, led the field.

Run the city's instant-runoff rules, and the Progressive incumbent, Bob Kiss, won.

Examine every head-to-head matchup hidden in those same ranked ballots, and the Democrat, Andy Montroll, beat both of them. [beat] While finishing third.

[pause]

One stack of ballots. Three defensible winners. The only thing that changed was the counting rule.

Burlington's voters repealed instant-runoff voting the next year. A decade later, they re-adopted a version of it.

That episode is our whole subject in miniature.

> A voting system is not neutral plumbing.

It is a choice about which information on the ballot counts, and how.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to describe five voting systems, each in a few sentences, without looking anything up. What the ballot asks. How the count proceeds. And where the system is used in the United States, as of mid-2026.

We will not yet ask which one is best. That comes next time, and the order matters. You can argue about tradeoffs intelligently only after you can describe the machinery.

We will take it in four steps.

First, plurality: the system most of the country uses now, and the pressure it puts on voters.

Second, ranked-choice voting, and how its count actually runs.

Third, a demonstration. One set of ballots, three counting rules, three winners.

And last, three systems that do not rank at all: approval, score, and STAR.

## Plurality, the system we have

Start with the one you already know.

Under plurality, the ballot asks one question: pick one candidate. The winner is whoever gets the most votes. That winning share is called a plurality, and it can fall far short of 50 percent.

This is the rule for the U.S. House and Senate. It is the rule for the presidential vote within most states, and for most state offices.

In 1860, Abraham Lincoln won the presidency with just under 40 percent of the popular vote. He did not need a majority, because plurality does not ask for one.

> Plurality asks for the most votes. It does not ask for a majority.

Now notice what that ballot cannot do. It cannot carry any preference beyond a single first choice. So if your favorite is unlikely to win, voting for your favorite can help elect the candidate you like least. [beat] That is the spoiler effect.

Some states patch the problem with a second election, called a runoff. Georgia, for example, requires one between the top two candidates if nobody clears a majority. But the patch has a price: a second campaign, a second election day, and typically a steep drop in turnout between the rounds.

Now the scholar's lens. It belongs to Maurice Duverger, a French political scientist. In his book Political Parties, he stated what is now called Duverger's law. Here it is in his own words.

"The simple-majority single-ballot system favours the two-party system."

[pause]

In plainer words: plurality voting pushes a country toward two parties.

He gave the law two engines.

The first engine is mechanical. A party that wins 15 percent of the vote everywhere wins zero seats anywhere. [beat] Plurality takes support that is spread out, and converts it into nothing.

The second engine is psychological. Voters see the mechanical effect coming, and they abandon third candidates in advance. Duverger described electors transferring their vote "to the less evil of its two adversaries in order to prevent the success of the greater evil."

[pause]

The less evil, to prevent the greater evil.

Let's put that to work. Suppose the polls show your favorite candidate running third in a plurality race.

What does Duverger's psychological effect predict you will do? And what follows for the whole system, if most voters reason the same way? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It predicts that you will abandon your favorite. You will vote for whichever of the top two you dislike less, because a vote for third place is mechanically wasted.

Now add that reasoning up across voters, and repeat it across elections. Third parties are starved of votes before the count even happens.

> A counting rule, not any law about parties, sustains the two-party system.

Every other system in this lecture is, in one way or another, an attempt to relax the pressures Duverger described. That is why the parties comfortable under the current rules are so often the most determined opponents of changing them.

## Ranked-choice voting

That is the system we have. Now the first of the alternatives.

A ranked-choice ballot asks you to rank the candidates: first choice, second, third, as far down as you care to go.

The count runs in rounds. If a candidate wins a majority of first choices, that candidate wins outright. If not, the candidate in last place is eliminated, and each of those ballots transfers to its next choice still in the race. Then the count repeats, until someone holds a majority of the ballots still in play.

Eliminate. Transfer. Repeat.

[pause]

The method is called the instant runoff, because it simulates a whole series of runoff elections from a single ballot. Ranked-choice voting, or RCV, is the umbrella term Americans now use for it.

Reading that rule and running it are different experiences. The lesson page has a simulator where you can step through a count, round by round. Here is one run.

Picture 100 ballots. One candidate opens with 38 first choices, a comfortable win if this were a plurality count. But that leader's voters ranked no one else, and the other 62 ballots chain toward a single rival as the eliminations go on. The final round is 62 to 38. [beat] The round-one leader has lost.

> Plurality reads the top line of each ballot. The instant runoff keeps reading down.

It keeps reading until a majority coalition emerges. And a candidate who leads on first choices can be sitting on a ceiling, which the transfers expose.

One caution before we go on. You will hear it said that ranked-choice voting guarantees the winner has majority support. Not quite. It guarantees a majority of the ballots still in play in the final round.

Some ballots rank only candidates who end up eliminated. Those ballots are called exhausted, and they drop out of the count. In Burlington, Bob Kiss won 48 percent of all the ballots cast. That was a majority only after the exhausted ballots were set aside.

[pause]

So where is ranked-choice voting used? As of mid-2026, the picture has two sides.

Maine was the first state. It adopted RCV by citizen initiative in 2016, and first used it statewide in June 2018.

Alaska adopted a combined system by initiative in 2020: a top-four primary, then RCV in the general election. In 2024, after a recount, that system survived a repeal initiative by 743 votes, out of more than 320,000.

Then come dozens of cities and counties, New York City and San Francisco among them. By FairVote's count, in February 2026, that makes 49 American jurisdictions in all, the two states included.

That is one side. The counter-trend is just as real. Nineteen states, most of them Republican-led, have passed laws banning RCV. In most cases, those laws also keep cities from adopting it.

## Same ballots, three winners

Now the demonstration I promised. It is the cleanest way to see that the counting rule is doing real work. There are numbers in it, so we will go slowly.

Imagine 100 voters and five candidates. In order of their first-choice support, they are Avery, Brooks, Cruz, Diaz and Ellis.

Avery has 35 first choices. Brooks has 25.

Cruz has 16. Diaz has 13. And Ellis has 11.

[pause]

So Avery leads. But there is one thing to know about the rest of the room. The voters for Cruz, Diaz and Ellis all prefer Brooks to Avery.

Now count those ballots three ways.

Rule one is plurality. Count first choices only. [beat] Avery wins, with 35.

Rule two is a top-two runoff, the kind Georgia holds. Avery and Brooks go on to a second election. The Cruz, Diaz and Ellis voters all break for Brooks. [beat] So Brooks wins, 65 to 35.

Rule three is the instant runoff. Follow the eliminations.

Ellis is in last place, so Ellis goes out first. Those 11 ballots list Cruz next, and Cruz rises to 27.

Diaz goes next. That is 13 more ballots for Cruz, who now has 40.

Three candidates remain. Cruz has 40. Avery still has 35. And Brooks, at 25, is now last. So Brooks is eliminated, and Brooks's voters listed Cruz next. [beat] Cruz beats Avery, 65 to 35.

[pause]

Avery under plurality. Brooks under a runoff. Cruz under the instant runoff.

> No one miscounted. The rules weigh different parts of the ballot.

Why do the two kinds of runoff disagree with each other? Because they choose different finalists. The ordinary runoff locks in the top two on first choices, Avery and Brooks. The instant runoff lets the transfers work first, and the transfers carry Cruz past Brooks before the final round.

There is one more twist hidden in these ballots. Brooks beats every rival head-to-head. A candidate who can do that is called a Condorcet winner. [beat] Yet under the instant runoff, Brooks loses. That is exactly what happened to Andy Montroll, in Burlington, in 2009.

Let's test what you have, with one change. Keep the same 100 ballots. But this time, the 13 Diaz voters rank Brooks second, instead of Cruz.

Here is where the count stands as Diaz goes out. Avery has 35. Cruz has 27, counting the Ellis ballots. Brooks has 25, and the 13 Diaz ballots now flow to Brooks. One reminder: the Cruz voters and the Ellis voters prefer Brooks to Avery.

Who is eliminated next? And who wins in the end? Take your time with it.

[pause 5s]

Cruz is eliminated. And Brooks wins.

Those 13 ballots lift Brooks to 38. So Cruz, at 27, is now the one in last place. Cruz's ballots list Brooks next: the original 16, plus the 11 that came from Ellis. Brooks beats Avery, 65 to 35.

One bloc of voters changed its second choice. The winner changed with it.

> Under the instant runoff, who is eliminated early decides who survives to the end.

The transfers are the whole game.

## Three systems that do not rank

Ranked-choice voting asks you to put the candidates in order. The last three systems ask for something else: a judgment on each candidate, one at a time.

Approval voting comes first. The ballot lists every candidate with a checkbox, and you mark every one you find acceptable. No ranking, no scores, no limit. The winner is whoever collects the most checks.

Mechanically, it is the smallest possible change to a plurality ballot. Same grid, same counting machines. You delete one rule, the rule that says vote for only one. That is why its advocates pitch it as reform without new infrastructure.

Its track record in American government is short and turbulent.

Fargo, North Dakota, adopted approval voting by initiative in 2018, the first American city to do so. It used the system for city elections from 2020 through 2024. Then, in April 2025, North Dakota's legislature banned approval voting and RCV statewide. [beat] Fargo's ordinance was void.

St. Louis, Missouri, adopted a variant in November 2020: an approval-voting first round, with the top two advancing to a runoff. That system survives. Missouri voters banned both reforms statewide in 2024, but the amendment grandfathered St. Louis.

Now a question about that ballot. Say there are five candidates, and you check three of them, including your absolute favorite.

How much more does your ballot help your favorite than your third choice?

[long pause]

Not at all. [beat] Approval is binary, so all three checked candidates get identical support from you. That flattening is the price of approval's simplicity.

And the last two systems were designed to fix exactly that.

Score voting asks you to grade every candidate on a numeric scale, such as 0 to 5, the way you would rate a restaurant. The winner is the candidate with the highest total.

Score is the most expressive of the five systems. It captures your ordering, and it also captures how much daylight you see between the candidates. You can give your favorite a 5 and your third choice a 2. A ranking cannot say that.

No U.S. government currently elects officials by score voting. It matters to us for two reasons. It is the system that the book Score the Vote, in this library, advocates. And it is the foundation of STAR, which was engineered to fix score voting's biggest strategic weakness. That story belongs to the next lecture.

STAR stands for Score Then Automatic Runoff, and the name is the recipe.

You begin as in score voting, rating every candidate from 0 to 5. Then the two highest-scoring candidates advance to an automatic runoff. In that runoff your full ballot is consulted again, and your vote goes to whichever finalist you scored higher. Score the two finalists equally, and you abstain from that round.

[pause]

So the scoring captures intensity, and the runoff restores a one-person-one-vote choice, head to head. Even a middle score leaves you a full-strength vote between the two finalists.

STAR is the youngest system of the five, and no government anywhere has adopted it. Its proving ground is Oregon. In May 2024, a citizen initiative to adopt it for city elections in Eugene reached the ballot, and lost by roughly two to one. Six months later, the small Oregon city of Oakridge rejected a trial of STAR by just 24 votes.

So, as of mid-2026, STAR is a proposal with an unusually rigorous simulation literature and no public-election track record. That combination is worth keeping in mind when you weigh claims about it.

## What to keep

Before we gather this up, try something. We have met five ballots, and each one asks the voter for something different. Can you still name what each of the five asks?

[long pause]

Plurality: pick one.

Ranked-choice: rank them.

Approval: check all you accept.

Score: grade everyone.

STAR: grade everyone, and then a runoff between the top two.

[pause]

There is a pattern in that list.

> Every reform system lets you say something plurality cannot.

A backup choice. A boundary of acceptability. A grade. The systems differ in what extra information they collect, and in how the count uses it.

Now let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A voting system is not neutral plumbing. It is a choice about which information on the ballot counts, and how. In Burlington, and in our 100 ballots, no one miscounted, and the winner still changed with the rule.

[beat]

Two. Plurality asks for the most votes, not for a majority. Its costs are the spoiler effect, and Duverger's law: a counting rule, not any law about parties, sustains the two-party system.

[beat]

Three. The instant runoff keeps reading down the ballot. Eliminate, transfer, repeat. Who is eliminated early decides who survives to the end. And the winner's majority is a majority of the ballots still in play.

[beat]

Four. Where things stand, as of mid-2026. Plurality is the rule for federal elections and in most states. RCV is used in Maine, in Alaska and in dozens of localities, and it is banned in nineteen states. Approval survives in St. Louis. No U.S. government uses score or STAR.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Mechanics, locations, and one demonstration that counting rules change outcomes: that is all it claims.

It has not told you which differences matter most. Whether the spoiler effect is worse than ballot exhaustion. Whether expressiveness is worth strategic fragility. Whether Burlington was an indictment of the instant runoff, or a fluke. Or why a mathematically literate person might still prefer plurality's brutal simplicity.

"Which is best" is not even the right question yet. The right questions are these. Best at what? Under what conditions? And at what cost in voter effort and counting complexity?

[pause]

The next lecture takes those up. It starts with a theorem, one that proves no system can win on every count at once.

For now, go back to Burlington, and to that year of argument. Three candidates, each with a defensible claim on the same election. [beat] Nobody had miscounted. The answer depended on the rule.
