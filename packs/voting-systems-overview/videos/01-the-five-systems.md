## One election, three winners

On March 3, 2009, the voters of Burlington, Vermont elected a mayor, and then spent a year arguing about who had actually won. Count only first choices, and Kurt Wright led the field. Run the city's instant-runoff rules, and Bob Kiss won. Examine every head-to-head matchup in those same ballots, and Andy Montroll beat both of them, while finishing third.

- First choices: Wright
- Instant runoff: Kiss
- Head-to-head: Montroll

> A voting system is a choice about which information on the ballot counts.

## Pick one, or rank

Most American elections use plurality. The ballot asks you to pick one candidate, and whoever gets the most votes wins, even far short of 50 percent.

- Plurality: pick one

Ranked-choice voting asks you to rank the candidates. If nobody has a majority of first choices, the last-place candidate is eliminated, and each of those ballots transfers to its next choice. Repeat until someone holds a majority of the ballots still in play.

- Ranked choice: rank them
- Last place is eliminated; ballots transfer

## The leader who loses

Take 100 ballots. Brooks opens with 38 first choices, a comfortable win in a plurality count. But the other 62 ballots chain toward Avila as two trailing candidates are eliminated. Avila wins the final round, 62 to 38.

- Round one: Brooks leads with 38
- Final round: Avila 62, Brooks 38

> Plurality reads the top line. The instant runoff keeps reading down.

## Check every one you accept

Approval voting lists every candidate with a checkbox. Mark every one you find acceptable, with no ranking and no limit. Whoever collects the most checks wins. It is the smallest possible change to a plurality ballot.

- Approval: check all you accept

[check q1]

## Grade them

Score voting asks you to grade every candidate on a scale, the way you would rate a restaurant. A ranking gives your order. A score also says how much daylight you see between candidates. STAR voting starts with scores from 0 to 5, then holds an automatic runoff between the two highest scorers.

- Score: order, plus how much
- STAR: scores, then an automatic runoff

> Every reform system lets you say something plurality cannot.

## What the count does not settle

By one February 2026 count, ranked-choice voting is used in 49 American jurisdictions. And nineteen states have passed laws banning it. Which differences matter most is a separate question, and this lesson leaves it open.

- In use: 49 jurisdictions
- Banned: 19 states

The lesson lets you step through a ranked count round by round, counts one stack of ballots three ways, and shows where each system is used as of mid-2026. The next lesson weighs the tradeoffs.
