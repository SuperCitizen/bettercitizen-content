## The rule that does not exist

In 1948, a young economist at the RAND Corporation was handed a Cold War problem: how do millions of individual preferences combine into a collective one? Kenneth Arrow went looking for a rule that met a short list of reasonable-sounding conditions. He proved, instead, that no such rule exists.

- Kenneth Arrow, 1948

> The argument is not about the flawless system. It is about which flaws.

## What Arrow proved

Arrow considered systems that take voters' rankings of three or more candidates. He asked for four conditions. Voters may rank in any order. If everyone prefers A to B, the outcome agrees. How the group ranks A and B does not depend on a third candidate. And no single voter decides everything.

- Unrestricted domain
- Unanimity
- Independence of irrelevant alternatives
- Non-dictatorship

No ranked voting system can satisfy all four at once. The theorem does not say that all systems are equally flawed. They fail different conditions, at different frequencies.

## Every system can be gamed

A second theorem, from Allan Gibbard and Mark Satterthwaite, closed the loophole. Any deterministic voting rule that can elect three or more possible winners, and is not a dictatorship, is manipulable: there are situations where some voter does better by voting insincerely. So asking whether a system can be gamed is the wrong question. Ask how often, how much information the voter needs, and whether the strategic move is natural or exotic.

- How often?
- How much information?
- Natural, or exotic?

> Is this system gameable? They all are.

## The squeezed center

Back to Burlington in 2009. Andy Montroll won every head-to-head matchup, and finished third under the instant runoff. The mechanism is called center squeeze. A candidate between two larger blocs is everyone's acceptable second choice, and too few voters' first choice. So elimination removes the consensus candidate first. Plurality fails the same way.

- Center squeeze
- Head-to-head winner, eliminated first

> Every no on the scorecard is the price of a yes elsewhere.

## A values choice

The American record on the alternatives is real but short, so strong claims about turnout or polarization should carry uncertainty. And which property matters most is not a technical question. A voter who prizes a simple, checkable count, and a voter who prizes consensus winners, are each making a defensible values choice.

- A short record
- A values choice, not a math error
