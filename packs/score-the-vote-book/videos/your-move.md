## The soft last chapter

Every reform book ends the same way. A final chapter goes soft: raise awareness, have conversations, stay engaged. It is a refusal to say what the author wants the reader to do.

This is the last chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. So here is what I think.

The difference between Fargo in 2016 and Fargo in 2018 was one engineer from a city task force who declined to let the question die.

> The movement's binding constraint is not evidence, legality or money. It's protagonists.

## One afternoon

Before a dollar or a petition, the chapter asks three questions about where a reader lives. Is the city a home-rule or charter city? Does the state's election code leave room? Michigan's cities passed ranked-choice amendments five times and implemented none. And has the state already legislated?

- Home-rule or charter city?
- Room in the state election code?
- Has the state already legislated?

Missouri and North Dakota have closed off these methods. In the other seventeen ban states, nearly all the bans prohibit ranking, so an approval or score campaign is likely the only reform legally available.

## The first phone call

Fargo's path was one citizen initiative: 1,349 valid signatures required, 2,600 collected, and a win with 63.5 percent. Before any of it comes the unglamorous call, to the city clerk, who knows the equipment and the deadlines.

- First call: the city clerk

And the last step is a plan for defending the win.

> Fargo won the vote in 2018 and lost the statute in 2025.

## Smaller moves

Most readers will not lead a charter initiative this year, and pretending otherwise is how action chapters curdle into guilt. The smaller moves are real: use the method where you already hold elections, join the organizations, make candidates answer, teach the one-sentence pitch.

- Clubs, unions, congregations
- Ask the candidates

There is no commission coming and no inevitable arc. Strip away every statute and study, and one sentence is left.

> Your ballot should let you say what you actually think about every candidate.

The lesson has the petition steps to put in order, the Tennessee case worked through, and the two organizations by name.
