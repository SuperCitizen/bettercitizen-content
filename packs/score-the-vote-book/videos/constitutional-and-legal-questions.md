## The ghost of Duluth

In 1915, the Minnesota Supreme Court looked at a Duluth city election and threw the voting method out. The case is Brown v. Smallwood. It is still waved at every city council that considers letting voters say more than one thing on a ballot.

- Brown v. Smallwood, 1915

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. Is score voting legal? The short answer is yes. The short answer is also lazy.

## One person, one vote

The phrase comes from Gray v. Sanders in 1963, and Reynolds v. Sims extended it in 1964. Those cases are about the weight of ballots, never the format of ballots. No voter's ballot may count for more than another's. Nothing says each voter makes exactly one mark.

- Gray, 1963, and Reynolds, 1964

In 2011, in Dudum v. Arntz, the Ninth Circuit upheld San Francisco's ranked ballots on that logic. On a score ballot, every voter gets the same candidates and the same scale.

> One person, one vote governs a ballot's weight, not its format.

## What Brown still does

Brown read the Minnesota constitution. It binds no other state, and it has persuaded no federal court.

In 2009 the Minnesota Supreme Court upheld instant runoff in Minneapolis. It did not overrule Brown. It distinguished it: instant runoff counts one of your choices at a time, and Duluth's system counted later choices alongside first ones. Approval and score voting count all your marks at once.

- 2009: distinguished, not overruled

> Minnesota is hostile terrain for score voting. That is the whole concession.

## A statute, not a robe

The real threat is legislation. Beginning with Tennessee in 2022, nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting. Most define what they ban as ranking candidates, counted in elimination rounds. Approval and score ballots involve no ranking and no rounds.

- 19 states ban ranked-choice voting

Two bans reach further. Missouri's constitution now allows only "a single vote for each candidate or issue", with St. Louis grandfathered. North Dakota banned approval voting by name, to reach Fargo.

- Missouri and North Dakota: the two exceptions

So a legislature that wants these ballots gone can say so. Seventeen of the nineteen have not.
