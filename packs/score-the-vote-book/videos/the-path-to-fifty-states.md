## Back to pick-one

Two days before I wrote this chapter, Fargo held a city election the old way, for the first time since 2018. Fargoans had not changed their minds. The legislature took the method away in April 2025, overriding a system city voters adopted with 63.5 percent.

- Adopted by 63.5%, removed by statute

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. A chapter with this title owes you honesty about that.

## The map as it is

One American city, St. Louis, elects its government by approval voting. No state uses any method of this kind. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, and the backlash is organized and winning legislatures.

- Approval voting: 1 city
- Ranked-choice bans: 19 states

> Cardinal voting's foothold is one city wide.

## What the bans say

Now the detail that keeps me writing. Nearly every ban statute prohibits ranking. Approval and score ballots do no ranking. In seventeen of the nineteen ban states, the statutes left them untouched. The two exceptions are Missouri and North Dakota.

- 17 of 19 bans cover only ranking

> Closed to one reform is not closed to reform.

## Doors, in order

The realistic sequence starts with home-rule cities, the way Fargo and St. Louis happened. Before any campaign comes a check of the state election code: five Michigan cities voted for ranked-choice charters that state law keeps from operating. Then counties and special districts, then one initiative state, with a simple measure. Legislatures come last.

- Home-rule cities first
- Check the state election code
- Then counties, then an initiative state
- Legislatures last

The risk is real at the first door. North Dakota erased Fargo.

## A sober decade

The forecast I will defend is a foothold becoming a beachhead inside ten years, or the honest conclusion that voters looked and declined.

> Fifty states is the title's ambition, not a forecast.
