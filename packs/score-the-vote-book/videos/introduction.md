## One mark

You walk into a booth. You pick one name. You leave. That single mark is supposed to carry your entire political judgment, and it can't.

[beat]

This is the introduction to Score the Vote, a book by Chris Borgia. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

- Score the Vote: one author's argument

> A ballot that says one thing throws away nearly everything you know.

## Three ballots, one voter

Every voting method is a question put to you. The ballots differ in how much of your answer they can hear. Take one voter and three candidates: Avila, Brooks and Chen.

The pick-one ballot hears her favorite, Avila, and nothing else.

- Pick one: your favorite, nothing else

The ranked ballot hears an order: Avila, then Brooks, then Chen.

- Rank: order, but not distance

The score ballot gives every candidate a number. Avila 5, Brooks 4, Chen 0. The highest total wins, in one count.

- Score: how much, for everyone

Now go back to the ranked ballot. Is Brooks a near-twin she would cheerfully accept, or a barely tolerable hedge against Chen? The ranking cannot say.

> A ranking keeps the order. It loses the distance.

## The claim

Ranked choice voting is the reform with the headlines and the momentum. Its advocates are my allies against the pick-one ballot, and I will give them their due.

But this book makes a sharper claim. Compare the systems on how satisfied voters are across millions of simulated elections, on how each degrades when voters get tactical, and on how simply each one counts. On those measures, ballots that rate win.

> The claim of this book: rating beats ranking.

## Three concessions

When the strongest argument is against me, you will hear it from me first. So, three concessions, up front.

In 1973 the philosopher Allan Gibbard proved that every non-trivial voting method can be manipulated. Score voting is included.

- No method is strategy-proof

The real-world track record is short and bruised. Fargo's approval voting was banned by its state legislature in 2025.

- A short, bruised track record

And tactical voters compress score ballots toward approval ballots, the two-level kind.

- Tactics push score toward approval

The honest question is never "can it be gamed?" It is "what happens when it is?"

## Nothing settled yet

So far I have only asserted that the pick-one ballot destroys information, and that a better ballot exists. Assertions are cheap.

- Asserted so far, not proved
