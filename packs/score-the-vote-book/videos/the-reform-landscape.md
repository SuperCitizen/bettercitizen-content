## Fargo, overruled

On April 16, 2025, North Dakota's governor signed House Bill 1297. It erased a voting system that the people of Fargo had adopted by nearly two-thirds in 2018, and used without incident in three city elections. Fargo had not asked.

- Fargo: adopted 2018, erased 2025

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. Before I argue for one reform over the others, you need the whole board.

## One problem, several fixes

Every reform on the map attacks the same defect. The pick-one ballot takes one mark per voter and ignores everything else you think.

Ranked-choice asks you to rank the candidates. Approval lets you check everyone you accept. Score and STAR grade candidates on a scale. Other families change who can endorse, how many seats a district has, or when the field narrows.

- Ranked-choice: rank the candidates
- Approval: check everyone you accept
- Score and STAR: grade on a scale

Only two families have been winning American elections at scale: ranked ballots and approval ballots. Both are now in open warfare with state legislatures.

## Advance and rollback

By FairVote's February 2026 count, ranked-choice voting runs in 49 jurisdictions. Nineteen states have banned it outright. Alaska's 2024 repeal failed by 743 votes, and Alaska votes again in November 2026.

- Ranked-choice: 49 jurisdictions
- Banned in 19 states

Approval voting is smaller. Fargo's is gone. St. Louis adopted it in 2020 with 68 percent, and survived a statewide ban in Missouri that grandfathered the one city already using it.

- Approval: St. Louis only

And pure score voting has never reached an American government ballot. Score and STAR have zero public adoptions.

- Score and STAR: zero public adoptions

> The reform map is expansion and rollback at once.

## The beachhead argument

Now my strategic claim, stated plainly so you can hold me to it. Approval voting is score voting with the smallest possible scale: 0 or 1. That makes it the smallest change to the ballot any reform can propose. Same grid, same machines, same count, with exactly one rule deleted.

- Same grid, same machines, same count

> Approval voting deletes one rule: vote for only one.

The destination is the 0 to 10 ballot this book defends. The path, I argue, runs through approval.

## What is not settled

Cities do not control their own election law. States do. This chapter does not settle whether the rollback wave crests or keeps building. Nor does it settle whether the beachhead argument survives the objections: legal, strategic and psychological.

- States control city election law
- Not settled: does the rollback keep building?
