## Why not STAR?

In October 2014, a conference room at the University of Oregon put score voting's advocates and ranked-choice people together. Out of that collision came a new method, proposed by Mark Frohnmayer. Today it is called STAR: Score, Then Automatic Runoff.

- STAR: Score, Then Automatic Runoff

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. And STAR is the system I get asked about more than any other. If you like score voting, why not STAR?

## Two stages

You score every candidate from 0 to 5. All the scores are added up, and the two candidates with the highest totals become the finalists.

- Score every candidate, 0 to 5
- Top two totals advance

Then your ballot is consulted one more time. Your full vote goes to whichever finalist you scored higher. If you scored them equally, you abstain from the runoff. The finalist preferred on more ballots wins, even if the other had the higher score total.

- Runoff: your higher-scored finalist gets your vote

> Intensity picks the finalists. Head-to-head preference picks the winner.

## Why bolt on a runoff?

Because of the most serious objection to pure score voting. A strategic voter's best score ballot tends toward the extremes: maximum for your side, zero for the rest.

STAR changes that. Flatten your ballot to all 5s and 0s, and if both finalists got your 5, you have silenced yourself in the runoff.

- Flattened ballots forfeit the runoff

> The runoff makes honesty rational.

That is genuinely clever, and score voting's advocates should say so without mumbling.

## The record, with no varnish

As of mid-2026, no government anywhere elects its officials with STAR voting. Lane County, Oregon said no in 2018, 47.6 percent to 52.4. Eugene, the city where STAR was invented, said no in May 2024, roughly two to one. Oakridge said no that November, by 24 votes.

- Lane County 2018: 47.6% yes
- Eugene 2024: two to one against
- Oakridge 2024: lost by 24 votes

## My verdict, and its limit

So why does this book advocate score, not STAR? "Highest total wins" is one sentence. A STAR result requires explaining why the higher-scoring candidate can lose the runoff. And each increment of mechanism is a surface the opposition attacks.

- Score: one sentence to explain

But a STAR ballot is a score ballot, counted with one extra safeguard.

> My quarrel with STAR is strategic, not mathematical.

Not settled: whether the runoff's strategy-resistance is worth the explanatory overhead in a real public campaign.

- Not settled: is the runoff worth explaining?

The lesson has the trade-off no method escapes, set out in a table, and STAR's record on a timeline.
