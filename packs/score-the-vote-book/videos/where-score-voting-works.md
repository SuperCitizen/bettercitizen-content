## How the Security Council chooses

The United Nations Security Council is choosing the tenth Secretary-General, and it is not using pick-one ballots. In secret straw polls, its members rate every candidate: "encourage," "discourage," or "no opinion."

- Encourage, discourage, no opinion

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

> The Security Council's straw poll is a three-level score ballot.

## Not a theory in search of a trial

Ballots like that are called cardinal, and they are old. From 1268 until 1797, Venice chose its doge through a protocol that ended with 41 electors. Election required at least 25 approvals.

- Venice: 25 of 41 approvals

In June 2020, Fargo, North Dakota ran the first approval-voting election for public office in modern U.S. history. Voters approved an average of 2.3 candidates each. St. Louis followed, adopting Proposition D with 68 percent.

- Fargo 2020: 2.3 approvals per voter
- St. Louis: Proposition D, 68%

> Cardinal voting is not a theory in search of a trial.

## Adopted from below, removed from above

In 2025, North Dakota's House Bill 1297 banned approval voting statewide. No Fargo electorate ever voted to repeal it. In Missouri, voters passed Amendment 7 in November 2024, with 68.4 percent. It bars any new city from adopting approval voting, and grandfathers St. Louis.

- North Dakota: House Bill 1297, 2025
- Missouri: Amendment 7, 68.4%

> Fargo is gone. Missouri is constitutionally closed.

## Two readings of the backlash

The reform advocate argues that Fargo and St. Louis were laboratories of democracy: small, reversible experiments, adopted by supermajorities. The results came back positive, and the experiments were terminated from the top. This reading points to what is missing: a city electorate voting to go back.

- Reform advocate: experiments ended from the top

The status-quo defender argues that uniformity is election administration, not a conspiracy. Different rules on one ballot invite voter error, confusion and litigation. This reading points to Missouri's voters, who ratified Amendment 7 themselves, and to thin evidence: two cities, five years, a handful of races.

- Status-quo defender: one consistent set of rules

Both readings are honest, and I hold the first. Neither side disputes that where cardinal ballots were used, voters used them competently and kept them willingly.

> The contest is over who gets to decide.

## What two cities cannot answer

Not settled here: whether score voting picks better winners than ranked-choice voting. Two cities and a diplomatic ritual can't answer that.

- Not settled: does it pick better winners?
