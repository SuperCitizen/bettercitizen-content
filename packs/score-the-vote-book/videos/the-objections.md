## A hand goes up

Every talk I give ends the same way. A hand goes up, and out comes one of five objections. All five are good. Three I can answer outright. Two I have to concede before I can answer.

- Five objections, two conceded

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

## The court case from 1915

The objection: courts already killed this. In Brown v. Smallwood, in 1915, Minnesota's supreme court struck down a Duluth ballot that added voters' second choices into the totals.

I concede that in Minnesota this precedent is alive. But Brown read the Minnesota constitution. It binds no other state and no federal court. And in 2009 Minnesota's own supreme court confined it, while upholding ranked ballots in Minneapolis.

- A litigation risk in one state

## Every score a 10 or a 0

The objection: with any sense of who the front-runners are, your best ballot is top marks for your side and zeros for the rest. The nuance is a mirage.

I concede the theory, without wriggling. An electorate of perfect strategists makes score voting behave like approval voting. Now look at what that is: a spoiler-proof system, good enough to anchor St. Louis's elections. Plurality's strategic end point is the one you live in: abandon your favorite, vote for the lesser evil.

- Strategy's floor: approval voting

> Score voting's worst case under strategy is another good voting system.

## The majority's favorite can lose

The objection: legitimacy means majority rule. I concede this one too: score voting fails the majority criterion. One hundred voters. 51 score A a 10 and B an 8. 49 score B a 10 and A a 0. A totals 510. B totals 898. B wins.

- A: 510
- B: 898

The majority barely prefers A. The minority regards A as a catastrophe. Electing B puts greatest total satisfaction ahead of the majority's favorite. This book makes that choice with open eyes, and you may weigh those values differently.

> Utilitarian or majoritarian: a disagreement about values, not arithmetic.

## Still open

No American government has yet run a public score-voting election. So the strategy concession rests on theory and simulation.

- No public score election yet

The lesson also takes the other two objections, one person, one vote and whether voters can handle a zero-to-ten ballot, and tests the claim that rival systems guarantee a majority.
