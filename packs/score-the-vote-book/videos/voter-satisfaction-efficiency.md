## A question that sounds unanswerable

Across every election that could ever happen, which voting method picks the best available candidate most often? You can't run that experiment on real electorates. You would need to rerun the same voters through five systems, and see into their minds.

- Same voters, five systems: impossible

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

## Build the voters

So researchers build the voters. They simulate an electorate whose preferences they know exactly, because they wrote them. Then they run the same voters through every method, thousands of times.

In each election, one candidate's victory would maximize the voters' total satisfaction. A method that always elects that candidate scores 100 percent. A method that picks winners at random scores 0.

- 100%: always the ideal winner
- 0%: a winner picked at random

> Voter satisfaction efficiency, VSE: the share of that gap a method closes.

## The numbers, with nothing airbrushed

The standard study was published in 2017 by Jameson Quinn. With honest voters, STAR scores about 98 percent, score voting about 96, and instant runoff about 92. Plurality scores 75, about 20 points under that cluster.

- STAR: about 98%
- Score: about 96%
- Instant runoff: about 92%
- Plurality: 75%

Now a correction aimed at my own side. Some advocates claim score voting holds 95-plus percent even with strategic voters. Quinn's numbers do not say that. When everyone strategizes, score falls to about 84. Approval stays between 89 and 95 across strategy mixes.

> Score voting: 96 with honest voters, 84 when everyone strategizes.

A fully strategic score ballot has no 3s on it. It is an approval ballot, and that floor is high.

## What a model cannot tell you

These numbers are model outputs, and models have authors. Change the voter distribution or the number of candidates, and the top methods reshuffle.

- Top methods reshuffle between models

The measure also builds in a moral assumption: the right winner maximizes average satisfaction across all voters. If you believe a bare majority's mild preference should always win, you are disputing the target, not the simulations.

- Assumes: maximize average satisfaction

What never reshuffles, across two researchers, seventeen years apart, with independent code? Plurality at the bottom, elimination-based ranking in the middle, cardinal methods at the top.

> A one-point gap is noise. The twenty-point gap is the signal.
