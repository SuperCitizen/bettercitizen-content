## The governor most voters opposed

In November 2010, Maine elected a governor whom 62 percent of its voters had just voted against. Paul LePage won with 37.6 percent. Two center-left candidates, Eliot Cutler and Libby Mitchell, split 54.7 percent between them.

- LePage: 37.6%, the winner
- Cutler and Mitchell: 54.7% together

Nobody cheated. Nobody miscounted. Pick one, most votes wins: that rule is called plurality.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

> The system performed exactly as designed, and that is the indictment.

## A ballot that deletes its evidence

Florida, 2000. George W. Bush beat Al Gore by 537 votes, with the presidency attached. Ralph Nader received 97,488 votes there.

- Margin: 537 votes
- Nader: 97,488 votes

Did Nader cost Gore the White House? I won't tell you so, not in that simple form. Estimates based on exit polls suggested Nader's voters leaned toward Gore, roughly two to one. Skeptics answer that roughly 308,000 registered Democrats in Florida voted for Bush.

People still argue, because the ballot recorded every first choice and destroyed every second choice.

> A spoiler debate is an electoral system deleting its own evidence.

## Duverger's law

In 1951 the French political scientist Maurice Duverger named what plurality does over time. It manufactures two-party systems, through two effects.

The mechanical effect is arithmetic. A party winning 15 percent everywhere wins zero seats anywhere.

- Mechanical: 15% everywhere, zero seats

The psychological effect is anticipation. Voters who can count abandon their sincere favorite in advance, to avoid wasting their vote.

- Psychological: voters defect in advance

Voting theory calls the defect favorite betrayal. Supporting your sincere favorite can help elect the candidate you most fear.

- Favorite betrayal

> The two-party stranglehold is downstream of the pick-one rule.

## What this does not settle

The law has a limit. Regionally concentrated parties can survive it, which is why Canada and the United Kingdom still seat third parties.

And none of this settles what should replace plurality. Maine's answer, in 2016, was ranked-choice voting. I will argue that Maine reached for the second-best tool. On the diagnosis, ranked-choice advocates, STAR advocates and I agree.

- Agreed: the diagnosis
- Not settled: the replacement
