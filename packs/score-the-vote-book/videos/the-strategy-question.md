## The movement's worst night

On the night of November 5, 2024, the election-reform movement lost everywhere at once. Colorado, Idaho, Oregon and Nevada all said no. Missouri wrote a ban into its constitution.

- November 5, 2024

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

> Strategy is the discipline of taking the map as it is.

## Can voters game it?

Won't people just max their favorite and zero everyone else? Some will. A theorem proved by Allan Gibbard in 1973 and Mark Satterthwaite in 1975 establishes that no reasonable voting rule is immune to strategic voting.

- No voting rule is immune

A voter who plays maximum hardball has turned a score ballot into an approval ballot.

> Score voting's worst case is its own simpler sibling: approval voting.

## The family argument

Those who want ballots to rate candidates hold three positions on strategy.

Approval-first is the Center for Election Science's position. It argues for winning with the simplest possible reform. It points to approval voting's record: Fargo adopted it with 63.5 percent, and St. Louis with 68.

- Approval-first: simplest reform, a real record

STAR-first is the Equal Vote Coalition's position. It argues that approval makes voters draw one crude line, and that approving a second choice can hurt a favorite. It points to STAR's automatic runoff, where honest scores can't betray you.

- STAR-first: scores, then an automatic runoff

Score now is this book's position. It argues that the destination is a 0 to 10 scale. It points to what approval voting is: score voting on a two-point scale, so every approval adoption adopts the principle.

- Score now: approval adopts the principle

## Compete with ranked-choice, or coexist?

FairVote, which stands behind ranked-choice voting, has a standing critique. Approving your second choice can defeat your first, so voters learn to mark only their favorite, and the method slides back toward plurality. It is the best argument they have.

- FairVote: approval slides toward plurality

In Seattle, in 2022, voters chose between the two reforms. Ranked-choice won, 75.4 to 24.6. I won't spin that.

- Seattle 2022: ranked-choice 75.4, approval 24.6

My answer is lanes. Where ranked-choice voting is alive, advocates of rating ballots should be allies. Where states have outlawed ranking, ranked-choice cannot run, while approval voting remains legal.

> Anything is better than plurality.

## What strategy cannot settle

The scoreboard says go local, go simple, go where the law is open. It does not say whether score voting's promised gains are real and measurable.

- Not settled: is the win worth having?
