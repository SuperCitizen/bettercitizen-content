## Elected with 16 percent

In 2016, eleven candidates ran for two seats on Fargo's city commission. The winners took 16 and 15 percent. A city task force studied the alternatives and recommended approval voting. The commission did nothing.

- Winners: 16% and 15%

An engineer from that task force, Jed Limke, founded a group called Reform Fargo and collected 2,600 signatures. In November 2018 the measure passed with 63.5 percent.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

## The anatomy of a win

Strip Fargo and St. Louis down to mechanism and four lessons fall out. The trigger was a failure people could see, not an abstract argument. The messenger was local and the expertise was imported, from the Center for Election Science. It cost less than you fear, and more than zero: Fargo needed 1,349 valid signatures, and about forty volunteers collected roughly double.

- A failure people can see
- Local messenger, imported expertise
- About double the signatures required

The fourth lesson is the hard one. Fargo's reform survived a 2023 repeal bill only because the governor vetoed it. In 2025 a new legislature and governor finished the job.

- A defense plan

> Winning is the start of the fight, not the end.

## Friendly fire

The movement's deadliest failure is not the two-party system. In Seattle in 2022 an approval initiative qualified, the council countered with ranked-choice, and approval lost the head-to-head, 75.4 to 24.6.

- Seattle 2022: 75.4 to 24.6

So I would write three rules into any organization's bylaws. Do not poach ground where ranked-choice is organized. Never co-sign a ban on a rival reform. Keep the comparison honest.

> Never co-sign a ban.

## What is not settled

The combination has won in two American cities, and nothing else has won even once. That does not settle scale.

> Two cities in eight years is an existence proof, not a movement.
