## 156 percent

On the night of March 2, 2021, St. Louis newsrooms reported a mayoral primary. Tishaura Jones, 57 percent. Cara Spencer, 46. Lewis Reed, 39. Andrew Jones, 14. The numbers summed to 156 percent.

- 57 + 46 + 39 + 14 = 156%

The explanation took one sentence: voters could approve of as many candidates as they liked. The city went to bed knowing who had advanced.

> Each percentage is the share of voters who approved that candidate.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

## The other first night

Three months later, New York City ran its first ranked-choice mayoral primary. The Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that included about 135,000 test ballots, and retracted it the same evening.

The error was human, not mathematical. Ranked ballots did not miscount anything; officials did. But one method produced a story an anchor could tell in a sentence, and the other produced days of tabulation drama.

- New York: about 135,000 test ballots
- St. Louis: totals the same night

## Our weakness

The diagnosis cuts against us before it cuts for us. Political journalism runs on the horse race, and ranked-choice voting is great copy: elimination rounds, transfers, leads that shrink. Score voting offers nothing like that. Highest average wins is the whole story, so there is no free publicity attached to the method.

- No rounds, no drama, no free publicity

The same dullness starves our opponents of chaos stories. Nobody has ever waited days for an approval-voting result.

> Score voting trades free drama for invulnerability to chaos coverage.

## Reach, not comprehension

Most Americans have never heard of score voting. The method takes one breath to teach: rate every candidate, zero to ten, like you would rate a restaurant. Highest average wins.

> The gap is a reach problem, not a comprehension problem.

What is not settled is whether that fluency can stand in for the awareness ranked-choice has bought with three decades of organizing.

The lesson has the Fargo and St. Louis usage numbers, the objection that star ratings get gamed, and the polling gap that campaigns in these cities still face.
