## Getting primaried

Ask a member of Congress what they are afraid of, and the honest ones do not say losing in November. They say getting primaried.

- The fear is the primary

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

## The machine

In 2022, roughly 21 percent of eligible voters took part in the primaries. One analysis found 83 percent of U.S. House seats so safe that the primary was the election. Put those together: about 8 percent of Americans effectively chose 83 percent of the House.

- Primary turnout: about 21%
- Safe House seats: 83%
- Chosen by about 8% of Americans

The primary is also counted by plurality. In a crowded field, a candidate with a devoted 30 percent faction beats five rivals splitting the other 70.

I will not caricature the voters. The best survey research finds primary voters similar to their party's rank and file.

> The machine's problem is arithmetic, not monstrous voters.

## St. Louis runs it backwards

St. Louis passed Proposition D with 68 percent in November 2020. All candidates appear on one March ballot. Every voter may approve as many as they want. The top two advance to an April runoff that the whole city decides.

- Approve as many as you want
- Top two go to a runoff

In 2021 Tishaura Jones won that runoff. In 2025 she stood for reelection, and 33.2 percent of March ballots approved her, against 68.2 for Cara Spencer. Spencer won the runoff.

> The same system that elected the mayor removed her.

## What three cycles cannot show

I will be careful here. The published research on how St. Louis candidates behave is still thin. And this is one heavily one-party city: three cycles is a record, not a literature.

- Three cycles, one city

The lesson walks the machine part by part, tests whether higher turnout alone would fix it, and sets St. Louis beside Alaska's top-four primary, where each voter still marks only one name.
