## A count nobody could check

On June 29, 2021, New York City's Board of Elections published the first preliminary ranked-choice tabulation of its mayoral primary. Then it un-published it. Roughly 135,000 test ballots had been counted along with the real ones.

- About 135,000 test ballots counted

The blunder was the Board's, not the ballot's. But in an instant-runoff count, every round depends on the round before. The corrected count had to be rerun from scratch.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author.

> Every voting system pays a complexity tax.

## The napkin test

Here is score voting's complete instruction set. Score every candidate from 0 to 5. Highest total wins. Approval voting's is shorter. Vote for every candidate you approve of. Most votes wins.

- Score: rate everyone, highest total wins
- Approval: mark all you approve

The ranked-choice instructions need rules for ranking, for elimination and for transfers, repeated until someone has a majority of the ballots still active. Most voters can fill out a ranked ballot. Far fewer can explain how the winner is determined.

Under the word count sits a structural difference. Score and approval are precinct-summable. Every precinct posts its totals, and anyone can add them. Instant runoff is not: no precinct can run an elimination until every ballot everywhere is in.

- Precinct-summable: anyone can add the totals

> With score and approval, the addition is the election.

## The error evidence, both directions

Stephen Pettigrew and Dylan Radley compared the same voters on the same physical ballots. Overvote rates ran 0.60 percent in ranked-choice races, against 0.04 percent in the races beside them: fifteen times higher.

- Ranked-choice races: 0.60% overvotes
- Other races, same ballots: 0.04%

Now the other side, at full strength. Critics of that study answer that most mismarks never void a ballot. They answer that fifteen times a small number is still a small number, and that error rates fall as electorates gain experience. All fair.

- Critics: most mismarks still count
- Critics: small numbers, falling with experience

What survives, I argue, is the asymmetry. And in an approval race, the overvote is impossible by definition: marking many candidates is the instruction.

## What structure cannot prove

I'll concede this. Two cities and a few cycles cannot support an error-rate literature for approval voting.

> The approval claim is structural, not yet statistical.

Not settled: whether simplicity should win, or whether rankings buy enough expressive value to justify their complexity tax.

- Not settled: should simplicity win?
