# The Media and Education Challenge

On the night of March 2, 2021, St. Louis newsrooms reported a set of election results that should have broken America's brains: Tishaura Jones, 57 percent. Cara Spencer, 46 percent. Lewis Reed, 39 percent. Andrew Jones, 14 percent. The numbers summed to 156 percent, the explanation took one sentence — voters could approve of as many candidates as they liked — and the city went to bed knowing exactly who had advanced to the April runoff. Same night. No rounds. No lawyers.

Three months later, New York City ran its first ranked-choice mayoral primary, and the Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that accidentally included about 135,000 *test ballots*, retracted it the same evening, and handed every enemy of election reform a news cycle they still quote. The error was human, not mathematical — ranked ballots didn't miscount anything; officials did. But the lesson for this chapter is about something else: one method produced a story a local anchor could tell in a sentence, and the other produced a multi-day tabulation drama with a built-in audience for failure. Media is not a footnote to reform strategy. For a movement trying to teach the public a new way to vote, media *is* the strategy.

## The horse-race problem: our weakness is our weakness

Let me state the diagnosis honestly, because it cuts against us before it cuts for us. Political journalism runs on the horse race — who's up, who's down, what just changed. Ranked-choice voting, whatever its flaws, is *great copy*: elimination rounds, transferred ballots, leads that shrink as the count proceeds, a week of "will it flip?" coverage. Alaska's 2022 special election got wall-to-wall national attention partly because the count itself was a cliffhanger. Score voting offers journalism nothing like that. "Highest average wins" is the whole story; the result arrives finished, like a box score. There is no round-by-round drama to narrate, which means there is no free publicity machine attached to the method.

That's the real reason cardinal voting trails [[term:ranked-choice-voting]] in coverage, and I'd rather name it than whine about bias. The flip side is what Fargo and St. Louis demonstrated: the same dullness that starves us of drama also starves our opponents of chaos narratives. Nobody has ever waited days for an approval-voting result. Nobody ever will, because the tabulation is addition — precinct counts sum like any other election, on the equipment cities already own. When the *New York Post* needed a ranked-choice fiasco, New York provided one; when opponents needed an approval-voting fiasco, Fargo gave them an election so boring it produced one good local headline about arithmetic. In a political environment where "too confusing" killed reform measures across four states in November 2024, boring is not a bug. Boring is the moat.

  Each percentage is the share of *voters* who approved that candidate, not a share of a fixed pie of votes — 57 percent of voters marked Jones, 46 percent marked Spencer, and many marked both. ## Two first nights, one teaching record

Education was supposed to be the killer objection — a new method, surely, means confused voters and spoiled ballots. Here's what the record actually shows from the only two American cities to try.

Fargo, June 9, 2020: the first approval-voting election in U.S. history, a seven-candidate city commission race for two seats. The instruction fit on a bumper sticker — *vote for all the candidates you approve of* — and voters used it: an average of 2.3 approvals per ballot, with results out election night. St. Louis, March 2021: 44,571 voters cast 69,661 approvals in the mayoral primary, 1.6 per ballot. Voters facing the method for the first time, in the middle of a pandemic, did not bullet-vote en masse and did not melt down; a majority used the new freedom immediately. By the 2025 mayoral primary the average was 1.4 approvals per ballot in a four-way race — lower, as you'd expect when one candidate (Spencer, at 68.2 percent approval) is consolidating support, and still well above the 1.0 that the "everyone will just bullet-vote" objection predicts.

Read that figure the way a newsroom would. It isn't just a result; it's a *diagnosis* of the whole field — every candidate's true breadth of support, in units (percent approval) that every American already knows how to read. Numbers from a [[term:plurality]] count can't say this: under pick-one rules, Reed's 39 percent of voters would have been invisible, split and buried inside other candidates' totals. And the diagnosis has predictive teeth. In the March 2025 primary, the incumbent mayor's approval came in at 33.2 percent against challenger Spencer's 68.2; five weeks later Spencer won the runoff with about 64 percent. The primary didn't just pick finalists. It published the incumbent's actual standing with her own electorate, and the general election confirmed it almost exactly.

## The thirty-second pitch and the fluency we already have

The education gap is real — most Americans have never heard of [[term:score-voting]], and any awareness of reform that does exist mostly attaches to ranked-choice. But notice what kind of problem that is. It is not a comprehension problem; it is a *reach* problem. The method itself requires no teaching beyond one breath: *Rate every candidate, zero to ten, like you'd rate a restaurant. Highest average wins.* There is no American adult for whom that sentence demands new concepts. We rate films, doctors, drivers, headphones, and hotel rooms; Olympic judging has used score ballots for a century; every app on your phone asks for stars. The cultural fluency that ranked-choice campaigns spend millions building — *here's how transfers work, here's why round three differs from round one* — score voting gets free, because the scoreboard idiom is already installed in every voter.

I'll concede the counterargument, because it's coming: "We've all seen star ratings gamed — review bombs, fake five-stars." True, and instructive. Online ratings are gameable because anyone can rate many times under many names. An election has one authenticated ballot per registered voter; the abuse vector isn't there. If anything, the analogy runs the other way: plurality voting is the system that behaves like a review bomb, forcing every voter to dump their entire judgment into a single all-or-nothing mark. Score voting is what honest rating looks like with the fraud problem already solved by voter registration.

The harder operational gap is polling. Pollsters know how to ask "whom will you vote for?"; an approval or score race needs "which candidates will you approve?" — and in St. Louis's first cycle, public polling was thin and mostly fell back on head-to-head matchups. That's a craft that will mature with adoption, as ranked-choice polling slowly has. Until then, campaigns in cardinal cities fly with less instrumentation than they're used to — a real cost, and one more reason the education work can't wait for the first big statewide fight.

## What this chapter settles, and what it can't

Settled: the explainability case is no longer hypothetical. Two cities ran first elections under a brand-new method with one-sentence instructions, same-night results, multi-approval voting from the start, and zero tabulation controversies — while the most-watched debut of the rival reform handed its opponents a gift. The media diagnosis is also clear: score voting trades free drama for invulnerability to chaos coverage, and after 2024 I'll take that trade every time.

Not settled: whether earned media and cultural fluency can substitute for the awareness that ranked-choice has bought with three decades of organizing — reach, not comprehension, remains the binding constraint. That is a movement-building problem, and the chapters on building the movement and the path to fifty states take it head-on. First, though, we have to talk about money.
