# Constitutional and Legal Questions

In 1915, the Minnesota Supreme Court looked at a Duluth municipal election and threw the voting method out. Duluth had adopted Bucklin voting — first choice, second choice, then as many additional choices as you liked — and the court declared it incompatible with the state constitution: "Preferential voting directly diminishes the right of a voter to cast an effective vote for the candidate of his choice." That case, *Brown v. Smallwood*, is now a hundred and eleven years old. It concerned a voting method nobody uses, under one state's constitution, over a dissent that called the majority's logic wrong on its face. And it is still the single most reliable scare cite in American election reform — waved at every city council that considers letting voters say more than one thing on a ballot.

So let's do this properly. Is score voting legal? The short answer is yes: the Constitution leaves the manner of elections overwhelmingly to states and, through them, to charter cities, and nothing in it requires [[term:plurality]] voting. But the short answer is lazy, and the people who oppose reform are not lazy. This chapter walks the actual legal terrain: what "one person, one vote" really requires, what *Brown v. Smallwood* really held, and where the genuine legal threat to cardinal voting lives. Spoiler: it doesn't wear a robe. It wears a statute.

## One person, one vote — what the doctrine actually demands

Start with the phrase opponents reach for first. "One person, one vote" comes from *Gray v. Sanders* (1963), where the Supreme Court struck down Georgia's county-unit system — a scheme that made a rural vote worth many times an Atlanta vote in statewide primaries. *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) extended the principle to legislative districts: districts must be roughly equal in population, because, in Chief Justice Warren's words, "legislators represent people, not trees or acres." Read those cases and notice what they are about: the *weight* of ballots, never the *format* of ballots. The constitutional command is that no voter's ballot count for more than another's — not that each voter make exactly one mark.

The modern test of that distinction is *Dudum v. Arntz* (9th Cir. 2011), where San Francisco voters challenged the city's ranked-choice system on precisely this theory. Judge Marsha Berzon's opinion dismantled it: "Each ballot is counted as no more than one vote at each tabulation step," and every vote is "afforded the same mathematical weight in the election." Ranking candidates isn't extra voting power, the court held, because every voter has the identical option to do it.

Now run approval and score voting through that logic. Under [[term:score-voting]], every voter receives the same ballot, the same candidates, the same scale. A voter who scores five candidates hasn't outvoted a voter who scores one — each has simply used more or less of an identical allotment, and in the final arithmetic no ballot contributes more than the maximum score to any candidate. The Olympic-judging analogy holds: a judge who rates every skater hasn't voted more times than a judge who rates only her favorite. She's done the same job more completely. The guarantee of [[term:equal-protection]], as the courts have construed it from *Gray* through *Dudum*, asks whether voters are treated equally. It does not ration ink.

<Checkpoint question="A skeptic says: 'My neighbor approved four candidates and I approved one, so she got four votes and I got one.' Using the Dudum logic, what's the precise flaw in that claim?">
  Weight, not marks, is what the doctrine measures. The neighbor added one increment to four different candidates' totals — an option the skeptic had on the very same ballot and declined to use. In any head-to-head comparison between two candidates, each ballot still moves the needle by at most one vote. *Dudum* called the equivalent objection to ranked ballots a confusion between "the option to rank multiple preferences" and "more heavily-weighted votes." The option is equal; therefore the power is equal.
</Checkpoint>

## The ghost of Duluth: what *Brown v. Smallwood* actually held

Honest advocacy means taking the opposition's best card seriously, so here is the whole of it. Duluth's Bucklin system counted a voter's later choices *into the same totals* as first choices: 12,313 people voted, and 18,860 marks figured in the count. "It was not a voting of man against man," the court wrote, and held that a vote "must be counted one, and it cannot be defeated or its effect lessened, except by the vote of another elector voting for one." Justice Hallam's dissent made the obvious reply — no voter had cast more than one vote *for any candidate* — and on rehearing the majority all but conceded it was outvoting the legal profession, then reaffirmed anyway.

What happened to *Brown* since matters more than *Brown* itself. In 2009, the Minnesota Supreme Court — in *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis* — unanimously upheld Minneapolis's instant-runoff system. But note carefully how it did so: it *distinguished* *Brown* rather than overruling it, reasoning that instant-runoff counts only one of your choices at any moment, while Bucklin counted later choices simultaneously alongside first choices. That narrow reading rescued ranked-choice voting in Minnesota. It did nothing for methods that count all your marks at once — which is exactly what approval and score voting do.

So I'll say plainly what some advocates won't: Minnesota is hostile terrain for cardinal voting, and will stay that way until its supreme court revisits a 1915 case or its voters amend their constitution. That is the full extent of the concession. *Brown* construed the Minnesota constitution, binds no other state, has persuaded no federal court, and stands against the great weight of modern doctrine, which — per *Gray*, *Reynolds*, and *Dudum* — measures equality by weight and opportunity. Outside one state, the ghost has no jurisdiction.

## The real threat wears a statute, not a robe

Here's the part of the legal landscape that should actually concern you, and the opening hiding inside it. Beginning with Tennessee in February 2022, nineteen states have enacted bans on ranked-choice voting — Florida later in 2022; Idaho, South Dakota, and Montana in 2023; six states including Missouri in 2024; six more in 2025; Indiana and Ohio by March 2026. That is a political fact of the first order, and the next chapters deal with it as one. But read the statutes the way a city attorney would, because the drafting matters enormously.

| Enactment | What the text prohibits | Reaches approval/score? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tennessee, § 2-8-117 (2022) | Methods in which "voters rank candidates in order of preference," tabulated in elimination rounds with vote transfers | No — no ranking, no rounds |
| Florida, § 101.019 (2022) | Ranking "in order of preference" with multi-round elimination until a majority | No — same definitional shape |
| Ohio, SB 63 (2026) | "Ranked choice voting or instant runoff voting," enforced by withholding Local Government Fund money from cities that use it | No — names ranking methods only |
| Missouri, Amendment 7 (2024) | Constitutional: voters "shall have only a single vote for each candidate or issue"; ranking prohibited; plurality winners required | **Yes** — but St. Louis's system is expressly grandfathered |
| North Dakota, HB 1297 (2025) | "Approval voting and ranked-choice voting," by name, with home-rule authority amended to match | **Yes** — drafted specifically to reach Fargo |

The pattern in that table is the strategic map. Most of the ban wave defines its target as *ranking* — voters ordering candidates by preference, counted in elimination rounds. Approval voting involves no ranking and no rounds; a 0-to-5 score ballot involves no ranking and no rounds. In most ban states, cardinal methods are not a loophole in the statute; they are simply not the thing the statute bans. A legislature that wanted to forbid them must say so separately — which is precisely what the two exceptions prove. Missouri's Amendment 7 had to reach for "a single vote for each candidate or issue" to catch approval voting, and even then its drafters carved out nonpartisan municipal systems already in effect on November 5, 2024 — a clause written around St. Louis, which keeps its approval voting to this day. And North Dakota's legislature, having watched its own ranked-choice language fail to touch Fargo, came back in 2025 with a bill that named approval voting explicitly — after Governor Doug Burgum had vetoed the 2023 attempt as one that "blatantly infringes on local control," a veto the Senate sustained 28–19 before the 2025 version (75–16 in the House, 38–8 in the Senate) finally passed under his successor.

Two honest readings follow. The discouraging one: where a legislature truly wants cardinal voting gone, it can write the three extra words, and North Dakota shows the home-rule shield failing against a determined statehouse. The encouraging one: as of mid-2026, seventeen of the nineteen ban states have not written those words — and in every one of them, approval voting is the only major reform still standing on legal ground.

## Home rule: the front door, and its limits

Both American cities that have used approval voting got it the same way: a home-rule charter plus a citizen [[term:ballot-initiative]]. Fargo amended its charter in 2018; St. Louis passed Proposition D in 2020. Charter authority is the front door for cardinal reform — but it has two locks, and you need both opened. First, your state must actually grant charter cities power over their election *method*. Second, the state's election code must not occupy the field anyway. Michigan is the cautionary tale on the second lock: Ferndale voted for ranked-choice voting in 2004, Ann Arbor in 2021, East Lansing, Kalamazoo, and Royal Oak in 2023 — five cities, every measure passed, zero implementations, because state election law leaves no room for the charters to operate. Adoption is not implementation. Read your state's election code before you celebrate your charter.

One more practical point, because city attorneys ask it immediately: implementation. Approval voting runs on standard optical-scan equipment — it is the familiar "vote for one" ballot with the limit removed, and tabulation is addition. Fargo and St. Louis both ran it on the machines they already owned. A multi-level score ballot (0–5, say) is also optical-scan-friendly — STAR advocates have certified-ready ballot designs — but I'll be straight with you: no American public election has yet run one, so equipment certification for full score voting remains a box nobody has had to check. Approval's box is checked, twice.

## What's settled, and what the next fight looks like

Settled, as much as law ever is: there is no federal constitutional problem with cardinal voting that any court has identified — no judge has ever struck down approval or score voting, and in Fargo and St. Louis nobody so much as brought a serious case. The attacks on approval voting in both cities came through legislatures, not courtrooms, and that tells you where the opposition thinks its odds are. The "one person, one vote" objection fails on the doctrine's own terms. And the ban wave, for all its breadth, mostly prohibits ranking — leaving the cardinal lane legally open in the large majority of states.

Not settled: Minnesota, until *Brown* is confronted directly. Whether more legislatures will follow North Dakota's drafting rather than Tennessee's. And the question no court has yet faced because no state has yet forced it — the test case that will come the first time a cardinal method wins something bigger than a city. When that case comes, it will be argued on *Gray*, *Reynolds*, and *Dudum*, and I like those cards. The objections you'll hear before then aren't legal ones, and they get the next chapter to themselves.
