# Building the Movement

In 2015, a Fargo city commission seat went to a candidate with 22 percent of the vote in a six-way field. The next year was worse: eleven candidates ran for two seats, and the winners took 16 and 15 percent — commissioners "elected" by roughly one voter in six. Embarrassed, the city commission created an Election and City Governance Task Force in late 2016 to figure out what was wrong. The task force held ten public meetings, studied the alternatives, and recommended approval voting. The commission received the recommendation and did precisely nothing.

That should have been the end of the story, because that's where reform recommendations go to die. Instead, an engineer from the task force named Jed Limke founded a group called Reform Fargo, recruited a volunteer corps, collected 2,600 signatures to put the question to voters directly, and in November 2018 watched Measure 1 pass 30,092 to 17,282 — 63.5 percent. No statewide organization ordered this. No billionaire seeded it. A city had a visible, undeniable [[term:plurality]] failure, one credible local person refused to drop the subject, and a national nonprofit showed up with expertise at exactly the right moment. That's the anatomy of every cardinal-voting win to date, and this chapter is about deliberately reproducing it.

## The organizations: who actually does what

A movement is not a vibe; it's a small number of organizations with budgets, and you should know them by name and function.

**The Center for Election Science** is the approval-voting organization — founded in 2011 by Clay Shentrup, Aaron Hamlin, and Warren D. Smith, professionalized by Open Philanthropy money ($598,600 in late 2017, then $1.8 million in 2019), and led since January 2024 by CEO Nina Taylor. CES is the movement's campaign-support shop: it backed Reform Fargo in 2018, supported the STL Approves coalition that passed Proposition D in 2020 with 68.15 percent, seeded exploratory groups in several metros, and advised the Utah Forward Party's December 2025 approval-voting vacancy election. If your city is going to run an approval campaign, CES is the phone call.

**The Equal Vote Coalition** is the STAR-voting organization — grown out of Mark Frohnmayer's 2014 Unified Primary campaign in Oregon, incorporated as a nonprofit in 2019, with a separate 501(c)(4), STAR Voting Action, built to run campaigns. Equal Vote is the movement's R&D lab and standards body: ballot designs, tabulation tools, the "equality criterion" framework for comparing methods. Its campaign record is losses — most painfully Eugene's Measure 20-349 in May 2024 — but its infrastructure and volunteer chapters are real assets the whole cardinal family draws on.

**[[org:fairvote]]** is the incumbent: three decades old, the engine behind ranked-choice voting's spread, and — let's be adults — a critic of approval voting, on later-no-harm grounds, not an ally of convenience. Around it sits the broader democracy-reform money: [[org:unite-america]] and [[org:representus]] fund campaigns for primary and voting reforms generally (RepresentUs backed Nevada's final-five effort), and groups like the [[org:league-of-women-voters]] supply the local credibility that no national org can — the St. Louis League's endorsement of Proposition D mattered more than any white paper.

## What Fargo and St. Louis actually teach

Strip the two wins down to mechanism and four lessons fall out.

**First: the trigger was a legible failure, not an abstract argument.** Fargo organized around "our commissioners win with 16 percent." St. Louis organized around decades of crowded, sub-majority Democratic primaries deciding the city's real elections. Find the number in your city that makes people wince; the theory chapter comes later.

**Second: the messenger was local and the expertise was imported.** Limke was a Fargoan who'd served on the city's own task force; STL Approves was St. Louis organizers with allies like Show Me Integrity and the League, while CES supplied method expertise and resources. Reverse the roles — national face, local hired help — and you get the pattern that lost statewide in 2024.

**Third: it costs less than you fear, and more than zero.** Reform Fargo needed 1,349 valid signatures and collected 2,600 with a volunteer "Approval Voting Army" of about forty people — a shoestring operation in a city of 125,000. St. Louis, a bigger and more contested city, took a professional campaign with national nonprofit backing. I won't pretend to give you audited budgets — campaign-finance reports at city scale are patchy — but the honest range runs from Fargo's five-figure volunteer effort to a serious six-figure city campaign. What both bought was the same two assets: a signature drive and an education program.

**Fourth: winning is the start of the fight, not the end.** Fargo's reform survived a 2023 repeal bill only because Governor Doug Burgum vetoed it as an infringement on local control — and then died in 2025 when a new legislature and governor finished the job. St. Louis survived because Missouri's Amendment 7 drafters chose to grandfather it. Every adoption needs a *defense* plan: allies in the legislature, a mayor willing to testify (Fargo's did), and a story of clean elections that makes preemption look like what it is — politicians overruling their own voters.

## Coalition hygiene: the rules that keep allies allied

The reform movement's deadliest failure mode isn't the duopoly; it's friendly fire. Seattle 2022 is the standing monument: an approval initiative qualified, the council countered with ranked-choice, and the head-to-head ended 75.4 to 24.6 against approval — months of money and volunteer hours spent making another reform look good. So, three rules I'd write into any cardinal organization's bylaws. **Don't poach live RCV ground.** Where ranked-choice has organization and legal room, cardinal campaigns should pick different cities — there are nineteen ban states' worth of territory RCV cannot touch. **Never co-sign a ban.** However annoying FairVote's criticism gets, a cardinal advocate who testifies for an RCV ban is teaching legislatures how to ban ballots generally — North Dakota's HB 1297 named approval voting two years after its RCV-only predecessor failed. **Keep the comparison honest.** Steel-man ranked-choice in public (this book devotes a chapter to it) and bank the credibility for when you need to disagree.

## What this chapter settles, and what it leaves to you

Settled: the movement's working anatomy. A legible local failure, a credible local messenger, imported expertise, a signature drive sized to double the requirement, and a defense plan for the morning after — that combination is two-for-two in American cities, and nothing else has won even once. Also settled: the org chart. CES for approval campaigns, Equal Vote for STAR infrastructure, FairVote as respected competition, the civic groups for legitimacy.

Not settled: scale. Two cities in eight years is an existence proof, not a movement. Where the playbook should be run next — which states' laws are open, which doors are closed, and what a sober decade actually looks like — is the next chapter's map. After that, the only variable left in this book is you.
