# The Strategy Question

## Can People Game It?

The biggest objection to score voting is strategic voting: "Won't everyone just give their favorite a 10 and everyone else a 0, turning it into approval voting?"

Some will. And here's the thing: that's fine.

## Strategic Score Voting Is Still Good

When voters "bullet vote" in score voting (max score for their favorite, zero for everyone else), the system degrades gracefully into approval voting — which is *still* a massive improvement over plurality. Under plurality, strategic voting leads to terrible outcomes. Under score voting, strategic voting leads to... pretty good outcomes.

This property is called "strategy resistance," and score voting has more of it than any other common system.

## RCV's Strategy Vulnerability

RCV is often sold as strategy-proof, but it isn't. It's vulnerable to "compromise strategy" (ranking a less-preferred frontrunner first to prevent a worse outcome) and "burial strategy" (ranking a strong competitor last even if they're your second choice). The 2009 Burlington, Vermont mayoral election is a textbook example of RCV producing a winner that a majority would have voted against in a head-to-head matchup.

## The Gibbard-Satterthwaite Theorem

No voting system with three or more candidates is completely immune to strategic voting. This is a proven mathematical theorem. The question isn't whether strategy exists — it's how badly strategy distorts results. Score voting handles it better than the alternatives.
