# The Math Chapter

## Voting Theory for Humans

You don't need a PhD to understand why score voting works. But a little math goes a long way in understanding why it works *better*.

## Arrow's Impossibility Theorem

Kenneth Arrow proved in 1950 that no rank-based voting system can simultaneously satisfy a set of basic fairness criteria. This theorem shook political science — and it applies to RCV and STAR. But here's the key: Arrow's theorem only applies to *ordinal* (ranking) systems. Score voting is *cardinal* (rating) — it operates outside Arrow's framework entirely.

## The Condorcet Criterion

A Condorcet winner is a candidate who would beat every other candidate in a head-to-head matchup. RCV doesn't always pick the Condorcet winner (Burlington 2009). Score voting doesn't guarantee it either — but it picks the Condorcet winner far more often, and when it doesn't, it picks someone with higher overall satisfaction.

## Bayesian Regret

Bayesian regret measures how much "regret" voters collectively experience from an election outcome compared to the optimal choice. In Warren Smith's extensive simulations, score voting produces the lowest Bayesian regret of any single-winner system — meaning it consistently finds the candidate that minimizes collective dissatisfaction.

## Why Cardinal Beats Ordinal

Rankings lose information. When you rank A > B > C, I don't know if you love A and like B, or love A and hate B slightly less than C. Scores preserve that information. More information means better outcomes.
