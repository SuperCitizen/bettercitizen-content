# The Objections

Every talk I give ends the same way. The slides are done, the St. Louis results are still on the screen, and a hand goes up — usually someone who has done real homework — and out comes one of five objections. I've come to love this moment, because all five objections are *good*. Three of them I can answer outright. Two of them I have to concede before I can answer, and the concessions are where this chapter earns its keep. An advocacy book that hides its concessions is a pamphlet. Here are the five, each stated the way its smartest proponent would state it.

## "It violates one person, one vote"

**The steel-man:** The Constitution's [[term:equal-protection]] guarantee, as elaborated from *Reynolds v. Sims* onward, demands that no citizen's vote count more than another's. A score ballot lets one voter hand out a 10 and another hand out sixty points across six candidates. That looks like unequal voting power — and courts take ballot equality seriously.

**The answer**, and it's doctrinal, not rhetorical: one person, one vote governs the *weight* of votes — malapportioned districts, where one person's ballot elects a representative for half as many people — not the *format* of ballots. Challenges to counting mechanics run through the balancing test of *Burdick v. Takushi* (1992), which gives states broad room over election machinery so long as every voter faces the same rules. The Ninth Circuit applied exactly this framework to San Francisco's ranked ballots in *Dudum v. Arntz* (2011) and was blunt: "the option to rank multiple preferences is not the same as providing additional votes, or more heavily-weighted votes, relative to other votes cast." Minnesota's supreme court said the same of Minneapolis's instant runoff in *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis* (2009). The reasoning extends to score ballots *more* cleanly than to ranked ones, because score voting is perfectly symmetric: every voter gets the identical instrument — the same scale, for every candidate — and every candidate's total weighs every voter's input identically. Your sixty points spread across six candidates and my lone 10 are the same power, differently spent. The Congressional Research Service's survey of these challenges (LSB10837) finds federal courts uniformly rejecting the "extra votes" theory. Equal protection requires equal *rules*, and score voting's rules are the most symmetric on offer.

## "Courts already killed this — in 1915"

**The steel-man:** This is the opposition's favorite legal exhibit, and unlike most scare-cites, it's real. In *Brown v. Smallwood* (1915), Minnesota's supreme court struck down Duluth's Bucklin system — a "preferential" ballot where, if no one won a majority of first choices, voters' second and additional choices were *added into the totals*. Sound familiar? Adding up multiple expressions of support per voter is structurally closer to approval and score voting than anything else a U.S. court has ever reviewed — and the court killed it, writing that when a voter votes, "his vote must be counted one, and it cannot be defeated or its effect lessened, except by the vote of another elector voting for one."

**The answer** starts with a concession: in Minnesota, this precedent is alive, and approval or score voting there would face a genuine constitutional fight. Now the boundaries, which the opposition reliably omits. First, *Brown* construed the *Minnesota* constitution; it binds no other state and no federal court, and no federal court has ever held an additive ballot unconstitutional. Second, Minnesota's own supreme court spent *Minnesota Voters Alliance* (2009) confining *Brown* to its mechanics, upholding ranked ballots in Minneapolis; it treated the 1915 case as being about how Bucklin counted, not as a general ban on expressive ballots. Third, the living counterexample: St. Louis has run approval voting citywide since 2021, through contested elections including the defeat of a sitting mayor, and no court has struck it down. One century-old state case, read narrowly by its own court, is a litigation risk in one state — not a legal death sentence for cardinal ballots. Plan for it; don't be paralyzed by it.

## "Strategic voters will turn every score into a 10 or a 0"

**The steel-man:** Voting theory's deepest result — Gibbard (1973) — proves every real voting rule is manipulable, and score voting's manipulation is embarrassingly easy to find: with any sense of who the front-runners are, your instrumentally optimal ballot is max-or-nothing. Top marks for your side of the race, zeros for the rest, no middle scores at all. An electorate of sharp strategists turns the elegant 0-to-10 ballot into a glorified checklist. The nuance is a mirage.

**I concede the theory — formally and without wriggling.** The optimal instrumental score ballot is min-max, and an electorate of perfect strategists makes score voting behave like approval voting. Here is why I'm still standing: *look at what the collapse collapses to.* Approval voting — the floor of strategic score voting — is an excellent voting system: spoiler-proof, summable, majority-of-checks transparent, and good enough in practice to anchor St. Louis's elections. Now run the same stress test on the alternatives, because Gibbard exempts no one. Plurality's strategic equilibrium is the one you live in: abandon your favorite, vote for the lesser evil, duopoly forever. Instant-runoff's strategic failure is favorite betrayal in the center squeeze — Alaska's August 2022 Palin voters would have done better ranking their favorite *lower*. Score voting is the system whose *worst case under strategy is another good voting system*. The collapse is the floor, not the ceiling: every honest voter — and real electorates are full of them — adds intensity information back in, and Quinn's Voter Satisfaction Efficiency simulations show score-with-strategists performing like approval, which still sits far above plurality. A system should be judged by its floor under pressure. This floor is the point.

<Checkpoint question="A rival campaign discovers your candidate's supporters are honestly scoring a compromise candidate 6. 'Exploit it,' a consultant says: 'tell OUR people to score everyone else 0.' Walk both electorates to election night. Who actually gained what?">
  The strategic camp converted its ballots to approval ballots — full support for their candidate, nothing else. Maximum pressure, but no more than one ballot's worth per voter: they cannot push their candidate's total above max-per-ballot, and they've surrendered any say among the others. The honest camp still moves the result between front-runners with its 6s. If everyone eventually mimics the strategists, the election becomes an approval election — spoiler-free and broadly representative, which is the conceded floor. Compare plurality, where the same arms race ends with voters abandoning their actual favorites entirely. Strategy degrades score voting into a good system; it degrades plurality into the system this book opened with.
</Checkpoint>

## "Voters can't handle a 0-to-10 ballot"

**The steel-man:** Real ballots are filled out by tired people in a hurry, not by theorists. Every degree of freedom invites error and demographic gaps in error rates — the ranked-ballot literature already shows modestly elevated overvote and spoilage rates versus plurality, concentrated in first uses. Eleven bubbles per candidate is begging for mistakes, and "it's like rating a restaurant" is marketing, not evidence.

**The answer** rests on the two American electorates that have actually used cardinal ballots. Fargo's voters, the first in the country, averaged 2.3 approvals per ballot in June 2020; St. Louis's averaged 1.56 in the March 2021 mayoral primary. Both electorates used the new latitude immediately — no transition crisis, no spoiled-ballot wave reported by either city's election authority. And note the structural fact that separates cardinal ballots from ranked ones: on an approval or score ballot, *marking many candidates is legal by definition*. The most common disqualifying error in American elections — the overvote — ceases to exist, because what used to be an overvote is now just a ballot. Ranked ballots can be voided by a duplicate ranking; a score ballot with two 7s is simply a voter's opinion. I'll concede what's left of the objection: a 0-to-10 scale asks for more decisions than a checkbox, and some voters will decline — they'll score one candidate 10 and stop. But follow that worst case through: a bullet-voted score ballot *is a plurality ballot*. The voter who ignores every new option is exactly where today's system puts everyone by force. Nobody is made worse off; the expressiveness is there for the millions who want it. We rate everything — and the one place Americans are forbidden to say "I like her, I can live with him, never him" is the ballot box.

## "It doesn't guarantee a majority winner"

**The steel-man:** Democratic legitimacy means majority rule. Score voting can elect a candidate who is the *first choice of a minority* — a broadly liked compromise over a candidate that 51 percent of voters ranked at the very top. However you dress it up, the majority's favorite lost. That should disqualify the method.

**I concede this one formally too** — score voting fails the majority criterion, and you should see the failure in numbers rather than take it on faith. One hundred voters, two candidates. Fifty-one voters score A=10, B=8. Forty-nine voters score B=10, A=0. Totals: A 510, B 898. B wins — though A was the majority's favorite.

Now look at what the ballots actually say, because this is the chapter's last and most important reframe. The majority barely prefers A — it rates B an 8, nearly indifferent. The minority regards A as a catastrophe — intensity the [[term:plurality]] ballot has no way to even record. Electing B trades a 2-point disappointment for 51 people against a 10-point disaster for 49. That is the **utilitarian winner** — greatest total satisfaction — over the **majoritarian winner**, and choosing it is a philosophical commitment this book makes with open eyes: elections should maximize how well the whole community is represented, not hand a bare majority everything on intensity it doesn't feel. You may weigh those values differently; that's a legitimate disagreement about democratic theory, not arithmetic. But before the majority criterion disqualifies score voting, apply it consistently: plurality routinely elects candidates most voters opposed, and instant-runoff's celebrated "majorities" are majorities of *continuing* ballots — in the four elections Burnett and Kogan examined, between 9.6 and 27.1 percent of ballots had exhausted before the final round, and no winner held a majority of all votes cast. No system on the table delivers guaranteed true majorities. The honest question is which compromise you choose on purpose.

| The objection | What I concede | Where the argument lands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One person, one vote | Nothing — the doctrine concerns vote weight, not ballot format | *Dudum*, *MVA v. Minneapolis*, and the symmetric ballot |
| *Brown v. Smallwood* | A real litigation risk in Minnesota | One state, read narrowly by its own court; St. Louis operates today |
| Strategic collapse | The theory, fully: optimal strategy is min-max | The floor is approval voting — a good system, not a failure mode |
| Ballot complexity | More decisions than a checkbox; some voters will bullet-vote | Overvotes become legal; bullet voting just recreates plurality; Fargo and St. Louis used the latitude |
| No guaranteed majority | The majority criterion, formally | Utilitarian vs. majoritarian is a values choice — and rivals' "majorities" are softer than advertised |

Read the table's middle column again — that's the part most advocacy never shows you. Two full concessions, one partial, and the case still stands; that is what it looks like when an argument is load-bearing rather than decorative.

<Checkpoint question="Transfer the framework: an IRV advocate tells you 'ranked-choice voting guarantees majority winners, unlike score voting.' Using this chapter's tools, what is the precise, fair response?">
  Both halves are too strong. IRV guarantees a majority of ballots *still active in the final round* — Burnett and Kogan documented exhaustion of 9.6 to 27.1 percent, with no winner holding a majority of all votes cast. Score voting fails the majority criterion by design, preferring the utilitarian winner when majority preference is weak and minority opposition is intense. So neither system delivers an unconditional "true majority"; they fail the slogan in different, principled directions. The fair debate is over which failure you'd rather own — and that's a question about values, which is why it belongs in the open rather than in a tagline.
</Checkpoint>

## What's still genuinely open

Discipline to the end: what this chapter did *not* settle. No American government has yet run a public score-voting election, so the strategic-collapse concession rests on theory and simulation, not on a documented score electorate; the Minnesota question is unlitigated for cardinal ballots specifically; and the utilitarian-versus-majoritarian choice is, finally, a question of political philosophy on which honest people will keep disagreeing after every fact in this book is granted. What I hope is no longer open is the meta-question: whether score voting's advocates can hear the best version of every objection and answer — or concede — in the open. The next chapters turn from defense to offense: how a movement actually wins.
