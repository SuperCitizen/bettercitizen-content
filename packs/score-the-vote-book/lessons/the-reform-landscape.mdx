# The Reform Landscape

On April 16, 2025, North Dakota Governor Kelly Armstrong signed House Bill 1297 and erased a voting system that the people of Fargo had adopted by nearly two-thirds in 2018 and used, without incident, in three city elections. "Now more than ever, we need a consistent, efficient and easy-to-understand voter experience across our entire state to maintain trust in our election system," Armstrong said. Fargo had not asked. This week — June 2026 — Fargo elected a mayor the old way: five candidates, pick one, most votes wins, majority optional.

Hold that image, because it tells you what this chapter is really about. The American reform landscape in mid-2026 is not a tidy march of progress. It is a contested map — reforms advancing by [[term:ballot-initiative]], getting repealed by legislatures, surviving by hundreds of votes, and occasionally being deleted from above. Before this book argues for one reform over the others, you need the whole board.

## One problem, five families of fixes

Every entry on the map below is attacking the same defect: [[term:plurality]] voting reads one mark per voter and ignores everything else you think. The families differ in *what extra information* they collect and *what they do with it*.

| Family | The ballot change | Where it stands, mid-2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ranked-choice (IRV) | Rank the candidates | 49 jurisdictions incl. Maine and Alaska (FairVote, Feb 2026); banned in 19 states; Alaska repeal vote pending Nov 2026 |
| Approval | Check everyone you accept | St. Louis (since 2021, with runoff); Fargo 2020–24, until the 2025 state ban |
| Score / STAR | Grade candidates on a scale | No public adoptions; STAR rejected in Lane County (2018), Eugene and Oakridge (2024) |
| Fusion | One candidate, multiple party lines | Fully alive in NY and CT; litigation to revive it in NJ and WI |
| Proportional (STV / Fair Rep. Act) | Multi-member districts, transferable ranked ballots | Cambridge, MA (since 1941); Portland, OR (first used 2024); H.R. 4632 parked in committee |
| Top-two / top-four primaries | All candidates, one primary ballot; survivors advance | CA and WA (top-two); Alaska (top-four); Nevada's top-five died at its second vote, 2024 |

A table never speaks for itself, so here is what it says: only two families have actually been winning American elections at scale — ranked ballots and approval ballots — and both are now in open warfare with state legislatures.

**The RCV family** — which gets a full, fair chapter of its own later in this book — is the movement's flagship: 49 jurisdictions by [[org:fairvote]]'s February 2026 count, anchored by Maine (statewide since 2018, for primaries and federal generals), Alaska (top-four plus ranked general since 2022), New York City, and now Washington, D.C., whose Initiative 83 passed with about 73 percent in 2024 and debuts in the June 2026 primaries — though the D.C. Council funded only the ranked-ballot half of the initiative, leaving its semi-open-primary provision waiting. The counterattack is just as organized: nineteen states, from Tennessee and Florida in 2022 through Ohio in March 2026, have banned RCV outright, and Alaska faces its *third* statewide vote on the system this November, after repeal qualified on December 31, 2025 with 42,837 verified signatures. The 2024 repeal failed by 743 votes. Nobody should pretend November is safe.

**The approval family** is smaller and bloodier, and it's where this book lives. Fargo adopted approval voting in 2018 — the first American city ever — used it in 2020, 2022, and 2024, and was overruled by its own state in April 2025. St. Louis adopted Proposition D in November 2020 with 68 percent: an open, nonpartisan approval round in March, top two to an April runoff. It has now run three citywide cycles, including the 2025 election in which an incumbent mayor was defeated — and it *survived* Missouri's Amendment 7 (November 2024), which banned ranked-choice and approval voting statewide by about two-to-one but grandfathered the one city already using it. St. Louis is, as of mid-2026, the only approval-voting government in America: the beachhead, under siege, holding.

<Checkpoint question="Fargo's approval voting was killed in 2025; St. Louis's survived a statewide ban the year before. Neither city voted to abandon its system. What's the structural lesson for reformers choosing where to campaign?">
  Cities don't control their own election law — states do. Fargo's reform died by ordinary statute in Bismarck; St. Louis's lived because Missouri's ban happened to run through a constitutional amendment that exempted existing users. A city win in a hostile or hostile-trending state sits exposed to preemption, which is why the movement's hardest strategic question isn't "which city next?" but "which states protect local choice — and can reform win statewide before a legislature slams the door?" Nineteen RCV bans say the window matters.
</Checkpoint>

## The families that haven't landed yet

**Score and STAR** — the cardinal family beyond approval — have simulations, organizations, and zero public adoptions. STAR's near-misses and defeats (Lane County 2018 by under five points; Eugene, its own hometown, two-to-one against in May 2024; Oakridge by 24 votes that November) get a full chapter later in this book. Pure [[term:score-voting]] has never reached an American government ballot. I'll argue that's a sequencing problem, not a verdict — but facts first: this family's record is organizational elections and one party-run statewide primary, nothing more.

**Fusion voting** is the revival nobody saw coming. Fusion — one candidate appearing as the nominee of multiple parties, their vote totals combined — was routine in the nineteenth century, then banned almost everywhere precisely because it made third parties dangerous. It survives fully only in New York and Connecticut. The revival runs through courtrooms: in New Jersey, the new Moderate Party sought to cross-nominate a congressman in 2022 and, backed by [[org:protect-democracy]] and allies from the ACLU to the Cato Institute, pressed a state constitutional challenge — which the Appellate Division rejected in February 2025, and the state supreme court declined review that December. In Wisconsin, United Wisconsin — co-chaired by a former Republican senate majority leader — filed a parallel suit in April 2025 that is still moving through state court as of this spring. Fusion's logic is different from new ballots: it doesn't change how you mark or count, it changes *who can endorse*. Watch it; it is the reform that major-party moderates find easiest to love.

**Proportional representation** is the deep-water reform: multi-member districts where 40 percent of the votes gets you about 40 percent of the seats. Cambridge, Massachusetts has elected its council by single transferable vote since 1941; Portland, Oregon joined in 2022 (Measure 26-228, about 57 percent) and ran its first proportional council elections in November 2024 — twelve councilors, three per district. The federal version, the Fair Representation Act (H.R. 4632, reintroduced July 23, 2025 by Representatives Beyer and Raskin), would do this for the entire U.S. House. It is a serious bill with no path to passage in this Congress, and its sponsors know it; it exists to define the destination.

**Top-N primaries** — Washington and California's top-two, Alaska's top-four — change *when* the field narrows rather than how you mark the ballot, usually paired with [[issue:nonpartisan-primaries]]. Nevada's top-five-plus-RCV initiative passed in 2022 and then died at its constitutionally required second vote in 2024 — a reminder that even winning once isn't winning. Louisiana, meanwhile, moved the *other* way, abandoning its all-comers ballot for closed congressional primaries starting in 2026. The door swings both directions.

## Where score fits: the beachhead argument

Now the strategic claim this book builds on, stated plainly so you can hold me to it.

Approval voting is score voting with the smallest possible scale — 0 or 1, unacceptable or acceptable. That makes it, mechanically, the *smallest change to the ballot any reform can propose*: same grid, same machines, same count, with exactly one rule deleted — "vote for only one." No new equipment, no transfer rounds to explain, no result a precinct captain can't verify with addition. When ranked-choice's documented weak points come up — exhausted ballots, the center squeeze in Alaska's 2022 special election, both examined later in this book — approval is the reform that structurally doesn't have those failure modes; when cost comes up, it's the reform that costs nothing. That is why a movement a fraction of RCV's size has two city-scale wins to its name — and why the legislatures hostile to reform bothered to ban approval *by name* in North Dakota and Missouri. They can see the beachhead too.

The destination is fuller expressiveness — the 0-to-10 ballot this book defends, which captures not just *whether* you accept a candidate but *how much*. The path runs through approval because every approval win normalizes the family's core idea: count every opinion a voter has, all at once, no eliminations. St. Louis is the working proof, and the primaries chapter will show what its model actually does to candidate incentives.

What this chapter settles: the verified state of every reform family as of mid-2026 — what's running, what's banned, what's pending, with the margins attached. What it doesn't settle: whether the rollback wave crests or keeps building (November in Alaska will say a lot), and whether the beachhead argument survives contact with the objections — legal, strategic, and psychological — that the objections chapter takes head-on.
