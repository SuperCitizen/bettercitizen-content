# Your Move

Every reform book ends the same way. Twenty chapters of evidence, then a final chapter that goes soft: *raise awareness, have conversations, stay engaged.* It's the authorial equivalent of approving every candidate — a refusal to say what you actually want the reader to do. I've spent this book arguing that your ballot should let you say exactly what you think. The least I can do is close by doing the same.

Here is what I think: the difference between Fargo in 2016 and Fargo in 2018 was not money, not a movement, and not a moment. It was one engineer from a city task force who declined to let the question die. The entire American record of cardinal voting — every adoption, both cities — traces to a handful of individually identifiable people who decided the broken thing in front of them was theirs to fix. The movement's binding constraint is not evidence (you've read eighteen chapters of it), not legality (the law is clearer than the opposition pretends), and not money (Fargo was won on signatures and shoe leather). It's protagonists.

## First, learn your terrain — it takes one afternoon

Before you donate a dollar or print a petition, answer three questions about where you live. They're answerable in an afternoon, and they determine everything.

**Is your city a home-rule or charter city?** Your city clerk's office can tell you in one phone call, and your charter is on the city website. If the charter governs how your officials are elected, you have a front door. If your city operates under general state law, your fight is at the statehouse, not city hall — different game, longer odds.

**Does your state's election code leave room?** Michigan's cities passed ranked-choice charter amendments five times over two decades and implemented none of them, because state election law leaves no opening. Read the elections title of your state code for what it prescribes about ballot form and counting — or ask the clerk, who knows.

**Has your state already legislated?** If you're in Missouri or North Dakota, cardinal methods are closed off — Missouri by constitution, North Dakota by the 2025 statute that ended Fargo's experiment. If you're in one of the other seventeen ban states, check the definitions section of the ban: nearly all prohibit *ranking*, which means an approval or score campaign is likely the only reform legally available to you — an argument, not an obstacle. And if your state has no ban, your terrain question is simply whether your city's charter opens the door.

Two numbers from the record to calibrate ambition. Fargo: 1,349 valid signatures required; Reform Fargo collected 2,600, had 1,923 accepted, and won with 63.5 percent — roughly forty volunteers, a local face, and national expertise on call. St. Louis: a professionally organized coalition campaign, Proposition D, 68 percent. Between those two poles sits every city in the open seventeen states and beyond. And before any of it, make the unglamorous call I keep insisting on: the clerk. Election administrators are the most under-consulted experts in reform politics, they know your equipment and your deadlines, and a clerk who says "we could run that" in a council hearing is worth a thousand white papers.

## If you can't run a campaign, run something smaller

Most readers won't lead a charter initiative this year, and pretending otherwise is how action chapters curdle into guilt. The smaller moves are real moves, in rough order of effort. Adopt it where you already have power: clubs, unions, congregations, co-ops, professional societies, student governments, and party committees run elections constantly — Utah's Forward Party just filled a state senate vacancy by approval vote, proving the org-scale version anchors the public-scale argument. Join the organizations: the Center for Election Science for approval voting, the Equal Vote Coalition for STAR — both run on small donors and volunteers, and both will tell you exactly what your state needs. Make candidates answer: city council candidates in plurality cities have never once been asked what they think of how they were elected; ask publicly. Teach the [[term:score-voting]] pitch: rate every candidate, zero to ten, like you'd rate anything else; highest average wins. You know from the media chapter that one sentence is genuinely all it takes — the gap is reach, and reach is made of people repeating sentences.

## The claim, one last time

Strip away every statute and study in this book and what's left is one sentence: **your ballot should let you say what you actually think about every candidate.**

That's the whole revolution. Not a partisan project — Fargo's reform passed in deep-red North Dakota, St. Louis's in a city that hasn't had a Republican mayor since 1949, by similar landslide margins. Not a complicated one — the fix is removing a restriction, not adding a mechanism: take the sentence "vote for one" and delete the limit. And not someone else's — there is no commission coming, no inevitable arc; there is a short list of people who decide the broken thing in front of them is theirs, and everyone else.

A pick-one ballot hands you a megaphone that can say a single word. The politics of [[term:plurality]] — the spoilers, the lesser-evilism, the candidates who win with 16 percent and govern like it — is just that constraint, compounded across decades, doing exactly what it must. You have now read what the constraint costs, what replacing it returns, where the law is open, what the playbook says, and what the first phone call is.

Democracy is not a spectator sport. Score the vote.
