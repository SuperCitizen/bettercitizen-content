# The Math Chapter

In 2012, the man whose theorem launched a thousand "no voting system is fair" headlines gave a podcast interview. Kenneth Arrow — Nobel laureate, author of the most famous impossibility result in social science — was asked what system he'd actually recommend. His answer, hedged the way real mathematicians hedge: "I'm a little inclined to think that score systems where you categorize in maybe three or four classes... is probably the best."

You don't need a PhD to follow why he said that. You need four results, two worked elections, and a tolerance for hearing the strongest case against my side — which I'll be making personally, with arithmetic you can check.

## Arrow: impossibility — for rankings

In *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951), Arrow proved that no method for converting individual *rankings* into a group ranking can satisfy a short list of innocent-sounding fairness conditions all at once, with three or more candidates in the race. Something always gives. This is the theorem people compress into "all voting systems are flawed."

But the fine print changes everything: the theorem's *inputs are orderings*. Arrow's framework speaks ordinal — first, second, third — and is silent about systems whose inputs carry more. [[term:score-voting]] is cardinal: a 5-4-0 ballot and a 5-1-0 ballot are different inputs, where every ranking flattens both into the same A-then-B-then-C. Cardinal methods don't *solve* Arrow's puzzle; they decline to enter it, by changing what voters are asked.

Advocates — my people — routinely oversell this as "score voting escapes the impossibility theorem," as though escaping Arrow meant escaping criticism. It doesn't, because of what came next.

## Gibbard: nobody escapes strategy

In 1973, the philosopher Allan Gibbard published a result with none of Arrow's escape hatches: *every* voting scheme in which chance plays no role, no individual is a dictator, and more than two outcomes are possible can be manipulated — situations exist where some voter gets a better result by misrepresenting her preferences. The proof doesn't care whether ballots are rankings, scores, or approvals. Strategy is universal. Forever. By theorem.

So the honest comparison between systems was never "which one is strategy-proof?" — that set is empty. It's "what does each system's strategy *look like*, and how badly does it degrade?" Hold that question; the worked examples answer it.

## The criteria scorecard

Voting theorists evaluate methods against named criteria — bright-line tests a method passes or fails by mathematical proof, not opinion. Here are the four that decide the score-versus-RCV argument, and notice the table's most important feature: *neither column sweeps*.

| Criterion | What it demands | IRV (ranked choice) | Score / approval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Later-no-harm | Expressing a backup choice can never hurt your favorite | **Passes** | **Fails** |
| Favorite betrayal | You never do better by demoting your true favorite | **Fails** | **Passes** |
| Monotonicity | Gaining support can never hurt a candidate | **Fails** | **Passes** |
| Condorcet | Always elect the candidate who beats all others head-to-head | **Fails** (Burlington 2009) | **Fails** (under some honest profiles) |

Read it row by row, concessions first. **Later-no-harm** is RCV's best card, and it's genuine: under [[term:instant-runoff]] counting, your second choice is consulted only after your first is eliminated, so it can never help defeat your first. Under score voting, the 3 you give a compromise candidate genuinely can help him edge out your 5. I concede the failure — and ask you to look at what the criterion forbids. A method can guarantee later-no-harm only by *refusing to count compromise information while your favorite lives* — which is precisely how IRV ends up blind to consensus candidates. Later-no-harm and finding-the-candidate-everyone-can-accept are not independently purchasable. You pick one.

**Favorite betrayal** is the mirror image. Score and approval pass: giving your true favorite the maximum can never backfire — the property [[term:plurality]] most catastrophically lacks. IRV fails it, and not just on paper. In Alaska's August 2022 special election, Graham-Squire and McCune's ballot-data analysis shows the count was non-monotonic: Sarah Palin's voters helped elect Mary Peltola — their *least* preferred candidate — by ranking Palin first. Had enough of them insincerely top-ranked Nick Begich, the Republican the ballots show beating everyone head-to-head, they'd have gotten an outcome they preferred. Top-ranking their favorite was, mathematically, the blunder. A system that punishes honesty about your favorite has reproduced plurality's original sin — quietly, probabilistically, but provably.

**Monotonicity** and **Condorcet** fill out the picture: more points can never hurt a candidate under score (addition is monotone), while IRV's elimination order can turn extra support into defeat, as Alaska showed in the wild. And the Condorcet row shows *both* methods failing — IRV's failure you've seen (Burlington elected Bob Kiss while the ballots showed 54 percent preferred Andy Montroll head-to-head); score's failure I will now construct myself.

## Worked example 1: the center squeeze, in arithmetic

One hundred voters, three candidates — Left, Center, Right. Honest opinions:

| Bloc | Voters | Honest score ballot (0–5) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Left partisans | 35 | Left 5, Center 3, Right 0 |
| Right partisans | 33 | Right 5, Center 3, Left 0 |
| Centrists leaning left | 16 | Center 5, Left 3, Right 0 |
| Centrists leaning right | 16 | Center 5, Right 3, Left 0 |

Now run the same hundred ballots through three counting rules:

| Rule | The count | Winner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plurality | Left 35, Right 33, Center 32 | **Left** |
| Instant runoff | Center eliminated first (32); transfers split 16–16; final round Left 51, Right 49 | **Left** |
| Head-to-head | Center beats Left 65–35; Center beats Right 67–33 | Center is the Condorcet winner |
| Score | Center 364, Left 223, Right 213 | **Center** |

Check the score line yourself: Center collects 5×32 from centrists plus 3×68 from both partisan blocs — 364. Left gets 5×35 plus 3×16 — 223. The candidate that two-thirds of the city prefers to *either* alternative finishes dead last on first choices, so plurality never sees him and IRV executes him in round one. Score voting finds him with addition, because breadth-plus-depth is exactly what a sum measures. This is the center squeeze from the last chapter, reduced to a table you can audit in sixty seconds.

<Checkpoint question="Stress-test it: move 4 of the 16 right-leaning centrists into the Right-partisan bloc (their ballots become Right 5, Center 3, Left 0). Recompute the score totals. Does Center survive? What would IRV do with the same shift?">
  Center loses those voters' 5s but keeps their 3s: 364 − 8 = **356**. Right gains 4 points per switched voter: 213 + 8 = **221**. Left stays at 223. Center still wins comfortably, 356 to 223 to 221 — the score count bends smoothly as opinion shifts. Under IRV nothing changes either (Center is still eliminated first, at 28) — but notice it was *already* electing Left; IRV's failure here isn't fragility, it's never seeing Center at all.
</Checkpoint>

## Worked example 2: the case against score voting, by its advocate

Fairness demands I build the reverse case with the same care. One hundred voters, two candidates:

| Bloc | Voters | A | B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mild majority | 55 | 5 | 4 |
| Intense minority | 45 | 0 | 5 |

Totals: A gets 275. B gets 220 + 225 = **445**. B wins in a landslide — *while 55 percent of voters prefer A*. A two-candidate race, a clear majority favorite, and score voting elects the other one. This is a real, provable property: score voting fails what theorists call the majority criterion, and any honest advocate must say so in plain English. I just did.

Here is why I accept it. Look at what the ballots say: fifty-five voters *almost equally happy either way* — a one-point preference — versus forty-five for whom the difference is everything they can express. Electing A buys the majority one point of happiness each and costs the minority five each. If an election should maximize how satisfied the city actually is, B is the *right* answer and the "failure" is the system working. If you instead hold majority rule as axiomatic — the majority must prevail however mild its preference — you are making a value choice, not finding a math error, and score voting is honestly not your system. That is the philosophical core of this whole debate, and I'd rather hand it to you than have a critic pretend I hid it.

The example has a second act, and it's the one Gibbard predicted. Suppose the majority wises up and exaggerates — all 55 mark A 5, B 0. Now A totals 275, B 225: **A wins**. The majority can always protect itself by exaggerating, so score's majority-criterion failures require a majority too honest to flex. Play the logic forward and you get the collapse from the VSE chapter: tactical scoring pushes every ballot to its endpoints, and a ballot of only 5s and 0s *is* an approval ballot. The concession and the comfort are the same fact: strategic score voting degrades into approval voting — the highest strategic floor ever simulated — and the feared "tyranny of the indifferent majority" evaporates under exactly the strategic behavior critics insist voters will exhibit. Warren Smith's Bayesian-regret simulations, which assume whole electorates of schemers, still place range voting at the low-regret end of the table. A machine whose failure modes police each other is not a fragile machine.

## What the math settles — and what it can't

The math settles the boundaries. Arrow: perfect ordinal aggregation is impossible, and the escape is cardinal information, not cleverer counting. Gibbard: strategy-proofness is impossible everywhere, so "can it be gamed?" is the wrong question and "what does gaming do to it?" is the right one. The criteria table: every method buys its virtues with named, provable failures — IRV purchases later-no-harm at the price of favorite betrayal, non-monotonicity, and center blindness; score purchases spoiler-immunity and safe honesty at the price of majority-criterion failures that its own strategic dynamics largely cancel.

What the math cannot settle is which trade you value, because that's philosophy wearing a calculator. My cards, face up: a democracy should never punish a voter for telling the truth about her favorite; it should use the intensity information voters are begging to give; and it should fail, when it fails, toward approval voting rather than toward 1992. The theorems don't compel those values. They just guarantee that whatever you value, you're choosing trade-offs — so choose them with the tables open.
