# Where Score Voting Works

As you read this, the United Nations Security Council is choosing the most powerful diplomat on Earth — the tenth Secretary-General — and it is not using pick-one ballots to do it. Four declared candidates entered the 2026 race: Michelle Bachelet of Chile, Rafael Grossi of Argentina, Rebeca Grynspan of Costa Rica, and Macky Sall of Senegal. Beginning in the summer of 2026, Council members rate *every* candidate in secret straw polls: "encourage," "discourage," or "no opinion." That is a three-level score ballot. Polling repeats, candidates read their averages and withdraw, and the process converges on the name the Council can collectively live with — which, for a job demanding consent from fifteen rivalrous governments, is precisely the right question to ask.

Hold that image. When the people who run the world's elections choose among *themselves*, they reach for cardinal ballots. The story of this chapter is that they're not unusual — and that the obstacle to using the same tool in American public elections has never been the voters.

## You already live in a scored world

Olympic judges score routines; the medal goes to the totals. Film festivals, wine competitions, and hiring committees run on numeric rubrics. Every star rating you've ever left is a five-level score ballot, aggregated into an average that strangers trust enough to act on. I won't pretend your delivery-app habits are constitutional evidence — rating a burrito carries no incentive to lie strategically. The point is narrower and stronger: the *skill* score voting asks of voters is one the whole population already exercises weekly. No reform on offer asks less new of the voter.

## Six centuries of cardinal ballots

The deep history runs through Venice. From 1268 until Napoleon ended the republic in 1797, Venice chose its doge through a famously baroque protocol — ten stages alternating random draws with committee votes — that terminated in something an election scientist instantly recognizes: 41 electors evaluating candidates, with election requiring at least 25 approvals. Approval-style ballots with a supermajority threshold kept the top job of a fractious commercial empire consensual for five centuries; the computer scientists Miranda Mowbray and Dieter Gollmann, analyzing the protocol in 2007, found it shrewdly engineered against capture by factions.

The modern world kept the idea. Latvia elects its parliament on open lists where a voter marks a "+" beside candidates she favors and strikes through names she opposes — each candidate's total is the list's votes, plus the pluses, minus the strikes. A three-level score ballot (−1, 0, +1), deciding who takes the seats of an EU member state, today.

## America's first try — and the courtroom that ended it

Starting in Grand Junction, Colorado in 1909, a ranked system called Bucklin voting — count first choices; if no majority, *add* second choices to the totals — spread to more than sixty American cities, including Denver and San Francisco.

Then came *Brown v. Smallwood*. After a 1915 judicial election in Duluth, the Minnesota Supreme Court struck Bucklin down under the state constitution, and the opinion's logic is striking to modern eyes: the court tallied 18,860 marks cast by 12,313 voters and recoiled — this "was not a voting of man against man." Marks per voter, rather than influence per voter, became the measure of equality. Taken literally, that logic menaces any multi-mark ballot, approval included — though when Minnesota's high court revisited the question in *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis* (2009), it upheld instant-runoff voting and read *Brown* narrowly, as a complaint about Bucklin counting a voter's later choices *simultaneously against* her first choice. Either way, the damage was done: Grand Junction abandoned its own invention by 1921, Denver repealed in 1935, and America's first alternative-ballot movement was dead by mid-century — killed less by voters than by courts and repeal campaigns. Reform that wins at the ballot box can still die in a courtroom, and an honest advocate plans for that from day one.

<Checkpoint question="A 1915 court counted 18,860 marks from 12,313 voters and declared the system unconstitutional. What would that court likely say about approval voting — and what is the strongest answer to it?">
  By its letter, the same objection applies: approval voting routinely counts more marks than voters (St. Louis's first approval primary logged about 1.6 approvals per ballot). The strongest answer attacks the premise: equality of *influence*, not equality of ink. Under approval, every voter faces the same ballot, may mark the same maximum, and weighs identically in every candidate-versus-candidate comparison — no one's marks outrank anyone else's. And unlike Bucklin, an approval mark never counts *against* your other choices, the specific defect Minnesota's modern court said *Brown* was really about.
</Checkpoint>

## The societies: adopted on the merits, dropped by the board

Approval voting's modern era began in professional societies. Starting in 1987–88, four scientific and engineering societies with several hundred thousand combined members adopted it for internal elections — the IEEE the marquee case — and a half dozen more followed. Steven Brams and Peter Fishburn, approval's leading theorists, documented the record in a 2005 paper whose title is concession and claim at once: "the mixed success of approval voting."

Mixed, because boards giveth and boards taketh away. The IEEE reverted to plurality in 2002. Dartmouth College, which adopted approval voting in 1990 for alumni-trustee elections drawing around 20,000 voters, scrapped it in a governance overhaul between 2007 and 2009 — after a run of insurgent petition candidates won seats over the establishment's nominees. Official explanations cited campaign costs, confusion, and bullet voting; critics, Brams among them, noted the ballot data showed the method working as designed, and that the overhaul happened to follow outcomes the administration disliked. I can't referee motives from outside. I can observe the pattern, because you're about to see it again with the lights on: when cardinal ballots produce winners the rule-makers didn't choose, the rule-makers change the rules.

## Fargo and St. Louis: the proof, and the price

Now the main event. In November 2018, Fargo, North Dakota became the first American city to adopt approval voting, by [[term:ballot-initiative]], 63.5 percent to 36.5. On June 9, 2020, it ran the first approval-voting election for public office in modern U.S. history — and the data answered the "voters will be confused" objection on contact. Voters approved an average of 2.3 candidates each in a seven-way commission race (under the old vote-for-two rules in 2018, the average was 1.8). The two winners earned approval from 55 and 53 percent of voters — broad mandates of a kind Fargo's splintered plurality races hadn't produced in years. The city used the system again in 2022 and 2024, without incident.

In November 2020, St. Louis followed: Proposition D, 68 percent. The design is approval-into-runoff — an open, nonpartisan approval primary, with the top two advancing to a head-to-head general, a [[term:runoff-election]] guaranteeing the winner a true majority test. In its March 2021 debut, voters averaged roughly 1.6 approvals per ballot; Tishaura Jones led with approval from 57 percent of voters and won the April runoff to become the city's first Black woman mayor. In 2025, the same machinery calmly produced the opposite result — incumbent Jones lost the runoff to Cara Spencer, 64 to 36. The system elects no party and protects no incumbent. It just counts.

Then the empire struck back, and I am going to give it to you straight.

In 2023, North Dakota's legislature passed HB 1273 to ban approval and ranked-choice voting statewide. Governor Doug Burgum — a Republican, vetoing a Republican bill — called it what it was: a measure that "blatantly infringes on local control." The House overrode his veto; the Senate sustained it, 28 to 19, four votes short. Fargo's reprieve lasted one session. In 2025, HB 1297 passed the Senate 38–8 and the House 75–16 — margins no veto could survive — and Governor Kelly Armstrong signed it on April 16, 2025, citing the need for "a consistent, efficient and easy-to-understand voter experience across our entire state." Fargo's approval voting is dead. In June 2026, the city held its first election since 2018 on pick-one ballots, and the result was a time capsule from this book's second chapter: a mayor elected with 47 percent in a multi-way field. The pathology Fargoans voted to fix, restored to them by law. No Fargo electorate ever voted to repeal approval voting. Bismarck repealed it for them.

Missouri's version ran through the voters themselves. In November 2024, Amendment 7 passed with 68.4 percent — a constitutional amendment whose ballot title led with making it explicit that "only" citizens may vote (already the law) and, further down, prohibited ranking candidates and limited each voter to "a single vote per candidate or issue." That last clause outlaws approval voting prospectively. One carve-out survived: any nonpartisan municipal election in a city whose ordinance was in effect as of November 5, 2024, requiring a preliminary election from which more than one candidate advances. Exactly one city fits that description. St. Louis's system is grandfathered — alive and in use through the 2025 cycle and as of mid-2026 — and every other Missouri city is constitutionally barred from ever copying it.

## Two readings of the backlash

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Reform advocate">
    Justice Brandeis's image of states — and by extension cities — as laboratories of democracy describes exactly what Fargo and St. Louis did: small, reversible experiments, adopted by supermajorities, generating real data. The results came back positive — voters used the wider ballot, winners earned broader mandates, counts finished election night — and the experiments were then terminated *from above*, by officials elected under the rules being protected. Note what's missing from every one of these stories: a city electorate voting to go back. Fargo never did. When the scoreboard says voters keep choosing this and incumbents keep un-choosing it, believe the scoreboard.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Status-quo defender">
    Uniformity is not a conspiracy; it's election administration. A state where city, county, and federal contests on one ballot follow different rules invites voter error, poll-worker confusion, and litigation — and "trust the count" is democracy's scarcest resource right now. Governor Armstrong's case for HB 1297 was exactly this: one consistent, easy-to-understand voter experience statewide. Missouri's voters — not a smoke-filled room — ratified Amendment 7 with 68 percent. And the reformers' own exhibit cuts both ways: two cities, five years, and a handful of races is thin evidence, not a verdict. [[term:federalism|Federalism]] includes the state's prerogative to set election standards; that's not sabotage, it's the state constitution doing its job.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Both readings are honest; I hold the first, and the rest of this book is my brief for why uniformly positive evidence plus strong theory beats incumbent caution. But notice what *neither* side disputes: where cardinal ballots have been used, voters used them competently and kept them willingly. The contest is over who gets to decide.

## What this chapter settles — and what it doesn't

Settled: cardinal voting is not a theory in search of a trial. It elected doges for five centuries and elects Latvian MPs today; it selects Secretaries-General; it ran four clean election cycles across two American cities, where voters demonstrably used the expanded ballot and never moved to repeal it. Also settled, because pretending otherwise would insult you: the movement just absorbed its worst two years. Fargo is gone. Missouri is constitutionally closed. Adopted-below, removed-above is now the central strategic fact of this reform — which is why later chapters treat legal architecture (statutes, constitutional language, grandfather clauses) as seriously as ballot design.

Not settled: whether score voting picks *better winners* than ranked-choice voting — two cities and a diplomatic ritual can't answer that. The simulation evidence already has (the voter-satisfaction chapter), and the math chapter finishes the argument. Next, though, fairness demands a full chapter on the rival that's actually winning statehouses: ranked-choice voting, credit where due.
