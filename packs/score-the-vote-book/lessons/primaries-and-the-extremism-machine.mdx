# Primaries and the Extremism Machine

Ask a member of Congress what they're afraid of, and the honest ones don't say "losing in November." November is booked. They say a verb that exists in no other democracy's vocabulary: getting *primaried*. The general election stopped being the election for most American offices years ago; what remains is a small, sleepy contest most voters skip — which decides nearly everything — followed by a loud, expensive one that decides nearly nothing. This chapter is about that machine: how it manufactures the politics you hate, and where, precisely, a better ballot breaks it.

## The machine, part by part

What you should have noticed: the three numbers don't belong in the same democracy. Roughly 21 percent of eligible voters participated in the 2022 primaries — the Bipartisan Policy Center's count is 21.3 — while [[org:unite-america]]'s analysis of the same cycle found that 83 percent of U.S. House seats were *safe*: so lopsided that the [[term:general-election]] was a formality and the primary was the election. Multiply low turnout by safe seats and you get the machine's output number: in 2022, about 8 percent of Americans cast the primary ballots that effectively chose 83 percent of the 435 House members. By Unite America's 2024-cycle update the squeeze was tighter still — 87 percent of seats, 7 percent of Americans.

Now assemble the machine. Part one: [[term:gerrymandering]] and geographic self-sorting make most districts safe, so the decisive round moves upstream to the [[term:primary-election]]. Part two: [[term:voter-turnout]] in that round is a fifth of the electorate, skewed toward the most engaged partisans. Part three — the part reformers underrate — the primary is counted by [[term:plurality]]. In a crowded field, a candidate with a devoted 30 percent faction beats five rivals splitting the other 70, so the rational strategy is not breadth but *heat*: own a faction, pray the field splits. Part four: every incumbent watches it happen to a colleague and updates. The lesson they learn is never be outflanked — don't cross the base, don't compromise visibly, don't give a challenger a lane. The machine doesn't need to fire often; like any deterrent, it works through fear.

One honesty checkpoint before the prescription, because this book doesn't get to caricature its own villain. The folk theory says primary voters are frothing extremists; the best survey research says otherwise. Sides, Tausanovitch, Vavreck, and Warshaw, matching turnout records to surveys across multiple cycles, found primary voters demographically and ideologically similar to their party's rank and file. The machine's problem isn't monstrous voters — it's *arithmetic*: a tiny, party-sorted electorate, counted by a method that rewards factional intensity, deciding seats the general electorate can't contest. Fix the arithmetic and you fix the incentive, no demonization required.

<Checkpoint question="A reform group proposes moving primaries to June and doubling their turnout, changing nothing else. Walk the machine's four parts: which ones does higher turnout actually fix?">
  Only part two, and only partly. The seats are still safe (part one), so the primary is still the election. The count is still plurality (part three), so a crowded field still rewards the candidate with the most intense faction — now a faction of a bigger electorate. And incumbents still fear the flank (part four), because the threat comes from being outflanked in a one-mark count, not from turnout per se. Turnout helps; it cannot, by itself, change what the count rewards. That's why this chapter's prescription targets the ballot, not the calendar.
</Checkpoint>

## The working model: St. Louis

Here is the part of this book where I get to stop simulating and point. St. Louis, Missouri — under Proposition D, passed with 68 percent in November 2020 — runs the model this chapter prescribes. All candidates, regardless of party, appear on one March ballot. Every voter may approve as many as they want. The top two finishers, full stop, advance to an April runoff that the whole city decides. No party gatekeeping, no spoilers, no "wasted vote" lecture — and the decisive round happens in the *higher*-turnout election, not the lower one.

It has now run three citywide cycles. Watch what the data does:

In the old St. Louis — partisan primaries, pick one — that 2021 field would have been a classic faction-splitter: three serious candidates dividing overlapping coalitions, winner possibly in the low thirties, game over in a city where one party's nomination decides the office. Under approval, the two candidates with the broadest support advanced, and Tishaura Jones won the April runoff 51.7 to 47.8 in an election with roughly 30 percent more ballots cast than the March round. The decisive choice moved downstream, to the bigger electorate — the exact opposite of the machine.

Then 2025 delivered the part no simulation can: accountability. Mayor Jones stood for reelection and the same system that elected her removed her — Cara Spencer drew approvals from 68.2 percent of March ballots to the incumbent's 33.2, then won the runoff 64.15 to 35.85. No spoiler dynamics to hide behind, no claim that a divided field stole it: the whole city, asked about every candidate, said no. I'll be careful here, because the published research on St. Louis candidate *behavior* is still thin and I won't dress anecdotes as findings. What the verified results show is structural: in two open contests and one incumbent defense, the broad-support candidates reached the runoff, the runoff happened at higher turnout, and an unpopular incumbent could be challenged without anyone gaming a split field. That is the extremism machine running backwards — and the legislature in Jefferson City found it threatening enough to ban the method statewide for everyone else, grandfathering St. Louis alone.

## Alaska's top-four, respectfully

The other live reform is Alaska's: one all-candidate primary, top four advance, ranked-choice general. This book's earlier chapters gave Alaska its due, and the headline case stands — Lisa Murkowski, censured by her own party for an impeachment vote, the textbook target of part four of the machine, survived the open primary and won the 2022 final count 53.69 to 46.31 because the *whole* electorate, not a partisan sliver, got the decisive say. Alaska proves the architecture: move the real choice to November, and the flank loses its veto.

But look closely at Alaska's primary ballot and you'll find the machine's part three still in it: *vote for one*. The top-four primary counts by plurality — it just takes four survivors instead of one, which blunts faction capture without removing the incentive. The largest field Alaskans faced under the new rules was forty-eight candidates for one House seat, and each voter could mark exactly one of them. That is precisely the situation where a pick-one count is weakest, and precisely where an approval or [[term:score-voting]] count belongs: vast field, low information, the goal being to find the four candidates with the broadest acceptance rather than the four loudest factions. St. Louis and Alaska aren't rivals; they're two halves of one design — Alaska moved the decision to the right round, St. Louis put the right ballot in it.

<Checkpoint question="Redesign Alaska's 48-candidate special primary with approval voting, leaving the top-four-plus-RCV general untouched. What specifically changes about who advances — and what objection from RCV advocates should you expect?">
  With pick-one, 48 candidates fracture the electorate so finely that a few percent of intense support can make the top four; factional standard-bearers are favored over broadly acceptable candidates. With approval, every voter grades the whole field, so the four survivors are the four with the widest acceptance — center-squeeze pressure is removed at the stage with the most candidates, where ranking everyone would be impractical anyway. Expect the later-no-harm objection: approving a second candidate can help them edge out your favorite for a slot. True — and the price of refusing it is the current rule, where supporting anyone but your favorite is impossible. With four slots, the trade strongly favors breadth.
</Checkpoint>

## What this chapter settles — and what it can't

Settled, on verified numbers: the machine is real (21.3 percent primary turnout; 83 percent of House seats decided upstream by 8 percent of Americans in 2022), its engine is arithmetic rather than evil voters, and the one American jurisdiction running an approval-into-runoff system has now demonstrated open competition, higher-turnout decisive rounds, and the peaceable removal of an incumbent. Not settled: whether St. Louis's results generalize beyond one heavily one-party city — three cycles is a record, not a literature — and whether any state will pair Alaska's top-N architecture with a cardinal primary ballot, the combination this book regards as the machine's true off switch. The chapters ahead take up the forces that guard the status quo: the money, and the stories told about reform.
