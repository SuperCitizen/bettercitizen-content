In October 2017, Maine's legislature did something cynical, even by the standards of legislatures.

Less than a year earlier, Maine's voters had passed ranked-choice voting at the ballot box. Now the legislature passed a law shelving the reform until 2021. The law would also repeal it automatically, unless a constitutional amendment arrived first. [beat] And that was an amendment the legislature itself had no intention of passing.

Reform groups answered with a people's veto campaign, in the dead of a Maine winter.

In June 2018, the voters overrode their own legislature, with 53.88 percent. And that same day, they walked into the booth and used ranked ballots to pick their party nominees.

It was the first statewide ranked-choice election in American history. [beat] It was held on the same day as the vote to save it.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter followed my own side's ballots into two American cities. It ended by promising a full chapter on the rival that is actually winning statehouses.

I am writing a book about why score voting is better than ranked-choice voting. I will get there, and I won't pull punches. But a movement that can't acknowledge what its rivals built doesn't deserve your trust. So let's start with the truth.

> Ranked-choice voting put electoral reform on the American map.

And it fought for every inch.

A word on names. The count behind ranked-choice voting is called instant runoff. You will hear both.

We will take it in three steps. First, the credit: what ranked-choice voting has actually won. Second, the other half of the ledger: three weaknesses, each documented in a real election. And last, where I land.

## What it has won

Here is the record through mid-2026.

Maine adopted ranked-choice voting by ballot initiative in November 2016, with 52.12 percent of the vote. Then it defended the reform twice: with the people's veto of 2018, and then in a court fight.

Alaska adopted its system in 2020, by 3,781 votes. And it kept it in 2024, when a repeal initiative failed after a recount. The margin was 743 votes, out of more than 321,000.

New York City, the largest electorate ever to use ranked ballots in this country, adopted it in 2019, with 73.5 percent of the vote.

And by FairVote's count, in February 2026, 49 American jurisdictions use ranked-choice voting, the two states included.

[pause]

The substantive wins are real too, not just the political ones. Three of them.

First, cost and turnout. One ballot, with an instant runoff, replaces a separate runoff election. Separate runoffs are expensive, and notoriously low in turnout. That argument alone has carried ranked-choice voting in city after city.

Second, tone. Three researchers, Donovan, Tolbert and Gracey, surveyed voters in ranked-choice cities and in matched plurality cities. People in the ranked-choice cities were significantly more satisfied with the conduct of local campaigns.

I'll be honest that the civility research since then has been mixed. But the structural incentive is not in dispute. When you need your rival's supporters to rank you second, there is a price for scorching the earth.

Third, and above all, ranked-choice voting proved the thing every reform needs proved first. American voters will change the ballot itself, on purpose. And they will defend the change when the political class tries to claw it back.

Nineteen state legislatures have now banned ranked-choice voting. That, too, is a kind of tribute. [beat] Nobody bans an idea that isn't winning.

Let's test what that record teaches. A consultant tells a reform group to skip the city campaigns: "just win one statewide initiative and you're done." Based on Maine's experience, what is wrong with that plan? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Winning the initiative was the beginning of Maine's fight, not the end. The legislature shelved the reform within a year. It survived only because voters mobilized a people's veto, and even then it needed a court fight.

> Reform is not a transaction. It is a siege.

Any reform you campaign for, ranked, approval, or score, needs a coalition built to defend it after election night.

## Alaska: the center squeeze

That is the credit. Now the other half of the ledger, held to the same standard: verified cases, not vibes.

Alaska, August 2022. A special election for the U.S. House, with three candidates. The Democrat was Mary Peltola. The two Republicans were Sarah Palin and Nick Begich.

It was ranked-choice voting's trial by fire, and the count worked exactly as designed. Begich was eliminated first, with 28.53 percent of the first choices. His ballots were transferred. And in the final round, Peltola beat Palin, 51.48 to 48.52.

Here is the problem. Two mathematicians, Adam Graham-Squire and David McCune, analyzed the full ballot data. They showed that Begich would have beaten Peltola head to head. He would have beaten Palin head to head, too.

A candidate whom a majority prefers to every rival has a name: the Condorcet winner. Begich was the Condorcet winner. [beat] And the instant runoff eliminated him first.

The reason is simple. Consensus candidates run short on first-choice votes.

> The center squeeze: a consensus candidate, short on first choices, is eliminated first.

It isn't a hypothetical anymore. It happened, in a statewide federal election, on the reform movement's biggest stage.

The same two mathematicians wrote a follow-up study, and it matters just as much, for honesty's sake. They examined 182 American instant-runoff elections, from 2004 to 2022. They found exactly two that failed to elect the Condorcet winner. One was that Alaska race. The other was Burlington, in 2009.

Rare, then. [beat] But notice when it strikes. It strikes in polarized, three-way races: a strong left, a strong right, and a broadly acceptable middle. Those are precisely the races that reform exists to fix.

Ranked-choice voting's failure mode is concentrated on its own sales pitch.

## Burlington: more votes, worse outcome

The other documented case is older, and stranger.

Burlington, Vermont, 2009, a race for mayor. Instant runoff elected the Progressive, Bob Kiss. Yet the ballot data showed the Democrat, Andy Montroll, beating Kiss head to head, 54 to 46. Montroll also beat the Republican, Kurt Wright, 56 to 44. That is another center squeeze.

But the ballots hid a second pathology, documented in a peer-reviewed case study by Ornstein and Norman.

Suppose roughly 750 of Wright's supporters had ranked Kiss first instead. Suppose, that is, that Kiss had gained support. [beat] Kiss would have lost.

Here is why. The change would have eliminated Wright earlier. That would have sent Montroll into the final round instead. And Montroll beat Kiss head to head.

Say it once more, because it sounds like a paradox. Voting for a candidate would have defeated him.

> Non-monotonicity: more support for a candidate can make that candidate lose.

Instant runoff is the only reform system on the table that has that defect. And Burlington's voters repealed instant runoff in 2010, 52 to 48.

Now be careful about what Burlington establishes. The defect is rare, and no voter did anything wrong.

Nor does Burlington show that plurality would have handled that race better. Plurality fails the same election differently. A pick-one count would never even have revealed that Montroll was the consensus candidate.

The honest claim is narrow, but real. Instant runoff's count can punish support itself. And no method that rates candidates can: not approval, not score, not STAR.

## The quiet leak

The third weakness doesn't make headlines, because it doesn't produce a scandal. It produces a slow leak.

Suppose you rank only candidates who get eliminated. Your ballot is then called exhausted. It simply drops out of the count.

So the final-round majority is a majority of the ballots still standing, not of the voters who showed up.

Two researchers, Craig Burnett and Vladimir Kogan, put real numbers on this in 2015. They examined more than 600,000 actual ballot images, from four instant-runoff elections.

Exhaustion ran from 9.6 percent of valid ballots, in San Leandro in 2010, to 27.1 percent, in San Francisco in 2011.

Here is the consequence. In all four elections, the winner held a majority of the continuing ballots. In none of them did the winner hold a majority of all the votes cast.

> Exhausted ballots: the final majority is a majority of ballots still standing.

Hear that the way a skeptic would. Ranked-choice voting's core promise, the majority winner, quietly shrinks to a majority of unexhausted ballots.

In Alaska's special election, 11,243 of Begich's ballots ranked no second choice, and vanished from the final round. And the 182-election study flags this as instant runoff's most common real-world failure. Not the exotic paradoxes. [beat] Routine majorities that aren't.

Let's work one. A city's instant-runoff race ends with the winner taking 52 percent in the final round. But 18 percent of the ballots were exhausted before that round.

A ranked-choice advocate says: "the winner had majority support." A plurality defender says: "see, RCV is a fraud." What is the precise truth? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

Both are overclaiming.

The winner had a majority of the continuing ballots. But only 82 percent of the ballots were still alive. And 52 percent of that is about 43 percent of all the votes cast. So majority support is unproven.

The plurality defender's gotcha proves too little, though. Under pick-one rules, the winner might have taken 35 percent, with no information about anyone's second preferences at all.

So the honest comparison is not ranked-choice voting against an imaginary majority machine. It is ranked-choice voting against systems that count every ballot in every round. Ranked ballots collect the data for that. Ballots that rate candidates actually do it.

[pause]

All of this has one cause. Instant-runoff counting reads ranked ballots one line at a time.

The information that Begich beat everyone head to head was sitting on the ballots. The count threw it away, by filtering candidates on first-choice strength before ever consulting the rest.

It is not a tabulation bug, and no voter-education campaign fixes it.

## Ally, not answer

That makes three weaknesses, each from a real election. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

The center squeeze, in Alaska. Non-monotonicity, in Burlington. And exhausted ballots, the quiet leak.

So here is where I land, and it is not where the louder critics land.

The people who call Alaska 2022 a "rigged" election are wrong on the arithmetic. Peltola won the final head-to-head count. And the old closed-primary system would almost certainly have nominated Palin, and never told us a consensus candidate existed.

Hold on to that distinction. "RCV failed here" is one claim. "The old system would have done better" is a different claim, and I am not making it.

> Ranked-choice voting is better than what it replaced.

The jurisdictions that adopted it are better off than they were.

But "better than plurality" is a low bar. And the documented record points at a common root. One Condorcet failure in Alaska. A Condorcet failure and a monotonicity failure in Burlington. Exhaustion rates of up to 27 percent.

> Ranked ballots collect rich information, and elimination-style counting wastes it.

The fix is not to retreat to pick-one voting. It is to stop filtering on first-choice strength, and to count everything voters say about every candidate, all at once. That is exactly what score voting does, and the comparison chapters make that case in detail.

One question to leave open. The center squeeze is rare: two elections in 182. It also strikes in exactly the races that reform exists to fix. Which of those facts should weigh more? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The credit. Ranked-choice voting put electoral reform on the American map. And reform is not a transaction. It is a siege.

[beat]

Two. The center squeeze. A consensus candidate, short on first choices, is eliminated first. Alaska 2022 and Burlington 2009: two cases in 182 elections.

[beat]

Three. Non-monotonicity, in Burlington. More support for a candidate can make that candidate lose.

[beat]

Four. Exhausted ballots. The final majority is a majority of ballots still standing.

[beat]

And five. "It failed here" and "the old system would have done better" are different claims. Ranked-choice voting is better than what it replaced. Ranked ballots collect rich information, and elimination-style counting wastes it.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it doesn't.

Settled: ranked-choice voting's achievements are real. Its three structural weaknesses are documented in real elections. And both things are true at once.

Not settled: whether any alternative actually avoids these failure modes without buying worse ones. Every voting system makes trade-offs. The next chapter takes up the reform that tried to engineer the trade-offs away. It is called STAR.

[pause]

For now, go back to Maine, in June 2018. Voters saved a reform and used it, on the same day.

[beat]

I can argue with the count they chose. I cannot argue with what they built.
