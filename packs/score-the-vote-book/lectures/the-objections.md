Every talk I give ends the same way.

The slides are done. The St. Louis results are still on the screen. And a hand goes up. Usually it belongs to someone who has done real homework, and out comes one of five objections.

[beat]

I've come to love this moment, because all five objections are good.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. So hold me to a rule. Each objection arrives the way its smartest proponent would state it. Only then do I answer.

And I cannot answer all of them outright. Two of them I have to concede before I can answer, and along the way I grant smaller points where they are due. The concessions are where this chapter earns its keep.

An advocacy book that hides its concessions is a pamphlet.

Here is the order. Two objections are legal, and they will sound familiar from the last chapter. One is about strategy. One is about the ballot itself. And the last is about majority rule.

## One person, one vote

The first objection, at full strength.

The Constitution guarantees equal protection. As that guarantee has been elaborated, from Reynolds v. Sims onward, it demands that no citizen's vote count more than another's. A score ballot lets one voter hand out a 10, and another hand out sixty points across six candidates. That looks like unequal voting power. And courts take ballot equality seriously.

[pause]

My answer is doctrinal, not rhetorical.

> One person, one vote governs the weight of votes, not the format of ballots.

Weight means malapportioned districts, where one person's ballot elects a representative for half as many people. Challenges to counting mechanics run through a different test, a balancing test, from a case called Burdick v. Takushi in 1992. It gives states broad room over election machinery, so long as every voter faces the same rules.

The Ninth Circuit applied exactly this framework to San Francisco's ranked ballots, in Dudum v. Arntz, in 2011. And it was blunt: "the option to rank multiple preferences is not the same as providing additional votes, or more heavily-weighted votes, relative to other votes cast."

Minnesota's supreme court said the same of Minneapolis's instant runoff, in 2009.

And the reasoning extends to score ballots more cleanly than to ranked ones, because score voting is perfectly symmetric. Every voter gets the identical instrument: the same scale, for every candidate.

Your sixty points spread across six candidates, and my lone 10, are the same power, differently spent.

The Congressional Research Service has surveyed these challenges. It finds federal courts uniformly rejecting the "extra votes" theory. Equal protection requires equal rules, and score voting's rules are the most symmetric on offer.

## Courts already killed this, in 1915

The second objection is the opposition's favorite legal exhibit. And unlike most scare cites, it's real.

The case is Brown v. Smallwood. In 1915, Minnesota's supreme court struck down Duluth's Bucklin system. That was a preferential ballot. If no one won a majority of first choices, voters' second and additional choices were added into the totals.

Adding up multiple expressions of support per voter is structurally closer to approval and score voting than anything else a U.S. court has ever reviewed. And the court killed it. When a voter votes, it wrote, "his vote must be counted one, and it cannot be defeated or its effect lessened, except by the vote of another elector voting for one."

[pause]

My answer starts with a concession. In Minnesota, this precedent is alive. Approval or score voting there would face a genuine constitutional fight.

Now the boundaries, which the opposition reliably omits.

First, Brown construed the Minnesota constitution. It binds no other state and no federal court. And no federal court has ever held an additive ballot unconstitutional.

Second, Minnesota's own supreme court confined Brown to its mechanics, in that Minneapolis case of 2009. It upheld ranked ballots. And it treated the 1915 case as being about how Bucklin counted, not as a general ban on expressive ballots.

Third, the living counterexample. St. Louis has run approval voting citywide since 2021, through contested elections, including the defeat of a sitting mayor. No court has struck it down.

> One century-old state case is a litigation risk in one state.

Not a legal death sentence. Plan for it. Don't be paralyzed by it.

An op-ed warns: "The Supreme Court ruled in Brown v. Smallwood that letting voters support multiple candidates is unconstitutional, so approval and score voting are non-starters." How many errors is that? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Two. It was the Minnesota Supreme Court, applying the Minnesota constitution, and that binds nowhere else. And Minnesota's own court later read the case narrowly, while upholding ranked ballots in Minneapolis.

But the case is real, and Bucklin's additive count makes it the closest historical cousin of approval voting. That is exactly why it matters: as a one-state litigation risk, not a nationwide bar.

## Strategy

The third objection. Here the real concessions begin.

Voting theory's deepest result comes from Gibbard, in 1973. It proves that every real voting rule can be manipulated. And score voting's manipulation is embarrassingly easy to find. With any sense of who the front-runners are, your best ballot for winning is max-or-nothing. Top marks for your side of the race. Zeros for the rest. No middle scores at all.

> The strategy objection: sharp strategists turn the 0-to-10 ballot into a checklist.

[pause]

I concede the theory, formally and without wriggling. The optimal score ballot, for a voter who only wants to win, is all maximums and minimums. And an electorate of perfect strategists makes score voting behave like approval voting.

Here is why I'm still standing. Look at what the collapse collapses to.

Approval voting is the floor of strategic score voting. And approval voting is an excellent system. It is spoiler-proof. It is good enough in practice to anchor St. Louis's elections.

Now run the same stress test on the alternatives, because Gibbard exempts no one.

Plurality's strategic equilibrium is the one you live in. Abandon your favorite. Vote for the lesser evil. Duopoly forever.

Instant runoff's strategic failure is favorite betrayal, in what is called the center squeeze. In Alaska, in August 2022, Palin voters would have done better ranking their favorite lower.

> My answer: score voting's worst case under strategy is another good voting system.

The collapse is the floor, not the ceiling. Every honest voter adds intensity information back in, and real electorates are full of honest voters. There are simulations by Quinn, of a measure called Voter Satisfaction Efficiency. They show score voting with strategists performing like approval, which still sits far above plurality.

A system should be judged by its floor under pressure. [beat] This floor is the point.

## The ballot itself

The fourth objection: voters can't handle a 0-to-10 ballot.

Real ballots are filled out by tired people in a hurry, not by theorists. Every degree of freedom invites error, and demographic gaps in error rates. The research on ranked ballots already shows modestly elevated overvote and spoilage rates versus plurality, concentrated in first uses. Eleven bubbles per candidate is begging for mistakes. And "it's like rating a restaurant" is marketing, not evidence.

[pause]

My answer rests on the two American electorates that have actually used cardinal ballots, the family that score ballots belong to. Fargo's voters were the first in the country. They averaged 2.3 approvals per ballot, in June 2020. St. Louis's averaged 1.56, in the mayoral primary of March 2021.

Both electorates used the new latitude immediately. No transition crisis. No wave of spoiled ballots reported by either city's election authority.

And note a structural fact. On an approval or score ballot, marking many candidates is legal by definition. The most common disqualifying error in American elections is the overvote. Here it ceases to exist, because what used to be an overvote is now just a ballot.

Now I'll concede what's left of the objection. A 0-to-10 scale asks for more decisions than a checkbox. Some voters will decline. They'll score one candidate 10, and stop.

But follow that worst case through.

> A bullet-voted score ballot is a plurality ballot.

The voter who ignores every new option is exactly where today's system puts everyone by force. Nobody is made worse off. And the expressiveness is there for the millions who want it.

## Majority rule

The fifth objection, and the deepest.

Democratic legitimacy means majority rule. Score voting can elect a candidate who is the first choice of a minority. It can choose a broadly liked compromise over a candidate that 51 percent of voters ranked at the very top.

> The majority objection: the majority's favorite lost, and that should disqualify the method.

[pause]

I concede this one formally too. Score voting fails the majority criterion. And you should see the failure in numbers, not take it on faith.

One hundred voters. Two candidates, A and B. Fifty-one voters give A a 10, and B an 8. Forty-nine voters give B a 10, and A a zero.

Add it up. A has 510. B has 898. [beat] B wins, though A was the majority's favorite.

Now look at what the ballots actually say. The majority barely prefers A. It rates B an 8. The minority regards A as a catastrophe. That is intensity, and the plurality ballot has no way to even record it.

Electing B trades a 2-point disappointment for 51 people against a 10-point disaster for 49.

B is the utilitarian winner: the greatest total satisfaction. A is the majoritarian winner. Choosing the utilitarian winner is a philosophical commitment, and this book makes it with open eyes. Elections should maximize how well the whole community is represented. They should not hand a bare majority everything, on intensity it doesn't feel.

You may weigh those values differently.

> Utilitarian or majoritarian: a disagreement about democratic theory, not arithmetic.

So take the side of that choice you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

But before the majority criterion disqualifies score voting, apply it consistently.

Plurality routinely elects candidates most voters opposed. And instant runoff's celebrated majorities are majorities of continuing ballots. Burnett and Kogan examined four such elections. Between 9.6 and 27.1 percent of ballots had exhausted before the final round. No winner held a majority of all votes cast.

No system on the table delivers guaranteed true majorities. The honest question is which compromise you choose on purpose.

An advocate for instant runoff tells you: "ranked-choice voting guarantees majority winners, unlike score voting." What is the precise, fair response? Think it through.

[long pause]

Both halves are too strong. Instant runoff guarantees a majority of the ballots still active in the final round. Score voting fails the majority criterion by design. It prefers the utilitarian winner when majority preference is weak and minority opposition is intense.

So neither system delivers an unconditional true majority. They fail the slogan in different, principled directions. The fair debate is over which failure you'd rather own, and that is a question about values.

One more question before we gather up. Under the strategy objection, I conceded a collapse. What does strategic score voting collapse into?

[long pause]

Approval voting.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep, one for each objection: what I concede, and where the argument lands.

One. One person, one vote. I concede nothing. The doctrine governs the weight of votes, not the format of ballots, and the score ballot is symmetric.

[beat]

Two. Brown v. Smallwood. I concede a real litigation risk in Minnesota. One state, read narrowly by its own court, and St. Louis operates today.

[beat]

Three. Strategic collapse. I concede the theory, fully. And my answer: score voting's worst case under strategy is another good voting system.

[beat]

Four. Ballot complexity. I concede more decisions than a checkbox, and that some voters will bullet-vote. But overvotes become legal, and a bullet-voted score ballot is a plurality ballot.

[beat]

And five. No guaranteed majority. I concede the majority criterion, formally. Utilitarian or majoritarian is a choice of values, and the rivals' majorities are softer than advertised.

[pause]

By my count, the case still stands after those concessions. That count is yours to check.

And here is what this chapter did not settle. No American government has yet run a public score-voting election. So the strategic-collapse concession rests on theory and simulation, not on a documented score electorate. The Minnesota question is unlitigated for cardinal ballots specifically. And utilitarian versus majoritarian is, finally, a question of political philosophy. Honest people will keep disagreeing about it after every fact in this book is granted.

What I hope is no longer open is one question. Can score voting's advocates hear the best version of every objection, and answer it, or concede it, in the open?

For now, go back to the end of that talk. The slides are done, and a hand goes up.
