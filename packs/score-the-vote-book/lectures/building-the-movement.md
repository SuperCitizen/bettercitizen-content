In 2015, a seat on the Fargo city commission went to a candidate with 22 percent of the vote. It was a six-way field.

The next year was worse. Eleven candidates ran for two seats, and the winners took 16 and 15 percent.

[beat]

Commissioners chosen by roughly one voter in six.

[pause]

The commission was embarrassed. In late 2016, it created a task force to figure out what was wrong. The task force held ten public meetings, studied the alternatives, and recommended approval voting. The commission received the recommendation. [beat] And did precisely nothing.

That should have been the end of the story, because that is where reform recommendations go to die.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting and its sibling, approval voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was defense: the best objections to score voting, answered or conceded in the open. This chapter turns to offense. How does a movement actually win?

It starts with what happened next in Fargo.

An engineer from that task force, Jed Limke, founded a group called Reform Fargo. He recruited a volunteer corps. They collected 2,600 signatures, to put the question to voters directly. And in November 2018, Measure 1 passed with 63.5 percent.

No statewide organization ordered this. No billionaire seeded it. A city had a visible, undeniable plurality failure. One credible local person refused to drop the subject. And a national nonprofit showed up with expertise, at exactly the right moment.

> That is the anatomy of every cardinal-voting win to date.

This chapter is about reproducing it, deliberately. We will take it in three steps. First, the organizations, and who actually does what. Second, what Fargo and St. Louis teach. And last, the rules that keep allies allied.

## Who actually does what

A movement is not a vibe. It is a small number of organizations with budgets, and you should know them by name and by function. There are three, and then a fourth kind.

First, the Center for Election Science, or CES. It is the approval-voting organization, founded in 2011 and professionalized by grants from Open Philanthropy. Think of it as the movement's campaign-support shop. It backed Reform Fargo in 2018. It supported STL Approves, the coalition that passed Proposition D in St. Louis in 2020, with just over 68 percent. And it advised Utah's Forward Party on an approval-voting vacancy election, in December 2025.

> For a city approval campaign, CES is the phone call.

[beat]

Second, the Equal Vote Coalition. It is the STAR-voting organization, grown out of Mark Frohnmayer's Unified Primary campaign in Oregon, in 2014. It has a separate arm, STAR Voting Action, built to run campaigns. Equal Vote is the movement's research lab and standards body: ballot designs, tabulation tools, a framework for comparing methods.

Its campaign record is losses, most painfully in Eugene, in May 2024. But its infrastructure and its volunteer chapters are real assets, and the whole cardinal family draws on them.

[beat]

Third, FairVote. FairVote is the incumbent: three decades old, and the engine behind the spread of ranked-choice voting. And, let's be adults, it is a critic of approval voting, not an ally of convenience. Its objection goes by the name later-no-harm.

Around FairVote sits the broader money of democracy reform. Unite America and RepresentUs fund campaigns for primary and voting reforms generally.

[beat]

And the fourth kind: the civic groups, like the League of Women Voters. They supply the local credibility that no national organization can. In St. Louis, the League's endorsement of Proposition D mattered more than any white paper.

So, once more, as an org chart. CES for approval campaigns. Equal Vote for STAR infrastructure. FairVote as respected competition. And the civic groups for legitimacy.

## What two wins teach

That is who. Now how. Strip the two wins, Fargo and St. Louis, down to mechanism, and four lessons fall out.

The first lesson. The trigger was a legible failure, not an abstract argument. Fargo organized around one line: "our commissioners win with 16 percent." St. Louis organized around decades of crowded Democratic primaries, won without a majority, that decided the city's real elections.

So find the number in your city that makes people wince. The theory comes later.

[beat]

The second lesson. The messenger was local, and the expertise was imported. Limke was a Fargoan who had served on the city's own task force. STL Approves was St. Louis organizers, with allies like the League, while CES supplied method expertise and resources.

> A local face out front. Imported expertise behind.

Reverse the roles, with a national face and local hired help, and you get the pattern that lost statewide in 2024.

[beat]

The third lesson. It costs less than you fear, and more than zero. Reform Fargo needed 1,349 valid signatures. It collected 2,600, with about forty volunteers, in a city of 125,000. That is a shoestring operation. St. Louis is a bigger and more contested city, and it took a professional campaign with national nonprofit backing.

I won't pretend to give you audited budgets, because campaign-finance reports at city scale are patchy. The honest range runs from Fargo's five-figure volunteer effort to a serious six-figure city campaign. And both bought the same two assets: a signature drive, and an education program.

[beat]

The fourth lesson.

> Winning is the start of the fight, not the end.

In 2023 came a repeal bill. Fargo's reform survived it only because Governor Doug Burgum vetoed the bill, as an infringement on local control. Then, in 2025, a new legislature and a new governor finished the job. St. Louis survived for a different reason. The drafters of Missouri's Amendment 7 chose to grandfather it.

So every adoption needs a defense plan. Allies in the legislature. A mayor willing to testify, as Fargo's did. And a story of clean elections that makes preemption look like what it is: politicians overruling their own voters.

[pause]

Let's put the anatomy to work. Your city's school-board races keep producing winners with under 25 percent, in fields of eight candidates. A retired principal, someone everyone respects, is furious about it. You have CES's playbook, and forty volunteers.

What is the sequence? And where will the fight actually be decided? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

Start with the trigger: those winners under 25 percent. Make that number famous. Then the messenger. The principal is a local face with standing, so the principal fronts the campaign, while CES supplies ballot language and method expertise.

Then the mechanism. Check the charter and the state election code first. Then run a signature drive sized like Fargo's, which means collecting roughly double your minimum.

And the fight is decided twice. Once at the ballot box. And again at the statehouse, afterward. So the defense plan starts the day you win, not the day preemption is introduced.

## The rules that keep allies allied

That is the playbook. Now a caution, and it is about our own side.

The reform movement's deadliest failure mode isn't the duopoly. [beat] It is friendly fire.

Seattle, in 2022, is the standing monument. An approval initiative qualified for the ballot. The council countered with ranked choice. And the head-to-head ended 75.4 to 24.6, against approval. Months of money and volunteer hours, spent making another reform look good.

So here are three rules I would write into any cardinal organization's bylaws.

Rule one. Don't poach live ranked-choice ground. Where ranked choice has organization and legal room, cardinal campaigns should pick different cities. And there is no shortage. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, and that is territory it cannot touch.

[beat]

Rule two. Never co-sign a ban. However annoying FairVote's criticism gets, a cardinal advocate who testifies for a ranked-choice ban is teaching legislatures how to ban ballots generally. North Dakota proved it. Its ban bill named approval voting.

> Never co-sign a ban. Ban architecture generalizes.

[beat]

Rule three. Keep the comparison honest. Steel-man ranked choice in public, as this book does for a whole chapter, and bank the credibility for when you need to disagree.

[pause]

Let's test the rules. Here are four things a cardinal advocate might do.

One: run an approval campaign in a state whose statute bans only ranked-choice voting. Two: testify alongside the sponsors of a ranked-choice ban, because the ban would clear the field for approval. Three: say in public that ranked choice fixed real problems in Maine and Alaska, while arguing that approval is simpler. Four: decline to qualify an approval initiative in a city where a ranked-choice campaign is already organized.

Which one breaks the rules? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The second. Helping to pass a ban on a rival reform is the one move that damages every reform.

The other three are the rules working as intended. Fill open territory. Steel-man your allies. And don't split reform votes on shared ground.

[pause]

One more question before we gather up. This chapter opened with an eleven-candidate race, and the two winning shares that embarrassed a city commission. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

16 and 15 percent. Roughly one voter in six. That was Fargo's legible failure, and everything else was built on it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The anatomy of a win. A legible local failure. A credible local messenger. Imported expertise. A signature drive sized to double the requirement. And a defense plan for the morning after.

[beat]

Two. The org chart. CES for approval campaigns. Equal Vote for STAR infrastructure. FairVote as respected competition. The civic groups for legitimacy.

[beat]

Three. The cost: less than you fear, and more than zero. A signature drive, and an education program.

[beat]

Four. Winning is the start of the fight, not the end. The fight is decided twice: at the ballot box, and at the statehouse afterward.

[beat]

And five. Three rules. Don't poach live ranked-choice ground. Never co-sign a ban. Keep the comparison honest.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles. That anatomy is two for two in American cities, and nothing else has won even once.

And here is what it does not settle: scale. Two cities in eight years is an existence proof, not a movement.

So try this. Two cities in eight years: is that a foundation, or a ceiling? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Where should the playbook be run next? Which states' laws are open, which doors are closed, and what does a sober decade actually look like? That is the next chapter's map. After that, the only variable left in this book is you.

For now, go back to Fargo, and a recommendation that a commission received and set aside.

The commission did nothing.

One engineer refused to drop the subject. That was enough to start.
