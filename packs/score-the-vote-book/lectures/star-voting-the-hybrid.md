In October 2014, a conference room at the University of Oregon hosted something rare in the reform world. Rival camps were actually arguing with each other, instead of past each other.

It was the Equal Vote Conference. It put score voting's advocates in the same room as ranked-choice people, FairVote's Rob Richie among them. And out of that collision came a new method.

It was proposed by an Oregon entrepreneur, Mark Frohnmayer, and at first it was called Score Runoff Voting. Today it has a shorter name: STAR. The letters stand for Score, Then Automatic Runoff.

[pause]

STAR is the youngest serious voting method in America. It is the only one engineered in direct response to a criticism of score voting. And it is the system I get asked about more than any other, usually in this form: "If you like score voting, why not STAR?"

Fair question.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter gave ranked-choice voting its due, and ended on a caution: every voting system makes trade-offs. This chapter takes up the reform that tried to engineer the trade-offs away.

I will tell you why this book argues for score voting and not for STAR. And I will answer honestly, which means starting with what STAR gets right.

We will take it in four steps. First, the mechanics, and what the runoff is for. Second, the record, with no varnish. Third, the trade STAR makes, stated precisely. And last, my verdict.

## Score, then automatic runoff

A STAR ballot asks you to score every candidate from 0 to 5. Five stars, exactly like every rating interface you have used this week.

Not 0 to 10. Pure score voting can use any range, and I have argued elsewhere for finer scales. But STAR, as actually proposed and balloted, is 0 to 5. Get this right. Opponents pounce on advocates who can't describe their own reform.

Then the count runs in two stages, and the second stage is the entire point.

Stage one is a score election. Every voter scores every candidate. Equal scores are allowed, and a blank counts as 0. All the scores are added up. Every ballot counts toward every candidate, with no rounds and no eliminations.

The two candidates with the highest totals become the finalists. That happens automatically, with no second trip to the polls.

This is where score voting would declare the highest total the winner. [beat] STAR keeps going.

Stage two is the automatic runoff. Your ballot is consulted one more time, and your full vote goes to whichever finalist you scored higher. Suppose you scored them 4 and 2. Your vote goes to the 4.

Suppose you scored them equally. Then you abstain from the runoff, and your ballot is counted as an explicit "no preference."

The finalist preferred on more ballots wins. That holds even if the other finalist had the higher score total.

> Under STAR, intensity picks the finalists. Head-to-head preference picks the winner.

Put another way, the scores decide who reaches the final two. Then a plain majority of preferences decides between them.

Why bolt a runoff onto a score election? Because of the most serious objection to pure score voting, one I will concede formally in the objections chapter. A strategic voter's best score ballot tends toward the extremes: the maximum for your side, and zero for the rest.

STAR changes that calculus. Let me show you with one ballot.

A voter loves candidate A, likes B, and despises C. Under STAR she marks A 5, B 5, and C 0. She wants to give B the maximum help against C.

Then the finalists turn out to be A and B. What did her strategy actually cost her? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Her entire say in choosing the winner. The runoff assigns each ballot to the finalist it scored higher. Her ballot scored A and B identically, so it abstains.

Now take the honest ballot: A 5, B 3, C 0. It still puts B well above C. And it still casts a full-strength runoff vote for A over B.

That is the mechanism doing its job.

> Under STAR, a flattened ballot can silence itself in the runoff.

Exaggeration isn't free, so honest gradations are the smart play. And the strategy-resistance isn't a slogan. In Jameson Quinn's Voter Satisfaction Efficiency simulations, STAR holds up near the top of the field, even with substantial shares of strategic voters.

> The case for STAR: the runoff makes honesty rational.

That is genuinely clever, and score voting's advocates should say so without mumbling.

## The record, with no varnish

That is the design. Now the part that advocates of every system are tempted to mumble: the electoral track record.

As of mid-2026, no government in the United States, or anywhere, elects its officials with STAR voting.

[pause]

That is not for lack of trying, and the trying has all happened in Oregon. Walk it in order.

November 2018. Lane County votes on adopting STAR for county elections. The measure fails, 47.6 percent to 52.4. That is the closest STAR has come to public adoption.

2020. The Independent Party of Oregon, the state's third-largest party, runs its statewide primary with STAR. It is the method's first real statewide political election, and it is conducted without reported ballot problems.

February 2024. A campaign for a statewide initiative halts, after gathering 33,683 signatures. It cites funding constraints, and the statewide route closes for the cycle.

May 2024. Eugene, the method's own hometown, votes on STAR. The measure fails, roughly two to one against.

November 2024. Oakridge, a small city in Lane County, votes on a pilot: STAR for three election cycles. It fails, 575 to 551. [beat] By 24 votes.

[pause]

Hear that list the way I had to. STAR's most sophisticated audience was Eugene, the city where it was invented, after a decade of local evangelism. And Eugene rejected it, two to one.

The near-misses show real persuadability. Lane County was lost by under five points, and Oakridge by 24 votes. The Eugene blowout shows something else: what happens when a two-stage explanation meets a skeptical electorate, and an opposition campaign.

Meanwhile, the method's actual operating experience consists of party-run elections, plus internal votes in organizations and in Oregon party bodies. Those ran fine. They are also not public elections, and I won't pretend otherwise.

> STAR in 2026: strong simulations, and no public adoptions.

That asymmetry is the central fact about STAR today.

## The trade, stated precisely

Here is the part most explainers fudge, so let's be exact. Voting theory has a known tension between two properties. And no method gets both.

The first property is called later-no-harm. It means that supporting a less-preferred candidate can never hurt a more-preferred one.

Instant-runoff voting, the count behind ranked-choice voting, satisfies it. Your second choice is never even read until your first is eliminated.

Approval, score and STAR all fail it. The 3 you give your second choice is counted immediately, alongside the 5 you gave your favorite. And it can be the margin by which the second choice overtakes the favorite.

[beat]

The second property is called no favorite betrayal. It means you never do better by ranking or scoring someone above your sincere favorite.

Score and approval satisfy it outright. Giving your favorite the maximum can never backfire on them. STAR holds it too, with rare edge cases.

Instant runoff fails it. Think of Alaska's special election, in August 2022, and the Republicans who ranked Palin first. They would have gotten a better outcome, Begich, by ranking their favorite lower.

[pause]

So, two properties. Later-no-harm, and no favorite betrayal.

> Later-no-harm or no favorite betrayal: no voting method gets both.

Instant runoff chose later-no-harm, and the price was Burlington and the center squeeze. Protecting your first choice from your second is exactly why consensus candidates die early.

Score and STAR chose the other side. Every preference counts immediately. Favorites are always safe to support fully. And the cost is real: supporting a compromise candidate genuinely can help them edge out your favorite.

STAR then spends a little complexity buying back strategy-resistance. Under score voting, exaggeration goes unpunished, and the ballot collapses toward approval. Under STAR, a flattened ballot forfeits the runoff.

The complexity shows in the count. Score totals can be summed precinct by precinct, in a single pass. STAR's score totals sum the same way, but its runoff needs pairwise tallies as well.

These are choices, not flaws. And you should distrust any advocate, of any system, who tells you their method holds every property.

Let's test it. An instant-runoff advocate objects. STAR fails later-no-harm, so voters will be afraid to score anyone but their favorite, and it will collapse into plurality. What is the strongest reply, from what you have heard so far? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The runoff is engineered against exactly that retreat. Give your favorite a 5 and everyone else a 0, and you do get that kind of safety. But you throw away your runoff vote whenever your favorite isn't a finalist.

And Quinn's simulations bear out that honest gradations remain individually rational under STAR, with strategic electorates.

Then comes the honest counter-concession. That is simulation evidence, plus party-election experience. It is not public-election data, because no government has run STAR yet.

## The pragmatic sibling

So why does this book advocate score, rather than STAR? Three reasons, none of them contempt.

First, transparency compounds. "Highest total wins" is one sentence. A STAR result requires explaining why the higher-scoring candidate can lose the runoff. That is a feature, by design: the majoritarian override, working as intended. But it is a feature you must re-explain in every contested election, forever.

Second, the runoff's majority winner. It is a majority between two finalists the scores pre-selected. That is a real guarantee, and a modest one. Don't let anyone inflate it into a majority mandate.

Third, Eugene. The empirical lesson of 2024, as I draw it, is that each increment of mechanism is a surface the opposition attacks. Reform campaigns die of complexity before they die of anything else.

Score's whole case is that expressiveness shouldn't cost simplicity.

> My case for score: the simplest expressive ballot is the one to organize around.

Approval voting is score's special case, with only 0 and 1. And it has already won cities on exactly that logic.

[pause]

Those three reasons are a judgment, not a finding. So before the rest of my verdict, set the two cases side by side. STAR's: the runoff makes honesty rational. Mine: the simplest expressive ballot is the one to organize around. Take the one you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

And one check, while you are there. Early on, I asked you to get one number right: the scale on a STAR ballot. Can you still say it?

[long pause]

0 to 5. Not 0 to 10.

Now the rest of the verdict. Keep the family resemblance in view. A STAR ballot is a score ballot, counted with one extra safeguard.

If STAR is what's on your ballot, if Oregon's movement finally lands one, vote yes without hesitation. I'll cheer.

Among the reforms on offer in 2026, STAR is score voting's closest kin, and plurality's furthest improvement.

> My quarrel with STAR is strategic, not mathematical.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The mechanics. Score every candidate from 0 to 5. The top two totals advance to an automatic runoff. Intensity picks the finalists, and head-to-head preference picks the winner.

[beat]

Two. What the runoff is for. Under STAR, a flattened ballot can silence itself in the runoff. The runoff makes honesty rational.

[beat]

Three. The record. Lane County in 2018, Eugene and Oakridge in 2024: three ballot measures, three losses. STAR in 2026: strong simulations, and no public adoptions.

[beat]

Four. The trade. Later-no-harm or no favorite betrayal: no voting method gets both. Instant runoff chose the first. Score and STAR chose the second.

[beat]

And five. My verdict. The simplest expressive ballot is the one to organize around. But if STAR is on your ballot, vote yes. My quarrel with STAR is strategic, not mathematical.

Settled: STAR's mechanics, its engineered answer to strategic exaggeration, its precise trade-offs, and its unvarnished record at the ballot box. 0 for 3.
