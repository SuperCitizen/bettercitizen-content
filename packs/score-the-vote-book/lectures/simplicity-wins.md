On June 29, 2021, New York City's Board of Elections published a count. It was the first preliminary ranked-choice tabulation of the city's mayoral primary, the most-watched ranked-choice count in American history.

[beat]

And then the Board un-published it.

Roughly 135,000 test ballots had been fed into the system during pre-election checks, and never cleared. They had been tabulated along with the real ones.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter laid out the whole map of reforms. In this chapter I start comparing them, on one measure: simplicity.

Let me be scrupulously fair about New York, because this story gets weaponized dishonestly. The blunder was the Board's, not the ballot's. Test data in a live count would corrupt a plurality election too.

But notice what the counting method did to the blunder. In a plurality race, contaminated totals are one subtraction away from clean ones. New York's count was an instant runoff, the count behind ranked-choice voting. There, every round's eliminations depend on the round before.

So the contamination ran through the entire elimination sequence. The corrected count had to be rerun from scratch. And the Board's public explanation had to begin by explaining how the count works at all.

For days, the country's largest city debated its next mayor through the fog of a tabulation error, inside a tabulation most voters couldn't check.

[pause]

Every voting system pays what I call the complexity tax.

> The complexity tax: the cost of being harder than "most votes wins."

It is paid three ways: in explanation, in administration, and in trust. This chapter is about who pays the least.

We will take it in four steps. First, the instructions and the count. Second, the evidence on ballot errors, in both directions. Third, the ballots that run out. And last, why simplicity is a strategy, not just a virtue.

## The napkin test

Here is score voting's complete instruction set. "Score every candidate from 0 to 5. Highest total wins."

Approval voting's is shorter. "Vote for every candidate you approve of. Most votes wins."

[beat]

Both fit on a napkin, or a yard sign. Both fit in the thirty seconds a poll worker actually gets with a confused voter.

Now write the instructions for ranked-choice voting. Rank the candidates in order of preference. Don't give two candidates the same ranking. Don't skip rankings. If no candidate has a majority of first choices, the last-place candidate is eliminated. Those ballots transfer to each voter's next choice. Repeat, until someone has a majority of the ballots still active.

[pause]

Every clause is a place for a voter to stumble, or for a skeptic to allege mischief. Most voters can fill out a ranked ballot. The data shows that. Far fewer can explain how the winner is determined. And a system whose count is a black box to its own voters is spending trust it may someday need.

There is a structural difference hiding under the word count, and it matters more than the word count. Under score and approval, every precinct can post its totals on election night. Anyone can add the postings. And the addition is the election. A count like that is called precinct-summable.

> Score and approval are precinct-summable: add the precinct totals, and you have the election.

Instant runoff is not summable that way. Let's see whether you can say why. Why can an approval count be finished precinct by precinct, when an instant-runoff count cannot? Think it through.

[long pause]

Because eliminations depend on global totals. No precinct can know who is eliminated until every ballot, everywhere, is in. A precinct's own local winner means nothing in the middle of an elimination. An approval total is pure addition.

It is about the math, not the machinery. And it is not that an approval ballot carries less. It carries more usable information than a pick-one ballot, and still counts by addition. More expression, same arithmetic. That combination is the whole pitch.

So think again about New York. Centralized tabulation, delayed results, reruns from scratch: these are not bugs in ranked-choice voting. They are load-bearing features of elimination counting.

## The error evidence, in both directions

That is the count. Now the ballot itself, and what voters do with it.

The largest published study of how ranked ballots get marked is by Stephen Pettigrew and Dylan Radley. They examined the ballot records of hundreds of ranked races. And here is what they found.

> The study's finding: about 1 voter in 20 mismarks a typical ranked ballot.

That means mismarked in at least one way. Then they compared the same voters, on the same physical ballots. They counted overvotes: ballots marking more rankings, or more candidates, than allowed. In the ranked races, the overvote rate was 0.60 percent. In the races next to them, the ones that were not ranked, it was 0.04 percent.

[beat]

Fifteen times higher.

[pause]

Now the other side, at full strength, because it is good. The study has critics, and they include a detailed review by the Mathematics and Democracy Institute. They give three answers.

First, most mismarks never void a ballot. A ballot with a skipped rank usually still counts, at the voter's highest valid choice. Second, fifteen times a small number is still a small number. And third, error rates fall as electorates gain experience with the format.

> The critics' answer: most mismarks never void a ballot, and the rates are small.

All fair. [beat] Here is what survives the critique: the asymmetry.

However small the absolute rates, ranked ballots generate an order of magnitude more marking errors than the simpler format beside them, on the same piece of paper. And razor-thin American elections are decided inside margins smaller than that gap.

[pause]

Now approval voting's quietly unanswerable card. The overvote is the dominant error a machine can reject.

> In an approval race, an overvote is impossible by definition.

Marking many candidates is not a spoiled ballot. It is the instruction. A whole category of disenfranchisement does not shrink under approval voting. It ceases to exist.

And what did real voters do with that freedom? In Fargo's first approval election, in June 2020, they averaged 2.3 approvals a ballot. In St. Louis's first, in March 2021, about 1.6. Voters used the wider ballot immediately, on existing machines, with results posted election night.

I will concede what an honest listener has already noticed. Two cities and a few cycles cannot support a body of error-rate research, like the one the critics and defenders of ranked choice are fighting over. The approval claim is structural: a deleted error category, and same-night addition. It is not yet statistical. I am comfortable resting on structure.

Let's test the structure. A county clerk asks you for two lists. One is the complete voter instruction for an approval race. The other is every way a voter could still spoil that race. How short is each list? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The instruction is two sentences. "Vote for every candidate you approve of. The candidate with the most votes wins."

The second list is nearly as short. Stray marks the scanner cannot interpret. Or skipping the race entirely. Those are the leftover errors every voting system shares. The big one, marking too many, is gone, because there is no such thing.

Draft the same two lists for ranked-choice voting, and you have found this chapter's argument from the administrator's side of the table.

## The ballots that run out

One more complexity cost. It is often counted as voter error, when it is really voter abandonment.

Under instant runoff, a ballot may rank only candidates who get eliminated. That ballot runs out. It counts in the early rounds, and then it sits out the finish. The name for it is an exhausted ballot.

Alaska's special election of 2022 shows the size of it. When ballots transferred from the candidate Nick Begich, 11,243 of them ranked neither remaining candidate. That was about 21 percent of his voters, absent from the final round that decided their seat. And the winner's majority was a majority of a shrunken denominator.

The defenders of ranked choice have a fair reply. Those voters chose brevity, they say. And a plurality election consults nobody's backup preferences at all.

> The defenders' reply: those voters chose brevity, and plurality asks for no backups.

True. [beat] But notice that score and approval make the entire dilemma unbuildable. There are no rounds to be exhausted from.

> Under score and approval, every ballot speaks in the only round there is.

## Simplicity is a strategy

First, a placement. Between score's one step and ranked choice's many sits STAR voting. You score the candidates 0 to 5. Then comes an automatic runoff between the two highest scorers, using each ballot's scores as the head-to-head vote.

STAR keeps the cardinal ballot, the rating ballot. It buys some strategic protection, at the cost of one extra counting step. I think it is a serious, well-engineered compromise, designed by people on my side of the family, and it gets a fair, full chapter later.

For now, the ordering. From simplest to most complex: approval, then score, then STAR, then instant runoff. Nothing in the STAR chapter will change that ordering.

[pause]

Now the turn. Reform has to be adopted before it can perform. That usually means a ballot measure, against an opposition whose entire campaign is "this is confusing."

So compare the opening bids. Approval voting debuted at 63.5 percent in Fargo, and 68 percent in St. Louis. Ranked-choice voting passed Maine with 52.12 percent. It passed Alaska with 50.55. Those were campaigns won on heroic margins of one or two points.

I will flag the confound myself. Two city electorates against two statewide ones. Different years. Different opposition spending. This is suggestive, not causal.

But every organizer who has knocked on doors for both reforms will tell you which pitch survives a doorstep conversation intact.

[pause]

One question before we gather up. At the start I named a tax, and since then I have measured it four ways. Can you still name the four?

[long pause]

The length of the instructions. Whether the count can be summed precinct by precinct. The room for error on the ballot. And exhaustion.

On every one of those four, the cardinal family sits at the simple end. Elimination counting sits at the complex end, paying for its expressiveness in administration and explanation.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The complexity tax: the cost of being harder than "most votes wins." It is paid in explanation, administration and trust.

[beat]

Two. Score and approval are precinct-summable. Instant runoff is not, because its eliminations depend on global totals.

[beat]

Three. The error evidence, at its honest size. About 1 voter in 20 mismarks a typical ranked ballot. Most mismarks never void a ballot, and the rates are small. The asymmetry is real, and its interpretation is contested.

[beat]

Four. In an approval race, an overvote is impossible by definition. And under score and approval, every ballot speaks in the only round there is.

[beat]

And five. The ordering, from simplest: approval, score, STAR, instant runoff. Simplicity is a strategy, not just a virtue.

[pause]

That much, I think, is settled: simplicity can be measured. Here is what is not settled. [beat] Whether simplicity should win.

Rankings buy expressive value, and they pay a complexity tax for it. Is the value worth the tax? You may already lean one way. Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Keep that case. This chapter does not decide it.

And a second open question. What happens to these plain cardinal ballots when voters turn tactical? That one has a chapter-length answer, and it is next: the strategy question.

[pause]

For now, go back to New York, and a count that most voters couldn't check.

Under score and approval, every precinct posts its totals, and anyone can add them.

[beat]

The addition is the election.
