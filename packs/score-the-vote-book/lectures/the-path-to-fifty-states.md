Two days ago, as I write this, Fargo held a city election the old way. Pick one name, and the most votes wins. It was the first time since 2018.

Not because Fargoans changed their minds. [beat] They never got the chance.

The legislature in Bismarck took the method away, in April 2025. It overrode a system that city voters had adopted themselves, with 63.5 percent of the vote, and had used without incident in three election cycles.

So the first chapter of cardinal voting's American story did not end with a verdict from voters. It ended with a preemption statute.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting and its sibling, approval voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter laid out how a city campaign wins, and who the organizations are. This chapter asks where that playbook can be run.

And a chapter called "The Path to Fifty States" owes you honesty about Fargo. The earlier drafts of this movement's story, mine included, were ascent narratives. Prove it in cities. Ride to the states. Tip the nation.

The map of 2024 to 2026 says otherwise. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting. The state that hosted approval voting's debut banned that too. Missouri wrote plurality into its constitution. And Utah let its ranked-choice pilot program quietly expire.

So this is not the itinerary of a victory lap. It is a map of the terrain as it lies in mid-2026, including the openings that the ban wave leaves wide open.

We will take it in four steps. First, the map as it is. Second, the doors, in order of how far they open. Third, three proofs that something like this already works. And last, a decade I am willing to defend.

## The map as it is

Take stock without flinching.

One American city elects its government by approval voting. That city is St. Louis, and it is protected by a grandfather clause, which its opponents had to write into Missouri's Amendment 7. Fargo is back on plurality. No state uses any cardinal method.

> Cardinal voting's American foothold is one city wide.

Now the incumbent reform. Ranked-choice voting runs statewide in Maine and Alaska, and in municipalities across fourteen states. It arrives citywide in Washington, D.C., where 73 percent of voters said yes in 2024. And yet it lost every statewide initiative it ran in 2024, and it is banned in nineteen states.

Put that together. Reform of every kind is a minority position on the map. The backlash is organized, and it is winning legislatures.

[pause]

So why am I still writing this book? Because the legal review that produced the ban map also produced its most important detail.

Nearly every ban statute prohibits ranking.

Tennessee's and Florida's laws define the forbidden method as voters ranking candidates "in order of preference," with elimination rounds. Ohio's law, from 2026, names "ranked choice voting or instant runoff voting." Approval and score ballots do none of that.

> In seventeen of the nineteen ban states, cardinal voting is untouched.

Not as a loophole. As a category those legislatures never legislated about.

The two exceptions are Missouri and North Dakota, and they prove how deliberate the others' silence is. Catching approval voting requires naming it.

Let's test that. A reform donor tells you: "Nineteen ban states means nineteen states are simply closed to election reform." What is wrong with that reading of the map? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The bans are drafted against ranking, almost universally. In all but Missouri and North Dakota, an approval or score ballot violates nothing in the ban. So seventeen states are closed to ranked choice, and legally open to cardinal methods, which are now the only reform that can run there.

Closed to one reform is not closed to reform. The statute's own definitions draw the lane.

## The doors, in order

So where does a movement go, with one city, honest enemies, and a favorable legal asymmetry? Through the doors, in order of how far they open. There are five.

The first door is the home-rule city: a city whose charter gives it authority over its own election method, in a state whose election code leaves room. This is how Fargo and St. Louis happened. A charter, plus a citizen initiative.

The risk at this door is preemption, and it is real. North Dakota erased Fargo. So pick states where the legislature is unlikely to retaliate. Or pick a state like Ohio, where constitutional home rule forces opponents to use funding pressure instead of an outright ban.

[beat]

The second is less a door than a check, made before any campaign. Does state law prescribe the form of a municipal ballot?

Michigan is the warning. Starting with Ferndale in 2004, five Michigan cities voted for ranked-choice charters. State law renders every one of them inoperative. Five adoptions. [beat] Zero implementations.

> Adoption is not implementation.

[beat]

The third door is counties and special districts: school boards, utility boards, counties with charter authority. These are low-profile offices, where the pathologies of plurality are most visible. Huge fields. Tiny winning shares. And the stakes feel local.

[beat]

The fourth door is an initiative state. Roughly half the states allow statutes initiated by citizens. The lesson of 2024 is to run simple and unbundled. Picture a one-sentence reform: remove the vote-for-one limit in municipal and primary elections. That is a different campaign from Colorado's bundled top-four overhaul, which lost 53.5 to 46.5.

A warning here too. Statewide is where the opposition spends. And Nevada showed that even a winning measure can die on a required second passage.

[beat]

The fifth door is the legislature, and it comes last. No legislature has ever adopted a cardinal method. Since 2022, statehouses are where reform goes to be banned. So the legislative play comes after local proof exists in that state. It is there to defend wins, not to lead.

> Home-rule cities first. Election-code checks always. Legislatures last.

Follow those doors, and the first phase writes itself. Hold St. Louis. Win two or three home-rule cities in states that can't or won't preempt. And keep every campaign small, so that one donor network's bad November can't wipe the movement out.

One thing the sequence can't show is tempo. Fargo took two years, from task force to adoption. St. Louis took roughly two, from petition to first election. The bans took four years to cover nineteen states. Movements at this scale move in cycles, not quarters.

## Three proofs, with their caveats

While American cities supply the adoptions, someone will ask you: has anything like this ever worked? Three running systems already answer, provided we are honest about what each one is, and isn't.

The first is Latvia, which elects its parliament with plus and minus marks. A voter chooses a party list. Then the voter may mark a plus beside any candidate on it, and strike through any candidate they oppose. Each candidate's final position is set by net marks: pluses minus strikes.

That is a three-level score ballot. Plus, blank, or minus, for every candidate on the list, counted nationally, for a sitting European parliament. There is a caveat, and I will come back to it.

[beat]

The second is the United Nations. Since 1981, the Security Council has winnowed candidates for Secretary-General through straw polls. Each member marks every candidate encourage, discourage, or no opinion. Plus one, minus one, or zero. A score ballot, used because picking one name at a time, among fifteen veto-wielding members, would deadlock.

The caveat: fifteen sophisticated voters choosing a diplomat is not a mass election. What it anchors is the method's seriousness, not its mass-market record.

[beat]

The third is American. In December 2025, the seat of a Utah state senator, Daniel Thatcher, came open. His Forward Party was entitled, under Utah's vacancy law, to choose the replacement. It opened the choice to every registered voter in the district, and ran it by approval voting. Emily Buss won, and took the seat.

The caveats: one vacancy, a party-run process, modest turnout. And also the first state-level use of approval voting in U.S. history, run without a hiccup.

> Three anchors: existence proofs to deploy, not adoptions to inflate.

[pause]

Now back to Latvia. An organizer cites it as proof that score voting already elects national governments. I told you how Latvians mark their ballots. What is the accurate version of that claim?

[long pause]

Latvian voters do cast plus and minus marks for each candidate. But only within one chosen party list, not across the whole field.

So it is evidence that ordinary voters handle per-candidate scoring at national scale. It is not evidence of full score voting electing a government.

> Precision is the advocate's armor.

Overclaim it, and you hand the opposition a correction. State it exactly, and it is unanswerable.

## A sober decade

Here is the timeline I will actually defend. It comes in three stretches.

Through 2028. Hold St. Louis, through every charter review and every repeal attempt. Win two or three home-rule cities in states that will not preempt. And build the playbook for clerks and equipment, so that each new campaign starts at mile ten.

[beat]

From 2028 to 2032. The first county or special-district adoptions. And the first serious statewide initiative, in a state chosen for its legal opening and its cheap media markets. Chosen, too, because its ban statute forecloses ranked choice. That makes cardinal reform the only live option there, and not a rival brand.

[beat]

2032 and beyond. If those proofs exist, and only if, the conversation about scaling up, and about upgrading from approval to score, stops being theoretical.

> Fifty states is the title's ambition, not a forecast.

The forecast is this. A foothold becomes a beachhead inside ten years. Or we reach the honest conclusion that voters looked, and declined.

Two outcomes, then: a beachhead, or a decline. Take the one you think less likely, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The map, as of mid-2026. One approval-voting city. Ranked choice statewide in Maine and Alaska. Nineteen ban states.

[beat]

Two. Nearly every ban prohibits ranking. In seventeen of the nineteen ban states, cardinal voting is untouched. The exceptions are Missouri and North Dakota.

[beat]

Three. The doors. Home-rule cities first. Election-code checks always. Legislatures last. And from Michigan: adoption is not implementation.

[beat]

Four. Three anchors, each with its caveat. Latvia, within one party list. The United Nations, with fifteen voters. Utah, with one vacancy.

[beat]

And five. Fifty states is the title's ambition, not a forecast. The forecast is a foothold becoming a beachhead inside ten years, or voters declining.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles. The path exists. It is narrower than the movement's own literature claimed five years ago. And its first miles run through city charters, in states whose ban statutes, by their own words, left the cardinal lane open.

Here is what it does not settle: whether enough people will walk it.

That is the next chapter's question, and the book's final one. What do you do about it?

[pause]

For now, go back to Fargo, voting the old way again. Its voters never repealed anything.

[beat]

The path is on the map. A map does not walk itself.
