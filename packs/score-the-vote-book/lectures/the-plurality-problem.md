In November 2010, Maine elected a governor whom 62 percent of its voters had just voted against.

[beat]

His name was Paul LePage, and he won with 37.6 percent of the vote. An independent, Eliot Cutler, took 35.9. The Democrat, Libby Mitchell, took 18.8. Two more independents split the rest.

Cutler and Mitchell were both center-left candidates, competing for overlapping voters. Together, their supporters outnumbered LePage's by more than seventeen points. [beat] It didn't matter.

Nobody cheated. Nobody miscounted. The system performed exactly as designed. [beat] And that is the indictment.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote, a book that argues for a different ballot. The "I" in it is the book's author. In this chapter I argue that Maine's result was no accident. It is what the pick-one ballot is built to do.

Maine's eventual answer came in 2016. By referendum, 52 percent to 48, it became the first state to adopt ranked-choice voting. Hold that thought. This book will argue that Maine reached for the second-best tool. But Maine got the diagnosis exactly right, and the diagnosis is where we have to start.

We will take it in four steps. First, what a single mark throws away. Second, the spoiler effect. Third, a law of politics with a French name. And last, what all of this does to you, the voter.

## One mark, zero nuance

The pick-one ballot has a formal name: plurality. Pick one, and the most votes wins.

Plurality voting asks you the shallowest possible question: which one? And then it treats your answer as your entire political existence.

You cannot say "this one, but I'd accept that one." You cannot say "anyone but him." You cannot tell enthusiasm from resignation. Whatever you actually believe gets compressed into a single mark. And in a tight election among several candidates, that compression throws away exactly the information that decides the result.

Go back to Maine with that in mind. The winner had 37.6 percent. The two center-left candidates split 54.7 percent between them. And no ballot in the state had a place to say "this one, but I'd accept that one."

> Under plurality, the largest splinter takes the whole office.

Notice how far that winner sat from a majority. A majority is not a technicality. It is the difference between "most of us chose this" and "the biggest faction got there first." Plurality elections routinely certify the second thing, and call it the first.

[pause]

Later in this book I will show you simulation tables, comparing every serious voting method across millions of model elections. I will flag their assumptions and their limits honestly when we get there. For now, one preview is enough. In every published table you will see in this book, plurality finishes last.

Researchers do argue. But they argue over which reform is best, not over which system is worst. That argument was settled long ago.

## The spoiler effect

That was one state, in one year. Now raise the stakes.

Florida, 2000. In the certified count, George W. Bush finished ahead of Al Gore by 537 votes, with the presidency attached. Ralph Nader received 97,488 votes in Florida.

Here is where I am supposed to tell you that Nader cost Gore the White House. And here is where I won't. Not in that simple form. The honest record is messier, and it has two sides.

One side points to exit polls. Estimates built on them suggested that Nader's voters leaned toward Gore over Bush, at roughly two to one. That would have flipped 537 votes many times over.

> The spoiler case: exit polls put Nader's voters roughly two to one for Gore.

The other side, the skeptics, points to party registration. Only about 24,000 registered Democrats voted for Nader in Florida. Roughly 308,000 registered Democrats voted for Bush. If you want to blame someone for Gore's loss, the skeptics say, the defectors dwarf the Nader voters.

> The skeptics' case: far more registered Democrats chose Bush than chose Nader.

So was Nader a spoiler? You may already lean one way. Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Decades later, people still argue. [beat] And that is the system's confession.

Ask what it would take to end the argument. What single piece of information would settle whether Nader cost Gore Florida? And where would that information sit on a score ballot? Think it through.

[long pause]

You would need one thing: each Nader voter's preference between Bush and Gore.

A plurality ballot never asks for it. The pick-one ballot took in 97,488 first choices, and destroyed every second choice. The information was in the voters' heads on election night, and the ballot had no place to put it. So the argument has run for a quarter century, on dueling surveys.

> A spoiler debate is an electoral system deleting its own evidence.

On a score ballot, the answer sits on the face of every ballot. Nader 5, Gore 3, Bush 0. Counted. Archived. Never argued about again. Under score voting, the spoiler debate is not just resolved. [beat] It becomes impossible to have.

[pause]

Eight years earlier, the same machine ran at larger scale. In 1992, Ross Perot won nearly 20 million votes. That was 18.9 percent, the strongest independent showing in eighty years. And he received zero electoral votes.

Whom did Perot spoil? We genuinely don't know. The ballots can't say. What we do know is that one in five American voters tried to leave the two-party menu. And the system absorbed that rebellion without registering so much as a dent.

## Duverger's law

Those are single elections. Now watch what the pick-one ballot does over decades.

In 1951, the French political scientist Maurice Duverger published a book called Political Parties. In it, he put a name on what plurality does over time. [beat] It manufactures two-party systems.

That claim is known as Duverger's law. It is the rare generalization in social science sturdy enough to keep the title. And it rests on two distinct mechanisms.

The first is the mechanical effect, and it is arithmetic. Plurality converts dispersed support into nothing. Spread your votes thin, and they win no seats at all.

The second is the psychological effect, and it is anticipation. Voters who can count do not wait to be mechanically erased. They abandon their sincere favorite in advance, to avoid "wasting" their vote. And donors and ambitious candidates abandon the party before the voters do.

> The mechanical effect kills third parties. The psychological effect persuades them to die quietly.

Let's run both. A third party polls a genuine 15 percent, spread evenly across every district in a plurality system. What do the two effects predict, in order? Take a moment.

[long pause]

First, the mechanical effect: zero seats. Support spread evenly means no pluralities anywhere. So a party winning 15 percent everywhere wins nothing anywhere.

Then the psychological effect. Voters, donors, and candidates see that arithmetic coming, and they leave in advance, rather than waste their participation. The party's support erodes before the next election.

Its vote share does not hold steady out of sincerity, because voters can count. And it does not win 15 percent of the seats. That would be a proportional system, not plurality.

[pause]

One caution, because the law has limits. Political scientists have spent seventy years refining its scope.

> Regionally concentrated parties can survive Duverger's law.

That is why Canada and the United Kingdom still seat third parties. But as a description of incentives in single-winner plurality districts, the law has held up disturbingly well.

Now understand what this means for the American duopoly, the hold of two parties. It is not primarily a story about culture, or money, or media. It is a story about ballot design. The two-party stranglehold is downstream of the pick-one rule.

Change the rule, and you change the equilibrium, the point where the system settles. That is precisely why the parties that benefit from the current equilibrium take such an interest in keeping the rule. You will see that pattern documented, statute by statute, later in this book.

## What honesty costs

Put the pieces together. There have been three so far. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

A winner most voters opposed. A spoiler argument nobody can settle. And a law that grinds third parties down.

Put those together, and you get the quiet scandal of American elections. Under plurality, dishonesty is the rational strategy for millions of voters, every cycle.

"Don't waste your vote" is not cynicism. It is correct advice, within the rules as written. Picture the voter who prefers a third candidate but marks a major-party ballot. She is not a coward. She is solving the optimization problem the ballot handed her.

I want to be precise about what is broken here, because the precision pays off later.

> Under plurality, backing your favorite can help elect the candidate you most fear.

And the defense is to vote for a lesser evil, which means abandoning your favorite entirely. Voting theory has a name for this flaw: favorite betrayal. Plurality forces favorite betrayal. And a century of American third-party collapse is mostly this one flaw, repeated.

The solution is not to shame strategic voters. It is to change the ballot, so that honesty stops being punished. That is the bar any reform must clear. And it gives us a sharp question to carry through this book.

Under the proposed system, what does it cost to support your true favorite?

[pause]

Under plurality, the cost can be the whole election.

Under ranked-choice voting, we will see, the cost shrinks but does not reach zero. Alaska's special election in 2022 made that vivid, and I will walk you through the ballot data in the math chapter.

Under score voting, the answer is structural. Your favorite gets your maximum score, always. And it never costs you a thing on any other line of the ballot.

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it doesn't.

Settled: the diagnosis. Plurality discards nearly all of what voters know, manufactures spoiler arguments nobody can resolve, suppresses honest support for outsiders, and, by Duverger's account, locks in the duopoly. None of that is contested among people who study voting systems. The worst-system verdict is as near to consensus as this field gets.

Not settled: what replaces it. Ranked-choice voting has its advocates. So does another proposal, called STAR voting. And I have mine.

> On the diagnosis, ranked-choice advocates, STAR advocates, and I agree.

We are allies until the moment the prescription is written. Maine has written one. The next chapter writes mine. It is simpler than you think. You already know how to do it. And you have probably done it this week.
