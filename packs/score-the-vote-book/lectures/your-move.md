Every reform book ends the same way. Chapter after chapter of evidence. Then a final chapter that goes soft.

"Raise awareness, have conversations, stay engaged."

[beat]

That is the authorial equivalent of approving every candidate. It is a refusal to say what you actually want the reader to do.

[pause]

This is the last chapter of Score the Vote. The book is an argument for score voting and its sibling, approval voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter drew the map: where the law is open, and where it is closed. This chapter is about the one thing a map cannot supply.

I have spent this book arguing that your ballot should let you say exactly what you think. The least I can do is close by doing the same.

So here is what I think. The difference between Fargo in 2016 and Fargo in 2018 was not money. It was not a movement, and it was not a moment. It was one engineer, from a city task force, who declined to let the question die.

The entire American record of cardinal voting is two cities. Every adoption traces to a handful of individually identifiable people, who decided the broken thing in front of them was theirs to fix.

So the movement's binding constraint is not evidence. You have had eighteen chapters of it. It is not legality. The law is clearer than the opposition pretends. And it is not money. Fargo was won on signatures and shoe leather.

> The movement's binding constraint is protagonists.

[pause]

We will take this in three steps, and then a last word. First, your terrain. Second, the petition path, by the numbers. And third, what you can do if a full campaign is not in you this year.

## Learn your terrain

Before you donate a dollar or print a petition, answer three questions about where you live. They are answerable in an afternoon, and they determine everything.

The first question. Is your city a home-rule or charter city? Your city clerk's office can tell you in one phone call. If the charter governs how your officials are elected, you have a front door. If your city operates under general state law, your fight is at the statehouse, not city hall. That is a different game, with longer odds.

[beat]

The second question. Does your state's election code leave room? Michigan's cities passed ranked-choice charter amendments five times, over two decades, and implemented none of them. State election law left no opening. So find out what your state's code prescribes about ballot form and counting. Or ask the clerk, who knows.

[beat]

The third question. Has your state already legislated?

In Missouri and North Dakota, cardinal methods are closed off. Missouri did it by constitution. North Dakota did it by the 2025 statute that ended Fargo's experiment.

In any of the other seventeen states with a ban, check the ban's definitions. Nearly all of them prohibit ranking. That means an approval or score campaign is likely the only reform legally available to you. [beat] An argument, not an obstacle.

And if your state has no ban, the question is only whether your city's charter opens the door.

> Three questions, one afternoon: home rule, the election code, the ban.

Let's test that. You live in Tennessee. Its statute bans methods where "voters rank candidates in order of preference," tabulated in elimination rounds. Your home-rule city elects its commissioners by plurality, routinely with under 30 percent of the vote.

What did your afternoon of terrain-reading just tell you? Take a moment.

[long pause]

That you may be standing in one of the most favorable spots on the national map.

The ban forecloses ranked choice, so no rival reform campaign is coming. But by its own definition, it says nothing about approval ballots, which involve no ranking and no rounds. A home-rule charter. A legible plurality failure. A legally open lane. That is the full Fargo setup. What remains is the petition arithmetic, and the coalition.

## The petition path

If the terrain checks out, the mechanics are not mysterious. Fargo's entire path fit on an index card: one citizen initiative, start to finish. And it is the same card anywhere a city has initiative power.

There are six steps, and the order matters.

First, confirm that the charter gives you authority, and that state law leaves room. Second, talk to the clerk and the county election office about ballot layout and equipment. Third, draft the amendment language, borrowing from the Fargo and St. Louis precedents.

[beat]

Fourth, qualify the petition, collecting roughly double the required signatures. Fifth, run the education campaign, all the way to the public vote. And sixth, plan the defense that follows a win: implementation, allies, and the statehouse.

> Sequence is strategy.

Two of those steps sit where they do for a reason.

The equipment conversation comes before the drafting. Language that fits the existing optical-scan machines removes the opposition's cheapest attack. Fargo and St. Louis both ran approval voting on equipment they already owned.

And the defense plan is a real step, not an afterthought. Fargo won the vote in 2018, and lost the statute in 2025.

[pause]

Now two numbers from the record, to calibrate your ambition.

Fargo needed 1,349 valid signatures. Reform Fargo collected 2,600, had 1,923 accepted, and won with 63.5 percent. That took roughly forty volunteers, a local face, and national expertise on call.

St. Louis was the other pole: a professionally organized coalition campaign, for Proposition D, which won 68 percent. Between those two poles sits every city in the open seventeen states, and beyond.

And before any of it, make the unglamorous call I keep insisting on. The clerk.

Election administrators are the most under-consulted experts in reform politics. They know your equipment and your deadlines. And a clerk who says "we could run that" in a council hearing is worth a thousand white papers.

Here is a question, then. Your terrain check has come back open. Which is the highest-leverage first move? Email a national organization, demanding that it target your city. Launch a petition drive at once, while enthusiasm is high. Meet your city clerk. Or start a statewide initiative, because cities are too small to matter.

[long pause]

Meet the clerk.

> The first move is the clerk: one hour that de-risks everything after.

The meeting confirms equipment, deadlines, and ballot-language requirements. Launch a petition before that conversation, and you may collect signatures for language that cannot be implemented. Michigan's five cities stand as the warning.

As for the others. National organizations respond to organized locals, not to demands. And 2024 settled what happens when reform skips the local proof and goes straight to statewide.

## If you can't run a campaign

Most of you won't lead a charter initiative this year. Pretending otherwise is how a chapter like this one curdles into guilt.

The smaller moves are real moves. Here are four, in rough order of effort.

One. Adopt it where you already have power. Clubs, unions, congregations, co-ops, professional societies, student governments and party committees run elections constantly. Utah's Forward Party just filled a state senate vacancy by approval vote. The version at the scale of an organization anchors the argument at the scale of the public.

[beat]

Two. Join the organizations. The Center for Election Science, for approval voting. The Equal Vote Coalition, for STAR. Both run on small donors and volunteers, and both will tell you exactly what your state needs.

[beat]

Three. Make candidates answer. City council candidates in plurality cities have never once been asked what they think of how they were elected. Ask them, publicly.

[beat]

Four. Teach the pitch. Here it is: rate every candidate, zero to ten, like you'd rate anything else. Highest average wins.

One sentence is all it takes. The gap is reach, and reach is made of people repeating sentences.

[pause]

One question before the end. Before anything else, I asked you to answer three questions about where you live. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Is your city a home-rule city? Does your state's election code leave room? And has your state already legislated?

## The claim, one last time

Strip away every statute and every study in this book, and what is left is one sentence.

> Your ballot should let you say what you actually think about every candidate.

That is the whole revolution. And it is three things it is often said not to be.

It is not a partisan project. Fargo's reform passed in deep-red North Dakota. St. Louis's passed in a city that hasn't had a Republican mayor since 1949. The margins were similar landslides.

It is not a complicated one. The fix removes a restriction. It does not add a mechanism. Take the sentence "vote for one," and delete the limit.

And it is not someone else's. There is no commission coming, and no inevitable arc. There is a short list of people who decide the broken thing in front of them is theirs. [beat] And there is everyone else.

[pause]

So let's gather the book's last chapter up. Four things to keep.

One. The binding constraint is protagonists. Not evidence, not legality, not money.

[beat]

Two. Three questions, one afternoon: home rule, the election code, the ban.

[beat]

Three. Sequence is strategy. The clerk first, double the signatures, and a defense plan for after the win.

[beat]

And four. If you can't run a campaign, run something smaller. Adopt it where you have power. Join. Ask the candidates. Teach the one sentence.

[pause]

I will not pretend this is settled. None of it is inevitable, and whether anyone moves is not something a book decides.

You now know what the constraint costs, and what replacing it returns. You know where the law is open, what the playbook says, and what the first phone call is.

[pause]

This book began in a voting booth. One name. One mark. And everything else you know, left unsaid.

A pick-one ballot hands you a megaphone that can say a single word. The politics of plurality is that constraint, compounded across decades. The spoilers. The lesser-evilism. The candidates who win with 16 percent, and govern like it.

[beat]

Democracy is not a spectator sport.

Score the vote.
