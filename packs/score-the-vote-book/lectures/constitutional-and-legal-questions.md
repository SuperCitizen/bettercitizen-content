In 1915, the Minnesota Supreme Court looked at a city election in Duluth, and threw the voting method out.

[beat]

Duluth had adopted a method called Bucklin voting. You marked a first choice, then a second choice, then as many additional choices as you liked. The court declared it incompatible with the state constitution. Here is the sentence it wrote.

"Preferential voting directly diminishes the right of a voter to cast an effective vote for the candidate of his choice."

[pause]

That case is Brown v. Smallwood. It is now a hundred and eleven years old. It concerned a voting method nobody uses, under one state's constitution, over a dissent that called the majority's logic wrong on its face.

And it is still the single most reliable scare cite in American election reform. It gets waved at every city council that considers letting voters say more than one thing on a ballot.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about money. This one is about law, so let's do it properly.

Is score voting legal? The short answer is yes. The Constitution leaves the manner of elections overwhelmingly to the states, and through them, to charter cities. Nothing in it requires plurality voting.

But the short answer is lazy, and the people who oppose reform are not lazy. So we will walk the actual terrain, in four steps. First, what "one person, one vote" really requires. Second, what Brown v. Smallwood really held. Third, where the genuine legal threat lives. And last, the front door that cities have used, and its locks.

I'll tell you now where the threat lives. It doesn't wear a robe. [beat] It wears a statute.

## What one person, one vote demands

Start with the phrase opponents reach for first.

"One person, one vote" comes from a case called Gray v. Sanders, in 1963. The Supreme Court struck down Georgia's county-unit system. That scheme made a rural vote worth many times an Atlanta vote, in statewide primaries.

A year later, Reynolds v. Sims extended the principle to legislative districts. Districts must be roughly equal in population. And the reason, in Chief Justice Warren's words: "legislators represent people, not trees or acres."

People, not trees or acres.

Now notice what those cases are about. They are about the weight of ballots, never the format of ballots. The constitutional command is that no voter's ballot count for more than another's. It is not that each voter make exactly one mark.

> One person, one vote is about a ballot's weight, not its format.

The modern test of that distinction came in 2011, in a case called Dudum v. Arntz. Voters in San Francisco challenged the city's ranked-choice system on precisely this theory. The Ninth Circuit heard it, and Judge Marsha Berzon wrote the opinion.

She dismantled the theory. "Each ballot is counted as no more than one vote at each tabulation step." And every vote, she wrote, is "afforded the same mathematical weight in the election."

Ranking candidates isn't extra voting power, the court held, because every voter has the identical option to do it.

[pause]

Now run approval voting and score voting through that logic. Under score voting, every voter receives the same ballot, the same candidates, the same scale. A voter who scores five candidates hasn't outvoted a voter who scores one. Each has used more or less of an identical allotment. And in the final arithmetic, no ballot contributes more than the maximum score to any candidate.

Think of Olympic judging. A judge who rates every skater hasn't voted more times than a judge who rates only her favorite. She has done the same job more completely.

Equal protection, as the courts have construed it from Gray through Dudum, asks whether voters are treated equally. [beat] It does not ration ink.

Let's test that. A skeptic says: "My neighbor approved four candidates and I approved one, so she got four votes and I got one." Using the logic of Dudum, what is the precise flaw in that claim? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Weight, not marks, is what the doctrine measures. The neighbor added one increment to four different candidates' totals. The skeptic had that option on the very same ballot, and declined to use it. And in any head-to-head comparison between two candidates, each ballot still moves the needle by at most one vote.

Dudum called the equivalent objection to ranked ballots a confusion, between "the option to rank multiple preferences" and "more heavily-weighted votes."

The option is equal. Therefore the power is equal.

## The ghost of Duluth

That is the federal doctrine. Now the opposition's best card. Honest advocacy means taking it seriously, so here is the whole of it.

Duluth's Bucklin system counted a voter's later choices into the same totals as first choices. In that election, 12,313 people voted. And 18,860 marks figured in the count.

"It was not a voting of man against man," the court wrote. And it held that a vote "must be counted one, and it cannot be defeated or its effect lessened, except by the vote of another elector voting for one."

Justice Hallam, in dissent, made the obvious reply. No voter had cast more than one vote for any candidate. On rehearing, the majority all but conceded it was outvoting the legal profession. Then it reaffirmed anyway.

[pause]

What happened to Brown since matters more than Brown itself.

In 2009, the Minnesota Supreme Court decided a case called Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis. Unanimously, it upheld Minneapolis's instant-runoff system.

But note carefully how it did so. It distinguished Brown. It did not overrule it. Instant runoff, the court reasoned, counts only one of your choices at any moment. Bucklin counted later choices simultaneously, alongside first choices.

That narrow reading rescued ranked-choice voting in Minnesota. It did nothing for methods that count all your marks at once. And counting all your marks at once is exactly what approval and score voting do.

So I'll say plainly what some advocates won't. Approval and score voting are the cardinal methods, and for them, Minnesota is a problem.

> Minnesota is hostile terrain for cardinal voting.

It will stay that way until its supreme court revisits a case from 1915, or its voters amend their constitution.

That is the full extent of the concession. Brown construed the Minnesota constitution. It binds no other state. It has persuaded no federal court. And it stands against the great weight of modern doctrine, which measures equality by weight and opportunity.

> Outside one state, the ghost has no jurisdiction.

A campaign mailer says Brown v. Smallwood "proves approval voting is unconstitutional." What does the case actually establish? And did the court of 2009 overrule it?

[long pause]

It establishes that Minnesota's constitution, as it was read in 1915, forbids counting a voter's multiple marks simultaneously. The Minneapolis case narrowed that reading, and left it standing. So it still shadows simultaneous-counting methods there, and only there. No federal court has ever adopted its reasoning.

## The threat that wears a statute

Here is the part of the legal landscape that should actually concern you. And here, too, is the opening hiding inside it.

Beginning with Tennessee in February 2022, nineteen states have enacted bans on ranked-choice voting. Indiana and Ohio had joined them by March 2026.

That is a political fact of the first order, and the next chapters deal with it as one. But read the statutes the way a city attorney would, because the drafting matters enormously.

Take Tennessee's. It prohibits methods in which "voters rank candidates in order of preference," tabulated in elimination rounds, with vote transfers. Florida's has the same shape. Ohio's names ranked choice voting and instant runoff voting, and nothing else.

Most of the ban wave defines its target that way: as ranking, counted in elimination rounds. Approval voting involves no ranking and no rounds. A score ballot involves no ranking and no rounds.

> In most ban states, cardinal methods are not what the statute bans.

They are not a loophole. A legislature that wants to forbid them must say so separately. And that is precisely what the two exceptions prove.

The first exception is Missouri. In 2024, its Amendment 7 wrote a rule into the state constitution: voters get only "a single vote for each candidate or issue." The drafters had to reach for that language to catch approval voting. Even then, they carved out nonpartisan municipal systems already in effect on November 5, 2024. That clause was written around St. Louis, which keeps its approval voting to this day.

The second exception is North Dakota. Its legislature had watched its own ranked-choice language fail to touch Fargo. A first attempt, in 2023, was vetoed by Governor Doug Burgum, as one that "blatantly infringes on local control." The Senate sustained his veto. Then, in 2025, under his successor, a bill finally passed. It named approval voting explicitly.

[pause]

Two honest readings follow.

The discouraging one. Where a legislature truly wants cardinal voting gone, it can write the three extra words. And North Dakota shows the home-rule shield failing against a determined statehouse.

The encouraging one. As of mid-2026, seventeen of the nineteen ban states have not written those words. In every one of them, approval voting is the only major reform still standing on legal ground.

Take the reading you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

## Home rule, and its two locks

Both American cities that have used approval voting got it the same way: a home-rule charter, plus a citizen ballot initiative. Fargo amended its charter in 2018. St. Louis passed Proposition D in 2020.

Charter authority is the front door for cardinal reform. But it has two locks, and you need both opened. First, your state must actually grant charter cities power over their election method. Second, the state's election code must not occupy the field anyway.

Michigan is the cautionary tale on the second lock. Five Michigan cities voted for ranked-choice voting, beginning with Ferndale in 2004. Every measure passed. [beat] Zero implementations. State election law leaves no room for the charters to operate.

> Adoption is not implementation.

Read your state's election code before you celebrate your charter.

One more practical point, because city attorneys ask it immediately. Can the equipment count these ballots?

Approval voting runs on standard optical-scan equipment. It is the familiar vote-for-one ballot with the limit removed, and tabulation is addition. Fargo and St. Louis both ran it on the machines they already owned.

A score ballot with several levels is friendly to the same equipment. But I'll be straight with you. No American public election has yet run one. So equipment certification for full score voting remains a box nobody has had to check. Approval's box is checked. [beat] Twice.

One question before we gather up. Go back to the very start. Whose constitution was the court reading in Brown v. Smallwood? And how many other states does that decision bind?

[long pause]

Minnesota's. And none.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. One person, one vote is about a ballot's weight, not its format. That runs from Gray and Reynolds to Dudum. The option is equal, so the power is equal.

[beat]

Two. Brown v. Smallwood read one state's constitution. The court of 2009 distinguished it, and did not overrule it. So Minnesota is hostile terrain for cardinal voting. Outside one state, the ghost has no jurisdiction.

[beat]

Three. The real threat wears a statute. Nineteen states ban ranked-choice voting, and in most ban states, cardinal methods are not what the statute bans. Missouri and North Dakota are the two exceptions.

[beat]

And four. Home rule is the front door, with two locks. Adoption is not implementation. And on equipment, approval's box is checked twice, while score voting's has never had to be.

[pause]

Here is what is settled, as much as law ever is. No court has identified a federal constitutional problem with cardinal voting. No judge has ever struck down approval or score voting. In Fargo and St. Louis, nobody so much as brought a serious case. The attacks in both cities came through legislatures, not courtrooms. That tells you where the opposition thinks its odds are.

And here is what is not settled. Minnesota, until Brown is confronted directly. Whether more legislatures will follow North Dakota's drafting, not Tennessee's. And a question no court has yet faced, because no state has yet forced it. That is the test case that will come the first time a cardinal method wins something bigger than a city.

When that case comes, it will be argued on Gray, Reynolds, and Dudum. I like those cards. But it has not been argued yet.

Law is not the only objection you will hear. The next chapter gives the objections the floor, each in its strongest form.

For now, go back to Duluth, in 1915. That case will be waved at the next city council too.

Ask where it comes from, and how far it reaches.
