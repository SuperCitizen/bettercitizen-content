Picture fifteen governments, most of them rivals, that have to agree on one person.

The job is Secretary-General of the United Nations: the most powerful diplomat on Earth. In 2026, four declared candidates entered the race to become the tenth. And the Security Council is not using pick-one ballots to choose among them.

[beat]

Beginning in the summer of 2026, Council members rate every candidate, in secret straw polls. Each candidate gets one of three marks: "encourage," "discourage," or "no opinion."

That is a three-level score ballot.

The polling repeats. Candidates read their averages and withdraw. And the process converges on the name the Council can collectively live with.

[pause]

A ballot that rates the candidates, on any scale, is called a cardinal ballot. Score voting uses one. So does approval voting, where you mark every candidate you approve of.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter rested on simulations. It ended on something no simulation can tell us: whether any of this survives contact with actual city halls, actual lawyers, and actual legislatures.

In this chapter I argue that the diplomats are not unusual. And that the obstacle to using the same tool in American public elections has never been the voters.

We will take it in four steps. First, the long record, from Venice to Latvia. Second, America's first try, and the courtroom that ended it. Third, two American cities: the proof. And last, the price those cities paid, and two honest readings of it.

## A scored world

Every star rating you have ever left is a five-level score ballot, folded into an average that strangers trust enough to act on.

I won't pretend your delivery-app habits are constitutional evidence. The point is narrower, and stronger. The skill score voting asks of voters is one the whole population already exercises weekly.

> No reform on offer asks less new of the voter.

Now the deep history, and it runs through Venice. From 1268, until Napoleon ended the republic in 1797, Venice chose its doge through a famously baroque protocol.

And it ended in something an election scientist recognizes at once: 41 electors evaluating the candidates, with at least 25 approvals required to win.

[beat]

Approval-style ballots, with a supermajority threshold. They kept the top job of a fractious commercial empire consensual for five centuries.

The modern world kept the idea. Latvia elects its parliament on open lists. A voter marks a plus beside the candidates she favors, and strikes through the names she opposes. Each candidate's total is the list's votes, plus the pluses, minus the strikes.

Plus one, zero, minus one. [beat] That is a three-level score ballot, deciding who takes the seats of an EU member state, today.

## The courtroom

Now America, where the first try came early. It started in Grand Junction, Colorado, in 1909, with a ranked system called Bucklin voting. The count went like this. Count the first choices. If nobody has a majority, add the second choices to the totals.

It spread to more than sixty American cities. Then came a case called Brown v. Smallwood. After a judicial election in Duluth, in 1915, the Minnesota Supreme Court struck Bucklin down under the state constitution.

The court tallied 18,860 marks, cast by 12,313 voters, and it recoiled. This, the court said, "was not a voting of man against man."

[pause]

Notice what the court measured. Marks per voter, rather than influence per voter, became the measure of equality.

Let's test that logic. A court has counted more marks than voters, and declared the system unconstitutional. What would that court likely say about approval voting? And what is the strongest answer to it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

By its letter, the same objection applies. Approval voting routinely counts more marks than voters.

The strongest answer attacks the premise. What matters is equality of influence, not equality of ink. Under approval voting, every voter faces the same ballot and may mark the same maximum. Every voter weighs identically in every comparison between two candidates. Nobody's marks outrank anybody else's.

And Minnesota's own high court supplies a second answer. In 2009 it revisited the question, in a case called Minnesota Voters Alliance v. City of Minneapolis. It upheld instant-runoff voting. And it read Brown narrowly, as a complaint about one thing: Bucklin counted a voter's later choices simultaneously against her first choice.

An approval mark never counts against your other choices.

Either way, the damage was done. Grand Junction abandoned its own invention by 1921. America's first alternative-ballot movement was dead by mid-century, killed less by voters than by courts and repeal campaigns.

> Reform that wins at the ballot box can still die in a courtroom.

An honest advocate plans for that from day one.

## The proof

Approval voting's modern era began somewhere quieter: in professional societies.

Starting in 1987, four scientific and engineering societies adopted it for their internal elections. The IEEE was the marquee case, and a half dozen more societies followed.

Approval's leading theorists, Steven Brams and Peter Fishburn, documented the record. Their title is concession and claim at once: "the mixed success of approval voting."

Mixed, because what a board gives, a board can take away. The IEEE reverted to plurality in 2002. Dartmouth College adopted approval voting in 1990, for its alumni-trustee elections. It scrapped it in a governance overhaul between 2007 and 2009, after a run of insurgent petition candidates won seats over the establishment's nominees.

The official explanations and the critics disagree about why, and I can't referee motives from outside. I can observe a pattern, and you are about to meet it again with the lights on. When cardinal ballots produce winners the rule-makers didn't choose, the rule-makers change the rules.

[pause]

Now the main event. In November 2018, Fargo, North Dakota, became the first American city to adopt approval voting. Its voters did it themselves, by ballot initiative, with 63.5 percent.

In June 2020, Fargo ran the first approval-voting election for public office in modern American history. And the data answered the objection that voters will be confused, on contact.

It was a seven-way race for the city commission. Voters approved an average of 2.3 candidates each.

The two winners earned approval from 55 and from 53 percent of voters: broad mandates, of a kind Fargo's splintered plurality races hadn't produced in years. The city used the system again in 2022 and 2024, without incident.

In November 2020, St. Louis followed, passing a measure called Proposition D with 68 percent. Its design adds a second step. First comes an open, nonpartisan approval primary. Then the top two advance to a head-to-head general election, a runoff that guarantees the winner a true majority test.

In its debut, in March 2021, Tishaura Jones led, with approval from 57 percent of voters. She won the April runoff, and became the city's first Black woman mayor.

In 2025, the same machinery calmly produced the opposite result. Jones, now the incumbent, lost the runoff to Cara Spencer, 64 to 36.

The system elects no party and protects no incumbent. [beat] It just counts.

## The price

Now the price, and I am going to give it to you straight.

In 2023, North Dakota's legislature passed a bill to ban approval voting and ranked-choice voting statewide. The governor, Doug Burgum, vetoed it. He was a Republican vetoing a Republican bill, and he said that it "blatantly infringes on local control."

The House overrode his veto. The Senate sustained the veto, 28 to 19, four votes short.

The reprieve lasted one session. In 2025, a new ban passed the Senate 38 to 8, and the House 75 to 16. No veto could survive margins like those. Governor Kelly Armstrong signed it, citing the need for "a consistent, efficient and easy-to-understand voter experience across our entire state."

Fargo's approval voting is dead.

[pause]

In June 2026, the city held its first election since 2018 on pick-one ballots. The result was a time capsule from this book's second chapter: a mayor elected with 47 percent, in a multi-way field. The pathology Fargoans voted to fix was restored to them by law.

> No Fargo electorate ever voted to repeal approval voting.

Bismarck repealed it for them.

A few minutes ago, in the story of the societies, I named a pattern. Can you still say it?

[long pause]

When cardinal ballots produce winners the rule-makers didn't choose, the rule-makers change the rules.

Missouri's version ran through the voters themselves. In November 2024, a constitutional amendment called Amendment 7 passed with 68.4 percent.

It prohibited ranking candidates. And it limited each voter to "a single vote per candidate or issue."

That last clause outlaws approval voting from here on.

One carve-out survived. It covers a nonpartisan municipal election, in a city whose ordinance was already in effect. And that ordinance has to require a preliminary election, from which more than one candidate advances.

So here is a question. After Amendment 7, is approval voting banned everywhere in Missouri? Think it through.

[long pause]

Not quite everywhere. Exactly one city fits that description. St. Louis's system is grandfathered. It was in use through the 2025 cycle, and it was still alive as of mid-2026.

Every other Missouri city is constitutionally barred from ever copying it. In Missouri, home rule no longer covers the ballot method.

> Cardinal ballots in America: adopted from below, removed from above.

## Two readings

There are two readings of the backlash, and both are honest.

The first belongs to the reform advocate, who starts from Justice Brandeis's image of states, and by extension cities, as laboratories of democracy. On this reading, that is exactly what Fargo and St. Louis were: small, reversible experiments, adopted by supermajorities, generating real data.

The reform advocate argues that the results came back positive: voters used the wider ballot, and winners earned broader mandates. Then the experiments were terminated from above, by officials elected under the rules being protected.

And the reform advocate points to what is missing from every one of these stories: a city electorate voting to go back.

> The reform advocate's case: voters keep choosing these ballots, and incumbents keep un-choosing them.

The second reading belongs to the status-quo defender. Put city, county and federal contests on one ballot, under different rules, and you invite voter error, poll-worker confusion, and litigation.

The status-quo defender argues that trust in the count is democracy's scarcest resource right now. And federalism includes the state's prerogative to set election standards.

And the status-quo defender points to Missouri, where voters, not a smoke-filled room, ratified Amendment 7. The reformers' own exhibit cuts both ways, too. Two cities, five years, and a handful of races is thin evidence, not a verdict.

> The status-quo defender's case: uniformity is not a conspiracy. It is election administration.

You probably lean toward one of those readings already. Take the one you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

I hold the first reading. The rest of this book is my brief for why uniformly positive evidence, plus strong theory, beats incumbent caution.

But notice what neither side disputes. Where cardinal ballots have been used, voters used them competently, and kept them willingly. The contest is over who gets to decide.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Rating candidates is old, and it is ordinary: Venice's doges, Latvia's parliament, the Secretary-General. No reform on offer asks less new of the voter.

[beat]

Two. America's first try was struck down in 1915, in Brown v. Smallwood, by a court that counted marks per voter. Minnesota's own high court later read that ruling narrowly. Reform that wins at the ballot box can still die in a courtroom.

[beat]

Three. Fargo and St. Louis. Voters used the wider ballot, and the winners earned broad approval. No Fargo electorate ever voted to repeal approval voting.

[beat]

Four. The price. North Dakota banned approval voting, and Missouri closed the door behind St. Louis. Adopted from below, removed from above.

[beat]

And five. Two honest readings of that price. Voters keep choosing these ballots, and incumbents keep un-choosing them. Or: uniformity is not a conspiracy, it is election administration.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it doesn't.

Settled: cardinal voting is not a theory in search of a trial. Also settled, because pretending otherwise would insult you: the movement just absorbed its worst two years. That is why later chapters treat legal architecture as seriously as ballot design.

Not settled: whether score voting picks better winners than ranked-choice voting. Two cities and a diplomatic ritual can't answer that. My answer rests on the last chapter's simulations, and the math chapter finishes the argument.

Next, though, fairness demands a full chapter on the rival that is actually winning statehouses. Ranked-choice voting, and credit where it is due.

[pause]

For now, go back to those fifteen governments, rating every candidate until one name is left that they can all live with. Fargo's voters asked for the same tool.

[beat]

The obstacle has never been the voters.
