In October 2013, New York City held an entire citywide election that almost nobody attended, and everybody paid for.

[beat]

The office was public advocate. Its budget was 2.3 million dollars a year. In the Democratic primary, no candidate had cleared 40 percent, so the law required a runoff. Poll sites opened. Machines went out. Workers were paid.

About 7 percent of voters turned out. And the bill was roughly 13 million dollars.

[pause]

That is more than five years of the office's entire budget, spent to run one extra round of pick-one voting. The city's own comptroller held the episode up as Exhibit A for ballot reform. And in 2019, New Yorkers voted to never do it again.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about media and reach, and it ended by saying we have to talk about money. So here we are.

I opened with New York because it is the one money claim voting reformers can make with receipts. The rest of the story is murkier. I'm going to tell it the murky way, because this book's credibility is worth more than a tidy chapter.

We will take it in three steps. First, a diagnosis: how the pick-one ballot hands donors a veto. Second, the evidence: what changing the ballot has been shown to do to money, and what it has not. And last, the one thing I claim a score ballot changes.

## The donor's veto

Start with the diagnosis. It doesn't require believing anyone is corrupt. It only requires that everyone involved can count.

Under plurality rules, the pick-one rules, a candidate's first task isn't persuading voters. It is persuading the people who fund campaigns that she can win.

Why? Because in a pick-one election, support for a candidate who can't win actively helps the candidate her supporters like least. Donors know this. Endorsers know this.

So before a single vote is cast, there is a quiet pre-election, conducted in fundraising calls. Viability is the only question on that ballot. And a small number of large checkbooks are the electorate.

Political scientists politely call this the psychological effect of plurality: voters and elites deserting candidates who might split the vote. Here it is operating one level upstream, on money.

> The spoiler effect isn't just a counting problem. It is a fundraising weapon.

Every outsider candidacy in living memory has met the same sentence, delivered to donors before voters ever got a say. Don't waste your money on a spoiler. You'll just elect the other side.

[pause]

Notice what that does to the money race. The two major parties don't merely compete against each other. They jointly hold a veto over which alternatives get to be real. And the veto costs them nothing, because the wasted-vote logic enforces it for them.

Think of a super PAC, deciding where to aim its independent spending after Citizens United. Or a dark-money group, picking which primary to flood. All of them route money through the same two-lane system. Under pick-one rules, a third lane is mathematically a donation to your enemy.

> The duopoly isn't protected by law. It's protected by arithmetic.

Let's test that. A respected independent with broad appeal considers a Senate run. Trace her first ninety days under plurality rules, before any voter sees a ballot. Where exactly does the money stop her? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Day one. Consultants tell her the polls show 18 percent support. Impressive, but third.

Day thirty. Major donors, who privately like her, decline. A third-place candidate, they say, "just elects the worse major-party option." And their names on her finance reports would burn bridges with the side they usually fund.

Day sixty. Coverage describes her as a spoiler, and cites her fundraising gap as proof she isn't serious.

Day ninety. She's out. [beat] And no voter ever weighed in.

Every actor behaved rationally, given pick-one counting. That is why this is a structural diagnosis, not a corruption story. Nobody had to be bought. Everybody only had to count.

## What the evidence shows, and what it doesn't

That is the diagnosis. Now the part an advocacy book is supposed to skip. Does changing the ballot change the money?

Here is the honest state of knowledge, as of mid-2026. Three findings. One is verified. One is not established. And one is adjacent, but real.

First, the verified one. Sometimes a ranked, approval or score count replaces a separate second election. When it does, the second election's price tag disappears, and so does the collapse in turnout in round two. That is New York's 13 million dollars. It is also the recurring runoffs that many cities and southern states still hold.

Even skeptical researchers treat this argument as the genuine one. Two of them are Burnett and Kogan, who are no friends of inflated reform claims.

> One count in place of a second election: that saving is verified.

But be precise, because precision is credibility. The saving comes from consolidating rounds. It does not come from cardinal ballots, the ballots this book argues for, as such.

St. Louis shows why. It deliberately kept a two-round design: an approval round, then a runoff. The city judged the head-to-head in April worth its cost. Score voting can finish in one round. Whether a city should is a design choice, not a law of nature.

So sit with that choice for a moment. One round, or two? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Second, what is not established. That any alternative ballot reduces total campaign spending, super PAC activity, or donor influence.

Reform advocates have claimed fundraising effects since the movement's earliest days. Burnett and Kogan catalogue the claims. But there is no peer-reviewed consensus that elections under ranked-choice or approval voting are cheaper, cleaner, or less dependent on large donors.

Maine and Alaska did not become low-money states. Alaska's races in 2022 drew heavy national and independent spending, under the brand-new rules.

> No ballot reform has been shown to get money out of politics.

Anyone who tells you a ballot reform will do that is selling past the evidence. And when our side does it, you should wince.

The defensible claim is narrower, and structural. Cardinal ballots remove one specific mechanism: the wasted-vote extortion that steers money away from outsiders. What candidates and donors do after that is a question only adoptions at scale will answer.

[pause]

Third, the adjacent finding. Ballot reform and money reform travel together politically.

Alaska's Measure 2 bundled its new voting system with dark-money disclosure requirements, in one initiative. And tellingly, a repeal is headed to Alaska's ballot in November 2026. It would strike the disclosure rules along with the voting system.

The coalitions overlap, and so do the opponents, for a reason. Both reforms attack gatekeeping power, just at different choke points.

Now a test. One reformer tells you that places adopting a new ballot see campaign spending fall. Another tells you that a single ballot ends the cost of a separate runoff. Which of them has the record behind them?

[long pause]

The second. Ending the runoff is the claim with receipts, and New York is the standard case. The first is the overclaim this chapter warns against. There is no peer-reviewed consensus that any ballot reform reduces total spending or outside money, and Alaska's first cycles drew heavy spending.

## What a score ballot changes about money

So if score voting won't drain the swamp, why does a money chapter belong in this book at all?

[beat]

Because of what it does to the entry price of politics.

Under score rules, supporting an outsider is free. You can give the independent a 10. You can give the major-party candidate you can live with an 8. And you have surrendered nothing. No spoiler risk. No wasted vote. No lesser-evil lecture.

Follow that one change up the money chain. The donor's veto depended entirely on the wasted-vote argument. Without it, funding a long shot is a normal speculative investment, not a gift to the opposition. The viability pre-election loses its enforcement mechanism.

And a candidate's task reverts to the one we pretend it already is. Become broadly worth an 8, a 9, a 10, to actual voters. That can be earned with town halls and credibility, as plausibly as with saturation advertising.

None of this guarantees that outsiders win. Majorities still rule the totals. And money will still buy attention, under any system humans run.

The claim is narrower, and it's the one I'll defend anywhere.

> Plurality makes the donor duopoly rational. An expressive ballot makes it merely traditional.

Rational, because under pick-one rules the arithmetic backs it. Traditional, because under a score ballot nothing backs it but habit. And traditions exposed to competition have a shorter life expectancy than rules.

Now apply this chapter's own standard to its author. In what you just heard, which claims are backed by evidence, and which are predictions still waiting for data? Think it through.

[long pause]

Two are backed by evidence. The viability veto, which follows directly from pick-one counting, and is observable in every cycle's donor behavior. And the end of runoff costs, where rounds are consolidated.

Three are structural predictions. That score ballots will redirect money toward outsiders. That they will lower entry costs in practice. And that they will erode the duopoly's funding gate. Those are plausible mechanisms, with no adoptions at scale to test them yet.

If you caught the difference without being told, this book has taught you its most transferable skill. Grade your own side's claims as strictly as the other side's.

One question before we gather up. At the very start, I gave you two numbers from New York: the cost of the runoff, and the yearly budget of the office being filled. Can you still call them up?

[long pause]

Roughly 13 million dollars for the runoff. And 2.3 million for the office.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Runoff consolidation saves real, documented money. One count in place of a second election: that saving is verified. But it comes from consolidating rounds, and one round or two is a design choice.

[beat]

Two. The donor's veto. Plurality's arithmetic, not a cabal, hands donors a viability veto over outsiders. The spoiler effect isn't just a counting problem. It is a fundraising weapon.

[beat]

Three. The broader promise is not supported by current evidence. No ballot reform has been shown to get money out of politics, and that promise shouldn't be made from our podiums.

[beat]

And four. My own claim, which is a prediction. Plurality makes the donor duopoly rational. An expressive ballot makes it merely traditional.

[pause]

Here is what stays open. Nearly everything about how candidates, donors and super PACs would adapt to cardinal ballots at state scale. No state has run one yet.

So the honest pitch is not that score voting fixes money in politics. It is this. Score voting fires the one bouncer money never had to pay: the wasted-vote rule. And it lets voters, not viability gatekeepers, decide who was worth taking seriously.

What happens after that is up to those voters, and the closing chapters are about that handoff. First, though, a different kind of gatekeeper. The next chapter asks whether any of this is legal.

[pause]

For now, go back to New York, in October 2013. A second round that almost nobody attended, and everybody paid for.

That saving, I can show you the receipts for.

[beat]

The rest, I have tried to label for what it is.
