It is the night of March 2, 2021, and the newsrooms of St. Louis are reporting an election result.

Tishaura Jones, 57 percent. Cara Spencer, 46 percent. Lewis Reed, 39 percent. Andrew Jones, 14 percent.

[beat]

Add those up. They come to 156 percent.

[pause]

Those numbers should have broken America's brains. They didn't. The explanation took one sentence: voters could approve of as many candidates as they liked. And the city went to bed knowing exactly who had advanced to the April runoff.

Same night. No rounds. No lawyers.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for score voting and its sibling, approval voting, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter used St. Louis as a working model of a better primary. This chapter goes back to that same night for a different reason: how it was told.

Because there is a second night to set beside it. Three months later, New York City ran its first ranked-choice primary for mayor. The Board of Elections published a preliminary tally that accidentally included about 135,000 test ballots. It retracted the tally the same evening. And it handed every enemy of election reform a news cycle they still quote.

Let me be exact about that error. It was human, not mathematical. Ranked ballots didn't miscount anything. Officials did.

But the lesson I draw is about something else. One method produced a story a local anchor could tell in a sentence. The other produced a tabulation drama lasting days, with a built-in audience for failure.

> For a movement teaching the public a new way to vote, media is the strategy.

We will take this chapter in three steps. First, the horse race, and why our weakness in the news is also our strength. Second, the teaching record, from the only two American cities to try. And last, the thirty-second pitch, and the gaps that are still open.

## The horse race

Let me state the diagnosis honestly, because it cuts against us before it cuts for us.

Political journalism runs on the horse race. Who's up, who's down, what just changed. And ranked-choice voting, whatever its flaws, is great copy. Elimination rounds. Transferred ballots. Leads that shrink as the count proceeds. A week of "will it flip?" coverage.

Alaska's special election in 2022 got wall-to-wall national attention, partly because the count itself was a cliffhanger.

Score voting offers journalism nothing like that. "Highest average wins" is the whole story. The result arrives finished, like a box score. There is no round-by-round drama to narrate, and so there is no free publicity machine attached to the method.

> Ranked choice is great copy. Score voting arrives finished, like a box score.

That is the real reason rating methods trail ranked-choice voting in coverage. I would rather name it than whine about bias.

[pause]

Now the flip side, which Fargo and St. Louis demonstrated. The same dullness that starves us of drama also starves our opponents of chaos narratives.

Nobody has ever waited days for an approval-voting result. I say nobody ever will, because the tabulation is addition. Precinct counts sum like any other election, on the equipment cities already own.

When the New York Post needed a ranked-choice fiasco, New York provided one. When opponents needed an approval-voting fiasco, Fargo gave them an election so boring that it produced one good local headline, about arithmetic.

And remember the environment. In November 2024, "too confusing" killed reform measures across four states. In that environment, boring is not a bug.

> Boring is the moat.

Put another way: score voting trades free drama for protection against chaos coverage. After 2024, I will take that trade every time.

Let's test the arithmetic that made the headline. A reporter calls approval-voting results "misleading," because the percentages add up to more than 100. What is the one-sentence correction? And what do the St. Louis numbers actually measure? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Here is the sentence. Each percentage is the share of voters who approved that candidate, not a share of a fixed pie of votes.

So 57 percent of voters marked Jones. And 46 percent marked Spencer. Many marked both.

> Each percentage is a share of voters, not a slice of a pie.

The numbers sound like approval ratings because that is literally what they are. They can't sum to 100 unless every voter marks only one candidate. And nothing is hidden. Every figure is a plain count of the ballots supporting each candidate.

## Two first nights, one teaching record

That is the media problem. Now education.

Education was supposed to be the killer objection. A new method, surely, means confused voters and spoiled ballots. Here is what the record shows, from the only two American cities to try.

First, Fargo. June 9, 2020, was the first approval-voting election in U.S. history: a city commission race, with seven candidates for two seats. The instruction fit on a bumper sticker.

"Vote for all the candidates you approve of."

And voters used it. They averaged 2.3 approvals per ballot, and the results were out on election night.

[beat]

Then St. Louis, in March 2021. In the mayoral primary, 44,571 voters cast 69,661 approvals. That is 1.6 per ballot.

These were voters facing the method for the first time, in the middle of a pandemic. They did not all vote for a single candidate, and they did not melt down. A majority used the new freedom immediately.

By the mayoral primary of 2025, a four-way race, the average was 1.4 approvals per ballot. That is lower, as you would expect when one candidate is consolidating support. Cara Spencer stood at 68.2 percent approval. And 1.4 is still well above the 1.0 that the "everyone will just bullet-vote" objection predicts. Bullet-voting means marking one candidate only.

> Voters used the new ballot at once. No city averaged one mark per ballot.

[pause]

Now go back to the four numbers from the opening night, and hear them the way a newsroom would. They are not just a result. They are a diagnosis of the whole field: every candidate's true breadth of support, in a unit every American already knows. Percent approval.

So here is a question. Suppose that 2021 primary had been run under the old pick-one rules. What information would have been lost? Think it through.

[long pause]

Not who finished first and second. What would be lost is how many voters supported each candidate overall.

A pick-one ballot records each voter's single choice, and discards the rest of their judgment. Lewis Reed was approved by 39 percent of voters. Under pick-one rules that breadth would have been invisible, split and buried inside other candidates' totals.

And the diagnosis has predictive teeth. In the March 2025 primary, the incumbent mayor's approval came in at 33.2 percent, against the challenger Spencer's 68.2. Five weeks later, Spencer won the runoff with about 64 percent.

The primary didn't just pick finalists. It published the incumbent's actual standing with her own electorate, and the next election confirmed it almost exactly.

## The pitch, and the gaps

So voters can use the ballot. Then what is the education gap?

It is real. Most Americans have never heard of score voting. And any awareness of reform that does exist mostly attaches to ranked choice.

But notice what kind of problem that is.

> The education gap is a reach problem, not a comprehension problem.

The method itself needs no teaching beyond one breath. Here is the pitch.

"Rate every candidate, zero to ten, like you'd rate a restaurant. Highest average wins."

[beat]

There is no American adult for whom that sentence demands new concepts. We rate films, doctors, drivers, headphones, and hotel rooms. Olympic judging has used score ballots for a century. Every app on your phone asks for stars.

Ranked-choice campaigns spend millions building that kind of fluency. Here is how transfers work. Here is why round three differs from round one. Score voting gets it free, because the scoreboard idiom is already installed in every voter.

[pause]

I will concede the counterargument, because it is coming. "We've all seen star ratings gamed." Review bombs. Fake five-stars.

True, and instructive. Online ratings can be gamed because anyone can rate many times, under many names. An election has one authenticated ballot per registered voter. The abuse vector isn't there.

If anything, I think the analogy runs the other way. Plurality voting is the system that behaves like a review bomb. It forces every voter to dump their entire judgment into a single, all-or-nothing mark.

[pause]

The harder gap is operational, and it is polling. Pollsters know how to ask "whom will you vote for?" An approval or score race needs a different question: "which candidates will you approve?"

In St. Louis's first cycle, public polling was thin, and it mostly fell back on head-to-head matchups. That craft will mature with adoption, as ranked-choice polling slowly has. Until then, campaigns in these cities fly with less instrumentation than they are used to.

That is a real cost. It is one more reason the education work can't wait for the first big statewide fight.

Before we gather up, go back to the very start. I set a second night beside St. Louis. What went wrong in New York? And was it the ranked ballots that failed? Take a moment.

[long pause]

A preliminary tally included about 135,000 test ballots, and was retracted the same evening. The ballots did not fail. The error was human, not mathematical. What the method supplied was the long count, and the audience waiting for it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Two first nights. St. Louis had totals the same night, explained in one sentence. New York had a retracted tally, a human error, and a news cycle that reform's opponents still quote.

[beat]

Two. Ranked choice is great copy, and score voting arrives finished, like a box score. That costs us free drama. It also denies opponents a chaos story. Boring is the moat.

[beat]

Three. Each approval percentage is a share of voters, not a slice of a pie. That is why the numbers sum past 100, and why they sound like approval ratings.

[beat]

And four. The education gap is a reach problem, not a comprehension problem. Fargo's voters averaged 2.3 approvals per ballot, and St. Louis's 1.6, the first time they tried.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles. The explainability case is no longer hypothetical. Two cities ran first elections under a brand-new method, with one-sentence instructions, same-night results, and zero tabulation controversies.

And here is what it cannot settle. Ranked choice has bought its awareness with three decades of organizing. Can earned media and cultural fluency substitute for that? That is not settled. Reach remains the binding constraint.

So try this before we move on. A count that makes a week of news: is that an asset for a reform, or a liability? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Hold on to that case. The record so far is two cities.

Reach is a movement-building problem, and later chapters take it head-on: building the movement, and the path to fifty states. First, though, we have to talk about money. That is the next chapter.

[pause]

For now, go back to that night in St. Louis. Four numbers that sum to 156 percent, and one sentence to explain them.

[beat]

The city went to bed knowing. That is the story we have to learn to tell.
