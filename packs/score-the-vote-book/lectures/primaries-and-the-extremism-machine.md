Ask a member of Congress what they are afraid of.

The honest ones don't say "losing in November." November is booked. They say a verb that exists in no other democracy's vocabulary.

[beat]

Getting primaried.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument for a different kind of ballot, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was the math: theorems, and elections worked by hand. This chapter is about a machine you can watch running.

For most American offices, the general election stopped being the election years ago. What remains is a small, sleepy contest that most voters skip, which decides nearly everything. It is followed by a loud, expensive one, which decides nearly nothing.

> For most American offices, the primary is the real election.

That machine manufactures the politics you hate. My claim in this chapter is that a better ballot breaks it, and I will show you precisely where.

We will take it in three steps. First, the machine, part by part. Second, a working model: St. Louis. And last, Alaska, respectfully, and what each city can teach the other.

## The machine, part by part

Before the numbers, commit to a guess. Take the 2022 midterms. What share of eligible voters took part in the primaries? And what share of seats in the U.S. House were effectively decided there? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Here are the numbers. Roughly 21 percent of eligible voters took part in the 2022 primaries. That count comes from the Bipartisan Policy Center.

For the same year, a group called Unite America found that 83 percent of House seats were safe. A safe seat is so lopsided that the general election is a formality. The primary is the election.

Multiply low turnout by safe seats, and you get the machine's output.

> In 2022, about 8 percent of Americans effectively chose 83 percent of the House.

Those numbers don't belong in the same democracy. And by Unite America's update for the 2024 cycle, the squeeze was tighter still: 87 percent of the seats, chosen by 7 percent of Americans.

[pause]

Now assemble the machine. It has four parts.

Part one. Gerrymandering and geographic self-sorting make most districts safe. So the decisive round moves upstream, to the primary.

Part two. Turnout in that round is a fifth of the electorate, skewed toward the most engaged partisans.

Part three is the part reformers underrate. The primary is counted by plurality: pick one, most votes wins. In a crowded field, a candidate with a devoted 30 percent faction beats five rivals who split the other 70. So the rational strategy is not breadth. It is heat. Own a faction, and pray the field splits.

Part four. Every incumbent watches it happen to a colleague, and updates. The lesson they learn is never to be outflanked. Don't cross the base. Don't compromise visibly. Don't give a challenger a lane.

The machine doesn't need to fire often. Like any deterrent, it works through fear.

[pause]

One honesty check before the prescription, because this book doesn't get to caricature its own villain.

The folk theory says primary voters are frothing extremists. The best survey research says otherwise. It comes from Sides, Tausanovitch, Vavreck, and Warshaw, who matched turnout records to surveys across multiple cycles. They found primary voters similar to their party's rank and file, in demographics and in ideology.

So the machine's problem isn't monstrous voters.

> The machine's problem is arithmetic, not monstrous voters.

In plainer words: a tiny, party-sorted electorate, counted by a method that rewards factional intensity, decides seats the general electorate can't contest. Fix the arithmetic and you fix the incentive. No demonization required.

Let's test that. A reform group proposes moving primaries to June and doubling their turnout. It changes nothing else. A moment ago I gave you four parts. Can you still name them? And which ones does higher turnout fix? Think it through.

[long pause]

Safe seats. Low turnout. A plurality count. And incumbents' fear of the flank.

Higher turnout fixes only part two, and only partly. The seats are still safe, so the primary is still the election. The count is still plurality, so a crowded field still rewards the most intense faction. It is now a faction of a bigger electorate. And incumbents still fear the flank, because the threat comes from being outflanked in a one-mark count.

> Turnout helps. It cannot, by itself, change what the count rewards.

That is why my prescription targets the ballot, not the calendar.

## The working model: St. Louis

That is the machine. Now the part of this book where I get to stop simulating, and point.

St. Louis, Missouri, runs the model this chapter prescribes. It does so under a measure called Proposition D, which passed with 68 percent in November 2020.

Here is how it works. All candidates, whatever their party, appear on one March ballot. Every voter may approve as many of them as they want. Then the top two finishers advance to an April runoff, which the whole city decides.

> St. Louis: approve as many as you want. The top two advance.

No party gatekeeping. No spoilers. No lecture about a wasted vote. And the decisive round happens in the election with higher turnout, not the lower one.

It has now run three citywide cycles. Watch what the data does.

Start with the first mayoral primary under the new rules, in March 2021. Tishaura Jones was approved on 57 percent of the ballots. Cara Spencer was approved on about 46 percent. Lewis Reed, on nearly 39. And a fourth candidate, Andrew Jones, on about 14.

[beat]

Add those shares up, and they come to about 156 percent of the ballots. So here is a question. Does that mean some voters got more influence than others? What did the ballot actually do? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Every voter had the same power: approve anyone you accept. The total runs past 100 because voters averaged 1.56 approvals each. A voter could back both Jones and Spencer, so candidates competed for shared support, instead of fighting to fracture each other's.

That overlap is the point. Support was measured, not split.

One caution on that. Approval sends the two broadest candidates forward. It does not guarantee that anyone clears half the ballots.

[pause]

Now picture that 2021 field in the old St. Louis, with partisan primaries and a pick-one ballot. It would have been a classic faction-splitter: three serious candidates dividing overlapping coalitions, and a winner possibly in the low thirties. Game over, in a city where one party's nomination decides the office.

Under approval, the two candidates with the broadest support advanced. Tishaura Jones then won the April runoff, 51.7 to 47.8. And that runoff drew roughly 30 percent more ballots than the March round.

The decisive choice moved downstream, to the bigger electorate. That is the exact opposite of the machine.

[pause]

Then 2025 delivered the part no simulation can. Accountability.

Mayor Jones stood for reelection, and the same system that elected her removed her. In March, Cara Spencer drew approvals on 68.2 percent of the ballots. The incumbent drew 33.2. Then Spencer won the runoff, with just over 64 percent.

There were no spoiler dynamics to hide behind, and no claim that a divided field stole it. The whole city, asked about every candidate, said no.

I will be careful here. The published research on how St. Louis candidates behave is still thin, and I won't dress anecdotes as findings. What the verified results show is structural, and it comes in three parts. In two open contests and one incumbent defense, the broad-support candidates reached the runoff. The runoff happened at higher turnout. And an unpopular incumbent could be challenged without anyone gaming a split field.

I call that the extremism machine running backwards. And the legislature in Jefferson City found it threatening enough to ban the method statewide for everyone else, grandfathering St. Louis alone.

## Alaska's top-four, respectfully

St. Louis is one live reform. The other is Alaska's.

Alaska holds one primary for all candidates. The top four advance. Then the general election is counted by ranked choice.

Earlier chapters of this book gave Alaska its due, and the headline case stands. Lisa Murkowski was censured by her own party for an impeachment vote. She was the textbook target of part four of the machine. She survived the open primary, and she won the final count in 2022 with 53.69 percent.

She won because the whole electorate, not a partisan sliver, had the decisive say. Alaska proves the architecture. Move the real choice to November, and the flank loses its veto.

[pause]

But look closely at Alaska's primary ballot, and you will find part three of the machine still in it. Vote for one.

The top-four primary counts by plurality. It takes four survivors instead of one, and that blunts faction capture. It does not remove the incentive.

The largest field Alaskans have faced under the new rules was 48 candidates for one House seat. Each voter could mark exactly one of them. With a field split that finely, a few percent of intense support can make the top four.

That is precisely where a pick-one count is weakest. And it is where an approval or score count belongs: a vast field, low information, and the goal of finding the four candidates with the broadest acceptance, not the four loudest factions. Ranking 48 candidates would be impractical anyway.

Supporters of ranked choice voting have an objection to that, and it is a true one. It is called later-no-harm. Approve a second candidate, and you can help that candidate edge out your favorite for a slot. I grant it. The price of refusing it is the current rule, where supporting anyone but your favorite is impossible. With four slots, I think the trade strongly favors breadth.

So St. Louis and Alaska aren't rivals. I see them as two halves of one design.

> Alaska moved the decision to the right round. St. Louis put the right ballot in it.

One question to carry out of this section. Alaska kept the ranked ballot for its general election. St. Louis uses approval and a runoff. One of those probably convinces you less. What is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case in mind.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. For most American offices, the primary is the real election. In 2022, about 8 percent of Americans effectively chose 83 percent of the House.

[beat]

Two. The machine has four parts: safe seats, low turnout, a plurality count, and incumbents' fear of the flank. Its problem is arithmetic, not monstrous voters. And turnout cannot, by itself, change what the count rewards.

[beat]

Three. St. Louis: approve as many as you want, and the top two advance to a runoff with higher turnout. In 2021 that sent the two broadest candidates forward. In 2025 it removed an incumbent, peaceably.

[beat]

And four. Alaska moved the decision to the right round. St. Louis put the right ballot in it. Alaska's primary is still a pick-one count.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. Two things.

Whether St. Louis's results generalize beyond one heavily one-party city. Three cycles is a record, not a literature.

And whether any state will pair Alaska's architecture with an approval or score ballot in the primary. That combination is what this book regards as the machine's true off switch.

The chapters ahead take up the forces that guard the status quo. The next one is about the stories told about reform: the media, and the work of teaching a new ballot. After it comes the money.

[pause]

For now, go back to that member of Congress, and the verb. Getting primaried.

[beat]

The fear is real. It is also arithmetic. And arithmetic can be changed.
