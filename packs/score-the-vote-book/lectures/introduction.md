You walk into a booth. You pick one name. You leave.

[beat]

That single mark is supposed to carry your entire political judgment. Every priority. Every reservation. Every "I'd settle for her."

It can't. [beat] It never could.

[pause]

A word about what you are hearing. This is the introduction to a book: Score the Vote, by Chris Borgia. It is a book that argues for something, a different kind of ballot. And the "I" in it is the author. The case you are about to hear is his.

[pause]

Democracy has a math problem.

A ballot that lets you say exactly one thing about a field of five candidates throws away nearly everything you know. And then we act surprised when the result doesn't look like what anybody wanted.

Two scenes, from the year I finished this book.

The first is at the United Nations. In mid-2026, the Security Council began choosing the tenth Secretary-General, from four candidates. It uses straw polls, in which each member marks every candidate "encourage," "discourage," or "no opinion."

When the stakes are real, and the diplomats are voting on each other, they do not pick one. [beat] They rate everyone. They have done it that way for decades.

The second scene is Fargo, North Dakota. In 2018, with 64 percent of the vote, Fargo's own citizens adopted a system called approval voting. They used it happily, three times, and never voted to repeal it. Then the state legislature banned it. In June 2026, Fargo held its first city election since the ban.

Back to pick-one. [beat] By law.

[pause]

This book stands between those two scenes. On one side is a method so natural that the world's most careful institutions quietly rely on it. On the other is a political machinery that keeps removing it from public ballots. Both halves are true, and I intend to prove both.

We will take this introduction in three steps. First, three ballots, and how much each one can hear. Second, the argument of this book, and who it argues with. And last, what I owe you: three concessions, made before the evidence begins.

## Three ways to ask the same question

Every voting method is a question put to you, the voter. The differences hide in how much of your answer the ballot can hear.

So take one voter and three candidates. Call them Avila, Brooks, and Chen.

The first ballot is the one most Americans use. Its name is plurality: pick one, and the most votes wins. On that ballot, our voter marks Avila, and that is all the ballot hears. Your favorite, nothing else.

[beat]

The second is the ranked ballot, the one behind ranked choice voting. On it, our voter puts Avila first, Brooks second, and Chen third. Now the ballot has heard an order.

[beat]

The third is the score ballot. Our voter gives Avila a 5, Brooks a 4, and Chen a 0. That is score voting, the subject of this book. Give every candidate a score, and the highest total wins. One count. No rounds, no eliminations, no transfers.

[pause]

Now go back to the middle ballot, and notice what it loses. Avila first, Brooks second. But is Brooks a near-twin you would cheerfully accept, or a barely tolerable hedge against Chen? [beat] The ranking cannot say.

> A ranking keeps the order. It loses the distance.

Social choice theorists have names for the two kinds of information. Ranked information is called ordinal. Scored information is called cardinal. An ordinal ballot tells the count who comes ahead of whom. A cardinal ballot tells it how much, for everyone. That one distinction will do more work in this book than any other idea in it.

Let's test it. Keep the same three candidates, and make the feelings your own. You love Avila. You would happily settle for Brooks. And you would consider moving if Chen won.

Which of the three ballots can carry the difference between your feeling for Brooks and your feeling for Chen? And what, exactly, do the other two lose? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the score ballot can carry it.

The pick-one ballot loses everything but your favorite, so to the count, Brooks and Chen look identical. The ranked ballot keeps the order: Brooks above Chen. But "happily settle" against "consider moving" is a distance, and a distance is precisely what a ranking cannot carry. Score voting was built to keep it.

## The argument of this book

That is the idea underneath the book. Now the argument, and the people I am arguing with.

The reform movement already knows the pick-one ballot is broken. Ranked choice voting has won states. Another reform, called STAR voting, has its evangelists.

> Ranked choice voting is the reform with the headlines and the momentum.

These reformers are allies in the fight that matters most, the fight against plurality. I will give them their due, by name, with their best evidence on the table.

But this book makes a sharper claim. Compare the systems head to head, on the measures that actually matter. There are four. How satisfied voters are with the outcomes, across millions of simulated elections. How gracefully each system degrades when voters get tactical. How simply each one counts. And how often each finds the candidate the whole electorate can live with.

On those measures, cardinal ballots win.

> The claim of this book: rating beats ranking.

Score voting, and its two-level little sibling, approval voting, are the best practical repair for American elections. That is the case. [beat] The next eighteen chapters are the evidence.

## What I owe you

Advocacy books have a credibility problem. So let me state my rules.

Every number, date, and case in this book is checked against primary sources: official tabulations, court opinions, the published studies themselves. And when the strongest argument is against me, you will hear it from me first.

So here are three concessions, up front.

The first. In 1973, the philosopher Allan Gibbard proved that every non-trivial voting method can be manipulated, by someone, somewhere.

> No voting method is immune to strategy. Score voting included.

Anyone who promises you a strategy-proof ballot is selling something.

The second concession.

> Score and approval voting have a short, bruised real-world track record.

Fargo's approval voting is dead. Its state legislature banned it in 2025, over the city's objections. And in Missouri, voters constitutionally barred any new city from following St. Louis. I will tell those stories straight, defeats included, because the pattern they reveal is itself part of the argument. Adopted from below. [beat] Removed from above.

The third concession.

> Tactical voters compress score ballots toward approval ballots.

In plainer words, a voter playing tactics squeezes a ballot of many levels down toward a ballot of two. Critics hold this up as a gotcha. I will show you the math, and explain why I consider approval voting a floor worth standing on, not a trapdoor.

[pause]

Now go back to the first of those three. A philosopher proved something in 1973. What did he prove? And given that, what can this book claim about strategy? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Allan Gibbard proved that every non-trivial voting method can be manipulated, cardinal ballots included. So this book cannot promise a ballot immune to strategy, and it does not.

Its claim is comparative, not absolute. The honest question is never "can it be gamed?" The honest question is "what happens when it is?" And that, it turns out, is where score voting shines. When voters scheme, score and approval hold up better than the alternatives.

[pause]

One promise, to set beside the concessions. This is not only a book of arguments. It is a toolkit.

By the end, you will be able to explain score voting to three people. Your skeptical uncle at Thanksgiving. The coworker who thinks voting reform sounds complicated. And the friend who has given up on politics entirely. Clearly, concisely, with the receipts.

And if you want to do more than nod along, the closing chapters get concrete: model ballot language, the organizations doing the work, and the campaigns worth your weekends.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The pick-one ballot hears your favorite, nothing else. The ranked ballot hears an order. The score ballot hears how much, for everyone.

[beat]

Two. A ranking keeps the order. It loses the distance. Ranked information is ordinal. Scored information is cardinal.

[beat]

Three. The claim of this book: rating beats ranking. On voter satisfaction, on tactics, on the simplicity of the count, and on finding the candidate the whole electorate can live with.

[beat]

And four. Three concessions. No voting method is immune to strategy, score voting included. The real-world track record is short and bruised. And tactical voters compress score ballots toward approval ballots.

[pause]

Here is what this introduction has not settled. [beat] Everything, so far.

I have asserted that the pick-one ballot destroys information, that the destruction has consequences, and that a better ballot exists. Assertions are cheap.

So before the proof begins, here is something to try. Rating, or ranking. One of the two probably sounds less convincing to you right now. Take that one. What is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep that case with you. If I am as good as my word, you will hear it again, from me.

The proof starts in the next chapter, with the system we already have. A governor elected with 37.6 percent of the vote. A presidency that turned on 537 votes, while more than 97,000 went to a third candidate. And a French political scientist who saw the whole pattern coming, in 1951.

[pause]

For now, go back to the booth. One name. One mark. And everything else you know, left unsaid.

[beat]

This book doesn't end when you close it. It ends when your ballot changes.
