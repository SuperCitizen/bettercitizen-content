Here is a question that sounds unanswerable.

Across every election that could ever happen, which voting method picks the best available candidate most often?

[beat]

Think about what it would take to find out. You would need to rerun the same voters through five different systems. And then you would need to read their minds, to learn how satisfied each outcome left them.

You can't run that experiment on a real electorate. So researchers do the next best thing. [beat] They build the voters.

They simulate an electorate whose preferences they know exactly, because they wrote them. They run those same simulated voters through plurality, instant runoff, approval, score, and STAR. They repeat that across thousands of randomized elections: polarized ones, fragmented ones, sleepy two-candidate ones. And then they measure how much satisfaction each method delivered, compared to the best any method could have done.

[pause]

That measure is called Voter Satisfaction Efficiency. VSE, for short.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter was about strategy: how a reform like mine can win. In this chapter I argue that the win is worth having, and these simulations are my best evidence.

They are also my biggest test as an honest advocate. The numbers help me enormously. [beat] And they do not say quite what some of my allies claim.

We will take it in four steps. First, the yardstick: what VSE measures, and what it assumes. Second, the numbers, with nothing airbrushed. Third, what strategy does to a score ballot. And last, two real elections, and the limits of any simulation.

## The yardstick

The standard study was published in 2017, by Jameson Quinn. He was then a PhD candidate in statistics at Harvard, and an officer of the Center for Election Science. He built his voters in realistic ideological clusters, not as random noise.

Here is how his yardstick works. In each simulated election, there is one candidate whose victory would maximize the voters' total satisfaction. Call that candidate the ideal winner.

A method that always elects the ideal winner scores 100 percent. A method that picks winners at random scores 0. Real methods land in between, and the share of that gap a method closes is its VSE.

> VSE: zero is a random winner. 100 is the ideal winner, every time.

Put another way, VSE grades a method, not an election. It tells you how much of the distance the method covers, from a random draw to the best pick available.

The idea has an older sibling. In 2000, the mathematician Warren D. Smith ran massive simulations of his own. He measured what is called Bayesian regret: the total satisfaction lost, relative to the ideal pick. It is the same quantity, with the sign flipped.

Smith's tables and Quinn's tables were built seventeen years apart, with different code and different models of the voter. Keep that independence in mind. It will matter when you hear how similar their conclusions are.

Now, one part of this yardstick is not math. It is a moral assumption, and you should hear it named.

VSE assumes that the right winner is the one who maximizes average satisfaction, across all voters. That means weighing a minority's intense preferences against a majority's mild ones. That is utilitarianism, named and on the table.

Not everyone accepts it. Here is the objection.

> The majority-rule objection: a bare majority should always win, however mild its preference.

If you believe that, notice what you are disputing. You are not disputing the simulations. [beat] You are disputing the target. The math chapter takes that argument head-on.

[pause]

Let's test the yardstick before we use it. Suppose a method's VSE is 92 percent. What does that actually mean? Does it mean that 92 percent of voters are satisfied with the winner? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It does not. It also does not mean that the winner received 92 percent of the vote. And it does not mean a 92 percent chance that the majority's favorite wins.

It means this. Averaged across the simulated elections, the method captures 92 percent of the satisfaction gap between a random winner and the ideal winner.

So VSE says nothing about any single election, any vote share, or any individual voter's happiness. And remember who the ideal winner is. It is the utilitarian pick, which is not always the majority's favorite.

## The numbers, with nothing airbrushed

That is the yardstick. Now the numbers. Start with honest voters, in Quinn's published simulations. Four methods.

STAR voting is a score ballot from 0 to 5, followed by an automatic runoff. Its VSE is about 98 percent.

Score voting, on a scale of 0 to 10: about 96.

Instant runoff, the count used in ranked-choice voting: about 92.

And plurality, the pick-one ballot: [beat] 75.

[pause]

So the reform methods cluster near the ceiling. Plurality sits in the basement, about twenty points below the cluster.

One caution, and it covers every number in this chapter. These are simulation results. They are estimates from a model, not measurements of real elections.

Honest voters are only half the story. The interesting half is what happens when the simulated voters scheme.

And here I owe you a correction, aimed at my own side. You will find advocates claiming that score voting holds 95-plus percent "even with strategic voters." Quinn's published numbers do not say that.

> Score voting: 96 with honest voters, 84 when everyone strategizes.

That is a twelve-point slide. It is nearly as steep as instant runoff's, which falls from about 92 to about 79.

I am showing you the real chart because the real chart is still my best argument. And an advocate who needs to retouch his own evidence has already lost.

[pause]

So what does the real chart say? Three findings survive every scenario Quinn published, and Smith's independent tables before him.

First. Plurality gives up roughly twenty points of satisfaction, compared with the reform cluster.

> Plurality is the basement, in every run, under every strategy assumption.

Second. The cardinal family owns the ceiling. Those are the ballots that rate candidates: score, STAR, and approval. Score and STAR lead the honest-voter chart. STAR barely degrades: it slips from about 98 to about 91 when voters scheme. And approval voting is remarkable for its floor. Even fully strategic approval voting stays around 89 percent.

Third. Instant runoff is real progress, with a real ceiling. It is a genuine gain over plurality, of ten points and more. But its strategic floor, 79, is the lowest of any reform shown. And even its honest performance trails the cardinal cluster.

## What strategy does to a score ballot

A moment ago I gave you two numbers for score voting. One with honest voters, and one when everyone strategizes. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

96, and 84. So why does score voting slide twelve points, while approval voting barely moves?

Because the best tactical play on a score ballot is exaggeration. You identify the two likely front-runners. You give the one you prefer the maximum, and the other the minimum. And then you push everyone else to an endpoint too.

A fully strategic score ballot has no 3s on it. It is an approval ballot wearing extra bubbles.

Let me walk one ballot through it. Picture a three-way race, and your honest scores. Your favorite gets a 5. An acceptable compromise gets a 3. And the front-runner you fear gets a 0.

Five, three, zero.

Then the polls arrive. The compromise and the feared front-runner are neck and neck. Your favorite is trailing far behind.

What does a coldly tactical voter do with that ballot? And what type of ballot has she just reinvented? Work it out.

[pause 5s]

She raises the compromise from a 3 to a 5. That maximizes her ballot's force in the only contest likely to matter. She keeps her favorite at 5, and the front-runner at 0.

Five, five, zero. [beat] Every score now sits at an endpoint, and that is an approval ballot.

Now notice what she did not have to do. She did not have to lower her true favorite.

> Strategy under score voting exaggerates. It never forces favorite betrayal.

Under plurality, the same tactical logic would have made her abandon her favorite entirely.

[pause]

One more piece explains the slide. Exaggeration has to be aimed, and voters aim it with guesses about who is really in contention. When those guesses are wrong, it backfires. In Quinn's runs, that mis-aimed aggression is what drags strategic score voting below approval's band.

Critics present this collapse as the gotcha that unravels score voting. And the collapse is real: a twelve-point slide, and a ballot with no 3s left on it.

> The critics' case: when voters scheme, score voting collapses into approval voting.

Take that as given, and walk through what it implies.

If voters are honest, score voting lands at 96, up near the ceiling. If voters are ruthless, score voting becomes approval voting. And strategic approval voting still outperforms strategic instant runoff by ten points, and plurality by more.

So the system's failure mode is a system most reformers would crawl over glass to get. That is what graceful degradation means. And it is why this book treats score and approval as one family, with one argument.

> The ceiling belongs to honest scorers. The floor is approval. The floor is high.

## The center squeeze

Go back to the third finding: instant runoff has a real ceiling. Simulations put a number on that. Two real elections show what it looks like, because instant runoff's signature failure has now happened twice, in marquee American races.

Burlington, Vermont, 2009. The Republican, Kurt Wright, led the first count. The Progressive, Bob Kiss, won after transfers. And the Democrat, Andy Montroll, was eliminated in the middle of the count.

Yet a 54 percent majority preferred Montroll over Kiss, head to head. A larger majority preferred him over Wright. He was eliminated for the crime of being everyone's strong second choice.

Burlington's voters repealed instant runoff the next year, 52 to 48.

Alaska repeated the shape at congressional scale, in a special election in August 2022. Nick Begich was preferred, head to head, over both Sarah Palin and Mary Peltola. [beat] And he exited first.

That shape has a name: the center squeeze. Elimination-based counting kills broad but not passionate candidates early, before their breadth can ever be counted. VSE is, in a sense, those two stories run at scale.

Cardinal methods are immune, for a boring structural reason. There are no eliminations. Every candidate's full support, mild and intense, sits in the totals from the first moment to the last.

Immune to the squeeze, mind you. Not to every failure. Score voting has its own worst case, and I will build it for you with my own hands, in the math chapter.

## Models have authors

Now the limits, stated plainly. VSE numbers are model outputs, and models have authors.

Change the distribution of voters, the number of candidates, or the information voters strategize with, and the ceiling reshuffles. In some of Quinn's own scenarios, STAR edges out score. So does a method called ranked pairs. In Smith's tables, range voting, another name for score voting, leads more uniformly.

So treat any single decimal as decoration.

What you should not treat as decoration is the structure that never reshuffles. Two researchers, seventeen years apart, with independent code and different assumptions, found the same three tiers. Plurality at the bottom, by a chasm. Elimination-based ranking in the middle. Cardinal methods at the top, with the gentlest worst cases.

The small gap between STAR and score is noise.

> The twenty-point gap between the cardinal family and plurality is the signal.

And that gap dwarfs the gaps the reform movement spends most of its energy fighting over.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. VSE is a yardstick built on simulated voters. Zero is a random winner. 100 is the ideal winner, every time. And the ideal winner is the one who maximizes average satisfaction: a utilitarian assumption, named and on the table.

[beat]

Two. The numbers, unretouched. Score voting: 96 with honest voters, 84 when everyone strategizes.

[beat]

Three. The findings that survive in both researchers' work. Plurality is the basement. The cardinal family owns the ceiling. And instant runoff is real progress, with a real ceiling.

[beat]

Four. Strategy under score voting exaggerates. It never forces favorite betrayal. A fully strategic score ballot is an approval ballot. So the ceiling belongs to honest scorers, the floor is approval, and the floor is high.

[beat]

And five. Models have authors. Any single decimal is decoration. The twenty-point gap between the cardinal family and plurality is the signal.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter settles, and what it doesn't.

Settled: the simulation literature's verdict on the shape of the problem. How far from the ceiling each family sits, and how each one degrades under strategy. Score voting beats instant runoff with honest voters and with strategic ones, in these models. And approval anchors the strategic world.

Not settled: everything simulations cannot reach. Whether real voters behave like modeled ones. And whether maximizing average satisfaction is even the right goal. That is the strongest philosophical objection to everything in this chapter. I take it up in the math chapter, with a worked example built against my own side.

Until then, try it yourself. A minority that cares intensely. A majority that cares mildly. Which winner should a count look for: the one with the highest average satisfaction, or the one the bare majority prefers? Take the answer you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Hold on to that case. No simulation can settle it, because it is a question about the target.

And there is one more thing a simulation cannot tell us: whether any of this survives contact with actual city halls, actual lawyers, and actual legislatures. That is exactly where the next chapter goes.

[pause]

For now, go back to the question that sounded unanswerable. Nobody can rerun a real election five ways, and then read the voters' minds. So two researchers built the voters, seventeen years apart.

[beat]

Change the model, and the decimals move. The shape does not.
