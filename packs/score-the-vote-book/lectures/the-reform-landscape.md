On April 16, 2025, the governor of North Dakota signed a bill. Its name was House Bill 1297.

[beat]

With that signature, he erased a voting system. The people of Fargo had adopted it in 2018, by nearly two-thirds. They had used it, without incident, in three city elections.

The governor, Kelly Armstrong, gave his reason. North Dakota needed, he said, "a consistent, efficient and easy-to-understand voter experience across our entire state."

[beat]

Fargo had not asked.

[pause]

In June 2026, Fargo elected a mayor the old way. Five candidates. Pick one. Most votes wins. [beat] Majority optional.

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter wrote my prescription: score voting. But before this book argues for one reform over the others, you need the whole board.

So hold on to Fargo, because it tells you what this chapter is about. The American reform landscape in mid-2026 is not a tidy march of progress. It is a contested map. Reforms advance by ballot initiative. They get repealed by legislatures. They survive by hundreds of votes. And occasionally, they are deleted from above.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two families of reform that have been winning elections. Second, what two cities teach about where to campaign. Third, the families that haven't landed yet. And last, where score voting fits: a claim I call the beachhead argument.

## Two families that win

Every reform on this map attacks the same defect. Plurality voting, the pick-one ballot, reads one mark per voter and ignores everything else you think. The families differ in what extra information they collect, and in what they do with it.

Here is the first thing the map says.

> Only two reform families have been winning American elections at scale.

They are ranked ballots and approval ballots. And both are now in open warfare with state legislatures.

Take ranked-choice voting first. Its ballot asks you to rank the candidates. It gets a full, fair chapter of its own later in this book, and it is the movement's flagship. By FairVote's count, in February 2026, it runs in 49 jurisdictions.

The anchors are Maine, statewide since 2018, and Alaska, since 2022. Then New York City. And now Washington, D.C., where an initiative passed in 2024 with about 73 percent.

The counterattack is just as organized. Nineteen states have banned ranked-choice voting outright. The first were Tennessee and Florida, in 2022. The nineteenth was Ohio, in March 2026.

> Ranked-choice voting runs in 49 jurisdictions. Nineteen states have banned it.

And Alaska faces its third statewide vote on the system, in November 2026. The last attempt at repeal, in 2024, failed by 743 votes. [beat] Nobody should pretend November is safe.

[pause]

Now the approval family. Its ballot asks you to check everyone you accept. It is smaller and bloodier, and it is where this book lives.

It has two cities. Fargo adopted approval voting in 2018, the first American city ever to do so. It used the system in 2020, in 2022, and in 2024. Then it was overruled by its own state.

St. Louis adopted its version in November 2020, with 68 percent. It works in two rounds. In March, an open, nonpartisan approval round. In April, a runoff between the top two.

St. Louis has now run three citywide cycles that way, including a 2025 election in which an incumbent mayor was defeated. And it survived a statewide ban. In November 2024, Missouri's Amendment 7 banned ranked-choice and approval voting, by about two to one. But it grandfathered the one city already using approval.

> As of mid-2026, St. Louis is America's only approval-voting government.

The beachhead. Under siege. [beat] Holding.

## Two cities, one lesson

Put those two cities side by side, and let's test what they teach.

Fargo's approval voting was killed in 2025. St. Louis's survived a statewide ban the year before. Neither city voted to abandon its system.

So what is the structural lesson, for reformers choosing where to campaign? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Here it is.

> Cities don't control their own election law. States do.

Fargo's reform died by ordinary statute, in Bismarck, at the state legislature. St. Louis's lived because Missouri's ban happened to run through a constitutional amendment, one that exempted existing users. The difference was not the city. It was the state above the city.

Put another way, a city win in a hostile state sits exposed. The legislature can override it, and the word for that is preemption. The same is true in a state that is trending hostile.

So the movement's hardest strategic question is not "which city next?" It is this: which states protect local choice? And can reform win statewide before a legislature slams the door?

Nineteen bans say the window matters.

## The families that haven't landed yet

That is the ground where reform has been winning. Now the rest of the board: four more families, taken briefly.

First, score and STAR. They are the rest of the cardinal family, the rating family, beyond approval. Their ballot asks you to grade candidates on a scale. They have simulations. They have organizations.

> Score voting and STAR voting have zero public adoptions.

STAR has been rejected three times. Lane County, in 2018, by under five points. Eugene, its own hometown, in May 2024, two to one against. And Oakridge, that November, by 24 votes. Those defeats get a full chapter later.

And pure score voting has never reached an American government ballot. This family's record is organizational elections and one party-run statewide primary, nothing more. I will argue that is a sequencing problem, not a verdict. But facts first.

[pause]

Second, fusion voting, the revival nobody saw coming. Under fusion, one candidate appears as the nominee of several parties, and their vote totals are combined. It was routine in the nineteenth century. Then it was banned almost everywhere, precisely because it made third parties dangerous.

Today it survives fully only in New York and Connecticut, and the revival runs through courtrooms. In New Jersey, a state constitutional challenge was rejected by the appeals court in February 2025. In Wisconsin, a parallel suit, filed that April, was still moving through state court in the spring of 2026.

Fusion's logic is different. It does not change how you mark a ballot, or how it is counted. It changes who can endorse. Watch it. It is the reform that major-party moderates find easiest to love.

[pause]

Third, proportional representation, the deep-water reform. It uses districts that elect several members each, so that 40 percent of the votes gets you about 40 percent of the seats.

Cambridge, Massachusetts, has elected its council this way since 1941. Portland, Oregon, ran its first proportional council elections in November 2024. And a federal bill, the Fair Representation Act, would do the same for the entire U.S. House. It is a serious bill with no path to passage in this Congress, and its sponsors know it. It exists to define the destination.

[pause]

Fourth, the top-two and top-four primaries. Washington and California use top-two. Alaska uses top-four. These change when the field narrows, not how you mark the ballot.

Nevada passed a top-five version in 2022. It died at its constitutionally required second vote, in 2024. Even winning once isn't winning. And Louisiana moved the other way, toward closed congressional primaries, starting in 2026.

The door swings both directions.

[pause]

Let's test the map. A friend tells you that approval voting is spreading. Fargo and St. Louis both use it, the friend says. What would you say back?

[long pause]

That it is backwards. North Dakota's House Bill 1297 banned approval voting, and erased Fargo's system, in April 2025. St. Louis alone remains.

And one more. Earlier, I gave you two numbers for ranked-choice voting. One for where it runs. One for where it is banned. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

49 jurisdictions. Nineteen states.

> The reform map is expansion and rollback at once.

That is the defining fact of 2026.

## The beachhead argument

That is the board. Now the strategic claim this book builds on, stated plainly, so you can hold me to it.

Approval voting is score voting with the smallest possible scale. 0 or 1. Unacceptable or acceptable.

That makes it, mechanically, the smallest change to the ballot any reform can propose. Same grid. Same machines. Same count. With exactly one rule deleted. [beat] "Vote for only one."

> My claim: approval voting is the smallest ballot change any reform can propose.

Here is what that means in practice. No new equipment. No transfer rounds to explain. No result a precinct captain can't verify with addition.

Ranked-choice voting has documented weak points, which this book examines later. One is exhausted ballots. Another is the center squeeze, in Alaska's 2022 special election. When those come up, approval is the reform that structurally does not have those failure modes. And when cost comes up, it is the reform that costs nothing.

That is why a movement a fraction of ranked choice's size has two city-scale wins to its name. And it is why legislatures hostile to reform bothered to ban approval by name, in North Dakota and in Missouri.

They can see the beachhead too.

[pause]

A beachhead is not a destination. The destination is fuller expressiveness: the 0-to-10 ballot this book defends. It captures not just whether you accept a candidate, but how much.

The path runs through approval, because every approval win normalizes the family's core idea. Count every opinion a voter has, all at once, with no eliminations. St. Louis is the working proof, and the chapter on primaries will show what its model does to candidate incentives.

Now a question I will leave with you. Score voting has zero public adoptions. I say that is a sequencing problem. Someone else may say it is a verdict. Take the reading you find less convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Hold on to that case. This chapter does not settle it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The reform map is expansion and rollback at once. Only two reform families have been winning American elections at scale: ranked ballots and approval ballots.

[beat]

Two. Ranked-choice voting runs in 49 jurisdictions. Nineteen states have banned it. And Alaska votes on repeal a third time, in November 2026.

[beat]

Three. Fargo was overruled, and St. Louis is America's only approval-voting government. Cities don't control their own election law. States do.

[beat]

Four. Score voting and STAR voting have zero public adoptions. Fusion, proportional representation and the top-two and top-four primaries are each fighting on ground of their own.

[beat]

And five. The beachhead argument. Approval voting is score voting with the smallest possible scale, and the smallest ballot change any reform can propose.

[pause]

What this chapter settles is the state of every reform family, as of mid-2026. What is running, what is banned, what is pending, with the margins attached.

Here is what it does not settle. Whether the rollback wave crests, or keeps building. November in Alaska will say a lot. And whether the beachhead argument survives contact with the objections: legal, strategic, and psychological. A later chapter takes those head-on.

The next chapter starts the comparison between the families, on one measure. Which ballot is the easiest to explain, to count, and to trust?

[pause]

For now, go back to Fargo. Its voters chose a system, used it in three elections, and never voted to remove it.

[beat]

Five candidates. Pick one. Most votes wins. Majority optional.
