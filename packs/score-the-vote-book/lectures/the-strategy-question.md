On the night of November 5, 2024, the election-reform movement lost everywhere at once.

[beat]

Colorado rejected a ranked-choice initiative, 53.5 to 46.5. Idaho crushed one, with barely 30 percent voting yes. Oregon said no by twenty points. Nevada had approved ranked-choice voting in 2022. It refused the required second passage, and killed it. Missouri went further, and wrote a ban into its constitution.

The only bright spots were small and local. Washington, D.C., adopted ranked-choice voting with 73 percent. And Alaska kept its system by 743 votes, out of 321,000. A near-death experience, confirmed by recount.

Within eighteen months, the count of states banning ranked-choice voting reached nineteen.

[pause]

This is a chapter of Score the Vote. It is an argument, and the "I" in it is the book's author. The last chapter argued that simple ballots are easier to count and to trust. This chapter asks what the people who want those ballots should do now.

I am not opening with the movement's worst night to depress you. I am opening with it because strategy is the discipline of taking the map as it is. Any reform book written after 2024 that doesn't reckon with that map is selling you something.

Two strategy questions get tangled together, so we will take this in four steps. First, briefly, whether voters can game score voting. Second, an argument inside my own camp. Third, how that camp should treat ranked-choice voting. And last, what the scoreboard says.

## The gaming question

Every conversation about voting methods eventually arrives at one question. "Won't people just max their favorite and zero everyone else?"

Some will. Here is the honest frame for thinking about it.

Allan Gibbard proved a theorem in 1973, and Mark Satterthwaite proved it in 1975. It establishes that no reasonable voting rule, with three or more candidates, is immune to strategic voting. Not plurality. Not ranked-choice voting. Not score voting. Nothing.

> No reasonable voting rule is immune to strategic voting.

So the question is never "can it be gamed?" The question is "what happens when it is?"

And this is where score voting's design earns its keep. Picture a voter who plays maximum hardball: top score for favorites, zero for everyone else. That voter has turned a score ballot into an approval ballot. The system's worst-case strategic behavior is its own simpler sibling, and that sibling is still a dramatic improvement on plurality.

> Score voting's worst case under strategy is approval voting.

Compare the failure modes elsewhere. Plurality under strategy collapses into lesser-evil voting and spoiler panics. And instant runoff, the count behind ranked-choice voting, is not the strategy-proof haven it is marketed as. In 2009, the mayoral race in Burlington, Vermont, eliminated the candidate who would have beaten either rival head to head. The city repealed the method the next year, 52 to 48.

Degrading into approval voting is the softest landing in the business. The math chapter quantifies all of this. For now, pocket the asymmetry.

## The family argument

Now the strategy question that actually keeps organizers up at night.

There is a camp that believes ballots should rate candidates, not rank them or pick one. Its name is the cardinal-voting camp, and it has its own internal fight. There are three positions. You should hear each at full strength.

The first is approval first. Its home is the Center for Election Science, founded in 2011. What it wants to protect is the ability to win. Its argument: win with the simplest possible reform. Approval voting needs no new equipment. It is the existing ballot with the "vote for one" limit deleted. And it survives the "too confusing" attack that helped kill the statewide measures of 2024, because there is nothing to explain.

For evidence, it points to the record. Approval voting is the only cardinal method ever used in American public elections. Fargo adopted it in 2018, with 63.5 percent. St. Louis adopted it in 2020, with 68 percent, and uses it still. And in December 2025, Utah's Forward Party filled a state senate vacancy by an approval vote, open to every registered voter in the district. That was the first state-level use of approval voting in American history.

> The approval-first case: win with the simplest possible reform.

[pause]

The second position is STAR first. Its home is the Equal Vote Coalition, Mark Frohnmayer's group. STAR voting was developed there, in Oregon, in 2014. What it wants to protect is the voter's honest expression.

Its argument starts with approval's weakness. Approval voting asks voters to draw one crude line: approve, or not. That is an expressive loss. It is also a strategic burden, because the decision to approve your second choice can hurt your favorite.

STAR stands for Score Then Automatic Runoff. You score everyone from 0 to 5. Then the two highest-scoring candidates go to an automatic runoff, decided by which of the two each ballot scored higher. To its advocates, STAR is the engineered endpoint: score voting with its main objection answered.

> The STAR-first case: your honest scores can't betray you in the runoff.

For evidence, it points to that design, and it makes an honest concession. No public jurisdiction has adopted STAR yet. In May 2024, Eugene, STAR's hometown, rejected it, with only about a third voting yes. STAR's advocates take that as a sequencing problem, not a verdict.

[pause]

The third position is score now, and it is this book's. What I want to protect is the full instrument: a scale from 0 to 10, which lets you say how much you support every candidate, not merely whether you would tolerate them.

My argument starts with what approval voting is. It is score voting on a two-point scale. So every approval adoption is an adoption of the cardinal principle, with the resolution turned down. Rate everyone. Sum the ratings. Highest total wins.

And for evidence, I point to the upgrade path. A city that has run approval voting has already accepted the logic, the equipment, and the habit. Widening the scale is an ordinance, not a revolution.

> The score-now case: approval is how score voting wins its first hundred cities.

I would rather have the principle in law at low resolution than the perfect scale in a white paper.

[pause]

You can hear my resolution inside that third position. This is a sequencing argument, not a schism. Approval, score, and STAR are one family. The family's scarce resources should flow to whichever member can actually win the jurisdiction in front of it. As of mid-2026, that has meant approval voting every time. [beat] It will not always.

Approval first. STAR first. Score now. Take the one you find least convincing, and make the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

Now a test of the record. A friend tells you that STAR voting has been adopted by several small cities, starting with Eugene in 2024. Is that right? And what is the cardinal family's record in public elections?

[long pause]

It is not right. Eugene rejected STAR in May 2024, so no public jurisdiction uses it. The family's entire record in public elections runs through approval voting, in two cities: Fargo and St. Louis. And that is exactly why the approval-first strategy has carried the day so far.

## Compete with ranked choice, or coexist?

That is the fight inside the family. Now the relationship that determines whether any of this works.

Ranked-choice voting is the incumbent reform. It is statewide in Maine and Alaska, and citywide in New York and dozens of other cities. An organization called FairVote has been behind it for three decades, and allied funders move real money.

And FairVote does not return the cardinal camp's ecumenical affection. Its standing critique is that approval voting flunks a test called later-no-harm.

> FairVote's critique: approving your second choice can defeat your first.

So voters learn to bullet-vote, FairVote argues, and the method slides back toward plurality. Take that argument seriously. It is the best one they have, and the math chapter answers it with data, not indignation.

Here is what happens when the two reforms fight. Seattle, November 2022. An approval-voting initiative qualified for the ballot. The city council countered with a ranked-choice alternative. And voters chose between them, head to head.

Ranked choice won the method question, 75.4 to 24.6. [beat] I won't spin that. It was a verdict on whose brand is stronger, on reform-friendly turf.

On ground where ranked choice is organized and legal, approval voting running against it is a donor-funded gift to the status quo.

[pause]

But study the map from 2024 again, because it assigns the lanes for us.

Where ranked-choice voting is alive, cardinal advocates should be allies, full stop. That means Maine, Alaska, D.C., the ranked-choice cities, and Michigan's pending statewide push in 2026. Every one of those fights is anything-but-plurality against the duopoly. And "anything is better than plurality" remains the movement's one non-negotiable slogan.

Now the nineteen ban states. Nearly all of the bans are drafted to outlaw ranking, as the legal chapter will show. Where ranking is outlawed, ranked-choice voting cannot run at all. Approval voting remains perfectly legal.

> Where a ban outlaws ranking, approval voting remains legal.

Seventeen of those nineteen states are an empty lane, and it is cardinal-shaped. Nobody has to lose a Seattle for the reform movement to contest them.

Let's put the lanes to work. You are organizing in a state whose statute bans methods in which "voters rank candidates in order of preference." A FairVote affiliate asks you to help repeal the ban. An organizer from the Center for Election Science asks you to launch an approval-voting campaign in your city. Which do you choose? Think it through.

[long pause]

Both. And notice that they don't conflict.

The approval campaign is the only reform that can win now. The ban doesn't touch it, because approval involves no ranking. Supporting repeal costs the approval campaign nothing, and it keeps the coalition whole.

The methods become rivals only when they are forced onto the same ballot against each other, as in Seattle. That is the one configuration the lanes doctrine exists to prevent.

## What the scoreboard says

The losses of 2024 and 2025 settle the tactical question this chapter opened with.

Statewide, bundled, big-money initiative campaigns for unfamiliar systems are not working. Colorado's was bankrolled by some of reform's wealthiest backers, and it lost by seven.

City-scale campaigns for simple reforms keep working. Fargo by 27 points. St. Louis by 36. D.C. by 46.

The strategy question answers itself, if you let the scoreboard talk.

> Go local, go simple, go where the law is open.

And treat every other anti-plurality reformer as a colleague who is wrong about something secondary.

One question before we gather up. Near the start, I described a voter playing maximum hardball on a score ballot. What does that ballot turn into?

[long pause]

An approval ballot. Score voting's worst case under strategy is approval voting.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. No reasonable voting rule is immune to strategic voting. The question is never "can it be gamed?" It is "what happens when it is?" And score voting's worst case under strategy is approval voting.

[beat]

Two. The family argument. The approval-first case: win with the simplest possible reform. The STAR-first case: your honest scores can't betray you in the runoff. The score-now case: approval is how score voting wins its first hundred cities.

[beat]

Three. FairVote's critique: approving your second choice can defeat your first. And Seattle's lesson: where ranked choice is organized and legal, running against it is a gift to the status quo.

[beat]

Four. The lanes. Where ranked choice is alive, be an ally. Where a ban outlaws ranking, approval voting remains legal.

[beat]

And five. The scoreboard. Go local, go simple, go where the law is open.

[pause]

Here is what this chapter has not settled. Is the thing we would be sequencing toward worth the climb? Are score voting's promised gains, in voter satisfaction and honest expression, real and measurable?

That is an empirical question. The next chapters take it up with simulations, election returns, and the strongest counter-evidence I can find.

[pause]

For now, go back to that night in November 2024, and the map it left behind.

Strategy tells you how to win.

[beat]

It is the evidence that has to tell you the win is worth having.
