## Start with a guess

Out of every 100 bills that members of Congress introduce, how many do you think become laws?

[pause]

In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced more than 16,000 bills. In those same two years, only 274 new laws were made.

- Bills introduced: more than 16,000
- New laws: 274

> Fewer than 2 of every 100 bills became laws.

## First stop: the committee

Congress has far too many bills for every member to study each one, so it splits up the work. A committee is a small group of members who study the bills on one subject, such as farming or schools.

- Committee: a small group, one subject

A committee can change the words of a bill and vote to send it on. It can also do nothing at all, and that is what happens to most bills. Nobody votes them down.

> Most bills just sit in a committee until time runs out.

[check q1]

## Second stop: the floor

A bill that gets out of committee goes to the floor. That is the name for the whole House or the whole Senate meeting together. To pass, a bill needs yes votes from more than half of the members who are voting. If everyone votes, that means 218 in the House and 51 in the Senate.

- House: 218 of 435
- Senate: 51 of 100

The Senate has one more hurdle. Senators are allowed to keep a debate going so that the vote never comes. Doing that is called a filibuster, and it takes 60 of the 100 senators to end one. So a bill with 55 senators on its side has more than half, and can still be stuck.

- End a filibuster: 60 of 100

[check q3]

## Third stop: the same words

A bill has to pass both the House and the Senate. Even that is not enough. The two houses have to pass the same bill, word for word. When their bills are a little different, members from both sides work out one final set of words. Then each house votes one more time.

- Both houses, the very same words

[check q4]

## Every stop, or only one

Some of these stops are written into the Constitution. Others are rules that Congress made for itself. Committees and the filibuster are that second kind, so Congress could change them if it wanted to.

- Some stops are Congress's own rules

> People for a bill must win every stop. People against it need one.

The lesson shows the numbers in a chart, and stops to ask what happens to a bill next. The last lesson takes a bill to the President's desk.
