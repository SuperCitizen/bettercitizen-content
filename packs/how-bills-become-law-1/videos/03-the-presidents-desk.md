## One pen in a big room

On June 17, 2021, a crowd gathered in a big room at the White House. Opal Lee was there. She had set out to walk to Washington for a Juneteenth holiday when she was 89. The President picked up a pen and signed the bill, and at that moment it became a law.

- June 17, 2021: the Juneteenth bill is signed

Signing is the ending most people picture. It is only one of four.

> Four things can happen at the President's desk.

## The clock and the veto

When Congress sends a bill to the President, a clock starts. The President has ten days to decide what to do, and Sundays do not count.

- 10 days, not counting Sundays

The President can sign the bill. Or the President can veto it, which means saying no to the bill and sending it back to Congress with the reasons written down. Congress can still win, but it takes two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate. That almost never happens. Presidents have vetoed bills 2,599 times since 1789, and Congress has overridden only 112 of those vetoes.

- Vetoes since 1789: 2,599
- Overridden by Congress: 112

## When the President does nothing

The last two endings look the same at first, because in both of them the President does nothing. What matters is whether Congress is still meeting. If it is, the bill becomes a law after ten days, with no signature.

- Congress still meeting: a law anyway

If Congress has gone home, the bill dies. That is called a pocket veto. Congress cannot override it, because the President never sent anything back for it to vote on.

- Congress gone home: a pocket veto

> Two of the four endings make the bill a law.

## Is it too hard to make a law?

Most bills fail, and people are still arguing about whether that is a problem.

Some people say the system is too slow. A problem can wait for years while a bill sits in a committee. To them, all those stops let a small group block what most of the country wants.

- One side: the stops block too much

Other people say the stops are doing their job. A law changes life for everyone, so they think it should have to win over many different people first. Alexander Hamilton made this point in 1788.

- Other side: the stops do their job

The lesson gives each side its reasons, with Hamilton's own words. It also lets you put every step in order, and ends with what you can do yourself.
