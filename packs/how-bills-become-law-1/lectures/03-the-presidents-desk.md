Picture a big room at the White House. It is full of people, and all of them are watching one desk.

The date is June 17, 2021. Opal Lee is in that room. We met her in the first lecture. When she was 89, she set out to walk to Washington, for a Juneteenth holiday.

[beat]

The bill has already passed the Senate and the House. So one step is left.

The President picks up a pen and signs the bill. And at that moment, it stops being a bill. [beat] It becomes a law.

[pause]

Signing is the ending most people picture. But it is only one of four things that can happen at the President's desk.

We will take this last lecture in three steps. First, those four endings. Second, the whole trip of a bill, from start to finish. And third, a question that people still argue about. Most bills fail. Is that a problem?

## Four things that can happen

When Congress sends a bill to the President, a clock starts. The President has ten days to decide what to do. And Sundays do not count.

Four things can happen. Count them with me.

One. The President signs the bill. The bill becomes a law.

[beat]

Two. The President vetoes the bill. To veto a bill means to say no to it. The President sends the bill back to Congress, with the reasons written down. And the bill is not a law.

[beat]

Three. The President waits, while Congress is still meeting. After ten days, the bill becomes a law, with no signature at all.

[beat]

Four. The President waits, but Congress has gone home. This time, the bill dies. That ending is called a pocket veto.

[pause]

> At the President's desk, a bill can end in four ways.

Sign. Veto. Wait while Congress is meeting. Wait after Congress has gone home. [beat] Two of those four make the bill a law.

Now look closer at the second ending, the veto. A veto does not have to be the end, because Congress can still win. That is called overriding the veto. But it takes two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate.

Since 1789, Presidents have vetoed bills 2,599 times. Congress has overridden only 112 of those vetoes.

> Congress can override a veto, but it almost never does.

Now the third and fourth endings. They look the same at first, because in both of them the President does nothing.

> When the President does nothing, what matters is whether Congress is still meeting.

If Congress is still meeting, the bill becomes a law after ten days. If Congress has finished its work and gone home, the bill dies.

And Congress cannot override that kind of ending. Why not? Because the President never sent anything back for it to vote on.

Let's test that. Congress sends a bill to the President, and keeps meeting. Twelve days go by, not counting Sundays. The President has not signed the bill, and has not vetoed it. [beat] What happened to the bill?

[long pause]

It became a law. The President had ten days to act, and did not. And Congress was still meeting. In that case, the Constitution says the bill becomes a law, just as if the President had signed it.

So a bill can become a law with no signature on it.

## The whole trip, start to finish

Over three lectures, we have now seen every stop a bill has to pass. So try putting them in order. Begin with a member of Congress, and end at this desk. Take a moment.

[pause 5s]

First, a member of Congress introduces the bill.

Second, a committee studies the bill and sends it on.

Third, the House and the Senate each vote yes.

Fourth, both houses agree on the very same words.

And fifth, the President signs the bill.

[pause]

A bill has to clear every one of those steps, in that order. And most bills stop at the second step. They never leave their committee.

## Most bills fail. Is that a problem?

So most bills fail. Is that a problem?

People have argued about that question for as long as there has been a Congress, and they are still arguing. Let's hear both sides.

Some people say the system is too slow. A problem can wait for years while a bill sits in a committee, or runs into a filibuster, the Senate hurdle we met last time. All those stops, these people say, do more than slow things down.

> One side says a small group can block what most of the country wants.

Other people say the stops are doing their job. A law changes life for everyone in the country, these people say, so it should not come easily.

> The other side says a law should have to win over many different people.

Alexander Hamilton helped write the Constitution, and he made this point in 1788. Stopping "a few good laws," he wrote, is a fair price for "preventing a number of bad ones."

[pause]

A few good laws, stopped. A number of bad ones, prevented.

Let's put his idea to work. A good bill fails in the Senate. What would Hamilton most likely say about it?

[long pause]

He would say it is a price worth paying. Hamilton admitted that hard rules would sometimes stop good laws. He thought that was worth it, because the same rules would stop bad laws too.

And the people who disagree with him? They say the price has become too high.

[pause]

Both sides have a point. You can decide which side you agree with more.

## Your part

One last thing, and it is about you.

Maybe you cannot vote yet. You are still not left out. Your representative and your two senators work for everyone who lives where you live. [beat] That includes you.

You can write to them, or call their offices, about a bill you care about. The House has a web page where you type in your ZIP code to find out who your representative is. When you write, say who you are and where you live. And name the bill, if you know its number.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. At the President's desk, a bill can end in four ways. Can you still name them? [long pause] Sign. Veto. Wait while Congress is meeting, and the bill becomes a law. Wait after Congress has gone home, and the bill dies.

[beat]

Two. A veto means saying no to a bill. Congress can override a veto, with two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate. It almost never does.

[beat]

Three. The whole trip has five steps, in order. A member introduces the bill. A committee sends it on. Both houses vote yes. Both houses agree on the same words. And the President signs.

[beat]

Four. People disagree about whether it should be this hard. One side says a small group can block what most of the country wants. The other side says a law should have to win over many different people.

[pause]

That argument is not settled, and it is yours to think about now.

So go back, one last time, to that room at the White House. Opal Lee was not a member of Congress. [beat] And her idea still became a law.
