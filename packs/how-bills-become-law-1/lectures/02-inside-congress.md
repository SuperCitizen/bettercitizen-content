Picture a tall pile of paper on a desk. It holds 100 bills. Each one is a plan for a new law, and a member of Congress has introduced every one of them.

Now take a guess. Out of those 100 bills, how many will become laws?

[long pause]

You might say half of them. Or maybe a quarter. The real number is much smaller.

[beat]

Fewer than two.

In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced more than 16,000 bills. In those same two years, only 274 new laws were made. That is fewer than two for every 100 bills.

> In 2023 and 2024, fewer than two of every 100 bills became laws.

So where do all the other bills go?

Last time, we watched an idea become a bill. This time, we follow that bill inside Congress, and it has three stops to get through. First, the committee. Second, the floor. And third, the same words in both houses. Then we will ask why the trip is so hard.

## First stop: the committee

Congress has far too many bills for every member to study each one. So it splits up the work.

It does that with committees. A committee is a small group of members who study the bills on one subject. The subject might be farming, or schools, or the military. The House has 20 main committees, and the Senate has its own set.

A committee can do a lot with a bill. It can hold a hearing. A hearing is a public meeting, where people who know the subject come to answer questions. Some of them support the bill, and some are against it. So the members get to hear more than one side.

After that, the committee can change the words of the bill. And it can vote on whether to send the bill on, to the whole House or the whole Senate.

[pause]

That is what a committee can do. But a committee can also do something else. [beat] Nothing at all.

And that is what happens to most bills. Nobody votes them down.

> Most bills just sit in a committee until time runs out.

Think about what that means. A bill does not have to lose a vote to fail. It only has to be left alone.

So the committee is the first stop. For most bills, it is also the last. They never get past it.

## Second stop: the floor

Some bills do get out of committee. Where do they go next? They go to the floor.

The floor is the name for the whole House, or the whole Senate, meeting together. On the floor, members give speeches for the bill and against it. Then they vote.

To pass, a bill needs yes votes from more than half of the members who are voting. If everyone votes, that means 218 in the House. [beat] In the Senate, it means 51.

[pause]

But the Senate has one more hurdle. Senators are allowed to keep a debate going, so that the vote never comes. Doing that has a name. It is called a filibuster.

And a filibuster is hard to end. It takes 60 of the 100 senators to end one.

So keep two numbers for the Senate. Passing a bill takes 51. [beat] Ending a filibuster takes 60.

> A bill can have more than half of the Senate and still be stuck.

In real life, that means most big bills need 60 senators on their side. And 51 is not enough.

Let's test that. A bill has 55 senators on its side. All 100 senators are there, and the other 45 use a filibuster. [beat] What happens to the bill?

[long pause]

The bill is stuck. Passing it would take only 51 votes, and it has more than that. But the Senate cannot get to that vote until the filibuster ends. And ending it takes 60. So the bill has more than half, and it is still stuck.

## Third stop: the same words in both houses

Now the third stop. Congress has two parts, the House and the Senate, and a bill has to pass both of them.

Even that is not enough, though.

> The House and the Senate have to pass the same bill, word for word.

Often, the two houses pass bills that are a little different. When that happens, members from both sides meet to work out one final set of words. Then each house votes one more time.

Here is another scene to try. A bill passes the House by 300 to 135. The Senate changes one sentence, and then passes the bill by 70 to 30. [beat] Can the bill go to the President now?

[long pause]

Not yet. Those are big wins, but the two houses have passed different words. One of two things has to happen. Either the House agrees to the Senate's change, or the two sides work out one set of words that both will pass.

Big vote counts do not matter until the words match.

## Why is it so hard?

We have now counted three stops inside Congress. Can you still name them, in order? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The committee. [beat] The floor. [beat] And the same words in both houses.

So where did these stops come from? Some of them are written into the Constitution. The Constitution says that a bill must pass both the House and the Senate.

Other stops are rules that Congress made for itself. It can do that, because the Constitution lets each house set its own rules. Committees are a rule of that second kind. So is the filibuster. And that means Congress could change them, if it wanted to.

[pause]

Now put the stops together, and you can see why so few bills make it.

> People for a bill must win at every stop. People against it, only once.

Say it one more way. To become a law, a bill has to get through every stop. To fail, it only has to stop once.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Very few bills become laws. In 2023 and 2024, it was fewer than two of every 100.

[beat]

Two. The first stop is the committee, a small group of members who study the bills on one subject. Most bills end there. Nobody votes them down. They just sit until time runs out.

[beat]

Three. On the floor, a bill needs more than half of the votes. That means 218 in the House and 51 in the Senate. But ending a filibuster in the Senate takes 60.

[beat]

Four. The House and the Senate have to pass the same bill, word for word. And the people for a bill must win at every one of these stops.

[pause]

Here is what we have not asked yet. Are all these stops a good thing? Or do they block too much? People disagree, and we will hear both sides next time.

And the trip is not over. A bill that gets through every stop still has one more place to go. The next lecture, our last, follows it to the President's desk.

For now, go back to that pile of 100 bills. Fewer than two of them will become laws. [beat] And now you know where the others stopped.
