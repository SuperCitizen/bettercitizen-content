Think of the longest walk you have ever taken. Maybe it was a mile. Maybe it was two. By the end, your legs were tired.

[beat]

Now picture setting out for a place about 1,400 miles away.

In 2016, a retired teacher named Opal Lee did that. She laced up her shoes in Fort Worth, Texas, and set out to walk toward Washington, D.C. [beat] She was 89 years old.

[pause]

She walked two and a half miles at a time. And she picked that number on purpose.

Here is what it stood for. President Lincoln gave an order that freed enslaved people in the South. But an order has to be carried out. Union soldiers did not reach Texas to carry it out until June 19, 1865. [beat] Between the order and that day, two and a half years went by.

That day is now called Juneteenth. And Ms. Lee wanted Juneteenth to be a holiday for the whole country.

[pause]

But a walk cannot make a holiday, no matter how long it is. Neither can a petition. A petition is a list of the names of people who agree with an idea. Even a petition with more than a million names on it cannot make a holiday.

Why not? Because a national holiday has to be written into a law. And laws are made by a group called Congress.

> Only Congress can make a law.

So how does an idea like hers get that far? That is our question, and we will take it in three steps. First, who can have an idea for a law. Second, who can turn that idea into something called a bill. And third, what happened to Ms. Lee's idea.

## Anyone can have the idea

Start with the idea itself. Ideas for laws come from everywhere. They come from voters and teachers. They come from doctors and farmers. They come from the President. Sometimes they come from a class of students.

> Anyone can have an idea for a law.

And anyone at all can write that idea down and send it to Congress.

Who in Congress? Everyone who lives in a state has three people there. One is a member of the House of Representatives. The other two are senators.

Their job is to speak for the people back home. So a letter from someone they represent is a good place for an idea to start.

[pause]

That is the easy part. An idea on paper is still only an idea.

## Only a member of Congress can introduce it

Here is the catch.

> Only a member of Congress can turn an idea into a bill.

That word, bill, is new. So let's slow down.

> A bill is a written plan for a new law.

A bill says exactly what the new law would do.

Two more words go with it. The member who brings a bill to Congress is called its sponsor. And bringing it is called introducing the bill.

Now put those words together. A sponsor introduces a bill. In plainer words, a member of Congress brings in a written plan for a new law.

Nobody else can do that part. The President can ask for a law, but the President cannot introduce a bill. A petition can show that many people agree, but it cannot introduce a bill either. Only a member of the House or the Senate can.

Let's test that with a scene. Your class has a great idea for a law about school lunches. You put every word of it on paper, carefully. [beat] Is it a bill yet?

[long pause]

Not yet. It becomes a bill only when a member of the House or the Senate introduces it. So your next step would be to find a sponsor. You could ask your own representative. Or you could ask one of your state's two senators.

And how does a member introduce a bill? In the House of Representatives, it is done with a box. The box is attached to the tall desk at the front of the room, and it is called the hopper. A member introduces a bill by dropping it into the hopper.

Once the bill is in the box, it gets a number. A bill that starts in the House gets two letters in front of its number, H and R. A bill that starts in the Senate gets the letter S.

So here is the whole path, in order. [beat] First, someone has an idea. Next, a member of Congress agrees to sponsor it. Then the idea is written down as a bill. Then the sponsor introduces the bill. And last, the bill gets its number.

## Three days in June

So what happened to Ms. Lee's idea?

It got its number in 2021. That year, a senator introduced a bill called the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act. The bill started in the Senate, so its number began with the letter S. It was S 475.

It was a short bill, too. Some bills run for hundreds of pages, because they change many laws at once. The Juneteenth bill fit on a single page. All it had to do was add one line to the country's list of holidays.

Then things moved fast.

On June 15, the Senate passed the bill. [beat] The next day, the House passed it too, by a vote of 415 to 14. [beat] On June 17, the President signed it. And Juneteenth became a national holiday.

Opal Lee was at the White House to watch. By then, she was 94 years old.

[pause]

Do not be fooled by those three days. Five years had gone by since her walk began. And most bills never get a week like that one.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Anyone can have an idea for a law. And everyone who lives in a state has three people in Congress to send it to: one representative and two senators.

[beat]

Two. Only a member of Congress can turn an idea into a bill. A bill is a written plan for a new law. The member who introduces it is called its sponsor.

[beat]

Three. In the House, a member introduces a bill by dropping it into a box. Can you still name that box? [long pause] It is the hopper. After that, the bill gets its number, with the letters H and R for a House bill, and the letter S for a Senate bill.

[beat]

Four. The Juneteenth bill passed the Senate, passed the House and was signed in three days. But the walk behind it had begun five years before.

[pause]

Here is what we have not seen yet.

> Getting a number is only the start.

A bill still has to get through Congress, and that is where most bills stop for good. Why do they stop there? The next lecture follows a bill inside to find out.

For now, go back to Fort Worth, and to a pair of shoes being laced. Opal Lee had the idea. A senator made it a bill. [beat] It took both of them.
