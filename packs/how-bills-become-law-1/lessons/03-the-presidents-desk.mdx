# How does a bill finally become a law?

On June 17, 2021, a crowd gathered in a big room at the White House. Opal Lee was there. She was the woman from the first lesson, who had set out to walk to Washington for a Juneteenth holiday when she was 89.

The bill had already passed the Senate and the House, so one step was left. The President picked up a pen and signed the bill, and at that moment it stopped being a bill and became a law.

Signing is the ending most people picture, but it is only one of four things that can happen at the President's desk.

## Four things that can happen

When Congress sends a bill to the President, a clock starts. The President has ten days to decide what to do, and Sundays do not count.

<Panel title="2. Veto it" tone="accent">

</Panel>
<Panel title="3. Wait, while Congress is meeting" tone="blue">

After ten days, the bill becomes a law with no signature.

</Panel>
<Panel title="4. Wait, after Congress has gone home" tone="gold">

The bill dies. This is called a pocket veto.

</Panel>

</Figure>

The second ending needs one more word. [[term:veto]] means saying no to a bill, and a President who vetoes a bill has to send it back to Congress with the reasons written down. Congress can still win, but it takes two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate, and that almost never happens. Presidents have vetoed bills 2,599 times since 1789, and Congress has overridden only 112 of those vetoes.

The third and fourth endings look the same at first, because in both of them the President does nothing. What matters is whether Congress is still meeting. If it is, the bill becomes a law after ten days. If Congress has finished its work and gone home, the bill dies. Congress cannot override that kind of ending, because the President never sent anything back for it to vote on.

<Checkpoint question="Congress sends a bill to the President and keeps meeting. Twelve days go by, not counting Sundays, and the President has not signed it or vetoed it. What happened to the bill?">

It became a law. The President had ten days to act and did not, and Congress was still meeting. In that case the Constitution says the bill becomes a law just as if the President had signed it.

</Checkpoint>

## Most bills fail. Is that a problem?

People have argued about that question for as long as there has been a Congress, and they are still arguing.

Some people say the system is too slow. A problem can wait for years while a bill sits in a committee or runs into a filibuster. To them, all those stops let a small group block what most of the country wants.

Other people say the stops are doing their job. A law changes life for everyone in the country, so they think it should have to win over many different people first. Alexander Hamilton, who helped write the Constitution, made this point in 1788. Stopping "a few good laws," he wrote, is a fair price for "preventing a number of bad ones."

Both sides have a point, and you can decide which one you agree with more.

## Your part

You cannot vote yet, but you are not left out. Your representative and your two senators work for everyone who lives where you live, and that includes you.

You can write to them or call their offices about a bill you care about. The House has a web page where you type in your ZIP code to find out who your representative is. When you write, say who you are and where you live, and name the bill if you know its number.

Opal Lee was not a member of Congress either, and her idea still became a law.
