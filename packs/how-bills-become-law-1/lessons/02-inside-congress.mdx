# What happens to a bill in Congress?

Start with a guess. Out of every 100 bills that members of Congress introduce, how many do you think become laws?

You might say half of them, or maybe a quarter. The real number is much smaller. In 2023 and 2024, members of Congress introduced more than 16,000 bills. In those same two years, only 274 new laws were made, which is fewer than 2 for every 100 bills.

So where do all the other bills go? Most of them never get past the first stop.

## First stop: the committee

Congress has far too many bills for every member to study each one, so it splits up the work. A *committee* is a small group of members who study the bills on one subject, such as farming, schools or the military. The House has 20 main committees, and the Senate has its own set.

A committee can do a lot with a bill. It can hold a *hearing*, which is a public meeting where people who know the subject come to answer questions. Some of them support the bill and some are against it, so the members get to hear more than one side. After that, the committee can change the words of the bill and vote on whether to send it on to the whole House or Senate.

A committee can also do nothing at all, and that is what happens to most bills. Nobody votes them down. They just sit in the committee until time runs out.

## Second stop: the floor

A bill that gets out of committee goes to *the floor*, which is the name for the whole House or the whole Senate meeting together. On the floor, members give speeches for and against the bill, and then they vote.

To pass, a bill needs yes votes from more than half of the members who are voting. If everyone votes, that means 218 in the House and 51 in the Senate.

The Senate has one more hurdle. Senators are allowed to keep a debate going so that the vote never comes. [[term:filibuster]] is the name for doing that. It takes 60 of the 100 senators to end one, so in real life most big bills need 60 senators on their side, and 51 is not enough.

<Figure caption="A bill can have more than half of the Senate and still be stuck, because ending a filibuster takes 60." source="U.S. House of Representatives, The Legislative Process; U.S. Senate, About Filibusters and Cloture">

| What it takes | Votes |
| --- | --- |
| Pass the House | 218 of 435 |
| Pass the Senate | 51 of 100 |
| End a filibuster | 60 of 100 |

</Figure>

## Third stop: the same words in both houses

Congress has two parts, and a bill has to pass both of them. Even that is not enough, though, because the House and the Senate have to pass the *same* bill, word for word.

Often the two houses pass bills that are a little different. When that happens, members from both sides meet to work out one final set of words, and then each house votes one more time.

<Checkpoint question="A bill passes the House by 300 to 135. The Senate changes one sentence and passes it by 70 to 30. Can the bill go to the President now?">

Not yet. The two houses have passed different words. Either the House has to agree to the Senate's change, or the two sides have to work out one set of words that both will pass. Big vote counts do not matter until the words match.

</Checkpoint>

## Why is it so hard?

Some of these stops are written into the Constitution, which says that a bill must pass both the House and the Senate. Other stops are rules that Congress made for itself, because the Constitution lets each house set its own rules. Committees and the filibuster are both rules of that second kind, which means Congress could change them if it wanted to.

Put the stops together and you can see why so few bills make it. The people who want a bill have to win at every stop, while the people who are against it only have to win at one.

A bill that does get through all of them still has one more place to go. The last lesson follows it to the President's desk.
