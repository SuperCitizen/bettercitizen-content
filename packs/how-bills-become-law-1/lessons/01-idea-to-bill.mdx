# How does an idea become a bill?

In 2016, a retired teacher named Opal Lee laced up her shoes in Fort Worth, Texas. She was 89 years old, and she had set out to walk toward Washington, D.C., which is about 1,400 miles away.

She walked two and a half miles at a time, and she picked that number on purpose. It stood for the two and a half years between President Lincoln's order freeing enslaved people in the South and June 19, 1865. That was the day Union soldiers reached Texas to carry the order out, and it is now called Juneteenth. Ms. Lee wanted Juneteenth to be a holiday for the whole country.

A walk cannot make a holiday, though, no matter how long it is. Neither can a petition, even one with more than a million names on it. A national holiday has to be written into a law, and only Congress can make a law. So how does an idea like hers get that far?

## Anyone can have the idea

Ideas for laws come from everywhere. They come from voters and teachers, from doctors and farmers, from the President, and sometimes from a class of students. Anyone at all can write an idea down and send it to Congress.

Everyone who lives in a state has three people in Congress to send it to: one member of the House of Representatives and two senators. Their job is to speak for the people back home, so a letter from someone they represent is a good place for an idea to start.

That is the easy part, though. An idea on paper is still only an idea.

## Only a member of Congress can introduce it

Here is the catch: only a member of Congress can turn an idea into a *bill*. A bill is a written plan for a new law. The member who brings it to Congress is called its *sponsor*, and bringing it is called *introducing* the bill.

In the House of Representatives, a member introduces a bill by dropping it into a box called the hopper, which is attached to the tall desk at the front of the room. Once the bill is in the box, it gets a number. Bills that start in the House begin with the letters "H.R.," and bills that start in the Senate begin with "S."

A bill can be long or short. Some bills run for hundreds of pages, because they change many laws at once. The Juneteenth bill fit on a single page, because all it had to do was add one line to the country's list of holidays.

<Checkpoint question="Your class has a great idea for a law about school lunches, and you write it down carefully. Is it a bill yet?">

Not yet. It becomes a bill only when a member of the House or the Senate introduces it. Your next step would be to ask your own representative, or one of your state's two senators, to be its sponsor.

</Checkpoint>

## Three days in June

Ms. Lee's idea got its number in 2021, when a senator introduced the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act. It was called S. 475.

Then things moved fast. On June 15, the Senate passed the bill. The next day the House passed it too, by a vote of 415 to 14. On June 17, the President signed it, and Juneteenth became a national holiday. Opal Lee was at the White House to watch, and by then she was 94 years old.

Do not be fooled by those three days. Five years had gone by since her walk began, and most bills never get a week like that one.
