# How power is divided in the federal government

Open a copy of the Constitution and look at its proportions before you read a single clause. Article I, which creates Congress, takes up roughly half the original document. Article II, the presidency, runs about a quarter. Article III, the entire federal judiciary, is a few short paragraphs — the Framers did not even settle how many judges there would be. The layout is an argument. The men who drafted it in the summer of 1787 had just fought a war against a king, and most of them feared legislatures more than they admitted and executives more than anything else. So they did something genuinely unusual for the time: they split the government's power into three pieces, gave each piece to a different institution, and wrote the institutions in descending order of how much they expected each to matter. This lesson is about that split — what each branch is, what it does, and why the division exists at all.

## Three vesting clauses, three jobs

Each of the first three articles opens with a *vesting clause* — a sentence that hands a kind of power to an institution:

- **Article I — the legislative branch.** *"All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States."* Congress — the House of Representatives and the Senate — writes federal law, levies taxes, and decides how every public dollar is spent.
- **Article II — the executive branch.** *"The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America."* The President carries out the laws Congress passes, commands the armed forces, conducts foreign relations, and appoints the officers of government — many of them only with Senate approval.
- **Article III — the judicial branch.** *"The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish."* Federal courts resolve disputes about what the law means and whether government action conforms to the Constitution.

Read the first two clauses again, side by side, because a one-word difference has fueled two centuries of argument. Congress gets the legislative powers *"herein granted"* — the ones listed, mostly in Article I §8: taxing, borrowing, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, declaring war, raising armies, and making all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out the rest. The President gets *"the executive Power,"* full stop, with no equivalent limiting phrase. Whether that unqualified wording grants a reservoir of authority beyond the specific powers Article II goes on to list is one of the longest-running fights in constitutional law, and it sits underneath most modern disputes about presidential power.

The figure's fine print matters as much as its headlines. The Constitution never says "435 representatives" — Congress capped the House by statute in 1929. It never says "nine justices" — the Court's size moved from six at the Founding to as many as ten during the Civil War before Congress settled on nine in 1869. Senators were chosen by state legislatures until the 17th Amendment (1913) switched to direct popular election. And federal judges serve for life — the Constitution says "during good Behaviour" — with salaries that cannot be cut, precisely so that the people who judge the government do not depend on the goodwill of the people they judge.

<Checkpoint question="Your representative announces: 'I'll cut off every dollar for this military operation.' Your neighbor scoffs: 'Only the President controls the military.' Who has the Constitution on their side?">
  Both hold real cards, which is the point. Article II makes the President commander in chief — operational command belongs to the executive. But Article I gives Congress the power to declare war, to raise and support armies, and to control all spending; no money leaves the Treasury without an appropriation. The representative can't direct troops, and the President can't fund them. The Constitution forces the two branches into each other's business on purpose.
</Checkpoint>

## Why divide power at all?

Madison's answer in [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 47 remains the canonical statement: *"The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective, may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."* Notice the precision. Tyranny, on this definition, is not bad policy or even bad rulers — it is a *structural condition*, the fusion of the power to make rules with the power to enforce them and the power to judge violations of them. A perfectly benevolent government with all three powers in one set of hands would still be, by Madison's lights, a tyranny waiting for its first bad day.

The idea wasn't original to him, and he said so. "The oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject," Madison wrote, "is the celebrated Montesquieu" — the French philosopher whose *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) argued from the English constitution that liberty survives only where the three powers are lodged separately. The American Framers turned Montesquieu's observation into hard architecture: three [[term:separation-of-powers]] compartments, each with its own selection method, term length, and constituency, so that no electoral wave, no faction, no ambitious individual could capture the whole machine in one stroke.

But Madison immediately added a correction that students miss and pundits mangle. Montesquieu, he insisted, "did not mean that these departments ought to have no *partial agency* in, or no *control* over, the acts of each other." Separation does not mean isolation. A President vetoing a bill is participating in legislation; a Senate confirming a judge is participating in staffing the judiciary — and both are features of the design, not breaches of it. What the doctrine forbids is the *whole* of one power falling into the hands that hold another. Keep that distinction; the entire next lesson lives inside it.

<Checkpoint question="A friend argues: 'The President's veto violates separation of powers — vetoing is a legislative act, and the President isn't the legislature.' How would Madison answer?">
  With Federalist 47's own caveat: separation of powers forbids the *accumulation of all* of one power in the same hands, not "partial agency" of one branch in another's function. The veto gives the President a share of the legislative process precisely so the executive can defend itself against legislative encroachment. The blending is the mechanism that *preserves* the separation.
</Checkpoint>

## The scholar's lens: separated institutions sharing powers

In *Presidential Power* (1960), the political scientist Richard Neustadt compressed the previous two paragraphs into one of the discipline's most quoted lines: "The Constitutional Convention of 1787 is supposed to have created a government of 'separated powers.' It did nothing of the sort. Rather, it created a government of separated institutions *sharing* powers." Look at any major federal function and you find every branch's fingerprints. A statute is drafted in Congress, shaped by the threat of a presidential veto, signed (or not) by the President, implemented by executive agencies, and construed by courts. A war is declared and funded by Congress, commanded by the President, and litigated — detention, surveillance, war powers — before judges. Even the judiciary's own composition is a joint product: the President nominates, the Senate confirms, and Congress decides how many judgeships exist at all.

Neustadt's reframing matters because it predicts how the system actually feels from inside: since no branch can finish anything alone, governing runs on bargaining, and a President's real power — Neustadt's famous claim — is less command than persuasion. Hold that thought when you reach the next lesson's inventory of checks.

## The branch the Founders feared — and the one that grew

If the branches were meant to be balanced, they were not expected to be equal. "In republican government," Madison wrote in Federalist No. 51, "the legislative authority necessarily predominates" — and in No. 48 he warned that the legislature was "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex." Congress sits closest to the people and holds the purse; that is why the Framers made it a [[term:bicameral]] legislature — two chambers with different terms, sizes, and constituencies, each positioned to slow the other. The presidency, by contrast, was thin on paper — and Article III was thinnest of all, a court with no army, no budget, and not even a fixed membership.

Two centuries inverted the expectation. The story of modern American government is substantially the story of executive growth: a standing military establishment, a foreign-policy apparatus, and — above all — the [[term:administrative-state]], hundreds of agencies exercising power delegated by Congress to write binding rules, enforce them, and adjudicate disputes under them. Critics have long noted that an agency combining those three functions looks, in miniature, like the very accumulation Federalist 47 warned about; defenders answer that agencies act only on authority Congress chose to delegate and under judicial supervision. The boundary keeps moving: in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024), the Supreme Court overruled the forty-year-old *Chevron* doctrine, which had required judges to defer to an agency's reasonable reading of an ambiguous statute. After *Loper Bright*, resolving statutory ambiguity is once again the courts' job — a significant transfer of interpretive power from the executive branch back to the judiciary, and a live illustration that the division of power among the branches is still being renegotiated in the 2020s.

> **Common misconception:** "Three branches" does not mean three equal workloads or three equal headcounts. Congress is 535 elected members; the executive branch employs millions of civilian and military personnel; the entire Article III judiciary is fewer than a thousand life-tenured judges. The branches are coordinate in *authority* — each supreme in its own function — not in size, speed, or visibility.

<Checkpoint question="An agency both writes safety regulations and fines companies for violating them. Is this the 'tyranny' of Federalist 47 — the accumulation of lawmaking and enforcement in the same hands?">
  It is the strongest modern echo of Madison's worry, which is why the administrative state is a perennial constitutional battleground. The standard defense: the agency's rulemaking power is *delegated* by a statute Congress can amend or repeal, its budget depends on Congress, its leadership on presidential appointment, and its decisions face judicial review — so all three constitutional branches retain handles on it. After *Loper Bright* (2024), courts no longer defer to the agency's own reading of ambiguous statutes, tightening one of those handles. Whether the handles are tight enough is a genuinely contested question, not a settled one.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson does NOT settle

This lesson covers the architecture: who holds which power and why the powers were separated. It deliberately does not settle the harder question — what happens when a branch reaches past its boundary, and what the other two can do about it. Madison himself warned in Federalist 48 that mere "parchment barriers" would never hold "against the encroaching spirit of power"; lines on paper need enforcement machinery. That machinery — vetoes and overrides, appropriations and [[term:impeachment]], appointments and [[term:judicial-review]] — is the subject of the next lesson, *Checks and balances*, where the steel mills of 1952, a one-house veto written into nearly 200 statutes, and a President's attempt to cancel spending line by line all meet the same constitutional test.
