# Checks and balances — how each branch can constrain the others

Separation of powers — the idea covered in the previous lesson — is only half
the design. The other half is *checks and balances*: each branch can stop or
limit the others, so no one institution becomes unconstrained.

## What each branch can do to the others

### Congress can limit the President
- **Refuse to fund** executive priorities (the appropriations power).
- **Override a veto** with two-thirds majorities in both chambers
  ([[term:veto-power]]).
- **Withhold consent** on appointments and treaties (advice-and-consent).
- **Impeach and remove** the President for "Treason, Bribery, or other high
  Crimes and Misdemeanors."

### Congress can limit the courts
- Create or eliminate inferior federal courts.
- Set the size of the Supreme Court.
- Restrict which cases the Supreme Court can hear (with limits).
- Impeach federal judges.

### The President can limit Congress
- **Veto** legislation, returning it with objections (Art. I, §7).
- Decline to enforce — though the constitutional limits on this are
  contested.

### The President can limit the courts
- Nominate every Article III judge.
- Issue pardons that overturn federal criminal convictions.

### The courts can limit Congress and the President
- **Judicial review.** Federal courts can hold a statute or executive action
  unconstitutional and refuse to enforce it. The power was not granted in so
  many words by Article III; the Supreme Court asserted it in
  *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): *"It is emphatically the province and duty of
  the judicial department to say what the law is."*

## A working example: how a bill becomes law

1. A member of the House or Senate introduces a bill.
2. Both chambers must pass identical text.
3. The bill goes to the President, who can:
   - Sign it (it becomes law).
   - Veto it (it returns to Congress with objections).
   - Take no action for ten days while Congress is in session (it becomes law
     without a signature) — or, if Congress has adjourned, the bill dies
     ("pocket veto").
4. If vetoed, both chambers can override with two-thirds majorities.
5. Once signed, the law can still be challenged in court and held
   unconstitutional under judicial review.

## Why "ambition must counteract ambition"

Madison's argument in [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 51 is psychological as much
as structural: *"If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If
angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on
government would be necessary."* The expectation is not that branches will
behave well from civic virtue alone; the expectation is that each branch will
jealously guard its own powers, and that this rivalry will keep any one branch
from accumulating more than its share.

## Where modern controversies live

Most modern disputes about presidential power, congressional oversight, and
the courts are *checks-and-balances disputes* in disguise: who has the last
word on a particular question? Future lessons cover specific cases —
executive orders, the War Powers Resolution, congressional subpoenas, and the
filibuster — but the framework is always the same: a branch is acting at the
edge of its authority, and the others have tools to push back.
