# Checks and balances — how each branch can constrain the others

On April 8, 1952, with a steelworkers' strike hours away and the Korean War consuming artillery shells faster than factories could forge them, President Harry Truman ordered his Commerce Secretary, Charles Sawyer, to seize the nation's steel mills and run them for the government. No statute authorized it; Truman claimed the emergency itself, plus his powers as commander in chief, was authority enough. Eight weeks later the Supreme Court told the President of the United States — 6 to 3, in *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* — to give the mills back. And here is the remarkable part, the part this lesson is really about: he did. The mills were returned, the strike ran fifty-three days, and the republic absorbed the blow. The previous lesson described how federal power is partitioned. This one is about the enforcement machinery — the tools each branch holds to stop the others, and the cases where the Supreme Court has had to referee.

## The theory: means and motive

Madison's argument in [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 51 is psychological as much as structural. Paper boundaries don't enforce themselves, so "the great security against a gradual concentration of the several powers in the same department consists in giving to those who administer each department the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others." Then the famous compression: *"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place."* And the still more famous justification: *"If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."*

The design assumption, in other words, is not civic virtue. It is jealousy. Each branch is armed against the others and expected to *want* to use its arms, so that encroachment meets resistance automatically, the way pressure meets counter-pressure in an arch. ## Congress's arsenal: money, confirmation, removal — and the override

Congress's deepest check is the one that never makes headlines: **the purse**. Article I §9 commands that "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law." Every soldier's salary, every agency's lease, every presidential priority runs through an [[term:appropriations]] act, which is why funding fights are separation-of-powers fights in disguise.

Next, personnel. The Senate's **[[term:advice-and-consent]]** power reaches cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and every Article III judge; treaties need two-thirds of senators present. And at the extreme sits **[[term:impeachment]]**: the House impeaches by majority; the Senate convicts and removes by two-thirds. The historical record shows how extreme a remedy it is. The House has impeached three presidents — Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1998), and Donald Trump (2019 and 2021, the only president impeached twice) — and the Senate convicted none of them; Johnson survived by a single vote, 35–19. Every official the Senate has *ever* removed — eight in total, across more than two centuries — has been a federal judge. Only two cabinet officers have ever been impeached: War Secretary William Belknap in 1876 and Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024, whose articles the Senate dismissed without trial.

Finally, the check this lesson's working example turns on: when the President vetoes a bill, Congress can **override** with two-thirds of each chamber — and the Supreme Court held in *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Kansas* (1919) that this means two-thirds of members *present and voting* (a quorum being present), not two-thirds of the full membership. How often does Congress actually win that fight? The official Senate tally tells the story.

| The veto in practice, 1789 – mid-2026 | Count |
| --- | --- |
| Regular (return) vetoes | 1,533 |
| Pocket vetoes (cannot be overridden) | 1,066 |
| Total vetoes | 2,599 |
| Vetoes overridden | 112 |
| Share of regular vetoes overridden | about 7% |
| Most vetoes by one president | Franklin Roosevelt — 635 (9 overridden) |
| First override | March 3, 1845 (over John Tyler's veto) |
| Most recent override | FY2021 defense bill — House 322–87, Senate 81–13 (Jan. 1, 2021) |

Read the table from the President's side: a veto sticks roughly 93 times out of 100, and pocket vetoes stick always. That success rate is why the veto's real power is mostly *anticipatory* — Congress reshapes bills in advance to avoid vetoes that are never cast, which no statistic captures. Congress overrode none of Joe Biden's 13 vetoes and, as of mid-2026, neither of Donald Trump's two second-term vetoes; the New Year's Day 2021 override of the defense-bill veto remains the most recent in American history.

<Checkpoint question="A vetoed bill returns to the House, where 430 members vote: 287 yea, 143 nay. Five members are absent. Has the House overridden — or does it need 290 votes (two-thirds of all 435)?">
  It has overridden. *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Kansas* (1919) settled that the Constitution's "two thirds" means two-thirds of members **present and voting** with a quorum, not of total membership. Two-thirds of 430 is 286.67, so 287 yeas clears the bar. (The bill still needs two-thirds in the Senate — overrides must succeed in *both* chambers.)
</Checkpoint>

## The President's tools: the veto as shield, and the limits of going it alone

Hamilton justified the veto in Federalist No. 73 in defensive terms: "The primary inducement to conferring the power in question upon the Executive is, to enable him to defend himself" against a legislature whose "propensity ... to intrude upon the rights, and to absorb the powers, of the other departments" the Framers took as given. The mechanics live in Article I §7. The President has ten days (Sundays excepted) to sign or return a bill with objections. Silence makes it law — *unless* Congress has adjourned and thereby "prevent[s] its Return," in which case the unsigned bill dies. That second path is the **pocket veto**, and it is absolute: there is nothing for Congress to override, because no veto message ever comes back. More than 40 percent of all vetoes in American history have been pocket vetoes.

Notice two things in that gauntlet. First, no single branch can carry a policy from idea to enforcement alone — every stage is a handoff to an institution with different incentives. Second, the only absolute move on the board is the pocket veto, which works precisely because it produces *nothing* — no message, no text, no target for a counter-move.

Beyond the veto, the President nominates every federal judge, wields the pardon power for federal offenses ("except in Cases of Impeachment"), and sets the enforcement agenda of the entire executive establishment — deciding, within statutory limits, where investigative and prosecutorial energy actually goes.

What the President may *not* do is treat the spending power as optional. When Richard Nixon "impounded" — simply refused to spend — billions Congress had appropriated for clean-water grants and other programs, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in *Train v. City of New York* (1975) that the statute gave him no such discretion, and Congress passed the **Impoundment Control Act of 1974**, requiring the President to spend what Congress appropriates unless Congress itself approves a cancellation. Fifty years later the fight reignited. In 2025 the second Trump administration froze or withheld funds across dozens of programs; the Government Accountability Office found in May 2025 that one freeze — of electric-vehicle-charging funds — violated the Act, with dozens of similar inquiries open; and the dispute reached the Supreme Court's emergency docket twice: a 5–4 order in March 2025 left in place a ruling restarting frozen foreign-aid payments, then a September 2025 stay let a $4 billion "pocket rescission" of foreign aid proceed while signaling that private parties likely cannot sue under the Act. As of mid-2026 the Court has spoken only through such interim orders; whether a President may ever refuse to spend appropriated money remains unresolved on the merits. It is Federalist 51 running in real time, with the purse itself as the prize.

<Checkpoint question="Congress passes a bill on December 20 and adjourns its session sine die on December 22. The President, who opposes the bill, does nothing at all. What happens — and what can Congress do about it?">
  The bill dies by pocket veto: the ten-day clock can't run to automatic enactment because Congress's adjournment "prevents its Return" (Art. I, §7). And Congress can do nothing directly — with no returned veto message, there is nothing to override. Its only remedy is to reintroduce the bill in the next session and start over, or avoid presenting bills on the eve of adjournment in the first place.
</Checkpoint>

## The judiciary's tool: saying what the law is

The courts hold one check, but it is the heaviest: **[[term:judicial-review]]**, the power to refuse effect to a statute or executive act that conflicts with the Constitution. Article III never grants it in so many words. Chief Justice John Marshall asserted it in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) — *"It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"* — holding unconstitutional a minor provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789, and in doing so claiming for the Court the role of constitutional referee. Hamilton had sketched the theory in Federalist No. 78: judges have a duty "to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void," yet the judiciary remains "beyond comparison the weakest of the three departments," holding "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment." The Court has no army and no budget; its rulings bind only because the other branches — and the public — treat them as binding. Truman handing back the steel mills in 1952 is the system's load-bearing habit, not a constitutional inevitability.

The scholar's — and the government lawyer's — framework for judging presidential action comes from *Youngstown* itself, not from the majority opinion but from Justice Robert Jackson's concurrence, which sorted presidential power into three situations. First: "When the President acts pursuant to an express or implied authorization of Congress, his authority is at its maximum, for it includes all that he possesses in his own right plus all that Congress can delegate." Second: "When the President acts in absence of either a congressional grant or denial of authority, he can only rely upon his own independent powers, but there is a zone of twilight in which he and Congress may have concurrent authority, or in which its distribution is uncertain." Third: "When the President takes measures incompatible with the expressed or implied will of Congress, his power is at its lowest ebb, for then he can rely only upon his own constitutional powers minus any constitutional powers of Congress over the matter." Truman lost because seizure fell in the third box — Congress had considered and rejected seizure authority. Seventy years on, Jackson's three boxes remain the first tool courts and executive-branch lawyers reach for in any separation-of-powers dispute, which is why this single concurrence is among the most-cited opinions in American law.

<Checkpoint question="A statute expressly forbids exporting a technology. Citing national security, the President orders an agency to license the exports anyway. Under Jackson's framework, which category — and what follows for the order's chances in court?">
  Category three: the President is acting *incompatibly with the expressed will of Congress*, so his power is "at its lowest ebb" — he must rely on his own constitutional powers *minus* Congress's powers over the matter. Since regulating foreign commerce is squarely an Article I power, a court would almost certainly strike the order down, exactly as the Court treated Truman's seizure. (Had Congress been silent, this would be a harder "zone of twilight" case.)
</Checkpoint>

## Shortcuts forbidden: the Court polices both directions

Two modern cases show that the referee blows the whistle on Congress and the President alike — and for the same infraction.

For decades Congress wrote itself a convenience: the **legislative veto**, a statutory clause letting one chamber (sometimes one committee) cancel an executive action by resolution — no bill, no second chamber, no presidential signature. In *INS v. Chadha* (1983) the Court struck it down, 7–2: anything that alters legal rights is legislation, and legislation requires the full Article I §7 sequence, both chambers plus presentment. Justice White's dissent protested that the ruling sounded the death knell for provisions in "nearly 200 statutes"; the majority answered, in effect, that convenience is not a constitutional amendment.

Then the mirror image. In 1996 Congress handed the President a convenience: the Line Item Veto Act, letting him cancel individual spending items inside bills he had signed. In *Clinton v. City of New York* (1998) the Court struck that down too, 6–3: the Constitution lets the President approve or return a bill *whole*, and a President who excises pieces of an enacted law is unilaterally amending statutes — something Article I gives him no power to do. Put *Chadha* and *Clinton* side by side and the principle is symmetrical: neither branch may re-engineer the lawmaking procedure to its own advantage, even when the other branch consents. (The same logic shadows today's impoundment litigation — a President who simply declines to spend appropriated funds has achieved a line-item veto by other means.)

Congress retains blunter tools against the judiciary itself. The Court's size is ordinary statute — six justices at the Founding, ten during the Civil War, nine since 1869 — so "court-packing" is always legally available and politically radioactive. Congress also controls most of the Court's appellate jurisdiction, a power it used in *Ex parte McCardle* (1869), repealing the Court's jurisdiction over a pending case to keep Reconstruction off the docket — and the Court acquiesced. And the interpretive boundary between courts and the executive moved again as recently as 2024, when *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* overruled *Chevron* deference and reclaimed for judges the power to resolve statutory ambiguity that agencies had effectively exercised for forty years.

You have now seen the whole arsenal in action. Before the closing argument, make sure each weapon is filed under the branch that actually holds it — the classic exam mistake is handing one branch's check to another.

## What this lesson does not settle

The tools are settled law; whether they still *work* as Madison predicted is the live argument. In an influential 2006 article, "Separation of Parties, Not Powers," Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes observed that Madison's machine presumes branches that jealously guard institutional turf — but modern officials identify with parties first. When one party holds the White House and both chambers, congressional "ambition" often goes quiet; under divided government it roars back. The checks, on this view, run on a partisan motor the Framers never designed for. Whether that is a fatal flaw or just the system's modern operating mode is contested among scholars and unresolved here. What you should carry forward is the framework: every modern showdown — funding freezes, subpoena fights, emergency declarations, [[term:executive-order]] challenges — is a branch acting near its boundary while the other two reach for the tools inventoried above. The companion pack, *How a bill becomes law*, shows this machinery from the inside, where most checks operate before any lawsuit is filed.
