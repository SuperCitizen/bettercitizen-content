# How power is divided in the federal government

The U.S. Constitution does not put all federal authority in one place. Articles
I, II, and III create three distinct branches of government, each with a job
the others cannot do.

## The three branches

- **Article I — Legislative branch.** Congress (the House of Representatives
  and the Senate) writes federal law, levies taxes, and decides how money is
  spent. *"All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress
  of the United States."*
- **Article II — Executive branch.** The President carries out the laws
  Congress passes, commands the armed forces, and appoints federal officers
  (with Senate confirmation for many). *"The executive Power shall be vested
  in a President of the United States of America."*
- **Article III — Judicial branch.** The Supreme Court and lower federal courts
  resolve disputes about what the law means and whether it conforms to the
  Constitution. *"The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in
  one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from
  time to time ordain and establish."*

## Why divide power at all?

Madison argued in [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 47 that *"the accumulation of
all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether
of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective,
may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."* The structural
choice to separate powers is not a stylistic preference — it is the
Constitution's primary defense against concentrated authority.

## What this lesson does NOT settle

This lesson covers structure, not contemporary disputes about how the branches
should interact. The next lesson — *Checks and balances* — examines how each
branch can constrain the others, and where modern controversies (executive
orders, judicial review, congressional oversight) actually live.
