## The layout is an argument

Open a copy of the Constitution and look at its proportions. Article One, which creates Congress, takes up roughly half the original document. Article Two, the presidency, runs about a quarter. Article Three, the entire federal judiciary, is a few short paragraphs.

- Article I: roughly half
- Article II: about a quarter
- Article III: a few short paragraphs

The Framers wrote the institutions in descending order of how much they expected each to matter.

> The layout of the Constitution is an argument.

## Two words: herein granted

Each article opens with a vesting clause, a sentence that hands a kind of power to an institution. Congress gets the legislative powers "herein granted": the ones listed. The President gets "the executive Power," with no equivalent limiting phrase.

- Congress: powers "herein granted"
- The President: "the executive Power"

Whether that unqualified wording grants a reservoir of authority beyond the powers Article Two goes on to list is one of the longest-running fights in constitutional law.

## Separate is not isolated

Madison's answer to why power is divided, in Federalist 47, is that the accumulation of all powers in the same hands "may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."

But separation does not mean isolation. A President vetoing a bill is participating in legislation. A Senate confirming a judge is participating in staffing the judiciary.

- The veto: a share in legislation
- Confirmation: a share in the judiciary

> Separation forbids holding the whole of another power, not sharing in it.

In 1960 the political scientist Richard Neustadt called the result a government of separated institutions sharing powers.

## The branch the Framers feared

The branches were not expected to be equal. "In republican government," Madison wrote in Federalist 51, "the legislative authority necessarily predominates." That is why the Framers made Congress bicameral: two chambers with different terms, sizes and constituencies, each positioned to slow the other.

- Feared: the legislature
- Remedy: two chambers

## The branch that grew

Two centuries inverted the expectation. The story of modern American government is substantially the story of executive growth: above all the administrative state, hundreds of agencies that write binding rules, enforce them, and adjudicate disputes under them.

Critics note that such an agency looks, in miniature, like the accumulation Federalist 47 warned about. Defenders answer that agencies act only on authority Congress chose to delegate, and under judicial supervision.

- Critics: three functions in one agency
- Defenders: delegated, and supervised by courts

> The division of power among the branches is still being renegotiated.
