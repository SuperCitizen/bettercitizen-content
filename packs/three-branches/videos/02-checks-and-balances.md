## He gave them back

On April 8, 1952, with a steelworkers' strike hours away, President Harry Truman ordered the nation's steel mills seized. No statute authorized it. Eight weeks later the Supreme Court, 6 to 3, in the case called Youngstown, told the President to give the mills back.

- April 8, 1952: the mills are seized
- Youngstown: 6 to 3

[beat]

And he did.

> The Court told the President to return the mills. He did.

## Means and motive

Paper boundaries do not enforce themselves. Madison's answer, in Federalist 51: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." The design assumption is not civic virtue. It is jealousy. Each branch is armed against the others, and expected to want to use its arms.

> The design assumes jealousy, not virtue.

Congress holds the purse, confirmation, and impeachment. The President holds the veto. The courts hold judicial review, which Chief Justice John Marshall asserted in Marbury v. Madison in 1803.

- Congress: purse, confirmation, impeachment
- The President: the veto
- The courts: judicial review

Congress can also override a veto, with two-thirds of the members present and voting in each chamber.

- Override: two-thirds, present and voting

## The lowest ebb

The framework for judging presidential action comes from Youngstown itself, from Justice Robert Jackson's concurrence. When the President acts with the authorization of Congress, his authority is at its maximum. When Congress has neither granted nor denied authority, there is a "zone of twilight." When he acts against the expressed or implied will of Congress, his power is "at its lowest ebb."

- 1. Congress authorizes: maximum
- 2. Congress silent: zone of twilight
- 3. Against Congress: lowest ebb

Truman lost because seizure fell in the third box. Congress had considered and rejected seizure authority.

## No shortcuts, in either direction

In INS v. Chadha, in 1983, the Court struck down the legislative veto, which let one chamber cancel an executive action by resolution. In Clinton v. City of New York, in 1998, it struck down the Line Item Veto Act, which let the President cancel individual spending items inside bills he had signed.

- Chadha, 1983: against Congress
- Clinton, 1998: against the President

> Neither branch may re-engineer the lawmaking procedure to its own advantage.

## Do the checks still work?

The tools are settled law. Whether they still work as Madison predicted is the live argument. In 2006, Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes observed that modern officials identify with parties first. When one party holds the White House and both chambers, congressional ambition often goes quiet. Under divided government it roars back. Whether that is a fatal flaw or the system's modern operating mode is contested among scholars.

- "Separation of Parties, Not Powers," 2006

The lesson has the veto tally, the pocket veto, and the impoundment fights that reignited in 2025.
