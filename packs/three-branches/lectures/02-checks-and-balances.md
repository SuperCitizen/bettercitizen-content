Last time, I promised you the steel mills of 1952. Here they are.

[beat]

It is April 8. A steelworkers' strike is hours away. And in Korea, a war is using up artillery shells faster than the factories can forge them.

President Harry Truman gives an order to his Commerce Secretary, Charles Sawyer. Seize the nation's steel mills, and run them for the government.

No statute authorizes it. Truman claims that the emergency itself, plus his powers as commander in chief, is authority enough.

Eight weeks later, the Supreme Court answers. By six votes to three, in a case called Youngstown, it tells the president of the United States to give the mills back.

[pause]

And here is the remarkable part. [beat] He did.

The mills were returned. The strike ran for fifty-three days. And the republic absorbed the blow.

[pause]

A president claimed a power. Another branch said no. And the no held. This lecture is about why.

Last time we drew the lines: which branch holds which power. Now comes the enforcement machinery: the tools each branch holds to stop the others, and the cases where the Supreme Court has had to referee.

By the end, you will be able to name what each branch can do to the other two. And you will be able to sort any clash between a president and Congress into one of three boxes.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the theory: why this design counts on ambition, and not on virtue.

Second, the arsenals of Congress and the president.

Third, the courts, and the test that came out of the steel mills.

And last, the two shortcuts I promised you: a one-house veto, and a president canceling spending line by line.

## Means and motive

Start with the theory. It comes from Madison, in Federalist 51, and it is as much about psychology as about structure.

Paper boundaries do not enforce themselves. So Madison would give the people who run each branch two things. In his words: "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others."

[beat]

Means, and motives. The tools to push back, and a reason to use them.

Then comes his famous compression. "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

[pause]

And then the line that is more famous still. "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."

[pause]

Madison is not counting on angels. So notice what his design does count on.

> The design does not count on virtue. It counts on jealousy.

Each branch is armed against the others, and each is expected to want to use its arms. So a branch that reaches too far meets resistance automatically, the way pressure meets counter-pressure in an arch.

Every branch checks both of the others, and not one of those checks is decorative. Each names a power that has actually been used. Let's take the arsenals one branch at a time.

## What Congress holds

Congress first. It holds four weapons: money, confirmation, removal, and the override.

Begin with money, because Congress's deepest check is the one that never makes headlines. It is called the purse, and Article One, section nine, commands it. "No Money shall be drawn from the Treasury, but in Consequence of Appropriations made by Law."

In plainer words, nothing is spent until Congress passes a law to spend it. That law is an appropriation. Every soldier's salary, every agency's lease, every presidential priority runs through an appropriation.

> No appropriation, no money. That is the power of the purse.

Which is why funding fights are separation-of-powers fights in disguise.

Second, confirmation. The Senate must approve the president's choices for high office. That power is called advice and consent, and it reaches cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and every federal judge under Article Three.

Third, at the extreme, removal. The House impeaches, by a majority. The Senate convicts and removes, by two-thirds.

How extreme a remedy is that? The House has impeached three presidents, and the Senate convicted none of them. Every official the Senate has ever removed, eight in all, has been a federal judge.

Congress has other holds on the courts as well. It creates them, and it sets their size and jurisdiction.

[pause]

The fourth weapon is the override, and it takes some arithmetic.

When the president vetoes a bill, Congress can make it law anyway, with two-thirds of each chamber. But two-thirds of what? Of every seat? Or of the members who are there, voting?

The Supreme Court settled that in 1919. Two-thirds means two-thirds of the members present and voting, so long as a quorum is present. It does not mean two-thirds of the full membership.

> An override takes two-thirds of those present and voting, in both chambers.

Let's test that with numbers.

A vetoed bill returns to the House. 430 members vote, and five are absent. [beat] The count is 287 in favor, and 143 against.

Has the House overridden the veto? Or did it need 290, which is two-thirds of all 435? Work it through.

[pause 5s]

It has overridden. Two-thirds of the 430 who voted comes to just under 287, so 287 clears the bar. The five empty seats do not count against the bill.

But the bill is not law yet. The Senate has to reach two-thirds as well.

How often does Congress win that fight? The Senate keeps the official tally. From 1789 to the middle of 2026, Congress overrode about 7 percent of regular vetoes.

[beat]

Now stand where the president stands. A regular veto sticks roughly 93 times out of 100.

That is why the veto's real power is mostly anticipatory. Congress reshapes its bills in advance, to avoid a veto. And a veto that is never cast shows up in no statistic.

## What the president holds

Now the president, and the other side of that same veto.

Hamilton, in Federalist 73, justified the veto in defensive terms. The chief reason for giving the executive this power, he wrote, is "to enable him to defend himself."

Against what? Against a legislature that tends, as the Framers took for granted, to intrude on the other branches and absorb their powers.

So the veto is a shield. Its mechanics sit in Article One, section seven.

When a bill is presented to the president, a clock starts. The president has ten days, not counting Sundays, to sign the bill or to return it with objections.

And if the president does neither? Silence makes the bill a law. [beat] With one exception.

If Congress has adjourned, so that the bill cannot be returned, the unsigned bill dies. That is the pocket veto. And the pocket veto is absolute. There is nothing for Congress to override, because no veto message ever comes back.

Nor is it a rarity. More than 40 percent of all the vetoes in American history have been pocket vetoes.

> A regular veto sticks about 93 times in 100. A pocket veto sticks always.

Try one. Congress passes a bill on December 20. On December 22, it adjourns and ends its session. The president opposes the bill, and does nothing at all.

What happens to the bill? And what can Congress do about it?

[long pause]

The bill dies, by pocket veto. The ten-day clock cannot run out and make it law, because the adjournment has prevented its return.

And Congress can do nothing directly. No veto message came back, so there is nothing to override. Its only remedy is to reintroduce the bill in the next session and start over.

Beyond the veto, the president nominates every federal judge, holds the pardon power for federal offenses, and sets the enforcement agenda for the whole executive establishment.

Now the limit, and it takes us back to the purse. The president may not treat the spending power as optional.

Richard Nixon tried. Congress had appropriated billions of dollars for clean-water grants and other programs, and Nixon refused to spend them. That refusal has a name: impoundment.

The Supreme Court ruled, unanimously, that the statute gave him no such discretion. And Congress passed the Impoundment Control Act of 1974. It requires the president to spend what Congress appropriates, unless Congress itself approves a cancellation.

Fifty years later, the fight reignited. In 2025, the second Trump administration froze or withheld funds across dozens of programs, and twice the dispute reached the Supreme Court's emergency docket.

As of mid-2026, the Court has spoken only through interim orders. Whether a president may ever refuse to spend appropriated money remains unresolved on the merits.

It is Federalist 51, running in real time. And the prize is the purse itself.

## What the courts hold

That leaves the third branch. The courts hold one check. But it is the heaviest.

A court can refuse to give effect to a statute, or to an act of the executive, when it conflicts with the Constitution. That power is called judicial review.

And here is a surprise. Article Three never grants judicial review in so many words. Chief Justice John Marshall asserted it, in 1803, in Marbury v. Madison. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

[pause]

To say what the law is. In that case, the Court claimed for itself the role of constitutional referee.

But a referee with what behind it? Hamilton, in Federalist 78, called the judiciary "beyond comparison the weakest of the three departments." It holds, he wrote, "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."

[pause]

Merely judgment. The Court has no army, and no budget.

> The Court's rulings bind only because everyone else treats them as binding.

Everyone else: the other branches, and the public.

So go back to 1952. When Truman handed back the steel mills, no army made him do it. That handing back is the system's load-bearing habit. It is not a constitutional inevitability.

The steel case left us something else: a framework. It came not from the majority opinion but from a concurrence, written by Justice Robert Jackson. He sorted presidential power into three situations. Think of them as three boxes.

Box one. The president acts with the authorization of Congress, express or implied. Then, Jackson wrote, "his authority is at its maximum." It includes everything he possesses in his own right, plus everything Congress can delegate.

Box two. Congress has neither granted the authority nor denied it. Then the president can rely only on his own independent powers. But there is, Jackson wrote, "a zone of twilight," where he and Congress may both have authority, or where its distribution is uncertain.

Box three. The president acts against the will of Congress, expressed or implied. Then "his power is at its lowest ebb." He can rely only on his own constitutional powers, minus any constitutional powers of Congress over the matter.

[pause]

> With Congress: maximum. Congress silent: twilight. Against Congress: lowest ebb.

Why did Truman lose? Because the seizure fell in the third box. Congress had considered seizure authority, and had rejected it.

Seventy years on, courts and executive-branch lawyers still reach for those three boxes first, in any separation-of-powers dispute.

So let's reach for them ourselves. A statute expressly forbids exporting a certain technology. The president cites national security, and orders an agency to license the exports anyway.

Which box is that? And what follows for the order's chances in court?

[long pause]

Box three. The president is acting against the expressed will of Congress, so his power is at its lowest ebb. And regulating foreign commerce is squarely a power that Article One gives to Congress.

So a court would almost certainly strike the order down, exactly as the Court treated Truman's seizure. Had Congress been silent, it would be a harder case, in the zone of twilight.

## Two shortcuts, refused

Now put the three arsenals together, and notice what follows. No single branch can carry a policy from idea to enforcement alone. Every stage is a handoff, to an institution with different incentives.

That is not convenient. And twice, in modern times, the Court has ruled on a way around it. Listen for the same infraction in both.

Here is the first. For decades, Congress wrote itself a convenience called the legislative veto. It was a clause in a statute that let one chamber, and sometimes one committee, cancel an executive action by resolution. No bill. No second chamber. No presidential signature.

In 1983, in a case called INS v. Chadha, the Court struck it down, seven to two. Its reasoning runs in two steps. Anything that alters legal rights is legislation. And legislation requires the full sequence of Article One, section seven: both chambers, and then presentment to the president.

Justice White, in dissent, protested that the ruling sounded the death knell for provisions in "nearly 200 statutes." The majority answered, in effect, that convenience is not a constitutional amendment.

[pause]

Then the mirror image. In 1996, Congress handed the president a convenience: the Line Item Veto Act. It let him cancel individual spending items inside bills he had signed.

In 1998, in Clinton v. City of New York, the Court struck that down too, six to three. The Constitution lets the president approve a bill whole, or return it whole. A president who cuts pieces out of an enacted law is amending statutes on his own, and Article One gives him no power to do that.

Set the two cases side by side, and the principle is symmetrical.

> Neither branch may rewrite the lawmaking procedure, even when the other consents.

Notice how that sentence ends. Congress itself passed the Line Item Veto Act. It consented. The Court still said no.

And here an earlier thread returns. A president who declines to spend appropriated funds has achieved a line-item veto by other means. That logic shadows today's impoundment litigation.

## What to keep

You have now seen the whole arsenal at work. Before we gather it up, a test of memory.

The classic mistake is to hand one branch's check to another. I will name five checks. For each, ask yourself which branch holds it.

The veto. [beat] Judicial review. [beat] Impeachment and removal. [beat] Appointments. [beat] The purse.

[pause 5s]

The veto is the president's. Judicial review belongs to the courts. Impeachment and removal belong to Congress: the House impeaches, and the Senate convicts.

Appointments begin with the president, who nominates judges and officers, subject to confirmation by the Senate. And the purse is Congress's: no money leaves the Treasury except by an appropriation made by law.

[pause]

Now let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The design does not count on virtue. It counts on jealousy. Every branch is armed against both of the others, and expected to use its arms.

[beat]

Two. The veto math. An override takes two-thirds of those present and voting, in both chambers. A regular veto sticks about 93 times in 100. A pocket veto sticks always.

[beat]

Three. The courts hold judgment, and nothing else. In Marbury, the Court claimed the power to say what the law is. And its rulings bind only because everyone else treats them as binding.

[beat]

Four. Jackson's three boxes, from the steel case. With Congress, maximum. Congress silent, twilight. Against Congress, lowest ebb.

[beat]

And five. No shortcuts. Chadha ended the one-house veto. Clinton ended the line-item veto. Neither branch may rewrite the lawmaking procedure, even when the other consents.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. The tools are settled law. Whether they still work as Madison predicted is the open argument.

In an influential article from 2006, called "Separation of Parties, Not Powers," Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes made an observation. Madison's machine presumes branches that jealously guard their institutional turf. But modern officials identify with their parties first.

When one party holds the White House and both chambers, the ambition of Congress often goes quiet. Under divided government, it roars back.

On this view, the checks run on a partisan motor that the Framers never designed for. Is that a fatal flaw, or just the system's modern operating mode? Scholars contest it, and it stays unresolved here.

What you can carry forward is the framework. Take any modern showdown: a funding freeze, a subpoena fight, an emergency declaration. Each is a branch acting near its boundary, while the other two reach for the tools you now know.

From here, the argument moves inside Congress. The companion pack, How a Bill Becomes Law, shows this machinery from within, where most checks operate before any lawsuit is filed.

For now, go back to April 1952. A Court with no army tells a president to give the steel mills back. [beat] And he does.

Nothing in the Constitution made that inevitable. It was a habit. [beat] And the habit bears the load.
