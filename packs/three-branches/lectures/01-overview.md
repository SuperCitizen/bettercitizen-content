Picture a copy of the Constitution in front of you. Don't read it yet. Just look at its shape.

[beat]

Article One creates Congress. It takes up about half of the original document.

Article Two creates the presidency. About a quarter.

Article Three creates the entire federal court system. [beat] A few short paragraphs. The Framers did not even settle how many judges there would be.

[pause]

Half. A quarter. A few paragraphs.

> That layout is an argument.

The men who drafted it, in the summer of 1787, had fought a war against a king. And in the ten years since, they had watched their own state legislatures overreach. Many of them, James Madison above all, had learned that an elected assembly could be just as dangerous as a king.

So they did something unusual for their time. They split the government's power into three pieces. They gave each piece to a different institution. And they wrote those institutions down in order, from the one they expected to matter most, to the one they expected to matter least.

[pause]

That split is our subject. We will take it in four steps.

First, the three branches themselves: what each one is, and what it does.

Second, why the Founders divided power at all.

Third, a correction that most people miss: the branches are separate, but their powers are shared.

And last, a twist. The branch the Founders feared most is not the branch that grew.

## Three branches, three jobs

Each of the first three articles opens the same way: with one sentence that hands a kind of power to an institution. That sentence is called a vesting clause.

Article One vests the legislative power in Congress. Congress is the House of Representatives and the Senate. It writes federal law. It levies taxes. And it decides how every public dollar is spent.

Article Two vests the executive power in a president. The president carries out the laws Congress passes, commands the armed forces, conducts foreign relations, and appoints the officers of government, many of them only with the Senate's approval.

Article Three vests the judicial power in one Supreme Court, and in whatever lower courts Congress chooses to create. Federal courts settle disputes about what the law means, and about whether the government's actions fit the Constitution.

[pause]

So. Congress writes the law. The president carries it out. The courts say what it means.

> Write. Carry out. Interpret.

If you keep nothing else from the next few minutes, keep those three verbs.

Now listen closely to the first two of those clauses. A small difference in their wording has fueled two centuries of argument.

Article One gives Congress the legislative powers "herein granted." [beat] Herein granted. That means the powers on the list.

And there is a list. Most of it sits in Article One, section eight. Taxing. Borrowing. Regulating commerce among the states and with other countries. Declaring war. Raising armies. And making the laws that are "necessary and proper" for carrying out all the rest.

Article Two gives the president "the executive power." [beat] And then it stops. No "herein granted." No phrase that limits it.

[pause]

Does that open wording give the president a reservoir of authority, beyond the specific powers the article goes on to name? That is one of the longest-running fights in constitutional law. And it sits underneath most modern disputes about presidential power.

So hold on to this.

> Congress has a list. The president's clause has no such limit.

One more thing before we move on. It is about what the Constitution does not say.

It never says there are 435 members of the House. Congress capped the House at that number with an ordinary law, in 1929.

It never says there are nine justices. The Supreme Court began with six. It had as many as ten during the Civil War. Congress settled on nine in 1869.

And voters did not always choose their senators. State legislatures chose them, until the Seventeenth Amendment, in 1913, switched to direct election.

What the Constitution does say is that federal judges hold office "during good behavior." In practice, for life. And their salaries cannot be cut. [beat] Why? So that the people who judge the government do not depend on the goodwill of the people they judge.

Let's test what we have so far. Here is a scene.

Your representative announces: "I will cut off every dollar for this military operation." Your neighbor scoffs: "Only the president controls the military."

Who has the Constitution on their side? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Both of them hold real cards. And that is the point.

Article Two makes the president commander in chief. Command of the troops belongs to the executive. But Article One gives Congress the power to declare war, to raise and support armies, and to control all spending. No money leaves the Treasury unless Congress appropriates it.

So the representative cannot direct the troops. And the president cannot fund them.

> The Constitution forces the two branches into each other's business. On purpose.

## Why divide power at all?

That is the what. Now the why. Why split power in the first place?

The classic answer comes from James Madison, in Federalist 47. Here are his words. Listen for how the sentence ends.

"The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective, [beat] may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny."

[pause]

The very definition of tyranny.

Notice how precise that is. For Madison, tyranny is not bad policy. It is not even bad rulers. It is a structure. It is what you have when the power to make the rules, the power to enforce the rules, and the power to judge who broke the rules all sit in the same hands.

Think about what follows from that. Imagine a perfectly kind government that holds all three powers. By Madison's lights, that is still a tyranny. [beat] A tyranny waiting for its first bad day.

> For Madison, tyranny is a structure: all three powers in the same hands.

Madison did not claim the idea as his own. He named his source. "The oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject," he wrote, "is the celebrated Montesquieu."

Montesquieu was a French philosopher. In 1748, in a book called The Spirit of the Laws, he looked at the English constitution and drew a lesson from it: liberty survives only where the three powers are held separately.

The American Framers took that observation and turned it into hard architecture. Three compartments. Each with its own way of being chosen, its own term of office, and its own constituency. So that no single election, no faction, and no ambitious individual could capture the whole machine in one stroke.

But Madison added a correction right away. Students miss it. Pundits mangle it. So let's get it right.

Montesquieu, Madison insisted, did not mean that the branches should have no partial agency in each other's work, or no control over each other's acts.

In plainer words:

> Separation does not mean isolation.

When a president vetoes a bill, the president is taking part in lawmaking. When the Senate confirms a judge, the Senate is taking part in staffing the courts. Those are not breaches of the design. They are features of it.

What the doctrine forbids is something else: the whole of one power falling into the hands that already hold another.

Not a share of another branch's power. [beat] The whole of it.

Keep that distinction. The entire next lecture lives inside it.

Let me put it to work. A friend says to you: "The president's veto violates the separation of powers. Vetoing is a legislative act, and the president is not the legislature."

How would Madison answer? Think it through.

[long pause]

He would answer with his own caveat from Federalist 47. Separation of powers forbids the accumulation of all of one power in the same hands. It does not forbid one branch from holding a partial share in another's work.

And he would go a step further. The veto gives the president a share in lawmaking precisely so that the executive can defend itself against a legislature that reaches too far.

> The blending is what preserves the separation.

Let me say that once more, because it sounds like a paradox. The branches stay separate because each one is allowed to check the others. Take away the overlap, and you take away the defense.

## Separated institutions, sharing powers

In 1960, the political scientist Richard Neustadt compressed all of this into one of the most quoted lines in his field. It comes from his book, Presidential Power.

The Constitutional Convention, he wrote, is supposed to have created a government of separated powers. And then: "It did nothing of the sort. Rather, it created a government of separated institutions sharing powers."

[pause]

Separated institutions. Sharing powers.

Test that against anything the federal government does.

Take a law. It is drafted in Congress. It is shaped by the threat of a presidential veto. It is signed, or not, by the president. It is put into practice by executive agencies. And it is interpreted by courts.

Take a war. Congress declares it and funds it. The president commands it. And judges hear the cases that come out of it: detention, surveillance, the war powers themselves.

Even the courts are a joint product. The president nominates the judges. The Senate confirms them. And Congress decides how many judgeships exist at all.

Every branch's fingerprints, on every major function.

Why does this reframing matter? Because it predicts how the system feels from the inside. No branch can finish anything alone. So governing runs on bargaining. And a president's real power, in Neustadt's famous claim, is less the power to command than the power to persuade.

## The branch they feared, and the branch that grew

One last turn. If the branches were meant to balance each other, were they expected to be equal?

[beat]

No.

"In republican government," Madison wrote in Federalist 51, "the legislative authority necessarily predominates." And in Federalist 48 he warned that the legislature was "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex."

The branch the Founders feared was Congress. It sits closest to the people. It holds the purse. That is why they split it in two: a bicameral legislature, two chambers with different terms, different sizes, and different constituencies, each placed to slow the other down.

The presidency, by comparison, was thin on paper. And Article Three was thinnest of all: a court with no army, no budget, and not even a fixed number of members.

[pause]

Two centuries turned that expectation upside down.

The story of modern American government is, in large part, the story of executive growth. A standing military. A foreign-policy apparatus. And above all, the administrative state: hundreds of agencies, using power that Congress delegated to them, to write binding rules, to enforce those rules, and to decide disputes under them.

Stop on that for a second. Write the rules. Enforce the rules. Judge disputes under the rules. [beat] Where have you heard that combination before?

[long pause]

It is Madison's definition, from Federalist 47. Critics have long pointed out that an agency combining those three functions looks, in miniature, like the very accumulation he warned about.

Defenders have an answer. An agency acts only on authority that Congress chose to delegate. Congress can amend or repeal the statute behind it. Congress controls its budget. The president appoints its leaders. And judges review its decisions. So all three constitutional branches keep a handle on it.

Are those handles tight enough? That is a genuinely contested question. It is not a settled one.

And the boundary keeps moving. For forty years, under a doctrine called Chevron, judges were required to defer to an agency's reasonable reading of an unclear statute. In 2024, in a case called Loper Bright, the Supreme Court overruled Chevron. Deciding what an unclear statute means is once again the courts' job.

That is a significant transfer of interpretive power, from the executive branch back to the judiciary. And it shows that the division of power among the branches is still being renegotiated, in the 2020s.

One caution before we gather this up. "Three branches" does not mean three equal workloads, or three equal headcounts. Congress is 535 elected members. The executive branch employs millions of people, civilian and military. The whole Article Three judiciary is fewer than a thousand judges with life tenure.

The branches are equals in authority: each one is supreme in its own function. They are not equals in size, or speed, or visibility.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Three articles, three branches, three verbs. Congress writes the law. The president carries it out. The courts interpret it.

[beat]

Two. Congress's powers are "herein granted": a list. The president's clause has no such limit. That difference in wording is still being argued over.

[beat]

Three. The numbers you may have memorized are not in the Constitution. Congress set the House at 435, in 1929. Congress set the Court at nine, in 1869.

[beat]

Four. For Madison, tyranny is a structure: all three powers in the same hands. Separation forbids the whole of one power from joining another. It does not forbid a share. In Neustadt's words: separated institutions, sharing powers.

[beat]

And five. The Founders feared the legislature. The branch that grew was the executive. And the lines between the three are still moving.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We have the architecture: who holds which power, and why. We have not asked the harder question. What happens when a branch reaches past its boundary? And what can the other two do about it?

Madison saw that problem too. In Federalist 48 he warned that mere "parchment barriers" would never hold against what he called the encroaching spirit of power. Lines on paper need machinery to enforce them.

That machinery is the subject of the next lecture: checks and balances. Vetoes and overrides. The power of the purse, and impeachment. Appointments, and judicial review. We will watch it work on the steel mills of 1952, on a one-house veto written into nearly two hundred statutes, and on a president's attempt to cancel spending line by line.

For now, go back to where we started. Half the document. A quarter. A few paragraphs. [beat] You know now what that layout was arguing. And you know how the argument turned out.
