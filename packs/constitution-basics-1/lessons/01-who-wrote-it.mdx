# Who wrote the Constitution, and why?

In May of 1787, a group of men walked into the State House in Philadelphia and closed the shutters on the windows. A hot summer was coming, and the room was going to be stuffy. They closed the shutters anyway, because they did not want anyone outside to hear them, and they promised each other to keep their talks a secret.

They had a reason to be careful. They had been sent to fix the rules of their country. Before the summer was over, they had decided to throw those rules away and write new ones.

## What is a constitution?

A *constitution* is the set of rules for a government. It says who is in charge of what, how leaders get their jobs, and what the government is not allowed to do.

Think of the rulebook for a sport. The rulebook does not say who will win. It says how the game is played, and no player gets to change it in the middle of a game. A constitution works the same way for a country.

The comparison is not perfect, though. A game ends, and a country keeps going for hundreds of years. So a constitution also needs a way to change its own rules, which is what the last lesson in this pack is about.

## The first rules did not work

The United States already had a set of rules in 1787. They were called the Articles of Confederation, and they made the national government very weak. There was a Congress, but there was no president and there were almost no national courts. Congress could ask the states for money, but it could not make them pay.

The thirteen states often acted like thirteen small countries, and many leaders feared that the United States would not last. That fear is what brought them to Philadelphia.

## A summer of arguments

The men in that room were called *delegates*, which means people sent to speak for others. Fifty-five of them came, from twelve states. Rhode Island refused to send anyone. The delegates chose George Washington to lead the meetings.

They argued for almost four months, and the biggest fight was about Congress. States with many people wanted more votes than states with few people. The small states wanted every state to have the same number of votes.

In the end they made a *compromise*, which is a deal where each side gives up something. Congress would have two parts. In the House of Representatives, a state with more people would get more members. In the Senate, every state would get two members, no matter its size. The deal passed on July 16, 1787, by a vote of five states to four.

<Checkpoint question="Why would a small state like Delaware say no to a plan where votes in Congress depended only on how many people a state had?">

Because the big states could outvote it every single time. Under the compromise, Delaware still has fewer members in the House, but in the Senate it has two votes, the same as the biggest state. That was enough for the small states to say yes.

</Checkpoint>

## A deal that left people enslaved

The delegates also argued about slavery, which was legal in much of the country at that time.

The Constitution of 1787 did not end slavery. The delegates made deals that protected it. One deal counted three out of every five enslaved people when deciding how many House members a state would get. That gave more power in Congress to the states with slavery, even though the enslaved people could not vote. Another deal said that Congress could not stop ships from bringing enslaved people into the country until 1808.

Slavery did not end until 1865, after the Civil War, when the Thirteenth Amendment was added to the Constitution.

## Signing, and then asking the states

On September 17, 1787, the Constitution was ready, and it carries 39 names. It still was not the law, because nine of the thirteen states had to approve it first. [[term:ratification]] is the word for that kind of approval.

People argued about it in newspapers and at meetings all over the country. On June 21, 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify, and the Constitution became the plan of the United States government. It still is.
