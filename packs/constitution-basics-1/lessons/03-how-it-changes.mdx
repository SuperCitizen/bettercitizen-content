# How can the Constitution change?

In 1982, a college student in Texas named Gregory Watson was looking for a topic for a paper. While doing his research, he came across a change to the Constitution that Congress had proposed back in 1789. It said that when Congress votes to raise its own pay, the raise cannot start until after the next election.

Not enough states had ever approved the idea, and almost everyone had forgotten it. But Watson noticed something. Nothing in the proposal said that time had run out. He wrote his paper to argue that the states could still approve it, and his teacher gave him a C.

He did not give up. He spent the next ten years writing letters to lawmakers in state after state, and in 1992 the idea he had found became part of the Constitution. In 2017, his university changed his grade to an A.

## What is an amendment?

A change to the Constitution is called an *amendment*. [[term:amendment]] is also the word for something added on, and that is how it works here. Nothing in the Constitution gets erased. Each new amendment is added at the end.

There are 27 amendments. Watson's is the last one, the Twenty-seventh.

## Two steps, and both are hard

The rules for amending the Constitution are in Article V, and they have two steps.

First, an amendment has to be *proposed*. Two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate must vote for it. There is another way to propose one, through a special meeting called by the states, but it has never been used.

Second, the amendment has to be *ratified*, which means approved by the states. Three-fourths of the states must say yes. Today that means 38 of the 50 states.

The two steps do different jobs. The first makes sure that most of Congress agrees, and the second makes sure that most of the states agree. One person who is left out may surprise you. The President has no part in amending the Constitution, and does not sign an amendment or get to veto one.

## Thousands of ideas, 27 amendments

Members of Congress have suggested about 12,000 amendments since 1789. Only 27 have made it all the way.

The ones that made it changed the country. The first ten, added in 1791, are the Bill of Rights. The Thirteenth ended slavery in 1865. The Nineteenth said in 1920 that women could not be kept from voting. The Twenty-sixth lowered the voting age to 18 in 1971, and it went from Congress to the Constitution in just 100 days.

## Should it be this hard?

Two of the men who built the country disagreed about that.

James Madison thought the Constitution should be changed rarely. He worried that if people rewrote the rules all the time, they would stop respecting them. A government needs the trust that only comes with time, he wrote.

Thomas Jefferson thought each new group of Americans should be free to fix what no longer worked. Making people live by old rules forever, he wrote, would be like making a man "wear still the coat which fitted him when a boy."

Both of them had a point. Rules that change too easily are hard to trust, and rules that can never change stop fitting the people who live under them. Article V sits in between: change is allowed, but only when most of the country agrees.

Gregory Watson showed that it can still be done, even by one person with a stack of letters.
