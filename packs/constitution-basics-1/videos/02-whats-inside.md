## Three big words

The Constitution starts with three words that are written much bigger than all the others: We the People.

Those words answer a question before anyone can ask it. Who gave this government the right to exist? The answer is not a king, and it is not the thirteen states. The answer is the people of the country.

> The government gets its power from the people.

## The first sentence

The opening sentence of the Constitution is called the Preamble. A preamble is an introduction. It does not give anyone any power. It says who is making the government, and what they want it to do.

- Preamble: an introduction, not a power

That one long sentence holds six goals. One is to establish justice, which means laws and courts that treat people fairly. Another is to keep the peace at home.

- Six goals in one sentence

## Seven parts

After the Preamble, the Constitution is split into seven parts called articles. The first three set up the three branches of government. Article One sets up Congress, which makes the laws. Article Two sets up the President, who carries out the laws. Article Three sets up the courts, which decide what the laws mean.

- Article I: Congress
- Article II: the President
- Article III: the courts

The last four articles explain how everything fits together.

[check q3]

## The highest law

Article Six says the Constitution is the highest law in the country. No law made by Congress, by a state or by a city is allowed to go against it. If one does, a court can throw that law out.

- Article VI: the highest law

> No law is allowed to go against the Constitution.

## What was missing

The Constitution of 1787 said very little about the rights of ordinary people, such as the right to speak freely. Many Americans thought that was a serious mistake. In 1791, ten changes were added to the Constitution. Together they are called the Bill of Rights.

- 1791: the Bill of Rights

The lesson puts all six goals into plain words, lists what each of the seven articles does, and names three big ideas that run all the way through the Constitution.
