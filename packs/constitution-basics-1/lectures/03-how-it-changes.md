Think of a time you worked hard on a paper for school. You were sure you had found something good. Then the grade came back, and it was lower than you had hoped.

[beat]

That happened to a college student in Texas, in 1982. His name was Gregory Watson.

Watson was looking for a topic for a paper. While doing his research, he came across a change to the Constitution that Congress had proposed back in 1789. It said that when Congress votes to raise its own pay, the raise cannot start until after the next election.

Not enough states had ever approved the idea, and almost everyone had forgotten it. But Watson noticed something. [beat] Nothing in the proposal said that time had run out.

So he wrote his paper to argue that the states could still approve it. [beat] His teacher gave him a C.

[pause]

He did not give up. He spent the next ten years writing letters to lawmakers, in state after state. And in 1992, the idea he had found became part of the Constitution.

How does an idea become part of the Constitution? By the end of this lecture, you will be able to explain it.

We will take it in four steps. First, what a change to the Constitution is called. Second, the two steps that every change has to pass. Third, how rarely a change makes it. And last, an old argument about whether it should be this hard.

## What is an amendment?

A change to the Constitution is called an amendment.

Amendment is also the word for something added on, and that is how it works here. Nothing in the Constitution gets erased. Each new amendment is added at the end.

> An amendment is a change to the Constitution, added at the end.

There are 27 amendments. Watson's is the last one, the Twenty-seventh.

## Two steps, and both are hard

So how does an amendment get added? The rules are in Article Five of the Constitution, and they have two steps.

Step one. An amendment has to be proposed. That happens in Congress. Two-thirds of the House must vote for it, and two-thirds of the Senate must vote for it too. Two-thirds means two out of every three.

> Step one: two-thirds of the House, and two-thirds of the Senate.

Step two. The amendment has to be ratified, which means approved by the states. Three-fourths of the states must say yes. That is three out of every four. Today, it means 38 of the 50 states.

> Step two: three-fourths of the states. Today, that means 38 states.

The two steps do different jobs. The first makes sure that most of Congress agrees. The second makes sure that most of the states agree.

One person is left out, and it may surprise you. [beat] The President.

The President has no part in amending the Constitution. The President does not sign an amendment, and does not get to veto one. To veto is to turn something down.

[pause]

Now picture a counter that shows how many states have said yes. At 37, the idea is one state short. It is not part of the Constitution yet. [beat] At 38, it is.

> One state can turn an idea into a part of the Constitution.

For Watson's amendment, the thirty-eighth yes came on May 7, 1992.

Let's test the two steps. Two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for an amendment. Then 30 states approve it. Is it part of the Constitution?

[long pause]

No. It passed the first step. But the second step takes 38 states, and it has only 30. It stays a proposal until eight more states say yes.

## Thousands of ideas, 27 amendments

So how often does an idea make it through both steps? Not often.

Members of Congress have suggested about 12,000 amendments since 1789. [beat] Only 27 have made it all the way.

> About 12,000 amendments have been suggested. Only 27 have made it.

But the ones that made it changed the country.

The first ten are the Bill of Rights. They were added in 1791.

The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery, in 1865.

The Nineteenth said, in 1920, that women could not be kept from voting.

And the Twenty-sixth came in 1971. It lowered the voting age to 18. And it went from Congress to the Constitution in just 100 days.

## Should it be this hard?

Two steps, and both are hard. Should it be this hard?

Two of the men who built the country disagreed about that.

James Madison thought the Constitution should be changed rarely. He worried that if people rewrote the rules all the time, they would stop respecting them. A government, he wrote, needs the trust that only comes with time.

Thomas Jefferson thought each new group of Americans should be free to fix what no longer worked. Making people live by old rules forever, he wrote in a letter, would be like making a man "wear still the coat which fitted him when a boy."

[pause]

The coat which fitted him when a boy. Jefferson was comparing old rules to a coat that a boy has outgrown.

Now try this. Here are three ideas. First, rules that worked long ago should never be touched. Second, rules should be able to change as people learn more. Third, rules should change every time a new leader is chosen. Which one would Jefferson agree with most?

[long pause]

The second. Jefferson thought laws should keep up as people learn.

Why not the third? Because he did not want change for its own sake. In the same letter, he said he was against changing laws too often.

So who was right? Both of them had a point. Rules that change too easily are hard to trust. And rules that can never change stop fitting the people who live under them.

[pause]

Article Five sits in between.

> Article Five allows change, but only when most of the country agrees.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. An amendment is a change to the Constitution. Nothing gets erased. Each new amendment is added at the end, and there are 27 of them.

[beat]

Two. An amendment has to pass two steps. Can you still say what they are?

[long pause]

Step one: two-thirds of the House, and two-thirds of the Senate. Step two: three-fourths of the states, which today means 38 states. And the President has no part in it.

[beat]

Three. It is rare. About 12,000 amendments have been suggested. Only 27 have made it.

[beat]

Four. Madison wanted change to be rare, so that people would keep respecting the rules. Jefferson wanted each new group of Americans to be free to fix what no longer worked. Article Five sits in between.

[pause]

So should it be this hard? This lecture has not settled that. Madison and Jefferson disagreed, and both of them had a point.

The next time someone wants to change the Constitution, you will know the two steps it takes, and both sides of the argument.

One last thing. Remember the C on Gregory Watson's paper. In 2017, his university changed his grade to an A.

[beat]

Watson showed that it can still be done, even by one person with a stack of letters.
