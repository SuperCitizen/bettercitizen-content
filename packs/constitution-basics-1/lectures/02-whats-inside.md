Think of a poster on a wall. Before you read anything else, your eye goes to the biggest words.

The Constitution works like that too. It starts with three words that are written much bigger than all the others.

[beat]

"We the People."

[pause]

Those three words answer a question before anyone can ask it. Who gave this government the right to exist?

The answer is not a king. And it is not the thirteen states. [beat] The answer is the people of the country, speaking for themselves.

Now let's open the Constitution up. By the end, you will be able to say what it holds, from its first sentence to its last part.

We will take it in four steps. First, the opening sentence, and the six goals inside it. Second, the seven parts that come after. Third, something that was missing. And last, three big ideas that run through all of it.

## The first sentence

The opening sentence of the Constitution is called the Preamble. A preamble is an introduction.

The Preamble does not give anyone any power. It does two other things.

> The Preamble says who is making the government, and what it is for.

We already have the who. We the People. Now for the what.

The Preamble is one long sentence, and it holds six goals. For each goal, I will give you the Constitution's own words, and then plain words.

The first goal is to "form a more perfect Union." In plain words, that means helping the states work together better than before.

The second is to "establish Justice." That means making laws and courts that treat people fairly.

The third is to "insure domestic Tranquility." That is, keeping the peace at home.

[beat]

That is half of them. Here are the other three.

The fourth is to "provide for the common defence." That means protecting the country from attack.

The fifth is to "promote the general Welfare." In plain words, helping people live well.

And the sixth is to "secure the Blessings of Liberty." That means protecting freedom.

[pause]

Listen to how that last goal ends. The freedom is for "ourselves and our Posterity."

Posterity means the people who come after you. The writers were making rules for people who had not been born yet. [beat] That includes you.

Let's put the six goals to work. A town builds a fire station and hires firefighters. Which goal from the Preamble is the town working on?

[long pause]

It fits best with keeping the peace and safety at home. The Preamble calls that "domestic Tranquility."

But you could also say it helps people live well. That is the "general Welfare."

Both answers are good ones. Many things a government does serve more than one of the six goals.

## Seven parts called articles

That is the first sentence. Now the rest.

After the Preamble, the Constitution is split into seven parts, and the parts are called articles.

The first three articles set up the three branches of government. Those are Congress, the President and the courts.

Article One sets up Congress, which makes the laws.

Article Two sets up the President, who carries out the laws.

Article Three sets up the courts, which decide what the laws mean.

[pause]

One, two, three. Make the laws. Carry them out. Decide what they mean.

The last four articles explain how everything fits together.

Article Four says how the states must treat each other.

Article Five says how the Constitution can be changed.

Article Six says the Constitution is the highest law in the country.

And Article Seven says how the Constitution would be approved.

[pause]

Stay with Article Six for a moment, because it does a big job.

> Article Six makes the Constitution the highest law in the country.

Here is what that means. No law made by Congress, by a state or by a city is allowed to go against the Constitution. If one does, a court can throw that law out.

Let's test that. A state passes a law that goes against the Constitution. What happens? And which article says so?

[long pause]

The Constitution wins, because of Article Six. A state law that goes against the highest law cannot stand.

Article Four is about the states too. But it says how the states treat each other, and that is a different subject.

## What was missing

Those are the seven parts. Now for what was missing.

The Constitution of 1787 spent most of its words on how the government would work. It said very little about the rights of ordinary people, such as the right to speak freely, or to follow any religion.

Many Americans thought that was a serious mistake. And some states agreed to approve the Constitution only because they were promised a list of rights.

[beat]

The promise was kept.

> In 1791, ten changes were added. Together they are called the Bill of Rights.

## Three big ideas

One last step. The Constitution is short enough to read in one sitting, and three ideas run all the way through it.

The first idea is that the people are in charge. The government gets its power from them.

The second idea is that power is split up. It is divided among three branches, and it is also shared between the national government and the states. That way, no one person or group holds all of it.

The third idea is that the government has limits. It may do only what the Constitution allows, and even the most powerful leader has to follow the rules.

> The people are in charge. Power is split up. The government has limits.

Now reach back to the start of this lecture. I gave you three words, written bigger than all the others. Which of the three big ideas do those words stand for?

[long pause]

The first one. The people are in charge. That is what "We the People" means.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The first sentence is the Preamble. It gives no one any power. It says who is making the government, and what it is for. And it holds six goals. Here they are once more, in plain words. Work together. Be fair. Keep the peace. Protect the country. Help people live well. Protect freedom.

[beat]

Two. Seven articles come next. The first three set up Congress, the President and the courts. Article Five says how to change the Constitution. And Article Six makes it the highest law in the country.

[beat]

Three. The list of rights came later. In 1791, ten changes were added, and together they are called the Bill of Rights.

[beat]

Four. Three big ideas run through all of it. The people are in charge. Power is split up. The government has limits.

[pause]

There is one thing we have not opened up yet. The Constitution has been changed 27 times since it was written. How is a change made? And why does it happen so rarely? The next lecture, the last in this series, takes up those two questions.

For now, picture those three big words one more time. We the People. [beat] The rules that follow them were made for people who had not been born yet. You are one of those people.
