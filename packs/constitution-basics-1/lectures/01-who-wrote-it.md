Think of a stuffy room on a hot day. The air does not move, and everyone wants a window open.

Now picture someone getting up and closing the shutters. On purpose.

[beat]

In May of 1787, a group of men did just that. They walked into the State House in Philadelphia, and they closed the shutters on the windows. A hot summer was coming, and the room was going to be stuffy. They closed the shutters anyway.

Why would they do that? [beat] They did not want anyone outside to hear them. And they promised each other to keep their talks a secret.

[pause]

They had a reason to be careful. They had been sent to fix the rules of their country. Before the summer was over, they had decided to throw those rules away and write new ones.

The new rules are called the Constitution. By the end of this lecture, you will be able to say who wrote it, and what they fought about.

We will take it in four steps. First, what a constitution is. Second, why the old rules did not work. Third, the biggest fight of that summer, and the deal that ended it. And last, what the new rules did about slavery, and how they became the law.

## What is a constitution?

Start with the word itself.

> A constitution is the set of rules for a government.

It says who is in charge of what. It says how leaders get their jobs. And it says what the government is not allowed to do.

Think of the rulebook for a sport. The rulebook does not say who will win. It says how the game is played, and no player gets to change it in the middle of a game.

A constitution works the same way for a country. It is the country's rulebook.

The comparison is not perfect, though. A game ends. [beat] A country keeps going, for hundreds of years. So a constitution also needs a way to change its own rules. That is the subject of the last lecture in this series.

## The first rules did not work

So a constitution is a set of rules. And in 1787, the United States already had one.

Those first rules were called the Articles of Confederation, and they made the national government very weak.

How weak? There was a Congress. But there was no president, and there were almost no national courts. Congress could ask the states for money, but it could not make them pay.

[pause]

Put plainly, a government that has to ask for its money can be told no.

> The Articles of Confederation made the national government very weak.

The thirteen states often acted like thirteen small countries. And many leaders feared that the United States would not last.

That fear is what brought the leaders to Philadelphia.

## A summer of arguments

The men in that room were called delegates. A delegate is a person sent to speak for others.

Fifty-five of them came. Twelve states sent them, and Rhode Island refused to send anyone. The delegates chose George Washington to lead the meetings.

Then they argued, for almost four months. The biggest fight was about Congress.

States with many people wanted more votes than states with few people. The small states wanted every state to have the same number of votes.

Here is a question for you. Take a small state, like Delaware. Suppose votes in Congress depended only on how many people a state had. Why would Delaware say no to that plan?

[long pause]

Because the big states could outvote it every single time.

[pause]

In the end, the two sides made a compromise. A compromise is a deal where each side gives up something.

Here is the deal they made. Congress would have two parts. One part is the House of Representatives. In the House, a state with more people would get more members. The other part is the Senate. In the Senate, every state would get two members, no matter its size.

> More people means more House members. Every state gets two in the Senate.

Now think about Delaware again. It still has fewer members in the House. But in the Senate it has two votes, the same as the biggest state. That was enough for the small states to say yes.

The deal passed on July 16, 1787. The vote was five states to four.

## A deal that left people enslaved

That was one deal. The delegates made others, and they are harder to hear about.

They also argued about slavery, which was legal in much of the country at that time.

> The Constitution of 1787 did not end slavery. The delegates' deals protected it.

Here are two of those deals.

One was about counting. It counted three out of every five enslaved people when deciding how many House members a state would get.

Now think back to the compromise. In the House, more people means more members. So what did that counting do for the states with slavery?

[long pause]

It gave them more members. That meant more power in Congress for the states with slavery, even though the enslaved people could not vote.

Another deal was about ships. It said that Congress could not stop ships from bringing enslaved people into the country until 1808.

[pause]

Slavery did not end until 1865, after the Civil War. That is when something new was added to the Constitution. It is called the Thirteenth Amendment.

## Signing, and then asking the states

On September 17, 1787, the Constitution was ready. It carries 39 names.

[pause]

But it still was not the law. Nine of the thirteen states had to approve it first. There is a word for that kind of approval. The word is ratify. To ratify is to approve and make official.

So people argued about the Constitution in newspapers, and at meetings all over the country.

Then, on June 21, 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify. The Constitution became the plan of the United States government.

[beat]

It still is.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A constitution is the set of rules for a government. It works like the rulebook for a sport.

[beat]

Two. The first rules, the Articles of Confederation, made the national government very weak. So try this one from memory. What were the delegates sent to Philadelphia to do? And what did they do?

[long pause]

They were sent to fix the old rules. They wrote new ones.

[beat]

Three. The biggest fight ended in a compromise, a deal where each side gives up something. More people means more House members. Every state gets two in the Senate.

[beat]

Four. The Constitution of 1787 did not end slavery. The delegates' deals protected it. Slavery lasted until 1865.

[beat]

And five. The Constitution was not the law until nine states ratified it. To ratify is to approve and make official.

[pause]

So now you know who wrote the Constitution, and what they fought about. You have not yet heard what it says. The next lecture opens it up.

For now, go back to that room in Philadelphia, with its shutters closed. The men inside were sent to fix the old rules. [beat] They wrote new ones. And those new rules are still the plan of the government today.
