The morning of April 29, 2026. The Supreme Court hands down a decision called Louisiana v. Callais.

The case had been argued once, set for reargument, and argued again. Then it was held for six more months, while two election cycles' worth of congressional maps hung on the answer.

Within a day, Louisiana had suspended its primaries for the U.S. House. Within a month, its legislature had erased a majority-Black district. [beat] A district that federal courts had spent two years ordering it to draw.

[pause]

Courts order a district into existence in 2024. Courts ratify its destruction in 2026. And in American law, that sequence is legally coherent.

To see how, you need the whole arc.

Last time, we stated three positions on gerrymandering, each at its strongest. This lecture is the legal record that all three have to live with. And the first thing to know about that record is its shape.

> Partisan claims and racial claims travel on separate legal tracks.

One track carries the claim that a map is too partisan. The other carries the claim that a map discriminates by race. They have separate histories, separate tests, and now separate fates.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to trace both tracks, say what Callais held, and name the doors that are still open.

We will take it in four steps.

First, how federal courts came into districting at all.

Second, the partisan track, which opened and then closed.

Third, the racial track, which stayed open and then narrowed.

And last, the lane that remains, in the state courts, and where the record leaves each of the three positions.

## Into the thicket

For the republic's first 170 years, federal courts refused to touch districting at all. In 1946, Justice Felix Frankfurter gave that refusal its slogan. "Courts ought not to enter this political thicket."

The cost of staying out compounded quietly. Tennessee's legislature, like many others, simply stopped redistricting after 1901. By 1960, urban districts held many times the population of rural ones. And the only people with authority to fix that were the legislators who benefited.

In 1962, a case called Baker v. Carr broke the deadlock. The Court held that a challenge to those unequal districts, under the Equal Protection Clause, was one a court could hear. The lawyer's word for that is justiciable. Keep the word. Whether a claim is justiciable, whether a court can hear it at all, is the question this whole story turns on.

The rule itself followed fast. By 1964 the Court had required equal population, in congressional districts and in both chambers of every state legislature. Chief Justice Warren put it in his most quoted line. "Legislators represent people, not trees or acres."

[pause]

One person, one vote. Within a decade, the problem of unequal district sizes was all but solved.

Now test how far that rule reaches. A state's districts each contain exactly 760,000 people. And yet the minority party, with 47 percent of the votes, takes 70 percent of the seats.

What part of one person, one vote has that state violated? Take a moment.

[long pause]

None of it. One person, one vote polices equal population across districts, and these districts are perfectly equal. A minority of the votes and a majority of the seats, with equal populations, is the gerrymandering problem. It is dilution by arrangement, and not by head-count.

So one person, one vote polices the size of districts, and not their arrangement. Indeed, the rule compels a redrawing after every census. And that redrawing is the very occasion gerrymanderers use.

Arrangement needed doctrine of its own. That doctrine runs on the two tracks.

## The partisan track

Take the partisan track first. Its story has three stages. Justiciable. Standardless. Then closed.

In 1986, the Court held that a claim of partisan gerrymandering is justiciable in principle. [beat] But no majority could say what the test was. And for eighteen years, no plaintiff could win under it.

In 2004 the experiment nearly ended. Four justices would have declared such claims nonjusticiable outright. Justice Kennedy, concurring alone, kept the door ajar. There was no manageable standard yet, he wrote, but perhaps one would emerge.

The political scientists took that invitation seriously. The efficiency gap, partisan symmetry, the simulated neutral maps: the tools from the first lecture were built, in large part, to answer Kennedy.

One more decision from those years matters enormously in 2026. Texas had redrawn its map mid-decade, in 2003, for nakedly partisan reasons. And the Court found mid-decade redistricting itself constitutionally unobjectionable.

Then, in 2019, a case called Rucho closed the door.

The Court reviewed two maps together, one gerrymander from each party, deliberately paired. North Carolina's congressional map. And Maryland's Sixth District.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for a Court divided five to four. Partisan-gerrymandering claims, he wrote, "present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts."

[pause]

Beyond the reach of the federal courts.

Notice what the majority did not deny. It did not deny that the practice is unjust. In its words, "excessive partisanship in districting leads to results that reasonably seem unjust." What it denied was that federal courts could police the practice without choosing among contestable theories of fairness.

Justice Kagan wrote the dissent, for four justices. Lower courts, she wrote, had already applied workable standards, assisted by math. And then: "Of all times to abandon the Court's duty to declare the law, this was not the one." She closed: "With respect but deep sadness, I dissent."

[pause]

> Rucho closed the federal courts to claims of partisan gerrymandering.

But Rucho left three doors open, and they define the strategy of the present day.

State courts may police partisan maps, under state constitutions. Congress may legislate, under Article One, section four. And claims of racial discrimination remained federal business.

That last sentence sounded very different before April 2026.

## The racial track

So turn to the racial track. The law of racial districting runs on two theories, and they point in opposite directions.

The first theory is statutory. It comes from Section Two of the Voting Rights Act, and it is called vote dilution. Since a 1986 case called Gingles, a minority group could compel the drawing of a district where it can elect candidates of its choice. The group had to be geographically compact, politically cohesive, and persistently outvoted by bloc voting.

The second theory is constitutional, and it cuts the other way. In 1993, in a case called Shaw v. Reno, the Court held that sorting voters by race triggers strict scrutiny, when race predominates over traditional districting principles. That holds even when the reasons are benign. Justice O'Connor warned that a bizarre majority-minority district "bears an uncomfortable resemblance to political apartheid."

> Section Two is the sword. Shaw is the shield. They run in opposite directions.

For thirty years, map-drawers have navigated the strait between the two. Draw too few minority districts, and face liability under Section Two. Draw them too race-consciously, and face Shaw.

Let's test that. A state draws a majority-minority district, to stay safe under the Voting Rights Act. A group of voters sues. They say the district itself is unconstitutional racial sorting.

Which body of law does their claim travel under? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The Equal Protection Clause, by way of Shaw. Section Two is the statute the state was complying with. These voters are raising the shield, against a district drawn to satisfy the sword.

Hold on to that posture. It is exactly the posture of Callais.

For a while, the strait seemed to stabilize. In 2023, a case called Milligan surprised nearly everyone. A Court divided five to four, with Roberts writing, reaffirmed Gingles. It ordered Alabama to draw a second district where Black voters could elect their candidate. And in 2024, for the first time in its history, Alabama had two Black members of Congress at the same time.

Then the constitutional theory tilted toward the states. In a 2024 case from South Carolina, the Court held that challengers alleging a racial gerrymander must overcome a presumption of legislative good faith. And where map-drawers say their motive was partisan, not racial, the burden of disentangling the two falls on the challengers.

## Callais

Then came Callais. It began as Milligan's sequel. Louisiana is one-third Black, and its 2022 map had one majority-Black district out of six. Federal courts held that the map likely violated Section Two. So in 2024, the legislature drew a map with a second such district.

Then a group of voters sued, describing themselves as "non-African American voters." The new map, they argued, was itself an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. [beat] The shield, against the sword.

The Court agreed, by six votes to three, with Justice Alito writing. Louisiana's remedial map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. And compliance with Section Two, as the lower courts had interpreted it, could not justify it.

The majority stopped short of striking Section Two down. But it reworked the Gingles framework, in ways that make new vote-dilution wins far harder. Here is the most consequential change. Challengers must now produce evidence supporting "a strong inference that the State intentionally drew its districts [beat] to afford minority voters less opportunity because of their race."

[pause]

A strong inference of intent.

Liability based on effects was the centerpiece of the 1982 amendments to Section Two. Callais narrowed it, toward a test of intent.

Put another way: it was once enough to show what a map did to minority voters. Now challengers must also show what the state meant it to do.

> After Callais, a Section Two claim must support a strong inference of racial intent.

The Court gave a warning with it. In a polarized two-party system, a Section Two claim can be a partisan claim, "repackaged" around Rucho.

The aftermath was immediate. You have already heard Louisiana's part: the primaries suspended, and the second district erased. In Alabama, by early June, the legislature's own 2023 map was restored for the midterms.

And two days before Callais, the Court had already reversed the panel that blocked Texas's mid-decade map of 2025 as a racial gerrymander. In an earlier order in that case, Justice Alito, concurring, had called the impetus for the map "partisan advantage pure and simple."

Let's test what changed. It is 2027. A state is 30 percent Black. Its map has one majority-Black district out of ten. Voting is racially polarized, and the plaintiffs' demographers produce a compact second district.

How would that claim have fared before 2026? And what more does Callais demand? Think it through.

[long pause]

Before 2026, those facts look much like Milligan, and Alabama lost. Compactness, cohesion and bloc voting were the heart of the case. A discriminatory effect was enough.

After Callais, the plaintiffs must also support a strong inference of intentional deprivation, based on race. Their illustrative maps must achieve all the state's legitimate goals, incumbent protection included. And the state may recharacterize its lines as partisan choices, beyond federal review.

Same facts. [beat] Opposite trajectory.

Now put three decisions together: Rucho, the South Carolina case, and Callais. A strategic loop emerges. Call it the Rucho two-step.

Accused of racial gerrymandering, a state can answer that it was partisan, not racial. And partisan gerrymandering is something no federal court may review. Accused under Section Two of diluting minority votes, the state can demand proof of racial intent, with politics as the innocent explanation.

Race and party are nearly collinear in the states where these cases arise. And the doctrine now resolves nearly every ambiguity in the map-drawer's favor.

Those are the mechanics. The genuine disagreement is over what they amount to.

Justice Kagan, in dissent, argues that the majority's "updates" in fact "eviscerate the law."

Defenders of the decision argue that this is exactly what colorblind constitutionalism requires, and that Callais finally ended decades of court-compelled racial sorting.

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## What is left

Rucho left three doors open. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

State courts. Congress. And racial claims.

You have just watched the third door narrow. The second, the power of Congress, stands as it was: no court has narrowed it. So take the first.

With the federal door shut on partisan claims, litigation moved to state constitutions. And it has real victories. In 2018, Pennsylvania's Supreme Court struck the state's congressional map. In 2022, New York's highest court voided a Democratic gerrymander. And Utah's courts enforced the voters' Proposition 4 against the legislature's repeal: the episode we tested last time.

There was a counter-theory, called the Independent State Legislature doctrine. Article One, section four, gives election authority to "the Legislature" of each state. So, in the strong version, state courts and state constitutions cannot constrain the legislature's rules for federal elections at all. Had that theory prevailed, every one of those state rulings would have been void as to congressional maps.

In 2023, in a case called Moore v. Harper, the Court rejected the strong version, six to three. State legislatures act within the ordinary bounds of state constitutional law, and state courts may review them.

But the opinion, by Chief Justice Roberts, kept a federal backstop. State courts may not "transgress the ordinary bounds of judicial review" so as to usurp the legislature's role. That line is deliberately undefined. And it invites a federal second look at an aggressive state ruling.

> The state-court pathway is open, with a yellow light.

So where does this record leave the three positions from last time?

Take the reform advocate first. Three instruments remain: state constitutions; state initiatives, in the roughly half of states that have them; and the power of Congress under Article One, section four. But Rucho closed partisan claims, Callais narrowed racial ones, and Moore put an undefined ceiling over state courts.

> For the reform advocate: judicial leverage is the weakest it has been since 1986.

So the honest reform position now treats litigation as a holding action, and legislation as the theory of change.

The partisan realist and the federalist stand closer together. The Court has largely adopted their jurisdictional map: politics for political questions, with Rucho expressly inviting Congress and the states to act. But the record complicates each position too.

> For the partisan realist: the partisan label now doubles as a racial-claim defense.

That is the Rucho two-step. And Callais complicates the federalist's tidy division, with courts out of partisan claims and in on racial ones.

> For the federalist: the racial lane courts were meant to patrol has narrowed.

So each of them should be able to say whether that outcome vindicates neutral text, or launders discrimination. And each should be able to say what evidence distinguishes the two.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Partisan claims and racial claims travel on separate legal tracks.

[beat]

Two. One person, one vote polices the size of districts, and not their arrangement. Equal districts can still be gerrymandered.

[beat]

Three. The partisan track was justiciable, then standardless, then closed. In 2019, Rucho closed the federal courts to claims of partisan gerrymandering.

[beat]

Four. On the racial track, Section Two is the sword, and Shaw is the shield. After Callais, a Section Two claim must support a strong inference of racial intent.

[beat]

And five. The state-court pathway is open, with a yellow light. Moore v. Harper kept state courts in, and kept a federal backstop over them.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Is Callais rightly decided? On one side stands its colorblind reading of the Reconstruction Amendments. On the other stands Justice Kagan's charge that the majority's updates eviscerate the law.

You may already lean one way. So take the side you find less convincing, and state the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

There is a second open question: whether Congress will ever use the section four power that everyone agrees it has.

From here, the argument moves to the states. The measurement question is settled enough: gerrymanders are detectable. The federal constitutional question is settled for now. What is still open sits at the state level, and the map war of last time shows how open it is.

So go back to Louisiana. A district ordered into existence in 2024, and erased in 2026. [beat] You can now say how the law made both of those coherent. Section Two ordered it. Shaw, by way of Callais, undid it.

Whether the law should work that way is the argument your own position has to survive.
