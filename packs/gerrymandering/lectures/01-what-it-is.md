February 11, 1812. The governor of Massachusetts, Elbridge Gerry, signs a redistricting bill. By the accounts of the time, he personally disliked it.

His party, the Democratic-Republicans, controlled the legislature. It faced a resurgence by its rivals, the Federalists. And it had drawn the state's senate districts to survive that resurgence.

One of those districts curled through Essex County in a long, lumpy arc. In the offices of the Boston Gazette, someone added wings, claws and fangs to its outline. On March 26, the Gazette printed the beast, over a new word. [beat] The Gerrymander.

That spring, the monster did exactly what it was built to do. The Federalists won the governorship, and Gerry himself was turned out. They also won more votes statewide in the senate races. [beat] And the senate went to Gerry's party anyway, by 29 seats to 11.

[pause]

> More votes, fewer seats: that inversion is what the word names.

Not the funny shape. The inversion.

Gerry died in 1814, as James Madison's vice president. His name stayed behind, attached to a practice that is older than the label, and very much alive in the midterms of 2026.

These days the word gets thrown around to mean any election map somebody dislikes. That makes it useless for thinking with. So by the end of this lecture, you will be able to define a gerrymander in mechanical terms, and to measure one. And you will be able to tell it apart from three things that are only called one.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the two moves that every gerrymander is made of.

Second, how to measure the damage, by counting wasted votes.

Third, three things that are called gerrymandering, and aren't.

And last, the three variants: partisan, racial, and incumbent protection.

## Two moves

Start with a definition. Then we will take it apart.

Gerrymandering is the deliberate manipulation of district boundaries, to produce an outcome that neutral line-drawing would not produce. The outcome can be partisan. It can be racial. It can be the protection of incumbents.

In plainer words: somebody moved the lines on purpose, to get a result that neutral lines would not have given.

Strip away the lawsuits and the map-making, and there are only two moves.

The first is packing. You concentrate the other party's voters into a few districts, which they win by overwhelming margins. Every vote past what they needed to win is surplus. It elects no one extra.

The second is cracking. You split the other party's remaining voters across many districts, so that they fall just short in every one. Every vote in a losing cause elects no one at all.

> Packing piles the other side's votes up. Cracking spreads them thin.

Both moves do the same thing by different routes: they waste the other side's votes. Too many in one place, or too few in every place. Meanwhile you spread your own voters as efficiently as you can. Comfortable wins. No blowouts. No losses.

Here is the whole logic in one small electorate. The lesson page has a simulator where you can try this. For now, picture it.

Fifty voters. Thirty of them favor party A, and twenty favor party B. That is 60 percent against 40. We will sort them into five districts of ten voters each, three different ways. Same voters, same houses, same opinions. Only the lines change.

Plan one is the baseline. The districts are compact, and party A's 60 percent of the votes becomes three seats out of five.

Plan two cracks party B. Its twenty voters are spread so thin that every district splits six to four. [beat] Party A takes all five seats. And every one of B's twenty votes is wasted in a loss.

Plan three runs the opposite move, against the majority. Party A's voters are piled into two districts, which it wins overwhelmingly. One of them it wins ten to nothing. [beat] Party B takes the other three. A party with 40 percent of the votes walks away with a majority of the seats.

[pause]

Same fifty people. For party A: three seats, five seats, or two.

And notice what none of this required. [beat] Ugly shapes. The districts in that third plan are reasonably tidy blocks.

> A gerrymander is defined by what it does to votes, not by its shape.

That is why a modern gerrymander can look perfectly innocent. It is drawn with precinct-level data and optimization software, and it can be more durable than anything Gerry's allies could draw by hand.

One more fact sets the stakes. Redistricting runs on a clock. Every state redraws after each census, once every ten years. The party holding the pen when the clock strikes can bank its advantage for a decade.

Let's test the two moves. You are the line-drawer. Your party expects 45 percent of the statewide vote, so the other party is the majority.

Which move do you use on them: packing, or cracking? And why won't the other one work? Take a moment.

[long pause]

You pack them.

Cracking only works against a side you can hold under half, in many districts at once. That is impossible when the other side has the statewide majority. Spread their voters evenly, and you hand them every seat. That was plan two, run against you.

So you do the opposite. You concentrate their surplus into a few overwhelming wins, and you assemble narrow majorities for yourself everywhere else. That was plan three.

## Measuring the waste

So that is how the moves work. Now, can anyone measure them?

For decades, the standard objection to policing gerrymanders was that fairness could not be measured. The modern answer from political science is that wasted votes can be counted.

In 2015, a law professor, Nicholas Stephanopoulos, and a political scientist, Eric McGhee, defined a measure called the efficiency gap. Here is how it works.

A vote is wasted in one of two ways. It is a surplus vote, in a district your side had already won. Or it is any vote at all, in a district your side lost.

Add up each party's wasted votes. Take the difference. Then divide by all the votes cast.

> The efficiency gap: the difference in wasted votes, divided by all votes cast.

Let me run it once. Picture a state with five districts, and 100 voters in each.

Party A wins two districts, with 80 votes apiece. Party B wins the other three, with 65 apiece.

Statewide, party A has 53 percent of the votes. [beat] And it loses, three seats to two.

Now count the waste. Party A wasted 165 votes: large surpluses in its two wins, and every vote in its three losses. Party B wasted 85.

The difference is 80 votes, out of 500 cast. That is an efficiency gap of 16 percent, in party B's favor. Party B turned votes into seats far more efficiently, because the map was built to waste party A's.

How large is too large? Stephanopoulos and McGhee proposed a threshold. Treat a plan as presumptively suspect when the gap passes 8 percent in a state legislature, or two seats in a congressional delegation, and stays there across elections.

Their measure anchored a challenge to Wisconsin's 2011 assembly map, in a case called Whitford. It is now a standard exhibit, in redistricting lawsuits and in journalism alike.

Let's test what the gap is really measuring. Go back to that five-district state, and suppose we undo some of the packing by swapping voters between two districts.

Party A used to win its first district 80 to 20. Now it wins 55 to 45. And a district it was losing becomes a win, 60 to 40.

Does the efficiency gap shrink, or grow? Think it through.

[long pause]

It shrinks, dramatically. In that first district, party A used to waste 30 surplus votes. Now it wastes only 5. And a district full of wasted losing votes has become a win.

So the gap measures one thing: how unequally the map wastes the two sides' votes. It rewards maps where both parties' wins are similarly efficient. [beat] It does not reward maps that look pretty.

The efficiency gap is not the only ruler. Another is called partisan symmetry, from the political scientists Bernard Grofman and Gary King. It asks a hypothetical. If the two parties' vote shares were swapped, would their seat shares swap too?

Now a caution, in two halves. No single measure is decisive, and the Supreme Court ultimately found none of them manageable by judges. That story belongs to the third lecture in this series. [beat] But the empirical claim, that gerrymanders can be detected and measured, is no longer seriously contested.

## What gerrymandering is not

That is what a gerrymander is, and how to measure one. Now the other half: what it is not. Three things are routinely called gerrymandering, and aren't.

First: districts that look weird because the geography is weird. A district that hugs a coastline, follows a river valley, or snakes along a mountain ridge is not compact. But its shape has nothing to do with wasting votes.

Compactness is a useful screening signal, and nothing more. And the reverse matters more. As those fifty voters showed, a tidy-looking map can be a ruthless gerrymander. Shape is neither necessary nor sufficient.

Second: lopsided outcomes that political geography produces without anyone's help.

In a 2013 paper, two researchers, Jowei Chen and Jonathan Rodden, ran thousands of computer-simulated districting plans for Florida. The plans had no intent behind them at all. [beat] Most of them favored Republicans anyway.

Why? Because Democrats cluster so densely in cities that they pack themselves.

This has been called the unintentional gerrymander. And it means that a bare mismatch between seats and votes is not proof of foul play.

So how do you tell geography from intent? The gold standard now is a comparison. You take the map that was enacted. You set it beside a whole ensemble of simulated neutral maps. And you ask one question: is the real map an outlier?

Many challenged maps are. They sit beyond even the most partisan of the simulated alternatives. That is the rebuttal to treating geography as a universal alibi.

> Lopsided proves nothing. An extreme outlier against neutral maps is the modern smoking gun.

Third: districts drawn to comply with the Voting Rights Act.

Section Two of that Act has long required states, under conditions the Supreme Court set out in 1986, to draw certain districts. Where a minority community is geographically compact and politically cohesive, the district must let that community elect the candidates of its choice. It is called a majority-minority district.

A district drawn to satisfy that mandate is a remedy for vote dilution, written into a statute. It is not a partisan weapon.

Though the same map can be both. And there is a line here that has been litigated for thirty years. On one side, the majority-minority district that the Voting Rights Act requires. On the other, the racial sort that the Equal Protection Clause forbids. The Supreme Court redrew that line again in 2026, in a case called Louisiana v. Callais. The third lecture takes it up in full.

## Three variants

So far I have talked as if every gerrymander were about party. It isn't. There are three variants, and they are worth keeping separate.

The first is partisan gerrymandering. It advantages a party. It is the 1812 original, and it is behind the headline modern cases. Here are two, one from each party.

North Carolina, 2016. The congressional map produced ten Republicans and three Democrats, on about 53 percent of the statewide vote. And the map's author, Representative David Lewis, said why, into a microphone.

"I propose that we draw the maps to give a partisan advantage to 10 Republicans and 3 Democrats [beat] because I do not believe it's possible to draw a map with 11 Republicans and 2 Democrats."

[pause]

Maryland, 2011. Democrats did the mirror image. They redrew the Sixth District to unseat a Republican who had held it for twenty years. The former governor, Martin O'Malley, later testified that flipping the seat "was certainly my hope, and it was part of my intent."

[pause]

The second variant is racial gerrymandering. It dilutes the votes of a racial group, or, in one line of cases, sorts voters by race to excess. It is governed by different law: the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, and the Voting Rights Act. And it can still be challenged in federal court, though on terms the Supreme Court narrowed sharply in 2026.

Here is the complication. In most of the states involved, Black and Latino voters vote heavily Democratic. So nearly every racial case has a partisan shadow. Disentangling race from party has become the central fight over evidence.

The third variant is incumbent protection, also called a bipartisan gerrymander. Here both parties' voters are packed into safe seats, so that sitting legislators of each party stop facing competitive elections. In 1973, the Supreme Court blessed a Connecticut plan of exactly this kind, a plan presented as political fairness.

It is rarely litigated. It is arguably the most common variant. And it corrodes in a different way. It does not skew which party wins. It removes the voters' practical ability to fire anyone. Real competition shifts to low-turnout party primaries, and incumbency is rewarded for its own sake.

Now the difficulty that will follow us through the next two lectures.

> One map can be partisan, racial and incumbent-protecting, all at once.

The law treats those three under different constitutional theories. The practice does not keep them apart. That is why the doctrine can feel so misaligned with what the map-drawers do.

One last test, and it draws on something from earlier. Here is a state.

The majority party wins 55 percent of the votes. It takes 70 percent of the seats. The districts are compact, and they follow county lines. Then a computer generates 50,000 neutral maps. The real map gives the majority party more seats than 49,900 of them.

Gerrymander, or geography? Think back to the test for telling them apart.

[long pause]

Almost certainly a gerrymander. And the compact shapes are irrelevant.

The comparison with neutral maps is the test built for exactly this question. Political geography is already baked into those 50,000 maps. So when the real map lands in the top fraction of one percent, the geography cannot be doing the work. [beat] The lines are.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull one thing back from earlier. I gave you two moves, and two ways a vote is wasted. Can you still name the moves, and say which kind of waste each one makes?

[long pause]

Packing makes surplus votes: too many, in districts already won. Cracking makes losing votes: too few, everywhere else.

So. Five things to keep.

One. A gerrymander is defined by what it does to votes, not by its shape. More votes, fewer seats: that inversion is what the word names.

[beat]

Two. There are only two moves. Packing piles the other side's votes up. Cracking spreads them thin. Both waste votes.

[beat]

Three. The waste can be counted. The efficiency gap is the difference in wasted votes, divided by all votes cast. No single measure is decisive, but gerrymanders can be detected and measured.

[beat]

Four. Three things are not gerrymanders: a strange shape, a lopsided result that geography produced, and a majority-minority district drawn as a Voting Rights Act remedy. Lopsided proves nothing. An extreme outlier against neutral maps is the modern smoking gun.

[beat]

And five. Three variants: partisan, racial, and incumbent protection. One map can be all three at once.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has made no argument about what, if anything, should be done. You have the mechanics. What remains contested is everything downstream. Is the practice a democratic emergency, or politics as usual? Who should hold the pen? And can any institution be trusted to police it?

You may already lean one way. So try something before you hear the arguments. Take the answer you find less convincing, emergency or politics as usual, and state the best case for it.
