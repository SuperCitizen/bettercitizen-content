August 2025. The Texas legislature redraws the state's congressional map. It does so four years early, in open pursuit of five more Republican seats.

Two months later, California's voters answer. They pass a measure called Proposition 50, by roughly 64 percent to 36. It shelves the map drawn by the state's celebrated independent commission. In its place goes a map drawn by the legislature: a counter-gerrymander, worth roughly five Democratic seats.

[beat]

Missouri and North Carolina follow Texas. Ohio's bipartisan commission cuts a deal, seven votes to none. And Indiana's Republican senate refuses the White House outright, 31 to 19.

By the filing deadlines of spring 2026, the battlefield for the U.S. House has a new shape.

> Between censuses, on purpose, both parties redrew congressional maps.

Call it the map war.

Last time, we took the gerrymander apart, and learned how to measure one. We stopped short of the argument: what, if anything, should be done about it?

Three positions are in play on that question. Each of them has to explain the map war. [beat] And each of them thinks the map war proves its point.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to state all three, each as its smartest adherent would. That way you can engage the position, and not a caricature of it.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the reform advocate's case. Second, the partisan realist's. Third, the federalist's.

I will build all three the same way. The claim. The value underneath it. The evidence. And then a test, at the point where the case is hardest to hold.

And last, one episode that all three claim as a win, and a sorting of what is settled from what is not.

## The reform advocate's case

Start with the reform advocate.

The reform advocate argues that partisan gerrymandering severs the connection between votes and seats. Suppose the party that wins fewer votes controls more seats, and gets there by drawing lines, not by persuading voters. Then democratic accountability fails at the root.

Why at the root? Because voters hold one instrument for correcting a government: the election. And that instrument has been rigged in advance, against correction.

What is the reform advocate trying to protect? Procedural integrity. Elections exist to translate preferences into representation. A process that systematically inverts that translation is not one the voters consented to, whoever benefits this cycle.

> The reform advocate's case: gerrymandering severs the connection between votes and seats.

Put another way, the complaint is about the process, and not about which party wins.

What does the reform advocate point to? Three things.

First, durability. Take Wisconsin's assembly map of 2011. The trial record in the Whitford case found an efficiency gap above 13 percent in 2012. And the advantage held, through wave elections in both directions.

Second, intent. It was once deniable. Now it is routinely on the record. North Carolina's David Lewis announced a "partisan advantage to 10 Republicans and 3 Democrats." Maryland's Martin O'Malley conceded, under oath, that flipping a district "was certainly my hope, and it was part of my intent."

Third, the remedy has a track record. The Brennan Center assessed the maps drawn in 2021 and 2022. Plans drawn by commissions largely preserved competitive districts, and showed less partisan bias. Legislatures under one-party control sharply reduced competition.

And voters may choose that remedy themselves. In 2015, in a case about Arizona's commission, the Supreme Court held, five to four, that voters may take the pen away from legislators by ballot initiative.

[pause]

Now test this case where it is hardest. California's commission was the model reform. And Proposition 50 suspended its map, by a 64 percent popular vote, to enact a deliberate partisan gerrymander.

What does that do to the reform case? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It complicates one premise, and it illustrates another.

It complicates the premise that a commission, once adopted, takes redistricting out of partisan politics. California's voters re-politicized the map the moment the national stakes got high enough. A serious reformer concedes this: unilateral procedural virtue can amount to unilateral disarmament. And the entry that stings is that the reformers' own side proved willing to gerrymander.

But it illustrates a deeper premise. Redistricting is a collective-action problem. Each state's reformers are safe only if rival states are constrained too. So the reform advocate reads the map war as the strongest evidence yet that unilateral virtue fails. And most reform advocates now argue for a national standard: legislation by Congress, under Article One, section four. No state, they argue, can safely stop alone.

Note also what Proposition 50 was not. It was a temporary override, approved by voters, with a return to the commission built in after the 2030 census. Voters in the targeted districts of Texas and Missouri gave no such consent.

A serious reformer concedes one thing more, about geography. The researchers Chen and Rodden found that Democrats' clustering in cities produces pro-Republican maps with no intent at all. So some of the asymmetry that reformers decry is geography, not malice. The reformer's best response is that this strengthens the case for institutional design. If single-member districts mis-translate votes even when drawn honestly, the answer is honest drawing, plus attention to how votes become seats. [beat] Not a shrug.

And what would prove the reform case wrong? Evidence that commission states come out statistically indistinguishable from legislature states, once geography is controlled for. That would mean the fix changes who draws, and not what gets drawn.

## The partisan realist's case

That is the first case. Now the second: the partisan realist.

The partisan realist argues that redistricting has always been political, and that no neutral alternative is hiding behind better institutions. An independent commission, on this view, is a political body one level removed. Its membership rules, its selection lotteries and its tie-breaking procedures were all negotiated by partisans who could count.

So the honest posture is to fight the map fight in the open, and to win elections. And it is to stop pretending that a question of who gets what has a technocratic answer.

What is the partisan realist trying to protect? Realism about institutions. Declaring an answer neutral does not remove the politics. It relocates the politics into the appointment of the neutrals, where voters cannot see it.

> The partisan realist's case: no neutral alternative exists. Maps follow power.

Put another way, you can move the politics. You cannot take it out.

What does the partisan realist point to? Three things.

First, history. The gerrymander is older than the Bill of Rights. In 1788, Patrick Henry's allies in Virginia drew the congressional district of his rival, James Madison. They folded in Antifederalist counties, and forced Madison to face James Monroe. The author of the Bill of Rights had to out-campaign a gerrymander.

Second, the Supreme Court. In 2019, in a case called Rucho, Chief Justice Roberts wrote that partisan-fairness claims "present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts." And the reason matters. To decide how much partisan advantage is too much, a court needs a baseline. Proportionality? Symmetry? Competitiveness? The Constitution nowhere supplies one.

Think of the rulers from last time: the efficiency gap, and partisan symmetry. To the realist, those are rival definitions of fairness. And choosing among them is itself a political act.

Third, the map war. The partisan realist reads it as exhibit A. Texas redrew because it could. California's electorate, celebrated for reform, voted 64 to 36 to gerrymander right back. Ohio's bipartisan commission cut its deal because both parties preferred a negotiated map to a constitutional stalemate. Everyone behaved as the realist predicts. Maps follow power.

[pause]

Now test this case where it is hardest. The realist's advice to the losing side is to win elections, and then change the map.

Set that advice beside what a gerrymander does. What goes wrong? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The advice is circular. The map itself decides who wins elections. In Wisconsin, the assembly majority survived a statewide majority of votes for Democrats.

A serious realist concedes this: the mechanism of accountability they invoke is the very thing being rigged.

And the map war has entries that sting. Indiana's senate refused the White House, 31 to 19. The realist reads that as a political judgment about backlash, and not as principle. But backlash keeps behaving like a real political force. Public revulsion at extreme maps passed Proposition 4 in Utah. It passed the commission initiatives in Michigan, Colorado and Arizona. The realist's account has to absorb that force, and cannot dismiss it.

And what would prove the realist case wrong? A fairness standard that judges can manage across election cycles, and that commands acceptance across party lines. That means outcomes that voters of both parties treat as legitimate, even when their own side loses seats. If that existed, and worked, "there is no neutral answer" would be false.

## The federalist's case

Now the third case: the federalist view, which holds that the states decide.

The federalist argues from the text of the Constitution. Article One, section four, assigns the rules for congressional elections to the legislature of each state, with a power in Congress to revise them. It gives no role to federal courts.

So when federal judges police maps, the federalist argues, that is a separation-of-powers wrong, even if gerrymandering is a civic wrong. The constitutional remedies are state politics and federal legislation, which the text does authorize.

What is the federalist trying to protect? Fidelity to the constitutional allocation of power. The Framers placed election regulation with the most politically accountable bodies, subject to a national check. A legislative check. [beat] Not a judicial one.

> The federalist's case: the Constitution names state legislatures and Congress, not federal courts.

Put another way, the question is not only whether gerrymandering is wrong. It is who was given the job of answering it.

What does the federalist point to? Three things.

First, the text itself. Here it is. Listen for who is named.

"The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof; [beat] but the Congress may at any time by Law make or alter such Regulations."

[pause]

The legislature of each state, and then Congress. For the federalist, what matters is who is missing. No court is named.

Second, the Rucho decision again. It vindicated the judicial-restraint half of this reading.

Third, the map war, with the states as laboratories. The federalist reads it as the system the Framers built, visibly processing a conflict. Ohio's voters had written a process into their state constitution, and it forced a bipartisan map without a single federal judge. California's voters used the lawmaking power that the Arizona case recognized, and used it in both directions. They created the commission, and in 2025 they suspended it. And the power of Congress under section four sits untouched.

[pause]

Now test this case where it is hardest. The clean version of the federalist view is: courts, out of districting.

But the Reconstruction Amendments and the Voting Rights Act are federal law. And federal courts have policed racial districting for sixty years.

What does that do to the clean version? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It makes the clean version unavailable. A serious federalist concedes this. The defensible position is narrower: courts out of partisan districting, and in on racial districting. And the awkwardness is that the same map is usually both.

There is a second sore spot, in that Arizona case. Four justices, Roberts among them, took "the Legislature" to mean the legislature, and not the initiative process. So the federalist's strict reading of the text cuts against the most successful reform mechanism at the state level.

And the entry that stings is Texas. Those laboratories are experimenting on other states' congressional delegations. The Texans' map was answered, not by Texans, but by Californians. That is a national prisoner's dilemma, and state-by-state accountability cannot reach it.

And what would prove the federalist case wrong? Sustained evidence that state-level processes cannot correct entrenchment: legislatures ignoring or repealing voter initiatives without consequence, and state courts shut out. And with it, evidence that Congress is structurally incapable of using its section four power. At that point, "the constitutional design handles this" would be false.

## One episode, three readings

Three cases. And one pattern across them.

> Every position claims the map war as vindication. Each has an entry that stings.

Here is a last test, and it needs all three cases at once. So call them back to mind first: the reform advocate's claim, the partisan realist's, and the federalist's.

[beat]

Now the episode. In Utah, voters passed an anti-gerrymandering initiative, Proposition 4. The legislature repealed it, and drew its own map. Then Utah's courts overruled the legislature, and enforced the initiative. The saga ended in 2026, with a map ordered by a court.

Which of the three positions can claim that episode as a win? And on what grounds? Think it through.

[long pause]

All three try. That is what makes it a good test.

The reform advocate claims it. Neutral criteria, adopted by voters, ultimately bound the politicians.

The federalist claims it. The correction ran entirely through state institutions: the initiative, the state constitution, the state courts. No federal judge was involved. That is the design, working.

And the partisan realist claims it. The realist notes who litigated, and who appealed. And the realist notes the practical effect of the "neutral" map: a new Democratic-leaning seat in Salt Lake County. Politics relocated, not removed.

[pause]

Your judgment about which of those readings is most persuasive is not far from your position on the whole issue.

So try one more thing. Take the position you find least convincing of the three. State the best case for it, the way its smartest adherent would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Between censuses, on purpose, both parties redrew congressional maps. Every position has to explain that.

[beat]

Two. The reform advocate's case: gerrymandering severs the connection between votes and seats. The value is procedural integrity. The concession is California: the model commission, suspended by the voters who created it.

[beat]

Three. The partisan realist's case: no neutral alternative exists, and maps follow power. The value is realism about institutions. The concession is that the advice to win elections is circular, when the map decides who wins.

[beat]

Four. The federalist's case: the Constitution names state legislatures and Congress, not federal courts. The value is fidelity to the constitutional allocation of power. The concession is the racial cases, where federal law brings the courts back in.

[beat]

And five: the line between what is settled and what is not.

Three things are settled. Here is the first.

> Settled: partisan gerrymandering exists, and its distortions are measurable and durable.

It is often documented in the drawers' own words. The second: in the 2021 round, maps drawn by commissions showed less partisan bias than maps drawn by legislatures. And the third: redrawing mid-decade is legally available, and now practiced by both parties.

Here is what is not settled. Three questions, of three different kinds.

One is empirical. How much of the asymmetry is intent, and how much is geography? And do the advantages of commissions survive the pressures that Proposition 50 revealed?

One is constitutional. The question is no longer whether federal courts will hear partisan claims. Rucho closed that. It is how far state constitutions reach, and how much of the pathway for racial claims survives a 2026 decision called Callais.

And one is about values. Does procedural integrity, or political realism, better describe what elections are for? And is "neutral rules" a coherent category at all?

That last question is contested, and the evidence cannot settle it.

[pause]

The next lecture supplies the legal record. How the federal courts came to shut their doors to partisan claims. What the Callais decision did to racial ones. And how far a state constitution can reach.

For now, go back to where we started. Texas draws, and California answers. [beat] You can now watch that with three sets of eyes.

And whichever case you come to hold, the mark of holding it well is the same. You can say what evidence supports it. [beat] And you can name what would change your mind.
