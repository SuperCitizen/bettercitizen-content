## A district ordered, then erased

On April 29, 2026, the Supreme Court decided Louisiana v. Callais. Within a day, Louisiana had suspended its House primaries. Within a month, its legislature had erased a majority-Black district that federal courts had spent two years ordering it to draw.

- Louisiana v. Callais: April 29, 2026

To see how that sequence is legally coherent, you need the two tracks the law runs on.

> Partisan claims and racial claims travel on separate legal tracks.

## Equal is not enough

In the 1960s the Court required districts of equal population: one person, one vote. That solved the problem of unequal district sizes. It does not reach a map whose districts are perfectly equal, and whose lines still turn a minority of votes into a majority of seats.

- One person, one vote: equal population
- Equal-population gerrymanders pass untouched

## The partisan track, closed

For years the Court held that partisan claims could be heard, and no majority could say what the test was. In 2019, in Rucho v. Common Cause, it closed the door, five to four. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that such claims "present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts." The dissent answered that lower courts had already applied workable standards.

- Rucho, 2019: 5 to 4

Rucho left other doors open. State courts may police partisan maps under state constitutions, and Congress may legislate.

- Still open: state courts, Congress

> Rucho: no federal court will hear a partisan gerrymandering claim.

## The racial track, narrowed

Under section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, a minority group that is compact, cohesive and persistently outvoted could compel a district where it can elect candidates of its choice. In 2023 the Court reaffirmed that, five to four. In Callais, six to three, it stopped short of striking section 2 down. But challengers must now support a strong inference that the state intentionally drew its districts to give minority voters less opportunity because of their race.

- 2023: reaffirmed, 5 to 4
- Callais, 2026: 6 to 3

> Callais kept section 2, and moved it toward a test of intent.

## Loophole, or the Constitution?

Critics argue that the cases now form a loop. Accused of a racial gerrymander, a state can answer that it was partisan, and no federal court may review a partisan one. Defenders argue that this is what a colorblind Constitution requires, and that Callais ended decades of court-compelled racial sorting.

- Critics: a loop favoring the map-drawer
- Defenders: a colorblind Constitution

The lesson does not settle that argument. It follows both tracks case by case, the aftermath in Louisiana, Alabama and Texas, and the state-court path that remains open.
