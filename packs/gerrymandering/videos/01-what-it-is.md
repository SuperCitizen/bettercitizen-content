## The monster of 1812

In 1812, Governor Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts signed a redistricting bill. One senate district curled through Essex County in a long, lumpy arc. The Boston Gazette added wings, claws and fangs to its outline, and printed the beast over a new word: the Gerry-mander.

That spring the Federalists won more votes statewide in senate races. The senate went to Gerry's party anyway, by 29 seats to 11.

- More votes statewide
- Lost the senate, 29 seats to 11

> More votes, fewer seats: that is what the word names.

## Two moves

Strip away the maps and there are only two moves. Packing concentrates the other party's voters into a few districts they win by overwhelming margins. Cracking splits the rest across many districts where they fall just short. Both moves waste the other side's votes.

- Packing: a few overwhelming wins
- Cracking: many narrow losses

Take fifty voters, 30 for party A and 20 for party B, in five districts of ten. Crack B so that every district splits six to four, and A sweeps all five seats. Pack A into two districts instead, and B takes the other three: a majority of the seats, on 40 percent of the votes.

- Crack B: A wins 5 of 5
- Pack A: B wins 3 of 5

> Same voters, same opinions. The lines change the delegation.

## Not the shape

None of this requires ugly shapes. The packed plan's districts are tidy blocks. And a district that hugs a coastline or follows a river valley is an odd shape for reasons that have nothing to do with wasting votes.

- Shape: neither necessary nor sufficient

> A gerrymander is defined by what it does to votes, not by its shape.

## The geography problem

A lopsided result is not proof either. In a 2013 paper, Jowei Chen and Jonathan Rodden ran thousands of computer-simulated plans for Florida, drawn with no intent at all. Most of them favored Republicans anyway, because Democrats cluster so densely in cities that they pack themselves.

- Thousands of intent-free maps
- Most favored Republicans anyway

So the test now is to compare an enacted map against simulated neutral maps, and ask whether it is an outlier. Many challenged maps are.

- The test: an outlier among neutral maps

## What the lesson adds

The lesson has a lab where one electorate is drawn three ways, and the arithmetic of the efficiency gap, which counts wasted votes. It separates three variants: partisan, racial and incumbent-protection. Whether anything should be done about any of it is contested, and that is the next lesson.
