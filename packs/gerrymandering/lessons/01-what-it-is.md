# What gerrymandering is — and what it isn't

The word gets thrown around as a synonym for "election map I dislike," which makes it useless for thinking with. Gerrymandering has a specific mechanical meaning. Start there.

## The 1812 origin (the easy part)

Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry signed a redistricting bill that produced a state senate district shaped like a salamander. A Boston Gazette cartoonist drew it as a winged dragon and labeled it the "Gerry-mander." The name stuck. The practice — drawing district lines to advantage the party in control of drawing them — predates the term by several decades and was already commonplace in the early Republic. Source: standard accounts in Cain (2015), summarized by the Brennan Center.

## The mechanical definition

Gerrymandering is the **deliberate manipulation of district boundaries to produce partisan, racial, or incumbent-protection outcomes that diverge from what would emerge from neutral line-drawing**. Two moves do most of the work:

- **Packing.** Concentrate the opposing party's voters into a small number of districts where they win by enormous margins. Their votes "pack" into safe seats; the rest of the map tilts toward the line-drawers.
- **Cracking.** Split the opposing party's voters across multiple districts so they're a minority in each. Their votes "crack" into futile losses everywhere they appear.

Both moves "waste" the opposing party's votes. Packed votes pile up beyond the threshold needed to win; cracked votes fall short of the threshold by design. The Stephanopoulos-McGhee "efficiency gap" measures exactly this — the difference in wasted votes between the two parties — and is the most-cited quantitative definition courts and political scientists use.

## What gerrymandering isn't

Three things are often called gerrymandering and aren't:

1. **Districts that look weird because of geography.** A district that hugs a coastline or follows a river isn't gerrymandered just because the shape is non-compact. Compactness is one signal among many; on its own it proves nothing.
2. **Districts that produce lopsided partisan outcomes.** A state where one party wins 55% of the vote and 70% of the seats may be gerrymandered, or it may reflect the geographic clustering of partisans in cities versus exurbs (a "natural gerrymander"). The map is suspect; the outcome alone is not the proof.
3. **Districts drawn to comply with the Voting Rights Act.** The VRA's Section 2 *requires* states to draw majority-minority districts in some circumstances. A district drawn to satisfy that requirement is doing the opposite of partisan gerrymandering — it's a remedy for under-representation, not a tool of partisan advantage. (The legal terrain here is contested; we'll return to it.)

## Three variants worth distinguishing

- **Partisan.** Drawn to advantage one party — the Massachusetts 1812 case, North Carolina 2016, Maryland 2011. The *Rucho* case (2019) is the SCOTUS decision that ruled partisan gerrymandering claims non-justiciable in federal court.
- **Racial.** Drawn to dilute the voting power of a racial minority. Subject to strict scrutiny under the 14th Amendment and the VRA's Section 2. *Allen v. Milligan* (2023) reaffirmed that racial-vote-dilution claims remain justiciable.
- **Incumbent-protection.** Drawn to pack incumbents of both parties into safer districts, reducing competition. Bipartisan by design; rarely litigated; arguably the most common variant.

The first is partisan. The second is constitutional. The third is structural. They're often confused because the same map can do all three at once.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives that this pack will steel-man in the next lesson:

- **Reform advocates** argue gerrymandering is corrosive to democratic legitimacy regardless of who's doing it, and propose independent commissions or proportional methods as remedies.
- **Partisan realists** argue redistricting is inherently political, has always been so, and that "neutral" line-drawing is a fiction that hides whichever party benefits from the rules nominally calling themselves neutral.
- **Federalists** argue the Constitution explicitly assigns redistricting authority to state legislatures (Art. I §4) and that judicial intervention — federal *or* state — substitutes one set of partisan judgments for another.

A useful position takes one of these views explicitly, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. The next lesson sharpens each.
