# The strongest case for and against

Begin with the finding that embarrasses everyone's favorite story. In the largest study of federal lobbying ever conducted, Frank Baumgartner, Jeffrey Berry, Marie Hojnacki, David Kimball, and Beth Leech tracked 98 randomly sampled policy issues across years of fighting — interviewing the lobbyists and staffers on every side — and published the results as *Lobbying and Policy Change: Who Wins, Who Loses, and Why* (2009). The side with more money won only slightly more often than the side with less. Differences in resources explained under five percent of the variance in outcomes. On roughly 60 percent of the issues, *nothing happened at all* — the status quo won. If lobbying were a vending machine — insert dollars, receive policy — none of that would be true. So the real debate is not "does money buy laws," cartoon-style. It is about what lobbying *does* do — who gets heard, what never reaches the agenda, and whether a system can be corrupt in structure even when almost no one is bribed. On that ground, three serious positions compete. Here is each at its strongest.

## The empirical ground all three camps stand on

Four findings from Lesson 1 set the table, and it pays to have them crisp. First, Olson: concentrated interests organize and diffuse ones don't, so the pressure system over-represents producers over consumers, the organized over the unorganized — by arithmetic, not conspiracy. Second, Schattschneider's accent: Lee Drutman's accounting finds business interests spend about $34 on lobbying for every $1 spent by labor unions and public-interest groups combined, and 95 of the 100 biggest lobbying spenders are business organizations. Third, Baumgartner: that lopsided chorus mostly *fails to change policy* — the status quo is heavily favored, and when change comes it usually requires new arguments or new coalitions, not just new money. Fourth, Hall and Deardorff's "Lobbying as Legislative Subsidy" (2006): most lobbying is not persuasion of enemies or purchase of friends but a *gift of capacity to allies* — research, draft bill text, talking points, whip counts — that lets an overstretched, like-minded legislator do more of what both already wanted. Each camp accepts these findings. Each draws a different moral.

## 1. The restriction-first case

Core claim: the scale and structure of modern lobbying are incompatible with democratic legitimacy, and only enforceable limits on access — not more disclosure — can fix it.

The intellectual anchor is Lawrence Lessig's *Republic, Lost* (2011). Lessig concedes the point the vending-machine story misses: quid-pro-quo bribery is rare. His claim is that the system is corrupt in a different, older sense — **dependence corruption**. The Framers designed a Congress "dependent on the people alone"; the modern Congress is dependent on funders, on lobbyist-supplied expertise (Hall and Deardorff's subsidy, read as a dependency relationship), and on the prospect of post-government careers in the influence industry. No individual transaction is illegal; the *economy of influence* as a whole bends outcomes toward those who can pay for the subsidy. On this reading, the revolving-door data from Lesson 1 — a majority of departing members who take outside jobs landing in the influence business, about half of registered lobbyists being former officials — is not a jobs statistic. It is the mechanism by which legislators' incentives are shaped *while they still hold office*.

The restriction-first program follows: lifetime or five-to-ten-year lobbying bans for former members (against the current two years for senators, one for House members); bans on registered lobbyists making or bundling campaign contributions to officials they lobby; closing the 20-percent loophole so "strategic advisers" register like everyone else; and serious penalties with a real enforcement agency behind them.

What would change this position: evidence that restrictions chill the petition right in practice — for instance, that broad registration rules sweep in citizen volunteers and small nonprofits — or evidence that the subsidy lobbyists provide is, on net, informational value Congress cannot replace.

## 2. The transparency-only case

Core claim: lobbying is the [[term:first-amendment]] petition clause in action; the constitutional and practical costs of restricting it are higher than reformers admit; the right regime is sunlight plus enforcement.

Start with the text: "the right of the people... to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Paid, professional petitioning is still petitioning — just as the right to counsel includes hired counsel. The Supreme Court has never upheld a flat restriction on lobbying activity; what it upheld in *United States v. Harriss* (1954) was *disclosure*, and even that survived only after the Court narrowed the statute to direct contacts. *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) treats political contributions as protected speech, which puts proposals to ban lobbyists' contributions on contested constitutional ground — the same [[term:campaign-finance]] logic the Court later extended to independent corporate spending in [[term:citizens-united]]. Bradley Smith's *Unfree Speech* (2001) supplies the broader theory: attempts to ration political influence inevitably empower incumbents and insiders to write rules against challengers and gadflies, and the cure becomes the disease.

The transparency advocate also has an empirical brief, not just a legal one. Baumgartner's findings cut their way: if money explains under five percent of outcomes, the corruption story is overdrawn, and draconian remedies are disproportionate to the documented harm. Hall and Deardorff's subsidy model cuts their way too: lobbying *transfers real information and analytical capacity* to a Congress that has gutted its own (a point the structural camp will pick up). Strip the subsidy and you get a less informed legislature, not a purer one. And the revolving door, on this view, is the labor market for policy expertise working — tighten it into a lifetime ban and talented people simply never enter public service. The honest reform agenda is to make the existing system visible and enforced: close the 20-percent loophole (transparency advocates favor *broader disclosure* even while opposing restrictions), fund LDA and FARA enforcement, and let voters punish what they see.

What would change this position: evidence that disclosure has no disciplining effect — that voters and journalists cannot or do not use it — or a demonstrated restriction design that blocks dependence-style influence without touching legitimate petitioning.

## 3. The structural-reform centrist case

Core claim: both other camps aim at lobbying when the problem is the *imbalance of capacity* around it. Don't shrink the influence industry; rebuild the institutions and counterweights that make a legislature hard to capture.

The centrist starts from a fact the subsidy model makes vivid: lobbyists are powerful partly because Congress chose to be weak. Congress abolished its Office of Technology Assessment in 1995 and has let committee staff and member budgets erode for decades; when the in-house experts left, the lobby filled the vacuum at its own price. If legislators depend on Drutman's 34:1 chorus for their information, the fix is to restore independent capacity — bigger, better-paid committee staffs, a revived OTA, stronger Congressional Research Service and budget-office support — so the subsidy becomes one input among several rather than the only analysis in the room. (Drutman himself, whose data powers the restrictionist case, lands here: his prescription is congressional capacity more than lobbying bans.)

The second structural move is on the demand side of Olson's problem: if diffuse interests can't self-organize, subsidize their organization. Public matching funds and democracy-voucher programs (New York City's multiple-match system; Seattle's vouchers) dilute the fundraising channel of dependence. Administrative-law tools — agencies obliged to seek out affected-but-unorganized voices in rulemaking — counter the bias of who shows up. None of these restricts anyone's petition rights, which is the centrist's quiet constitutional advantage: everything on this list survives *Buckley* untouched.

What would change this position: evidence that capacity-building doesn't actually reduce influence asymmetries (legislators with bigger staffs might just process more lobbying), or that the agenda is politically utopian — a Congress that defunded its own expertise must vote to rebuild it.

## Where the three camps actually disagree

| | Restriction-first | Transparency-only | Structural-reform |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diagnosis | Dependence corruption (Lessig) | Information problem, overstated harm | Capacity imbalance |
| Reads Baumgartner as | Bias lives in the agenda, not outcomes | Proof the corruption story is overdrawn | Status-quo bias = institutional weakness |
| Reads Hall & Deardorff as | Dependency mechanism | Valuable information transfer | Symptom of gutted in-house capacity |
| Core remedy | Bans: revolving door, lobbyist money | Disclosure + enforcement | Staff, OTA, public financing |
| Constitutional exposure | High (*Buckley*, petition clause) | None | Low |

Read the table down its middle row: the deepest divide is not over facts but over which fact is *load-bearing*. All three camps accept the same studies; they disagree about whether the system's sin lives in outcomes, agendas, or institutions.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled, or close to it:** disclosed lobbying has grown enormously since the 1970s and set records in 2024 and 2025; the business-versus-everyone asymmetry is large (34:1); the revolving door is a structural career path; documented quid-pro-quo bribery is rare; resources alone are weak predictors of policy outcomes.

**Genuinely contested:** whether dependence corruption is real corruption or a metaphor stretched past its evidence; whether disclosure disciplines anyone in a fragmented media environment; whether tighter revolving-door bans would drain expertise from government or cleanse its incentives; whether capacity-building is achievable and sufficient. A defensible position picks one frame, says why, and names the evidence that would dislodge it — Lesson 3 maps which of these arguments is actually live in current policy fights.
