# The current policy landscape

Here is a ritual nearly as reliable as the inaugural address: within days of taking office, a new president signs an ethics executive order binding appointees to lobbying-related pledges — and within days of leaving, or shortly after, the pledges dissolve. Bill Clinton imposed a five-year lobbying ban on senior appointees in 1993, then revoked it in his final month. Barack Obama's Executive Order 13490 (2009) barred appointees who had recently been registered lobbyists from the agencies and issues they had lobbied; Donald Trump's EO 13770 (2017) imposed its own five-year pledge, which he revoked on January 19, 2021 — his next-to-last day; Joe Biden's EO 13989 (2021) rebuilt a version the following afternoon. Each order was announced as a draining of the swamp. Each bound only one administration's people, only as long as the administration chose. That whiplash is the essential fact about the modern lobbying-policy landscape: the durable rules are the statutes — the LDA of 1995 and HLOGA of 2007 — and almost everything since has been reversible decoration at the margins.

## The statutory floor and the executive overlay

Think of the regime in two layers. The **statutory floor** is what Lessons 1 and 2 described: LDA registration and quarterly disclosure (thresholds inflation-indexed every four years — $3,500 in quarterly income per client for firms and $16,000 in quarterly expenses for in-house operations as of January 1, 2025, with the next adjustment due in 2029), HLOGA's gift bans and cooling-off periods, FARA for foreign principals, and the criminal post-employment rules of 18 U.S.C. §207. Congress has not significantly amended this floor since 2007. Every spending record of the past decade — including 2025's $5.08 billion — has been set under rules written before the iPhone had an app store.

The **executive overlay** is whatever the sitting administration adds for its own appointees: bans on hiring registered lobbyists, recusal requirements covering former clients and employers, pledges not to lobby the agency (or the administration) for some years after leaving. These pledges can be broader than statute — Obama's and Biden's both restricted "golden door" entries *into* government, which no statute does — but they share three weaknesses. They cover only executive appointees, never Congress. They are enforced, if at all, by the administration that wrote them. And they evaporate at the next inauguration, or sooner by waiver: every recent administration has granted exceptions when it wanted a particular person. For a learner, the practical rule is to check the current order's text rather than assume continuity — the overlay as of mid-2026 is whatever the incumbent administration last signed, and it binds no one after.

<Checkpoint question="A president wants to permanently bar former cabinet secretaries from ever lobbying their old departments. Can an executive order do it?">
  **No — not permanently and not alone.** An EO binds the signer's own appointees by contract-like pledge, lasts only as long as it isn't revoked (including by the next president), and can be waived by the administration itself. A *permanent*, government-wide ban requires statute — amending 18 U.S.C. §207 — which is why reformers' serious proposals all run through Congress, and why they stall there: the people who would vote on the ban are the ban's future subjects.
</Checkpoint>

## What's actually on the table in Congress

The proposals that recur, cycle after cycle, sort by the camp they serve (Lesson 2's taxonomy doing real work):

- **Enforcement and coverage fixes** (transparency-only camp's agenda, acceptable to all three). Give LDA enforcement a real home — currently the U.S. Attorney for D.C. handles referrals, and prosecutions are rare — raise penalties, and close the 20-percent "strategic adviser" loophole so shadow lobbyists register. The Congressional Research Service's review of the LDA's first twenty years catalogued exactly these gaps; they remain the consensus reform shelf.
- **Revolving-door extensions** (restriction-first). Stretch the Senate's two years and the House's one to five, or to a lifetime ban for members. Versions have been introduced repeatedly by members of both parties — Elizabeth Warren and Rick Scott introduced a bipartisan lifetime-ban bill in 2026, and Ted Cruz and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez publicly agreed to draft one in 2019 — an unusual coalition that tells you the idea polls well and threatens incumbents' futures, which is also why it never reaches a floor vote.
- **Lobbyist money bans** (restriction-first, constitutionally exposed). Bar any registered [[term:lobbyist]] from contributing to, or bundling for, officials they lobby. *Buckley v. Valeo* treats contributions as protected speech subject to anti-corruption regulation, so a narrow pay-to-play rule is defensible but litigation is guaranteed — and the broader the ban, the weaker its odds.
- **FARA modernization** (bipartisan since the Mueller-era revival). Clarify the statute's aging definitions, narrow the commercial exemptions that let foreign state-owned enterprises lobby under the lighter LDA regime, and add civil enforcement tools so the Justice Department has options between a warning letter and a felony charge.
- **Capacity and counterweight measures** (structural-reform). Restore the Office of Technology Assessment, raise committee-staff pay, and expand public campaign financing. These travel in appropriations and democracy-reform packages rather than as "lobbying bills," which is precisely the structural camp's point: the lobbying fix that never says "lobbying."

None of the restriction-side proposals has passed since 2007. The honest summary: HLOGA was scandal-driven legislation, and absent an Abramoff-scale scandal, the floor stays where it is.

## The states as the live laboratory

While the federal floor sits still, states have kept building. Every state has its own lobbyist-registration law, and many are stricter than Congress's: most states impose some cooling-off period on their own former legislators — one or two years is the common pattern — and several ban campaign contributions from lobbyists outright or freeze them during legislative sessions, rules that have survived state-level constitutional challenges where narrowly drawn. A handful of states publish lobbying data in formats that put the Senate's federal database to shame. The [[term:federalism]] lesson cuts both ways, though: state ethics commissions are chronically underfunded, state-level "shadow lobbying" is even less visible than federal, and a determined interest can simply shift spending to the venue with the weakest rules — the same regulatory arbitrage that FARA's commercial exemption enables internationally.

<Checkpoint question="Why have lobbyist contribution bans survived in several states while the equivalent federal proposal is considered constitutionally risky?">
  Because the surviving state bans are *narrow*: they target the specific corruption risk *Buckley* recognizes (money flowing from people currently seeking official action to the officials they're lobbying — classic pay-to-play), often limited to sessions or to the lobbyist-official pair. A broad federal ban on all political giving by anyone registered under the LDA would burden far more protected activity than the anti-corruption rationale covers. Constitutional survival tracks tailoring, not geography — which is the design lesson federal drafters take from the states.
</Checkpoint>

Read the spacing between the dots, not just the dots: 129 years passed before the first statute, each major tightening (1946, 1995, 2007) followed exposure of abuse, and the executive-order ethics pledges (1993–present) come and go in the gaps. The pattern political scientists draw from it: lobbying law in America is scandal-driven and episodic — long equilibria punctuated by a plea deal — which is why most observers expect the next major statute to follow the next major scandal, not the next white paper.

## What's likely next

Reading the landscape as of mid-2026, four live currents are worth watching. **Foreign influence stays hot:** FARA enforcement and amendment proposals retain rare bipartisan energy, because "foreign agent" is the one kind of lobbyist with no domestic constituency. **Enforcement-funding fights recur** every appropriations cycle, with transparency advocates pushing to give the LDA an enforcer with teeth. **The shadow-lobbying boundary** — that 20-percent line — is the likeliest target of the next genuine reform, since closing it offends no one's constitutional theory. And **artificial-intelligence lobbying** has become both a subject (the technology sector's Washington spending has surged with every regulatory debate) and a tool (drafting comment letters and [[term:astroturfing]] campaigns at volumes the disclosure system was never built to count). What is *not* likely: structural change to the LDA-HLOGA floor without a scandal to drive it — and even then, any floor-vote path runs through the Senate [[term:filibuster]]. ## What a learner should take away

This pack has given you machinery (Lesson 1), arguments (Lesson 2), and now the state of play. A defensible position on U.S. lobbying policy requires four commitments: a view on whether the current scale is compatible with democratic legitimacy; a view on which reforms — restrictions, transparency, structure — would actually help; a view on how to weigh petition-clause protection against dependence-corruption concerns; and an honest account of what evidence would change your mind. What this lesson did not settle is deliberate: it told you what is on the table, not what to order.
