Picture a vending machine. Dollars go in at the top. A policy drops out at the bottom.

[beat]

That is everyone's favorite story about lobbying. Insert dollars, receive policy.

Now set that story beside the largest study of federal lobbying ever conducted.

Frank Baumgartner and four colleagues took 98 policy issues, sampled at random, and tracked them across years of fighting. They published the results in 2009.

The side with more money won only slightly more often than the side with less.

Differences in resources explained under five percent of the variance in outcomes.

And on roughly 60 percent of the issues, nothing happened at all. [beat] The status quo won.

[pause]

If lobbying were a vending machine, none of that would be true.

So the vending machine is a cartoon.

> The real debate is not whether money buys laws. It is what lobbying does.

That means who gets heard, and what never reaches the agenda. And it means asking whether a system can be corrupt in its structure, even when almost no one is bribed.

On that ground, three serious positions compete. Last time we built the machinery. This time, the argument. By the end, you should be able to state each position at its strongest, and to say what the evidence settles and what is still contested.

We will take it in four steps. First, the empirical ground that all three camps stand on. Second, the three cases, one at a time. Third, one scene, run through all three. And last, what is settled and what is not.

Each case will be built the same way: what it protects, its argument, the evidence it points to, its program, and what would change it.

## The ground all three stand on

Four findings set the table. You met most of them last time, and it pays to have them crisp.

First, Olson. Concentrated interests organize, and diffuse ones do not. So the pressure system over-represents producers over consumers, and the organized over the unorganized. By arithmetic, not conspiracy.

Second, Schattschneider's accent, with numbers on it. By Lee Drutman's accounting, business interests spend about 34 dollars on lobbying for every dollar spent by labor unions and public-interest groups combined.

Third, Baumgartner. That lopsided chorus mostly fails to change policy. The status quo is heavily favored. And when change does come, it usually requires new arguments or new coalitions, not just new money.

Fourth, a finding that is new to us. It comes from a 2006 paper by Hall and Deardorff, called "Lobbying as Legislative Subsidy."

Most lobbying, they found, is not the persuasion of enemies, and it is not the purchase of friends. It is a gift of capacity to allies. Research. Draft bill text. Talking points. Whip counts. It lets an overstretched, like-minded legislator do more of what both already wanted.

> Most lobbying is a subsidy to allies, not persuasion of enemies.

Who organizes. The 34-to-1 chorus. The favored status quo. The subsidy. [beat] Each camp accepts all four findings. Each draws a different moral.

Let's test the third finding before anyone uses it.

Baumgartner's team found that resources explain under five percent of lobbying outcomes. A classmate concludes: "So lobbying asymmetry doesn't matter."

What is the strongest rejoinder, from within the same study's framework? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Start with what an outcome study measures. It measures the fights that made it onto the agenda.

Now suppose the 34-to-1 asymmetry shapes which problems get defined as issues at all. Schattschneider called that the mobilization of bias. Then the fights that surface can be evenly matched, while a deep inequality lies in what never surfaces.

And the status-quo bias is not neutral. Where existing policy already reflects decades of organized-interest influence, a system in which nothing happens 60 percent of the time preserves that advantage.

So the finding disciplines the vending-machine story. It does not vindicate the system.

Keep both halves of that sentence. Different camps lean on different halves.

## The restriction-first case

Begin with the restriction-first reformer. What this reformer is protecting is democratic legitimacy.

The core claim is this. Modern lobbying, in its scale and its structure, is incompatible with that legitimacy. And only enforceable limits on access can fix it. Not more disclosure.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Its intellectual anchor is Lawrence Lessig, in a 2011 book called Republic, Lost.

Lessig concedes the point the vending-machine story misses: quid-pro-quo bribery is rare. He argues that the system is corrupt in a different, older sense. He calls it dependence corruption.

The Framers designed a Congress "dependent on the people alone." The modern Congress, on this argument, is dependent on three other things. On funders. On expertise supplied by lobbyists: the Hall and Deardorff subsidy, now understood as a dependency. And on the prospect of careers in the influence industry, after government.

No individual transaction is illegal. But the economy of influence, taken as a whole, bends outcomes toward those who can pay for the subsidy.

> The restriction-first case: no bribe is needed. The dependence is the corruption.

The evidence this camp points to is the revolving door. A majority of departing members who take outside jobs land in the influence business. About half of registered lobbyists are former officials.

On this reading, that is not a jobs statistic. It is the mechanism that shapes legislators' incentives while they still hold office.

The program follows, in four parts. Lobbying bans for former members that run five to ten years, or a lifetime, against the current two years for senators and one for House members. A ban on registered lobbyists making or bundling campaign contributions to officials they lobby.

Closing the 20-percent loophole, so that "strategic advisers" register like everyone else. And serious penalties, with a real enforcement agency behind them.

And what would change this position? Evidence that restrictions chill the petition right in practice: for instance, that broad registration rules sweep in citizen volunteers and small nonprofits. Or evidence that the subsidy lobbyists provide is, on net, informational value that Congress cannot replace.

## The transparency-only case

Now the transparency-only advocate, built the same way. What this advocate is protecting is the right to petition.

The core claim is this. Lobbying is the First Amendment's petition clause in action. The constitutional and practical costs of restricting it are higher than reformers admit. And the right regime is sunlight plus enforcement.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. It starts with the text. The First Amendment protects "the right of the people," in its words, "to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Paid, professional petitioning is still petitioning, just as the right to counsel includes hired counsel.

Then the cases. The Supreme Court has never upheld a flat restriction on lobbying activity. What it upheld, in United States v. Harriss in 1954, was disclosure. And Buckley v. Valeo, in 1976, treats political contributions as protected speech, which puts proposals to ban lobbyists' contributions on contested constitutional ground.

Then the broader theory, from Bradley Smith, in a 2001 book called Unfree Speech. Attempts to ration political influence, Smith argues, inevitably empower incumbents and insiders to write rules against challengers and gadflies. The cure becomes the disease.

> The transparency-only case: paid petitioning is still petitioning. Answer it with sunlight.

The evidence this camp points to is the research you have already heard. Baumgartner first. If money explains under five percent of outcomes, the corruption story is overdrawn, and draconian remedies are disproportionate to the documented harm.

Then Hall and Deardorff. Lobbying transfers real information and analytical capacity to a Congress that has gutted its own. Strip the subsidy, and you get a less informed legislature, not a purer one.

And the revolving door, on this reading, is the labor market for policy expertise, working. Tighten it into a lifetime ban, and talented people simply never enter public service.

The program follows, in three parts. Close the 20-percent loophole: this camp favors broader disclosure, even while it opposes restrictions. Fund the enforcement of the LDA and of FARA. And let voters punish what they see.

And what would change this position? Evidence that disclosure has no disciplining effect: that voters and journalists cannot use it, or do not. Or a demonstrated restriction design that blocks dependence-style influence without touching legitimate petitioning.

[pause]

Two cases so far. Let's set them against each other.

A bill would bar registered lobbyists from contributing to any campaign of an official they lobby. Which Supreme Court case is the first obstacle? And which camp treats that obstacle as decisive? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The case is Buckley v. Valeo. It held that campaign contributions are protected First Amendment activity. Protected, though open to regulation. So a targeted ban on one profession's contributions invites strict constitutional scrutiny.

The transparency-only camp treats that as nearly decisive. The remedy, it argues, burdens core political rights, to address a harm the Baumgartner evidence suggests is overstated.

The restriction-first camp answers that Buckley itself blesses anti-corruption rationales. Dependence corruption, it argues, is exactly the interest that justifies a narrow rule, of the pay-to-play kind.

> For any ban on lobbyist money, Buckley v. Valeo is the hinge.

Both camps swing on it.

## The structural-reform case

Third, the structural-reform centrist, built the same way. What this centrist is protecting is a legislature that is hard to capture.

The core claim is this. Both other camps aim at lobbying, when the problem is the imbalance of capacity around it. Do not shrink the influence industry. Rebuild the institutions and the counterweights.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. It starts from a fact that the subsidy finding makes vivid.

> The structural-reform case: lobbyists are powerful partly because Congress chose to be weak.

Congress abolished its Office of Technology Assessment in 1995. It has let committee staff and member budgets erode for decades. When the in-house experts left, the lobby filled the vacuum, at its own price.

So if legislators depend on that 34-to-1 chorus for their information, the fix is to restore independent capacity. Then the subsidy becomes one input among several, and not the only analysis in the room.

The evidence this camp points to is that record: the abolished office, the eroded staff. And it points to Lee Drutman himself. His data powers the restriction-first case. His own prescription is congressional capacity, more than lobbying bans.

The program follows, in two moves. The first is capacity. Bigger, better-paid committee staffs. A revived Office of Technology Assessment. Stronger support from the Congressional Research Service and the budget office.

The second move works on the demand side of Olson's problem. If diffuse interests cannot organize themselves, subsidize their organization. Public matching funds, as in New York City, and democracy vouchers, as in Seattle, dilute the fundraising channel of dependence.

And tools of administrative law can oblige agencies to seek out the voices that are affected but unorganized. That counters the bias of who shows up.

None of this restricts anyone's petition rights. That is the centrist's quiet constitutional advantage: everything on the list survives Buckley untouched.

And what would change this position? Evidence that capacity-building does not actually reduce the asymmetries of influence: legislators with bigger staffs might just process more lobbying. Or evidence that the agenda is politically utopian. A Congress that defunded its own expertise must vote to rebuild it.

## One scene, three readings

Three cases. Before we set them side by side, pull something back. All three camps stand on the same four findings. Can you still name the four?

[long pause]

Olson: concentrated interests organize, and diffuse ones do not. Drutman's numbers: 34 dollars to one. Baumgartner: the status quo is heavily favored. And Hall and Deardorff: lobbying as a subsidy to allies.

Now watch what each camp does with the same study.

Take Baumgartner. The restriction-first camp reads it to mean that the bias lives in the agenda, not in the outcomes. The transparency-only camp reads it as proof that the corruption story is overdrawn. The structural-reform camp reads the status-quo bias as institutional weakness.

The same goes for Hall and Deardorff's subsidy. To the first camp it is a mechanism of dependency. To the second, a valuable transfer of information. To the third, a symptom of in-house capacity that was gutted.

> All three camps accept the same studies. They disagree about which fact is load-bearing.

Put another way, the deepest divide is not over facts. It is over whether the system's sin lives in outcomes, in agendas, or in institutions.

To feel the difference, run one scene through all three.

A senator retires on Friday. On Monday she joins a K Street firm as a "strategic adviser." She makes no lobbying contacts, exactly as the two-year cooling-off period requires. And she plans precisely who should make them.

Here is the restriction-first reading. Everything went wrong, and all of it legally. Her market value is the access and the obligations she accumulated in office, and she could see this payday coming while she was still casting votes.

That is dependence corruption, operating in real time. No bribe, no broken rule, and incentives bent years before the move. The remedy is to close the gap between contact and influence: a five-year or lifetime ban that reaches strategy and supervision, not just the phone call.

Here is the transparency-only reading. Nothing went wrong that sunlight cannot police. She is selling procedural expertise that the private sector legitimately needs, and the cooling-off period is working exactly as written.

Her firm's clients, issues and fees will surface in its quarterly filings, though Congress should close the 20-percent loophole, so that advisers like her register too. A ban broad enough to stop her would also deter talented people from ever entering public service, and it would burden petitioning the First Amendment protects.

Here is the structural-reform reading. The scene is a symptom, not the disease. She commands those fees because Congress gutted its own expertise: it abolished its technology office, and it underpaid its committee staff. That made her procedural knowledge artificially scarce.

Ban her, and the scarcity remains. Someone else sells it. The fix is upstream: rebuild in-house analytical capacity and pay staff competitively, so that what she sells becomes one input among several.

[pause]

Notice that no reading disputes a fact asserted in another. The disagreement is entirely about which feature of the scene is the problem. Career incentives. Missing disclosure. Or missing capacity.

Now choose the reading you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its strongest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Before the gather, sort what the evidence settles from what it cannot.

Settled, or close to it. Disclosed lobbying has grown enormously since the 1970s, and it set records in 2024 and 2025. The asymmetry between business and everyone else is large: 34 to 1.

The revolving door is a structural career path. Documented quid-pro-quo bribery is rare. And resources alone are weak predictors of policy outcomes.

Genuinely contested. Whether dependence corruption is real corruption, or a metaphor stretched past its evidence. Whether disclosure disciplines anyone, in a fragmented media environment.

Whether tighter revolving-door bans would drain expertise from government, or cleanse its incentives. And whether capacity-building is achievable, and sufficient.

Those are contested questions, not settled ones.

[pause]

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The vending machine is the wrong picture. All three camps stand on the same four findings: who organizes, the 34-to-1 chorus, the favored status quo, and lobbying as a subsidy to allies.

[beat]

Two. The restriction-first case: no bribe is needed; the dependence is the corruption. Its remedy is bans: on the revolving door, and on lobbyist money.

[beat]

Three. The transparency-only case: paid petitioning is still petitioning; answer it with sunlight. Its remedy is disclosure plus enforcement.

[beat]

Four. The structural-reform case: lobbyists are powerful partly because Congress chose to be weak. Its remedy is capacity: staff, a revived technology office, and public financing.

[beat]

And five. All three camps accept the same studies. They disagree about which fact is load-bearing. And for any ban on lobbyist money, Buckley is the hinge.

[pause]

What this lecture has not settled is which frame to choose. A defensible position picks one frame, says why, and names the evidence that would dislodge it.

The next lecture maps which of these arguments is actually in play, in the policy fights of today.

For now, go back to the vending machine. The largest study we have took it apart. [beat] What stands in its place is three serious arguments about the same facts. You can now state all three.
