There is a story Washington loves to tell about itself.

President Ulysses Grant, the story goes, liked to escape the White House for the lobby of the Willard Hotel. A cigar. A brandy. Then the favor-seekers would find him there, and swarm him. And Grant, irritated, branded them with a name: "those damn lobbyists."

[beat]

It is a perfect origin myth. Vivid, cynical, and set two blocks from the White House.

It is also false.

[pause]

Newspapers were calling a man named William Irving a "lobby member" of the New York legislature in 1817. The word "lobbying" appears in American print by the 1820s, decades before Grant's presidency. And the men who worked the lobby of the state capitol in Albany were a fixture of American politics before Grant was born.

That is worth knowing for more than trivia, because it tells you what kind of thing lobbying is. It is not a modern corruption of the system. Petitioning the government is protected by the First Amendment, in its petition clause. And that includes paying someone skilled to petition for you.

> Lobbying is as old as the Republic, and the petition clause protects it.

So what is new? Three things. The scale. The professionalization. And the asymmetry of the modern apparatus.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to describe the machinery: what the law sees, what it misses, and what the money does. We will take it in four steps.

First, what federal law counts as lobbying, and how much falls outside the count.

Second, the three laws that hold the system up, and the scandal that produced the newest one.

Third, the revolving door between government and the influence business.

And last, the money: how much, from whom, and what it actually buys.

## What the law counts

Start with the foundation of the federal regime: the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, the LDA. Its definitions are narrower than everyday usage, and the gap between the two is our first subject.

Under the LDA, you are a lobbyist only if three things are true.

One. A client or an employer pays you.

Two. You make more than one lobbying contact. A lobbying contact is a communication to a covered official, in Congress or in the executive branch, about policy, legislation or nominations.

Three. You spend at least 20 percent of your time for that client on lobbying activity, over a quarter.

> Paid. More than one contact. Twenty percent of the time.

Meet all three, and you register. Registrants file a report every quarter: their clients, their issues, the chambers and agencies they contacted, and the money spent.

Now hear those three tests again, because each one is a door out of the disclosure system.

Stay under 20 percent of your time for each client, and you are not a lobbyist. You are a "strategic adviser."

Make one contact instead of two, or none at all, and nothing triggers registration. And a great deal of influence work needs no contact with an official at all. Running a coalition's strategy. Drafting testimony for others to deliver. Placing op-eds.

Three more kinds of that work have names, and the names are worth having.

A grassroots campaign mobilizes large numbers of ordinary constituents to call, write and show up.

A grasstops campaign recruits the few constituents a member actually heeds: a hometown employer, a mayor, a major donor.

And astroturfing is the manufactured cousin of grassroots: a campaign engineered to look like spontaneous public demand.

[beat]

Grassroots, grasstops, astroturf. None of them counts as a lobbying contact, so none of them shows up in the disclosed totals.

How much of the industry sits outside the count? Two political scientists, Timothy LaPira and Herschel Thomas, went looking. They sampled the directories of Washington's influence industry, and checked them against LDA filings. They concluded that roughly half of the people working in federal policy advocacy in 2012 were unregistered.

[beat]

Roughly half. The term for them is shadow lobbyists: people doing lobbying-shaped work, outside the disclosure regime.

So here is a rule for every number you will hear from now on. Treat it as a floor.

> The visible industry is the part that chose to be visible.

Walk through one campaign with me, and watch where the law is looking.

A client hires a K Street firm, with a narrow objective: insert, amend, kill or protect one specific provision. Strategists map the committees and draft the ask, often as bill text that is ready to file.

Then comes air cover: a coalition, commissioned studies, op-eds, and grassroots or grasstops voices, so that the ask sounds like demand from the home district.

Then the contacts. Registered lobbyists meet members, committee staff and agency officials. Then the fine print, where the effort concentrates: amendments in committee, report language, comment letters to regulators.

And only then, the quarterly report. Only now does the campaign join the visible totals.

[pause]

The law reliably sees one of those stages: the contacts. Once someone registers, the planning and research behind their contacts becomes reportable too. But the air cover never does. And a campaign that staffs its strategy with people under the thresholds keeps entire stages off the books.

Put another way, the LDA photographs the contacts. The rest of the campaign happens outside the frame.

Let's test that with a case.

A former Senate staffer is paid 40,000 dollars a quarter by a pharmaceutical company. She spends 15 percent of her time advising the company's executives on whom to call, and what to say. She personally makes no calls to officials.

Must she register under the LDA? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. She fails two of the three tests. She makes no lobbying contacts, and the statute requires more than one. And she is under the 20 percent threshold.

Her work is lobbying in the ordinary sense of the word. Legally, it is invisible. That is exactly the shadow-lobbying gap.

## Three laws and a scandal

That is what the law counts. Now the laws themselves. Three statutes form the skeleton, with the ethics rules of each chamber around them.

The first you have just met. The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 replaced a toothless law from 1946, and built the modern system of registration and reporting.

The second law is the newest, and it came out of a scandal.

Jack Abramoff was the most lucrative lobbyist of his era. On January 3, 2006, he pleaded guilty to conspiracy, to honest-services fraud, and to tax evasion.

He and a partner had charged their clients, Native American tribes with casinos, tens of millions of dollars. All the while they were secretly splitting kickbacks, and showering officials with trips, meals and skybox tickets. A sitting member of the House, Bob Ney of Ohio, went to prison.

Congress responded with the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, known as HLOGA.

It made reports quarterly, instead of twice a year. It banned gifts and meals from registered lobbyists to members and staff. It required disclosure when lobbyists bundle campaign contributions.

And it lengthened the cooling-off period, the ban on lobbying that a member faces after leaving office. The Senate's doubled, to two years.

> The 2007 act is what a scandal looks like when it becomes statute.

The third law is the oldest of the three. The Foreign Agents Registration Act, FARA, dates from 1938, and was passed amid worry about Nazi propaganda. Anyone doing political work for a foreign government or a foreign party must register with the Justice Department. And they must disclose far more than the LDA demands.

For decades, FARA sat nearly dormant. Then it roared back, when the special-counsel investigations after 2016 produced FARA charges against figures like Paul Manafort. So with FARA, the perennial question is enforcement, not the text of the statute.

[pause]

So, three laws. Registration and disclosure, in 1995. A scandal's reforms, in 2007. And foreign agents, from 1938.

Around them sit the executive orders. Every administration since Clinton's has imposed its own ethics pledge on its appointees. That is a moving target, and the third lecture in this series takes it up.

## The revolving door

Now the most studied structural feature of the industry: the flow of people between government and influence work. It is called the revolving door.

Federal law does restrain it. The restraints are real, but they are narrow.

A former senator may not make lobbying contacts with anyone in Congress for two years. A former House member faces a ban of one year. And senior officials of the executive branch face bans of one or two years on contacting their former agencies.

One word carries all of that. [beat] Contacts.

Note what the bans do not touch. A former member can immediately advise clients. Supervise other lobbyists. Strategize. Fundraise.

> The cooling-off period chills the phone call, not the career.

And the careers happen anyway, at scale. The group Public Citizen tracked the members who left after the Congress of 2017 to 2019. Of those who took jobs outside government and politics, 59 percent landed at lobbying firms, consulting shops or trade associations. In other words, in the influence business.

A member's salary is 174,000 dollars. What a former member sells on K Street commands multiples of that: procedural fluency, committee relationships, the ability to get a meeting.

But what exactly is being sold? Is it expertise, or is it access? That question matters enormously to the reform debate, and there is evidence on it.

Studies of lobbyists' earnings find that a revolver's fees drop sharply when the old boss leaves office. That suggests the asset is access to specific people, not just expertise.

Let's put the rule to work.

Senator X retires in January 2027, and joins a lobbying firm in February. That spring, there are two things she might do.

One: call her former chief of staff, who is now a committee's staff director, about a client's bill.

Two: coach the firm's junior lobbyists on exactly whom to call, and what to say.

Which of those may she legally do? Think it through.

[long pause]

Only the second. The two-year cooling-off period bars her own lobbying contacts with all of Congress, until 2029. It does not bar strategic advice, or supervision, or planning.

This is why critics call the cooling-off period a speed bump rather than a wall. And it is why reformers focus on the gap between two words: contact, and influence.

## The money, and what it buys

Three laws, and one door. Now follow the money. And remember the rule: every one of these numbers is a floor.

In 2025, registered federal lobbying spending reached 5.08 billion dollars. That was a record, and the first year over 5 billion.

For perspective, go back to 1998, the first full year of comparable data. Disclosed spending then was about 1.5 billion dollars.

And the people? About 13,000 registered lobbyists were active in 2024. That is roughly 24 for every member of Congress. [beat] Before counting the shadows.

Who spends it? The two perennial leaders are the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Realtors. Both are trade and professional associations: aggregators of thousands of businesses, not individual corporations.

And notice what most of the money buys. Unglamorous maintenance. A tax provision. Procurement language. A regulatory comment letter. A statutory exemption. The big televised fights are a small share of the work.

[pause]

Now the deeper question. Why does business dominate the disclosed numbers?

The canonical answer comes from Mancur Olson, in a 1965 book called The Logic of Collective Action. It is the single most useful frame in this whole series, so take it slowly.

Small groups with concentrated stakes organize easily. Each member's payoff from lobbying is large. Free-riding is visible. Coordination is cheap.

Large groups with diffuse stakes mostly do not organize. Consumers. Taxpayers. The public. Each person's stake is pennies, and the temptation to let someone else do the work is overwhelming.

Here is that logic in a single example. Picture a tariff. It costs 330 million consumers two dollars each. And it delivers 660 million dollars to a dozen firms.

Who will lobby for it? [beat] The dozen firms, ferociously. And who will oppose it? Almost no one.

> The asymmetry in Washington's chorus is not a conspiracy. It is arithmetic.

Five years before Olson, another scholar, Schattschneider, had put the consequence in one line. Listen for how it ends.

"The flaw in the pluralist heaven is that the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent."

[pause]

A strong upper-class accent.

And yet. Here is the wrinkle that keeps this an honest field. [beat] Money does not simply purchase outcomes.

Two studies make the point.

The first is a 1990 paper by Richard Hall and Frank Wayman, called "Buying Time." They found that what moneyed interests really get is legislative effort. Contributions and lobbying mobilize friendly legislators to show up, to offer amendments, and to work the bill in committee, far more than they flip anyone's vote. The action is in bill text and committee detail, not in the roll call.

The second is the largest study of federal lobbying ever conducted, by Frank Baumgartner and his colleagues. They took 98 issues, sampled at random, and tracked them for years. They found three things.

The side with more resources won only slightly more often than the side with less.

Resources explained under five percent of the variance in outcomes.

And on about 60 percent of the issues, nothing changed at all.

> The status quo, not the highest bidder, is the system's great winner.

One last test. Drug manufacturers lobby intensely, and successfully, on the formulas Medicare uses to reimburse them. Meanwhile a proposal to lower drug prices for all consumers stalls for a decade.

Which scholar's framework most directly predicts that pattern? And why?

[long pause]

Olson's. Reimbursement formulas concentrate enormous benefits on a small, easily organized group: the manufacturers. Price reform spreads small benefits per person across millions of unorganized consumers. So the concentrated side shows up, and the diffuse side free-rides.

Schattschneider describes the resulting bias of the chorus. Baumgartner explains why even the organized side often fails to move policy. But the asymmetry in who organizes is Olson's logic of collective action.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull one thing back. Near the start, I gave you the three tests that make someone a lobbyist under federal law. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Paid. More than one contact. Twenty percent of the time.

Now let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Lobbying is as old as the Republic, and the petition clause protects it. What is new is the scale, the professionalization and the asymmetry.

[beat]

Two. The law's lobbyist is narrow: paid, more than one contact, 20 percent of the time. Influence work structured to stay just outside those tests is shadow lobbying, and it is roughly half the industry.

Grassroots campaigns mobilize the many. Grasstops campaigns recruit the few a member heeds. Astroturf is manufactured to look spontaneous. None of the three is counted. So every disclosed number is a floor.

[beat]

Three. Three laws. The LDA of 1995 built registration and reporting. The 2007 act, HLOGA, tightened it after the Abramoff scandal. And FARA, from 1938, covers work for foreign governments.

[beat]

Four. The revolving door is the career path from government service into the influence business. The cooling-off period, two years for senators and one for House members, chills the phone call, not the career.

[beat]

And five. Disclosed spending passed 5 billion dollars in 2025. Olson explains who shows up: concentrated interests organize, and diffuse ones mostly do not. But money does not simply purchase outcomes. The status quo, not the highest bidder, is the system's great winner.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture is not. It is not yet a verdict. You know the machinery now. You do not yet have an argument about whether any of it is acceptable.

One answer is that this is petition-clause liberty, working as designed. Another is that it is structural corruption. A third is that it is an infrastructure problem, and a fixable one.

Three serious positions claim that ground: the restriction-first reformer, the transparency-only advocate, and the structural-reform centrist. The next lecture states each of them at its strongest.

For now, go back to the Willard Hotel, and to the story about Grant. The story is false. Men were working the lobbies before he was born. [beat] What is new is how large the business has grown, how professional, and how lopsided. Whether that is acceptable is the argument still to come.
