Here is a ritual, nearly as reliable as the inaugural address.

A new president takes office. Within days, that president signs an ethics order, binding appointees to pledges about lobbying. [beat] And within days of leaving office, or shortly after, the pledges dissolve.

Watch it happen, four times.

Bill Clinton imposed a five-year lobbying ban on his senior appointees, in 1993. He revoked it in his final month.

Barack Obama's order, in 2009, barred appointees who had recently been registered lobbyists from the agencies and issues they had lobbied.

Donald Trump's order, in 2017, imposed its own five-year pledge. He revoked it on January 19, 2021. [beat] His next-to-last day.

And Joe Biden rebuilt a version the next afternoon.

[pause]

Each order was announced as a draining of the swamp. Each bound only one administration's people, and only as long as the administration chose.

That whiplash is the essential fact about the lobbying-policy landscape today.

> The durable rules are the statutes. Almost everything since has been reversible decoration.

The statutes in question are the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, and the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, known as HLOGA.

This series began with the machinery, and went on to the arguments. Now, the state of play. By the end, you should be able to say what is law, what is proposed, what a president can do alone, and where the next fights are likely.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two layers of the rules: a statutory floor, and an executive overlay. Second, what is on the table in Congress, sorted by the camp each proposal serves. Third, the states, which have kept building. And last, the pattern in two centuries of lobbying law, and what it forecasts.

## The floor and the overlay

Think of the regime in two layers.

The bottom layer is the statutory floor, and you know its parts already. Registration and quarterly disclosure, under the LDA. The gift bans and cooling-off periods of the 2007 act. FARA, for those who work for foreign principals. And the criminal rules on what officials may do after they leave government.

Congress has not significantly amended that floor since 2007. The registration thresholds do rise, but by automatic indexing to inflation, every four years. The next adjustment is due in 2029.

So every spending record of the past decade has been set under those rules. That includes the record of 2025: 5.08 billion dollars. [beat] All of it, under rules written before the iPhone had an app store.

The top layer is the executive overlay. It is whatever the sitting administration adds, for its own appointees. Bans on hiring registered lobbyists. Recusal requirements, covering former clients and employers. Pledges not to lobby the agency, or the whole administration, for some years after leaving.

These pledges can be broader than statute. Obama's and Biden's both restricted the way in: what are called golden door entries into government. No statute does that.

But the pledges share three weaknesses.

One. They cover only executive appointees, never Congress.

Two. They are enforced, if at all, by the administration that wrote them.

Three. They evaporate at the next inauguration, or sooner, by waiver. Every recent administration has granted exceptions when it wanted a particular person.

> An executive order binds one administration's people, for as long as it chooses.

So the practical rule is to check the text of the current order, and never to assume continuity. As of mid-2026, the overlay is whatever the incumbent administration last signed. It binds no one after.

Let's test the two layers against each other.

A president wants to bar former cabinet secretaries, permanently, from ever lobbying their old departments. Can an executive order do it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. Not permanently, and not alone. An executive order binds the signer's own appointees, by a pledge that works like a contract. It lasts only as long as it is not revoked, and the next president can revoke it. And the administration itself can waive it.

A permanent, government-wide ban requires a statute. That is why reformers' serious proposals all run through Congress. And it is why they stall there. [beat] The people who would vote on the ban are the ban's future subjects.

## On the table in Congress

So the action is in Congress. The same proposals recur there, cycle after cycle, and they sort by the three camps from the last lecture: restriction-first, transparency-only, and structural reform.

There are five families of proposal. For each one, I will give you the same three things: which camp it serves, what it would do, and where it stands.

First, enforcement and coverage fixes. This is the transparency-only camp's agenda, and it is acceptable to all three.

It would give LDA enforcement a real home. Today, referrals go to the U.S. Attorney for D.C., and prosecutions are rare. It would raise penalties. And it would close the 20-percent loophole, the "strategic adviser" loophole, so that shadow lobbyists register.

Where it stands: the Congressional Research Service catalogued exactly these gaps, in its review of the LDA's first twenty years. They remain the consensus reform shelf.

> The transparency-only shelf: a real enforcer, and the 20-percent loophole closed.

Second, revolving-door extensions. This is restriction-first.

It would stretch the cooling-off periods, two years in the Senate and one in the House, to five years. Or to a lifetime ban for members.

Where it stands: versions have been introduced repeatedly, by members of both parties. Elizabeth Warren and Rick Scott introduced a bipartisan lifetime-ban bill in 2026. Ted Cruz and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez publicly agreed to draft one in 2019.

That unusual coalition tells you the idea polls well. It also threatens incumbents' futures, which is why it never reaches a floor vote.

Third, bans on lobbyist money. This is restriction-first too, and it is constitutionally exposed.

It would bar any registered lobbyist from contributing to, or bundling for, officials they lobby.

Where it stands: Buckley v. Valeo treats contributions as protected speech, subject to anti-corruption regulation. So a narrow pay-to-play rule is defensible, but litigation is guaranteed. And the broader the ban, the weaker its odds.

> The restriction-first shelf: longer revolving-door bans, and bans on lobbyist money.

Fourth, FARA modernization. This one belongs to no single camp. It has been bipartisan since the revival of the statute in the Mueller era.

It would clarify the statute's aging definitions. It would narrow the commercial exemptions, which let foreign state-owned enterprises lobby under the lighter LDA regime. And it would add civil enforcement tools, so that the Justice Department has options between a warning letter and a felony charge.

Fifth, capacity and counterweight measures. This is structural reform.

It would restore the Office of Technology Assessment, raise committee-staff pay, and expand public campaign financing.

Where it stands: these measures travel in appropriations and democracy-reform packages, not as lobbying bills. And that is precisely the structural camp's point.

> The structural-reform shelf: the lobbying fix that never says "lobbying."

[pause]

Now the honest summary. None of the restriction-side proposals has passed since 2007. The 2007 act was scandal-driven legislation. And absent a scandal on the scale of Abramoff, the floor stays where it is.

## The states

While the federal floor sits still, the states have kept building.

Every state has its own lobbyist-registration law, and many are stricter than Congress's. Most states impose some cooling-off period on their own former legislators; one or two years is the common pattern.

Several ban campaign contributions from lobbyists outright, or freeze them during legislative sessions. And those rules have survived constitutional challenges at the state level, where they were narrowly drawn.

A handful of states even publish lobbying data in formats that put the Senate's federal database to shame.

But the lesson of federalism cuts both ways. State ethics commissions are chronically underfunded. Shadow lobbying at the state level is even less visible than it is federally. And a determined interest can simply shift its spending to the venue with the weakest rules.

That move is regulatory arbitrage. It is the same arbitrage that FARA's commercial exemption enables internationally.

Here is a question that ties the states back to Congress.

Lobbyist contribution bans have survived in several states. The equivalent federal proposal is considered constitutionally risky. Why the difference? Think it through.

[long pause]

Because the state bans that survived are narrow. They target the specific corruption risk that Buckley recognizes: money flowing from people currently seeking official action, to the officials they are lobbying. Classic pay-to-play. And they are often limited to legislative sessions, or to the pair of one lobbyist and one official.

A broad federal ban, on all political giving by anyone registered under the LDA, would burden far more protected activity than the anti-corruption rationale covers.

> Constitutional survival tracks tailoring, not geography.

In plainer words, what saves a ban is how tightly it is drawn, not which government drew it. That is the design lesson federal drafters take from the states.

## The pattern, and the forecast

Now step back. Two centuries of lobbying law compress into a handful of dates. As I give them to you, listen to the spacing between the dates, not only to the dates.

1817. Newspapers brand William Irving a "lobby member" of the New York legislature. The trade is older than every statute that regulates it.

1946. The first federal lobbying statute. It arrives 129 years after the word reached print, and it is nearly toothless from birth.

1954. In United States v. Harriss, the Supreme Court saves that act from a First Amendment challenge, but only by construing it down to direct contacts.

1995. The Lobbying Disclosure Act replaces it, with the modern regime of registration and reporting. The same year, Congress abolishes its Office of Technology Assessment, and the lobby fills the expertise vacuum.

January 2006. Abramoff pleads guilty, and Representative Bob Ney follows him to prison. The scandal supplies what white papers never had. [beat] Votes for reform.

2007. The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act. Quarterly reports, the lobbyist gift ban, bundling disclosure, a two-year Senate cooling-off period. It is the last structural amendment to the statutory floor.

And 2025. Disclosed lobbying crosses 5 billion dollars for the first time, under rules last tightened in 2007.

[pause]

Long silences, short bursts. And each major tightening, in 1946, in 1995 and in 2007, followed the exposure of abuse.

> Lobbying law in America is scandal-driven and episodic.

Political scientists describe it as long equilibria, punctuated by a plea deal. Which is why most observers expect the next major statute to follow the next major scandal, and not the next white paper.

And the executive-order pledges? From 1993 to the present, they come and go in the gaps. Earlier I gave you the three weaknesses those pledges share. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

They cover only executive appointees, never Congress. They are enforced by the administration that wrote them. And they evaporate at the next inauguration, or sooner, by waiver.

So what is likely next? Reading the landscape as of mid-2026, four currents are worth watching.

One. Foreign influence stays hot. FARA enforcement, and proposals to amend it, retain a rare bipartisan energy. The reason is that the foreign agent is the one kind of lobbyist with no domestic constituency.

Two. Fights over enforcement funding recur in every appropriations cycle, with transparency advocates pushing to give the LDA an enforcer with teeth.

Three. The shadow-lobbying boundary, that 20-percent line. It is the likeliest target of the next genuine reform, since closing it offends no one's constitutional theory.

Four. Artificial intelligence. It has become a subject of lobbying: the technology sector's spending in Washington has surged with every regulatory debate. And it has become a tool of lobbying: drafting comment letters and astroturfing campaigns, at volumes the disclosure system was never built to count.

And what is not likely? Structural change to the statutory floor, without a scandal to drive it. Even then, any path to a floor vote runs through the Senate filibuster.

The timeline is the forecast.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The rules have two layers. The statutory floor has not been significantly amended since 2007. The executive overlay binds one administration's people, for as long as it chooses.

[beat]

Two. A permanent, government-wide ban requires a statute. And a statute runs through Congress, where the people voting on the ban are its future subjects.

[beat]

Three. The proposals sort by camp. The transparency-only shelf: a real enforcer, and the 20-percent loophole closed. The restriction-first shelf: longer revolving-door bans, and bans on lobbyist money. The structural-reform shelf: the lobbying fix that never says "lobbying." And FARA modernization, which is bipartisan.

[beat]

Four. In the states, the contribution bans that survived are the narrow ones. Constitutional survival tracks tailoring, not geography.

[beat]

And five. Lobbying law in America is scandal-driven and episodic. Long silences, short bursts. The timeline is the forecast.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled, and that is deliberate. It told you what is on the table, not what to order.

A defensible position on lobbying policy requires four commitments. A view on whether the current scale is compatible with democratic legitimacy. A view on which reforms would actually help: restrictions, transparency, or structure. A view on how to weigh petition-clause protection against dependence-corruption concerns. And an honest account of what evidence would change your mind.

Before you make those commitments, one drill. Take the camp you find least persuasive: restriction-first, transparency-only, or structural reform. State its best case, the way its strongest advocate would.

For now, go back to the ritual. A new president, a pen, an ethics order, announced as a draining of the swamp. [beat] You know now who it binds, and for how long. And you know where the durable rules are written.
