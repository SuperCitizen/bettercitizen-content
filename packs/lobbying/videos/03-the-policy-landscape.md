## A ritual in Washington

Within days of taking office, a new president signs an ethics executive order, binding appointees to pledges about lobbying. Within days of leaving, or shortly after, the pledges dissolve.

Bill Clinton imposed a five-year lobbying ban on senior appointees in 1993, then revoked it in his final month. Donald Trump imposed his own five-year pledge in 2017, and revoked it on January 19, 2021, his next-to-last day. Joe Biden rebuilt a version the next afternoon.

- Clinton: imposed 1993, revoked
- Trump: imposed 2017, revoked 2021
- Biden: rebuilt 2021

## Two layers

Think of the rules in two layers. The floor is statute: the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, and the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007. Congress has not significantly amended that floor since 2007.

- The floor: statutes of 1995 and 2007

On top sits whatever the sitting administration adds for its own people. Those pledges cover only executive appointees, never Congress. They are enforced, if at all, by the administration that wrote them. And they evaporate at the next inauguration, or sooner, by waiver.

- The overlay: one administration's pledges

> The durable rules are the statutes. Almost everything since is reversible.

## Long silences, short bursts

The phrase "lobby member" appears in print in 1817. The first federal lobbying statute came in 1946, 129 years later. The modern disclosure law came in 1995.

- 1817 to 1946: 129 years

Then, in January 2006, a lobbyist named Abramoff pleaded guilty. The 2007 act followed. Each major tightening followed the exposure of abuse.

- Abramoff's plea, then the 2007 act

> Lobbying law in America is scandal-driven and episodic.

## What is on the table

The proposals that return, cycle after cycle, sort by the camp they serve. Enforcement and coverage fixes are the transparency-only agenda, and acceptable to all three camps. Longer revolving-door bans, and bans on lobbyists' campaign money, are restriction-first. Modernizing the foreign agents law is bipartisan. Restoring Congress's own expertise is structural reform.

- Enforcement and coverage fixes
- Revolving-door and lobbyist money bans
- Foreign agents law, modernized
- Capacity and counterweights
