## The story Washington tells

Washington loves a story about itself. President Ulysses S. Grant, the tale goes, liked to escape the White House for a cigar and brandy in the lobby of the Willard Hotel. Favor-seekers swarmed him there, and he branded them "those damn lobbyists."

[beat]

It is a perfect origin myth. It is also false.

- The Willard Hotel story: false

## Older than Grant

Newspapers were calling a man a "lobby member" of the New York legislature in 1817, decades before Grant's presidency.

- "Lobby member" in print: 1817

Petitioning the government, including paying someone skilled to petition for you, is protected by the First Amendment's petition clause.

> Lobbying is not a modern corruption. It is as old as the Republic.

What is new is the scale.

## Three tests, three doors out

Federal law defines a lobbyist narrowly. Under the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, it is someone who is paid by a client, makes more than one lobbying contact with an official, and spends at least 20 percent of their time for that client on lobbying.

- Paid by a client
- More than one lobbying contact
- 20% of their time

Each test is a door out. Stay under 20 percent, or make no calls yourself and advise on whom to call and what to say, and you never register.

Two political scientists concluded that roughly half of the people working in federal policy advocacy in 2012 were unregistered. They are called shadow lobbyists.

- Shadow lobbyists: roughly half

> The visible industry is the part that chose to be visible.

[check q1]

## A floor, not a measurement

Registered federal lobbying spending reached 5.08 billion dollars in 2025, the first year over 5 billion. Treat that number as a floor.

- Disclosed in 2025: $5.08 billion

## What the money buys

And yet, money does not simply purchase outcomes. One study found that the real product is legislative effort: friendly legislators show up, offer amendments and work the markup, far more than anyone's vote is flipped.

- Effort from allies, not flipped votes

In the largest study of federal lobbying, the side with more resources won only slightly more often, and on about 60 percent of issues nothing changed at all.

- About 60% of issues: no change

> The status quo, not the highest bidder, is the system's great winner.
