## Not a vending machine

In the largest study of federal lobbying ever conducted, researchers tracked 98 randomly sampled policy issues. The side with more money won only slightly more often. On roughly 60 percent of the issues, nothing happened at all.

- 98 issues tracked
- Roughly 60%: the status quo won

If lobbying were a vending machine, insert dollars, receive policy, none of that would be true.

> The real debate is about what lobbying does do.

Three serious positions compete.

## The restriction-first case

The restriction-first reformer argues that the scale and structure of modern lobbying are incompatible with democratic legitimacy. Lawrence Lessig concedes that bribery is rare. He names the problem dependence corruption. The Framers designed a Congress dependent on the people alone. The modern Congress depends on funders, on lobbyists' expertise, and on the prospect of careers in the influence industry.

- Dependence corruption

This camp points to the revolving door. It asks for enforceable limits, such as lobbying bans of five years or more for former members.

> The restriction-first case: no single act is illegal, and the whole still bends.

## The transparency-only case

The transparency-only advocate argues that lobbying is the First Amendment's petition clause in action. Paid, professional petitioning is still petitioning, just as the right to counsel includes hired counsel.

- The petition clause

This camp points to the same study: if money explains under five percent of outcomes, the corruption story is overdrawn. It asks for sunlight plus enforcement, so that voters can punish what they see.

> The transparency-only case: paid petitioning is still petitioning. Sunlight, plus enforcement.

## The structural-reform case

The structural-reform centrist argues that the problem is the imbalance of capacity around lobbying. Lobbyists are powerful partly because Congress chose to be weak.

- An imbalance of capacity

This camp points to 1995, when Congress abolished its Office of Technology Assessment, and the lobby filled the vacuum. It asks for rebuilt expertise inside Congress, and for help organizing the diffuse interests that cannot organize themselves. Nothing on its list restricts anyone's lobbying.

> The structural-reform case: rebuild what makes a legislature hard to capture.

## Same studies, three morals

All three camps accept the same studies. They disagree about whether the system's sin lives in outcomes, agendas, or institutions.

- Outcomes, agendas, or institutions

The lesson gives each camp its full case, and the evidence that would change its mind. It also runs one retiring senator through all three lenses.
