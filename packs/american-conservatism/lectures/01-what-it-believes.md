November 19, 1955. A man of twenty-nine publishes the first issue of a new magazine. His name is William F. Buckley Jr., and the magazine is National Review.

His opening statement reads more like a dare. The magazine, he writes, "stands athwart history, yelling Stop, at a time when no one is inclined to do so."

[pause]

Athwart history, yelling Stop.

That line is usually quoted as a joke about reaction. In context it was something stranger: an announcement that a thing most American intellectuals believed did not exist was about to be built.

Five years earlier, the critic Lionel Trilling had written that in the United States, liberalism was "not only the dominant but even the sole intellectual tradition." Conservative impulses, he said, expressed themselves not in ideas, but in "irritable mental gestures which seek to resemble ideas."

[pause]

Within a generation, the movement that Buckley's magazine assembled had nominated a candidate for president, built a network of institutions, and elected Ronald Reagan. Whatever else one says about American conservatism, Trilling's dismissal did not survive contact with it.

So what does that tradition believe? The hardest thing about understanding any ideology, your own included, is hearing it the way its adherents hear it, and not the way its opponents caricature it. By the end, you should be able to state the core conservative commitments in language conservatives would themselves recognize.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the commitments most conservatives share.

Second, the five thinkers who supplied them.

Third, the coalition that carried them into power: a synthesis called fusionism.

And last, where that synthesis came apart, and the three readings of the inheritance that contend today.

One thing to hold on to before we start.

> Conservatism is a family of positions, not a creed.

Its members disagree with each other constantly, and the next lecture is entirely about those disagreements. This one is about what the family shares.

## What the family shares

Most American conservatives, whatever their more specific orientation, share six commitments. I will state each one the way a conservative would state it.

First, order before freedom. Liberty is real, and it is valuable. But on this view it is not the precondition for civilization. [beat] Order is. Without functioning institutions, the rule of law, the family, religious communities, a constitutional structure, individual liberty is fragile or meaningless.

So conservatives do not describe themselves as against liberty. They argue something more exact.

> The conservative claim: the conditions of liberty must be cultivated.

And they argue that movements which put liberation ahead of those conditions end by undermining both.

Second, the inheritance of accumulated wisdom. The voice here is Edmund Burke's, in 1790. "Society is indeed a contract," Burke wrote. But not a contract among the currently living. It is "a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."

[pause]

The living, the dead, and those who are to be born.

The argument runs like this. Institutions refined by generations of experience encode knowledge that abstract reasoning cannot reproduce. So the presumption favors reform that respects the inherited structure, over revolution from first principles.

That is not opposition to change. Burke again: "a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation." The skepticism is aimed at change of one kind: root and branch.

Third, limits to state power. Government is necessary, but dangerous. Power concentrated at the federal level, and especially in the executive, is more easily abused than power dispersed among levels and branches. So conservatives treat the Constitution as an instrument of restraint, which is why originalism became the tradition's dominant judicial philosophy.

Ronald Reagan compressed that temperament into a quip, in 1986. "The nine most terrifying words in the English language are 'I'm from the government and I'm here to help.'"

Fourth, the family and civil society come first. The family, the congregation, the voluntary association, the local institution: on this view, these do most of the actual work of social order. The state's role is to support them, not to displace them.

Fifth, skepticism of ideology and abstraction. Conservatives are wary of grand frameworks that propose to solve human problems through Reason, with a capital R. They point to the revolutions guided by abstract designs: the French, the Russian, the Maoist. On their account, that is a track record of disaster. So conservative thought tends to be empirical, particular, and attentive to circumstance.

And sixth, moral and cultural claims. On this view, moral and cultural concerns cannot be separated from political ones. The liberal separation of values from politics is, to a conservative, an evasion. Every regime teaches some morality. Pretending otherwise just means teaching one unannounced.

[pause]

Six commitments. Order before freedom. Inherited wisdom. Limits to state power. Family and civil society first. Skepticism of abstraction. And morality as part of politics.

Let's put them to work. Here is a scene.

A governor proposes a new, generous child benefit, administered through the tax code, and argues that it strengthens families. A critic objects: "Real conservatives oppose government programs."

Why might a conservative support that benefit? And what feature of its design would decide the question? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The answer sits in the fourth commitment. The state's job is to support the family, not to displace it. So a transfer that flows to parents, and leaves the decisions with them, can be conservative in design.

The deciding feature is this. Does the program reinforce the institution, with money to the family and choices kept local? Or does it substitute for the institution, with state provision replacing what families do?

As for the critic: "opposes all programs" describes libertarianism's reflex. It is not conservatism's test.

## Five thinkers

Those are the commitments. Now, who supplied them? Five thinkers, and one contribution from each.

First, Edmund Burke, the tradition's intellectual father, though in British parliamentary terms he was a Whig and a reformer. His Reflections on the Revolution in France appeared while most English observers still admired that revolution. It predicted the descent into terror and military despotism, and it made the classic case against politics by first principles.

Conservatives still cite it, because the argument generalizes. It is their statement of why "tear it down and rebuild rationally" keeps producing rubble.

Second, Russell Kirk, who made American conservatism self-aware. In 1953, in The Conservative Mind, he argued that a conservative tradition ran in an unbroken line from Burke to the twentieth century. And from that line he distilled six canons of conservative thought. One of the six is the conviction that civilized society requires orders and classes.

One caution, because this is the most common citation error in writing about Kirk. Forty years later, in 1993, he restated the tradition in an essay called Ten Conservative Principles. People say the ten principles are in The Conservative Mind. [beat] They are not. The 1953 book states six canons. The ten principles are a separate essay, four decades later.

And the two lists differ instructively. The 1993 version drops the language of orders and classes, and adds prudence, imperfectability, and voluntary community. Kirk was softening the aristocratic edges for a democratic audience.

Third, Friedrich Hayek, the Austrian-born economist, who supplied the economic backbone. His book The Road to Serfdom, in 1944, argued that central economic planning concentrates power until it destroys political freedom. It sold spectacularly in postwar America, and the conservative movement absorbed it.

The absorption was awkward, and Hayek knew it. In 1960 he published an essay titled "Why I Am Not a Conservative." Conservatism, he objected, "cannot offer an alternative to the direction in which we are moving." It can slow the train. It cannot switch the tracks.

Fourth, Leo Strauss, a German émigré and political philosopher at Chicago, who gave the movement philosophical depth. In Natural Right and History, in 1953, he took on modern relativism and historicism: the view that all standards are products of their time. He argued that this view corrodes the very possibility of calling anything just, and that classical political philosophy still teaches truths about virtue and the good regime.

And fifth, Buckley himself, who built the room where these strands could argue. National Review gave the movement a voice, policed its boundaries, and welded the factions into a political force.

[pause]

Five names. Burke, for inheritance. Kirk, for self-awareness. Hayek, for economics. Strauss, for philosophy. Buckley, for the room.

How do five such different thinkers end up in one movement? The standard map comes from George H. Nash, in a history published in 1976. Nash's thesis is that in 1945, no self-conscious conservative movement existed. There were three separate revolts against the liberal consensus of the New Deal. Traditionalists, alarmed by cultural dissolution. Classical liberals, alarmed by collectivism. And former Communists, alarmed by Soviet totalitarianism.

"American conservatism" is the name for what happened when those three revolts noticed each other.

> American conservatism was a coalition from birth.

Let's test that against the oddest fact so far. Hayek wrote an essay explaining why he was not a conservative. Yet he is on every list of American conservatism's foundational thinkers. What does that contradiction reveal about the structure of the tradition? Think it through.

[long pause]

It reveals a coalition, not a single philosophy. Hayek's classical liberalism is friendly to change, driven by markets, and suspicious of inherited hierarchy. That sits in genuine tension with Burke's traditionalism, and Hayek said so himself.

The postwar movement absorbed him anyway. It needed an economic doctrine and a common enemy more than it needed philosophical consistency.

And the tension never disappeared. Hold on to that, because it comes back.

## The fusion

A coalition needs something to hold it together. From 1955 until roughly 2008, that something had a name: fusionism.

Take the three revolts Nash described, and call them streams. In the abstract, they sat in tension. The traditionalists were skeptical of pure individualism. The free-market classical liberals were skeptical of any traditionalism that could justify state coercion. And the anti-communists supplied the unifying enemy: the Soviet Union.

A senior editor at National Review, Frank Meyer, argued that these streams were not merely allied. They were philosophically complementary. Listen to how his argument locks together.

Virtue is only virtuous if it is freely chosen. So the traditionalists need liberty.

[beat]

Freedom only survives among people with settled moral habits. So the libertarians need tradition.

[beat]

And both required defense against an existential threat. So anti-communism bound the package.

> Meyer's fusionism: virtue needs liberty, liberty needs tradition, and anti-communism binds them.

Notice that the glue comes in two kinds. One is philosophical: the claim that the streams complete each other. The other is practical: a shared enemy. We will need that distinction in a few minutes.

Whatever its philosophical soundness, fusionism worked politically.

In 1964, Barry Goldwater captured the Republican nomination.

In November, he was buried. Lyndon Johnson won 486 electoral votes. Goldwater won 52.

[pause]

But the defeat built the infrastructure of victory: a donor list, a cadre of activists, and a television star named Ronald Reagan.

In 1980, Reagan won 44 states, and the coalition had its triumph. The program was tax cuts, deregulation, a Cold War defense buildup, social conservatism, and judicial originalism. That was the package, and it defined Republican politics for a generation after Reagan left office.

Keep the package and the tradition apart in your mind. The package belongs to one coalition, in one era. The tradition underneath it goes back to Burke.

## Where the synthesis frayed

Here is the turn.

> Fusionism was built for a world with a Soviet Union in it.

After 1991, that world was gone. Four developments then strained the coalition, slowly and then suddenly.

First, the unifying enemy disappeared. The Soviet collapse removed anti-communism from the package. The next year, Pat Buchanan ran an insurgent primary challenge: nationalist, protectionist, restrictionist, and contemptuous of the free-trade consensus. He lost. His issues didn't.

Second, the wars. Afghanistan and Iraq, with their ambiguous-to-bad results, discredited the neoconservative wing in much of the movement.

Third, globalization. Free trade produced gains for the economy as a whole, but it concentrated the losses in specific manufacturing regions. And the voters in those regions were increasingly Republican voters. The 2008 financial crisis and the Tea Party revolt then revealed a populist energy that the movement's institutions channeled, but did not control.

Fourth, the realignment of 2016. Donald Trump faced the unified opposition of the fusionist establishment. National Review itself devoted an entire issue to stopping him. He won the nomination, and the presidency, anyway. The populism he rode reaffirmed social conservatism, while breaking with free-trade orthodoxy, with immigration expansionism, and with the politics of entitlement reform.

In March 2019, the rupture became explicit. A group of writers published a manifesto titled "Against the Dead Consensus." It declared: "There is no returning to the pre-Trump conservative consensus that collapsed in 2016."

What conservatism after fusionism amounts to in government is, as of mid-2026, still being tested.

Now a question that reaches back. I said Meyer's glue came in two kinds, one philosophical and one practical. Can you still say what each one was? And which of those developments did the most damage to which kind? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The practical glue was anti-communism, and the Soviet collapse removed it. With no existential enemy, nothing forced the streams to keep their tensions suppressed: state power for cultural ends against market freedom, the nation against the globe.

The philosophical glue was Meyer's claim that freedom and tradition complete each other. Globalization attacked that claim directly. Working-class conservative voters experienced economic freedom and settled community as opposed goods, not complementary ones.

And 2016 was the moment the suppressed argument became the main event.

## Three readings of the inheritance

So the conservative landscape after 2016 is not a single position. It is several.

Underneath them sits one contested question, and notice that the contest is internal. Within the tradition, how should the inheritance be understood? Three perspectives answer. Each gets the same hearing: what it emphasizes, and what it argues.

The traditionalists emphasize cultural and religious continuity, family, and community. They argue for skepticism of root-and-branch change, including revolutionary change proposed in conservatism's own name.

> The traditionalist reading: conserve continuity, and distrust revolution, even in conservatism's name.

The free-market classical liberals emphasize economic freedom, limited government, and the rule of law. They argue that the populist and nationalist turns abandon what made the movement worth joining.

> The classical-liberal reading: economic freedom and limited government made the movement worth joining.

The civic-republican, or national, conservatives emphasize the political community as a unit, and national solidarity. They argue that state action to support family, industry and virtue is legitimate, and that the fusionist settlement should not be mistaken for the whole tradition.

> The national-conservative reading: fusionism was one historical phase, not conservatism itself.

Now take the reading you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Conservatism is a family of positions, not a creed. Its test for a program is whether it supports the family and civil society, or displaces them.

[beat]

Two. The shared core has six parts. Order before freedom. Inherited wisdom. Limits to state power. Family and civil society first. Skepticism of abstraction. Morality as part of politics. Under all six runs one claim: the conditions of liberty must be cultivated.

[beat]

Three. Five thinkers: Burke, Kirk, Hayek, Strauss and Buckley. And one fact about Kirk. The Conservative Mind, in 1953, states six canons. The ten principles are a separate essay, from 1993.

[beat]

Four. American conservatism was a coalition from birth: three revolts that noticed each other. Meyer's fusionism held them together. Virtue needs liberty, liberty needs tradition, and anti-communism binds them.

[beat]

And five. Fusionism was built for a world with a Soviet Union in it. After 1991 it frayed, and in 2016 the suppressed argument became the main event. Three readings now contend for the inheritance: the traditionalist, the classical-liberal, and the national-conservative.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Which of those streams has the better claim to the inheritance? And what would each one actually do with power?

That is the subject of the next lecture, which is entirely about the family's disagreements.

For now, go back to November 1955, and a man of twenty-nine standing athwart history. [beat] Trilling heard only irritable mental gestures. You can now name the commitments, the thinkers, and the coalition his dismissal did not survive. And you know that the argument over the inheritance is not finished.
