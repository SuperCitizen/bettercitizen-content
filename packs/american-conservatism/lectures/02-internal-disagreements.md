May 29, 2019. The op-ed editor of the New York Post, Sohrab Ahmari, publishes a short essay in a publication called First Things. Its title is "Against David French-ism."

You might expect David French to be a progressive. [beat] He was not.

French was a writer at National Review. An evangelical. A religious-liberty litigator, with a long pro-life record. By any checklist, a conservative in excellent standing.

That was Ahmari's point.

French's style of conservatism was procedural and civil, committed to neutral rules that protect everyone's liberty equally. And that style, Ahmari argued, was unilateral disarmament in a culture war. In his words, "civility and decency are secondary values." What the moment required was fighting to win.

The conservative world spent that summer arguing about one of its own. That fall, the two men met in packed public debates.

[pause]

Nothing in recent memory better illustrates a rule about American politics.

> The bitterest arguments often run within left and right, not between them.

Last time, we met conservatism as a family of positions, held together for decades by the fusionist synthesis, and fractured in important ways since 2016. This time, we listen to the family argue.

By the end, you should be able to tell the three major streams apart, and to predict where each one lands on an issue. And you should be able to state each stream's strongest case against the other two.

We will take it in four steps. First, the three streams, each as its own adherents would describe it. Second, one question that sorts them. Third, the argument itself, with each stream prosecuting its case against the others. And last, the issues, and what the argument has done to the coalition.

One rule holds throughout. Every stream gets the same four things: what it wants to protect, who speaks for it, what it argues, and what it points to.

## The traditionalist stream

Begin with the traditionalist.

What this stream wants to protect is cultural and religious continuity. The institutions, customs and beliefs inherited from earlier generations are presumptively good, and they should be preserved unless they are shown to be substantially flawed. Change should be incremental, and respectful of what is being changed.

Who speaks for it? Patrick Deneen. R. R. Reno. Rod Dreher. Yuval Levin, in part. And the circle around First Things.

Here is what the traditionalist argues. Three claims.

First, the progressive project of putting every inherited institution under rational scrutiny tends to dissolve those institutions, not improve them. Marriage, family, religious community and cultural tradition are particularly vulnerable. And once dissolved, they do not come back easily.

Second, the trouble is not only on the left. Classical liberalism, even in its conservative variant, tends to atomize society. Its emphasis on individual rights, market exchange and procedural neutrality hollows out the civic and religious institutions that give life meaning. The canonical statement is Patrick Deneen's book of 2018, Why Liberalism Failed. Deneen's wing of the stream shades into post-liberalism: the view that the liberal experiment itself has failed, and not merely its progressive variant.

Third, the remedy. The right response is not cultural traditionalism driven by the state, which this stream calls mostly impossible. It is conservation of the institutions that remain, and the patient work of rebuilding civil society. Yuval Levin's book of 2020 makes that explicit. Its title: A Time to Build.

And what does the traditionalist point to? To civic and religious institutions that have been weakening for decades. On this view, the trend is not natural drift. It is the consequence of policy choices that put individual mobility ahead of community continuity.

> The traditionalist case: conserve what remains, and rebuild civil society from below.

Put another way: the work is patient, it is local, and it is not mainly the state's.

## The classical-liberal stream

Now the classical liberal, on the same four counts.

What this stream wants to protect is limited government, individual liberty, free markets, and the rule of law. Economic freedom, on this view, is a foundation of political freedom, and state power is the central threat to both. Government's job is to protect property, enforce contracts, and defend against violence. Otherwise, it should let the productive activities of free people produce broad prosperity.

Who speaks for it? George Will. Charles Cooke. Kevin Williamson. And the older editorial tradition of National Review and the Wall Street Journal.

Here is what the classical liberal argues. Three claims.

First, on markets. Distorting them, through regulation, subsidies or industrial policy, generally produces less wealth, not more.

Second, on culture. Federalism and constitutional structure are the right framework for cultural disagreements. Different states and communities can adopt different policies. People can sort themselves. No national majority needs to impose its cultural preferences on everyone. And the state should not promote any particular religious or cultural framework. George Will's book of 2019, The Conservative Sensibility, argues that classical liberalism is genuinely compatible with conservative virtue. Perhaps more so than traditionalism.

Third, on trade and immigration. The concentrated losses that free trade brings to some industries are real. But they are addressable through transition assistance, not through tariff-led re-industrialization. And immigration is, on net, a positive for the economy and for American dynamism.

And what does the classical liberal point to? To free markets as the most powerful force for poverty reduction in human history. And to the enormous gains from free trade, for American consumers and for the global poor.

> The classical-liberal case: state power is the central threat to economic and political freedom.

Put another way: let the states differ, let people move, and keep the national government neutral.

Two streams so far, and one to come. Before the third, check your memory. What does each of the first two want to protect?

[long pause]

For the traditionalist, cultural and religious continuity. For the classical liberal, liberty under limited government. Hold on to both, because the third stream quarrels with each of them.

## The national-conservative stream

And third, the stream called civic-republican, or national conservative. The same four counts.

What this stream wants to protect is the political community as a unit. The nation-state has borders, a culture, families, and an industrial base. Those have legitimate claims, which markets and abstract principles do not trump. So government action in service of national solidarity, family and productive industry is legitimate, and sometimes necessary.

Who speaks for it? Yoram Hazony. Sohrab Ahmari, whose essay opened this lecture. Gladden Pappin. Reihan Salam. And, in politics, JD Vance.

Here is what the national conservative argues. Three claims.

First, the nation is the natural unit of political life. Hazony makes that case at book length, in Conservatism: A Rediscovery, from 2022. Liberalism's universalism, he argues, disregards the particular bonds of nation, language and shared history that make political life possible. And that charge includes conservative classical liberalism.

Second, government should support the family, industry, and the working class. Tariffs, industrial policy, a larger child tax credit, and skills-based immigration are appropriate actions for the state.

Third, cultural conservatism requires political success. Pure cultural traditionalism, in retreat from politics, leaves the institutions of the state to progressive capture. The right response is to win political contests, and to use government legitimately in support of traditional families, civic education and productive industry.

And what does the national conservative point to? To a generation of stagnant working-class wages, declining family formation, and de-industrialization. On this view, free-market orthodoxy produced them. The coalition's commitment to that orthodoxy was a strategic error, and it produced the populist insurgency that the older establishment blames on others.

> The national-conservative case: cultural conservation requires winning political power, and using it.

Put another way: retreat from politics, and the institutions of the state go to the other side.

## One question that sorts them

Three streams, each on its own terms. Before we let them argue, let's see whether you can tell them apart.

A think-tank essay argues three things. Washington should leave marriage and schooling entirely to the states. Families should be able to relocate toward communities they prefer. And the federal government should stay neutral among competing cultural visions.

Which stream is speaking? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The classical liberal. Federalism, plus neutrality, plus the right of exit: treating those as the framework for cultural disagreement is this stream's signature.

And the other two would each object. A traditionalist would object that communities cannot survive if exit is costless and constant. Mobility is part of what dissolved them. A national conservative would object that neutrality is an illusion. Some vision always wins, so conservatives should contend for it.

So here is the fastest way to tell the three streams apart. Put one question to each of them.

> The sorting question: who settles cultural questions, and how?

One pair is the easiest to confuse, so let's separate it.

Two writers both want to reverse the decline of marriage and religious practice. The first proposes federal family benefits and state curriculum mandates. The second replies that the state is too blunt and too easily captured, and that the real work belongs to parishes, schools and families.

Which streams are arguing? Think it through.

[long pause]

The first is a national conservative. The second is a traditionalist.

They are easy to confuse because they share the cultural diagnosis almost completely. They split over the instrument. National conservatives hold that cultural conservation requires winning and using state power. Traditionalists hold that the state is too blunt an instrument, and that the work is institutional rebuilding from below.

And a classical liberal would have gone a step further back, and questioned whether the goal belongs in politics at all.

## The argument, joined

Summaries flatten. The fastest way to feel these disagreements is to let each stream make its case against the other two, as its own writers would put it.

So for the next few minutes, each stream speaks to its rivals directly. When you hear the word "you", it is one conservative talking to another.

The traditionalist goes first. To the classical liberals.

You are too focused on economic and political liberty, and too comfortable with the cultural change that markets bring. A community hollowed out by a closed factory, or by an attention economy, is just as hollowed out as one regulated into dependency. Yet your framework can only see the second harm. Markets are a good servant and a bad master.

[beat]

And to the national conservatives.

The state is too blunt an instrument for cultural renewal, and too easily captured. Whatever cultural machinery you build in Washington, the next administration inherits. The real work is closer to the parish, the family and the local school than to any federal department. And it cannot be won in an election, because it was not lost in one.

[pause]

Now the classical liberal. To the traditionalists.

You too easily accept government action when it serves cultural ends you favor. But the precedent does not stay yours. The conservation of institutions is best done by free people, in free markets and free associations. A tradition that needs the state's scaffolding has already died.

[beat]

And to the national conservatives.

Free markets, free trade, a generally welcoming immigration policy: those are the movement's core commitments. You have traded them for short-term political alignment. Tariffs and industrial policy will be captured by the well-connected, as they always are. And the result is a conservatism that conserves nothing of conservative substance.

[pause]

Last, the national conservative. To the traditionalists.

Withdrawal is surrender. Cultural preservation requires political power. Political power requires winning elections. And winning requires a coalition that includes the working class your localism cannot reach. While you rebuild one parish, the institutions of the state educate everyone's children.

[beat]

And to the classical liberals.

You confuse one historical variant, the postwar fusionist settlement, with conservatism as such. The older tradition, Burke included in his economic moments, was far more comfortable with state action in service of national interests. Your orthodoxy cost the working class its towns and its wages. And the populist insurgency you lament is the bill arriving.

[pause]

Six cases, each in its own strongest terms. This lecture does not rank them.

Now choose the stream you find least convincing. State its best case against the other two, the way its own writers would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Where each stream lands

Arguments over principle turn into positions on issues. Take two.

Tariffs. The classical liberal is strongly opposed. The national conservative generally supports them. The traditionalist is skeptical, because a tariff is state action.

Immigration. The classical liberal is generally welcoming. The traditionalist is restrictionist, on cultural grounds. The national conservative is restrictionist too, and wants immigration based on skills.

On some things the streams stand together. All three support school choice. All three support constitutional originalism, though the national conservative's support is qualified.

That is a flattened summary, and reality is more complicated. But the rough contours are these. Classical liberals are most comfortable with markets, trade and immigration. National conservatives are most comfortable with state action on industry and family. Traditionalists are uncomfortable with both extremes, and emphasize institutional rebuilding.

Let's test those contours. A bill pairs two things: a permanent, generous child tax credit, and a 10 percent tariff on all imported manufactured goods.

Predict how each stream votes. And say which vote is hardest to call. Take a moment.

[long pause]

The national conservative votes yes, twice. Transfers that support families, plus tariff-led re-industrialization: that is the program.

The classical liberal votes no on the tariff, without hesitation, and is lukewarm on the credit. This stream is skeptical of welfare expansion, though transfers routed to families divide it.

The traditionalist is the hard call. Yes on the child credit, which supports the family and does not displace it. But uneasy about the tariff, which is exactly the kind of economy-steering state action this stream distrusts.

And the bundling is the point. Bills like this are built to split the old coalition.

That old coalition was fusionist, and it kept all three streams in a single party. How?

> Fusionism held the streams together by lowering the stakes of their disagreements.

The unifying enemy was Communism. Free trade was producing visible economic gains. And cultural change was slow. Under those three conditions, the streams could coexist.

Then the conditions changed: economic shocks after 2008, a political realignment after 2016, a cultural intensification after 2020. Each made the disagreements more visible.

So the current Republican coalition is not fusionist. It is a contested mixture of all three streams, plus populist elements that do not fit cleanly in any of them.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The bitterest arguments often run within left and right, not between them. Ahmari against French was one conservative against another.

[beat]

Two. The traditionalist case: conserve what remains, and rebuild civil society from below. Its voices include Deneen and Levin.

[beat]

Three. The classical-liberal case: state power is the central threat to economic and political freedom. Its voices include George Will.

[beat]

Four. The national-conservative case: cultural conservation requires winning political power, and using it. Its voices include Hazony and Ahmari.

[beat]

And five. The sorting question: who settles cultural questions, and how? Put it to each stream, and the three come apart. They come apart again on tariffs, on immigration, and on the role of the state.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Fusionism held the streams together by lowering the stakes of their disagreements. Can any new synthesis do for these streams what Meyer's fusionism did for their predecessors? Or does one stream simply win?

A defensible position within the tradition now requires three things. A view on which stream best fits current circumstances, or on what synthesis is achievable. A view on the issues where the streams diverge. And an honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

The next lecture widens the lens. It examines how the conservative tradition relates to the other major American political traditions.

For now, go back to the summer of 2019, and a conservative world arguing about one of its own. [beat] It was not a quarrel between left and right. It was a family argument. And you can now state all three sides of it, each in its own strongest terms.
