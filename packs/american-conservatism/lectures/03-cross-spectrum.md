February 2025. Two senators introduce a bill together.

One is Bernie Sanders, the Senate's only democratic socialist. The other is Josh Hawley, one of its loudest national conservatives.

Their bill proposes exactly what its name says. The 10 Percent Credit Card Interest Rate Cap Act would set a hard federal ceiling on credit-card interest. And it would fulfill, of all things, a Trump campaign promise.

The banking lobby objected within days.

[pause]

The standard ideological map objected too, without a word. A socialist and a conservative, co-sponsoring a usury cap. [beat] On that map, it is a paradox.

But it is a paradox only if you believe the American left-right axis is as wide as political rhetoric suggests. It is more compressed than that. Conservatives and their critics share more substantive premises than either side acknowledges. And the deepest disagreements often run within traditions, not between them.

So this lecture maps borders, not battle lines.

Last time, we stayed inside the family, and listened to three streams of conservatism argue. This time we walk the family's borders. By the end, you should be able to say what conservatism shares with each neighboring tradition, and where the dividing line falls. And you should be able to tell American conservatism from the European kind.

We will take it in four steps. First, the border with progressivism. Second, two quieter borders: with libertarianism, and with communitarianism. Third, a border with Europe's conservatives. And last, the places where the borders are being crossed right now.

## The border with progressivism

Begin with the neighbor conservatism fights most loudly. Despite the political conflict, conservatism and American progressivism share a surprising amount. Three things.

First, a critique of laissez-faire individualism. Traditionalists and progressives are both critical of the atomization that pure classical liberalism produces. In 2017, Mark Lilla published The Once and Future Liberal, written from the progressive side. It makes substantially the same critique that Patrick Deneen makes from the traditionalist side. Both worry that pure individualism dissolves the institutions that give life meaning.

Second, concern about the working class. National conservatives and pro-labor progressives share more economic prescriptions with each other than either shares with classical-liberal Republicans. Industrial policy. Transfers that support families. Skills-based education. Restraints on financialization. Those are bipartisan in substance, even when the vehicle is partisan.

Third, skepticism of unaccountable expertise. Populist conservatives and progressives are both skeptical of technocratic governance that bypasses democratic accountability. The objects of that skepticism differ. Conservatives focus on universities and bureaucratic agencies. Progressives focus on corporate power and Wall Street. The underlying concern is similar.

[pause]

So what divides them? Here is the primary disagreement of substance. Listen for the verb, because each side gets the same one.

Progressivism generally treats traditional institutions as objects of critique and reform. Family structures. Religious communities. Gender roles.

Conservatism generally treats them as objects of conservation.

> The dividing line: are traditional institutions objects of reform, or of conservation?

In plainer words: both sides are looking at the same institutions. One side's first question is what to reform. The other's is what to conserve.

This lecture will not settle that disagreement. So try something harder. Whichever side of that line you find less convincing, state its best case, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The border with libertarianism

Now two borders where the fighting is quieter. The first is with libertarianism.

The classical-liberal stream of conservatism and libertarianism share four things. A strong commitment to free markets and free trade. Skepticism of state power. Federalism and constitutional limitation. And individual liberty as a high-priority value.

> The classical-liberal stream overlaps with libertarianism so heavily that the distinction sometimes blurs.

The traditionalist and national-conservative streams stand further from libertarianism than the classical-liberal stream does.

So where do conservatives and libertarians diverge? In three places.

On institutions. Conservatives hold that family, religion and community matter independent of voluntary choice. Libertarians hold that such institutions are instrumentally valuable, to the extent that individuals voluntarily commit to them.

On law. Conservatives generally support some level of social conservatism in law, such as abortion restrictions. Libertarians do not.

And on the world. Conservatives generally support some level of foreign-policy engagement and immigration regulation. Libertarians lean toward non-intervention and openness.

Take the first of those three. Let's test it.

A century-old system of fraternal lodges is dying. A libertarian and a Burkean conservative both oppose federal money to rescue it. Same conclusion. So what distinguishes their reasoning? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The libertarian values the lodges only insofar as individuals freely choose them. They are worth exactly what freely choosing individuals will give them.

The Burkean thinks their accumulated practice has worth independent of current choices. The lodges are repositories of accumulated wisdom. So their decay is a civilizational loss, a real cost, even when every exit was voluntary and no one was coerced.

That is why the conservative will pour energy into a rescue outside the state, one that a strict libertarian would never prioritize.

Identical votes can conceal opposite premises. [beat] Watch for that distinction. It predicts where the two traditions split, on everything from zoning to social media.

One more thing about this border. The blur is not special to it. From the other flank, a book by John Tomasi, Free Market Fairness, shows the same blur at the boundary between libertarianism and egalitarian liberalism. Take that as evidence of something general.

> Every border on the ideological map is a gradient, not a wall.

## The border with communitarianism

The second quiet border is with communitarianism. Here the overlap is substantial, particularly with the traditionalist stream.

The communitarians include Charles Taylor, Michael Sandel, Amitai Etzioni and Daniel Bell. They make many of the same arguments the traditionalists make. Four of them.

Individual identity is constituted by community membership. It does not come before it.

Civic and religious institutions are necessary for human flourishing.

Pure rights-based liberalism produces atomized, alienated individuals.

And public policy should support family, neighborhood, and voluntary association.

So where is the difference? Mostly in vocabulary, and in political alignment. Communitarianism emerged from the political left, in the 1980s and 90s, and tends to be aligned with progressive policy commitments. Traditionalist conservatism tends to be aligned with the right. But the philosophical commitments are closely related.

> The traditionalist stream and communitarianism differ mostly in vocabulary and political alignment.

Here is a standard to hold yourself to. Three books sit on a continuum that crosses partisan lines: Reno's Return of the Strong Gods, Hazony's Conservatism: A Rediscovery, and Sandel's Democracy's Discontent. If you can place a book on that continuum from its argument alone, without checking the author's politics, you understand this map.

Try it on a pair from earlier. I named two writers who make substantially the same critique of individualism, from opposite sides. Who wrote from which side?

[long pause]

Mark Lilla wrote from the progressive side. Patrick Deneen wrote from the traditionalist side. Substantially the same critique, from across the partisan line.

## A border with Europe

Now a border of a different kind. European conservative parties differ from American conservatives. Think of the Christian democracy tradition in Germany, the Conservatives in the U.K., the Republicans in France. Four differences.

The welfare state. European conservatism generally accepts a large one. American conservatism has historically opposed its expansion, with national conservatives as partial exceptions.

The state and the economy. European conservatism is comfortable with industrial policy and corporatist arrangements. American conservatism has had a free-market default since the fusionist era.

Religious roots. European conservatism is traditionally Catholic or Anglican, and often comfortable with established churches. American conservatism is heavily influenced by Protestant evangelicalism, and its baseline is disestablishment.

And the constitutional frame. European conservatives work in parliamentary systems of varied structure. American conservatism is shaped by federalism, and by the Constitution's structure for protecting rights.

Put those four together, and a pattern emerges.

> American conservatism is, in several respects, the historical outlier.

What Americans call conservatism is fused to free-market economics, and to constitutional anti-statism.

Why does that matter? Because it shapes what conservative policy looks like in each place. And because American national conservatives, Hazony and Vance among them, look across that border on purpose. The European traditions they find there do not map cleanly onto American free-market defaults.

> American national conservatives are often deliberately drawing on European conservative traditions.

Let's test it. A German Christian Democrat campaigns on generous family allowances, industrial policy for strategic sectors, and public respect for the church. An American commentator calls the platform "big-government liberalism."

What is the commentator getting wrong? Think it through.

[long pause]

Categories, not facts. By European standards, that is a thoroughly conservative platform. Christian Democracy has long accepted a large welfare state and corporatist economics, in defense of family, faith and social order.

The American equation of conservatism with free-market minimalism is a feature of the fusionist settlement, after 1955. It is not a feature of conservatism as such. That is exactly the historical point American national conservatives now press, and it is why they borrow so freely from European models.

## Where the borders are crossed

Last step. Five places where the partisan alignment is misleading.

Big Tech. Populist conservatives and progressive Democrats have both supported antitrust action against the major technology platforms. The vehicle varies: a Senator Klobuchar bill, a Senator Hawley bill. The substantive concern is shared.

Large-scale finance. Go back to the two senators from the opening. Their interest-rate cap is one of several overlapping proposals on credit-card rates and banking regulation. The two senators' constituencies differ. On these specific bills, their policy is nearly identical.

Industrial policy. The CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 was an explicit industrial-policy bill. It passed with Republican votes in both chambers, backed by center-left Democrats, by national conservatives, and even by some classical-liberal Republicans concerned specifically about China.

One caution. The industrial provisions of the Inflation Reduction Act passed on party lines.

> Convergence in substance is not always convergence in votes.

Family policy shows the same gap. Expanding the child tax credit has bipartisan backing as policy, even when it does not have bipartisan vote totals. In 2024, a bipartisan tax bill included credit provisions that both parties' policy teams supported. It fell short on cloture.

And trade with China. Restraint-leaning conservatives and progressive advocates of economic justice have converged on a more skeptical view of free trade with China. Their reasons overlap in part: national security, working-class wages, the resilience of supply chains.

[pause]

Be exact about what those five show. They do not mean the traditions agree at the philosophical level. They mean that the policy landscape, viewed substantively, is more cross-cut than the partisan landscape suggests.

And survey research backs the map. In 2021, the Pew Research Center built a political typology from a survey of more than 10,000 adults, not from pundits' categories. It sorted the public into nine groups. Four of them were Republican-oriented: Faith and Flag Conservatives, Committed Conservatives, the Populist Right, and the Ambivalent Right.

Those four disagree with each other on trade, on immigration, and on the size of government, in ways that track the streams from last time remarkably well. And Pew found its populist cluster notably more critical of large corporations than the rest of the coalition. On that question, it sat closer to voters across the aisle.

The conservative voter, like the conservative tradition, turns out to be a coalition.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The American left-right axis is more compressed than political rhetoric suggests. Every border on the ideological map is a gradient, not a wall.

[beat]

Two. With progressivism, conservatism shares a critique of individualism, a concern about the working class, and a skepticism of unaccountable expertise. The dividing line: are traditional institutions objects of reform, or of conservation?

[beat]

Three. The classical-liberal stream overlaps with libertarianism so heavily that the distinction sometimes blurs. The traditionalist stream and communitarianism differ mostly in vocabulary and political alignment. And identical votes can conceal opposite premises.

[beat]

Four. American conservatism is, in several respects, the historical outlier, fused to free-market economics and constitutional anti-statism. And American national conservatives are often deliberately drawing on European conservative traditions.

[beat]

And five. On Big Tech, finance, industrial policy, family policy and trade with China, the borders are being crossed. But convergence in substance is not always convergence in votes.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Are those convergences durable realignments, or marriages of convenience? And can any tradition's account of community, market and nation absorb its rivals' best points without dissolving?

That argument is still open, and it is being conducted in public, in real time.

This is the last lecture of the series, so there is no next one to point to. There is only where the argument goes from here.

A defensible position about American conservatism, whether you identify with the tradition or not, requires three things. A view on which stream best fits current conditions, or on what synthesis is achievable. A view on how the tradition relates to its critics: where the strongest critiques bite, and where they miss. And an honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson has a place to declare a position of your own. It also has a drill that pushes you to construct the strongest version of the views you do not hold. That discipline is where defended positions become refined ones.

For now, go back to February 2025. A socialist and a national conservative, with one bill between them. [beat] It looked like a paradox. It was a border. And you now know how to find the others.
