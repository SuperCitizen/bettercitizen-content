# How conservatism relates to other traditions

In February 2025, the Senate's only democratic socialist and one of its loudest national conservatives introduced a bill together. [[person:bernie-sanders]] and [[person:josh-hawley]]'s 10 Percent Credit Card Interest Rate Cap Act proposed exactly what its name says — a hard federal ceiling on credit-card interest, fulfilling, of all things, a Trump campaign promise. The banking lobby objected within days. The standard ideological map objected implicitly: a socialist and a conservative co-sponsoring a usury cap is a paradox only if you believe the American left-right axis is as wide as political rhetoric suggests. It is more compressed than that. Conservatives and their critics share more substantive premises than either side acknowledges, and the deepest disagreements often run *within* traditions rather than between them — which is why this lesson maps borders rather than battle lines.

## What conservatism shares with progressivism

Conservatism and [[ideology:american-progressivism]] share a surprising amount, despite the political conflict:

- **Critique of laissez-faire individualism.** Both traditionalists and progressives are critical of the individualist atomization that pure classical liberalism produces. Mark Lilla's *The Once and Future Liberal* (2017) — written from the progressive side — makes substantially the same critique that Patrick Deneen makes from the traditionalist side. Both worry that pure individualism dissolves the institutions that give life meaning.
- **Concern about the working class.** National conservatives and pro-labor progressives share more economic policy prescriptions than either shares with classical-liberal Republicans. Industrial policy, family-supporting transfers, skills-based education, restraints on financialization — these are bipartisan in substantive terms even when partisan in vehicle.
- **Skepticism of unaccountable expertise.** Both populist conservatives and progressives are skeptical of technocratic governance that bypasses democratic accountability. The objects of skepticism differ (conservatives focus on universities and bureaucratic agencies; progressives focus on corporate power and Wall Street) but the underlying concern is similar.

What divides them: progressivism generally treats traditional institutions (family structures, religious communities, gender roles) as objects of critique and reform; conservatism generally treats them as objects of conservation. This is the primary substantive disagreement.

## What conservatism shares with libertarianism

The classical-liberal stream of conservatism overlaps so heavily with [[ideology:libertarianism]] that the distinction is sometimes blurred. Both share:

- Strong commitment to free markets and free trade.
- Skepticism of state power.
- Federalism and constitutional limitation.
- Individual liberty as a high-priority value.

Where they diverge:

- Conservatives believe institutions (family, religion, community) matter independent of voluntary choice; libertarians treat all such institutions as instrumentally valuable to the extent individuals voluntarily commit to them.
- Conservatives generally support some level of social conservatism in law (e.g., abortion restrictions); libertarians do not.
- Conservatives generally support some level of foreign-policy engagement and immigration regulation; libertarians lean toward non-intervention and openness.

The traditionalist and national-conservative streams are further from libertarianism than the classical-liberal stream is. From the other flank, John Tomasi's *Free Market Fairness* (2012) shows the same blur at the boundary between libertarianism and egalitarian liberalism — evidence that every border on the ideological map is a gradient, not a wall.

## What conservatism shares with communitarianism

Substantial overlap, particularly with the traditionalist stream. [[ideology:communitarianism]] (Charles Taylor, [[person:michael-sandel]], Amitai Etzioni, Daniel Bell) makes many of the same arguments the traditionalists make:

- Individual identity is constituted by community membership, not prior to it.
- Civic and religious institutions are necessary for human flourishing.
- Pure rights-based liberalism produces atomized, alienated individuals.
- Public policy should support family, neighborhood, voluntary association.

The differences are mostly in vocabulary and political alignment. Communitarianism emerged from the political left in the 1980s-90s and tends to be aligned with progressive policy commitments; traditionalist conservatism tends to be aligned with the right. But the substantive philosophical commitments are closely related.

Reno's *Return of the Strong Gods*, Hazony's *Conservatism: A Rediscovery*, and Sandel's *Democracy's Discontent* sit on a continuum that crosses partisan lines. If you can place a book on that continuum from its argument alone — without checking the author's politics — you understand this lesson.

## "American" vs. "European" conservatism

European conservative parties — the [[ideology:christian-democracy]] tradition in Germany, Conservatives in the U.K., Republicans in France — differ from American conservatives in ways the following table compresses:

| Dimension | European conservatism | American conservatism |
|---|---|---|
| Welfare state | Generally accepts large welfare states | Has historically opposed expansion (national conservatives are partial exceptions) |
| State and economy | Comfortable with industrial policy and corporatist arrangements | Free-market default since the fusionist era |
| Religious roots | Traditionally Catholic or Anglican; often comfortable with established churches | Heavily influenced by Protestant evangelicalism; disestablishment baseline |
| Constitutional frame | Parliamentary systems, varied structures | Shaped by U.S. federalism and the Constitution's rights-protection structure |

Read the table column by column and a pattern emerges: what Americans call "conservatism" is in several respects the historical outlier, fused as it is to free-market economics and constitutional anti-statism. These differences matter because they shape what "conservative" policy looks like in each context — and because national conservatives in the U.S. (Hazony, Vance) are often deliberately drawing on European conservative traditions that don't map cleanly onto American free-market defaults.

## Current points of cross-ideological convergence

A few areas where the partisan alignment is misleading:

**Big Tech regulation.** Both populist conservatives and progressive Democrats have supported antitrust action against major technology platforms. The vehicle (a Senator Klobuchar bill, a Senator Hawley bill) varies but the substantive concern is shared.

**Skepticism of large-scale finance.** The Sanders–Hawley interest-rate cap from this lesson's opening is one of several overlapping proposals on credit-card rates and banking regulation. The two senators' constituencies differ; on these specific bills, their policy is nearly identical.

**Industrial policy + supply-chain reshoring.** The CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 — an explicit industrial-policy bill — passed with Republican votes in both chambers, backed by center-left Democrats, national conservatives, and even some classical-liberal Republicans concerned specifically about China. (The Inflation Reduction Act's industrial provisions, by contrast, passed on party lines — a reminder that convergence in substance is not always convergence in votes.)

**Family policy.** [[issue:child-tax-credit]] expansion has bipartisan policy backing, even when it doesn't have bipartisan vote totals. The 2024 bipartisan tax bill (which fell short on cloture) included CTC provisions both parties' substance teams supported.

**Trade with China.** Both restraint-leaning conservatives and progressive economic-justice advocates have converged on a more skeptical view of free trade with China, for partially overlapping reasons (national security, working-class wages, supply-chain resilience).

These convergences don't mean the traditions agree at the philosophical level. They mean that the policy landscape, viewed substantively, is more cross-cut than the partisan landscape suggests.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position about American conservatism — whether you identify with the tradition or not — requires:

1. A view on **which stream of the tradition best fits current conditions** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **how the tradition relates to its critics** — where the strongest critiques bite and where they miss.
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

The Coach in this app will engage you on each of these. The Mirror will save your declared position; the steel-man drill will push you to construct the strongest version of the views you don't hold.

A useful next step: declare a position at `/me/positions/new` with a slug like `conservatism-stream` (which stream best fits current conditions?) or `conservatism-vs-x` (where does conservatism best engage with another tradition?). The discipline of having to construct the strongest version of a view you reject is where defended positions become refined ones.

What this lesson did *not* settle: whether the convergences above are durable realignments or marriages of convenience — and whether any tradition's account of community, market, and nation can absorb its rivals' best points without dissolving. That is the live argument, and it is being conducted in public, in real time.
