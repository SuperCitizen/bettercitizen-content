# Internal disagreements within the tradition

On May 29, 2019, the op-ed editor of the *New York Post*, Sohrab Ahmari, published a short essay in *First Things* titled "Against David French-ism." Its target was not a progressive. David French was a *National Review* writer, an evangelical, a religious-liberty litigator with a long pro-life record — by any checklist, a conservative in excellent standing. That was Ahmari's point. French's style of conservatism — procedural, civil, committed to neutral rules that protect everyone's liberty equally — was, Ahmari argued, unilateral disarmament in a culture war: "civility and decency are secondary values," and what the moment required was fighting to win. The conservative world spent that summer arguing about one of its own, including packed public debates between the two men that fall. Nothing in recent memory better illustrates the rule this lesson teaches: the bitterest arguments in American politics often run not between left and right but *within* them.

Conservatism is a family of positions. The post-WWII fusionist synthesis held the family together for decades, but it has fractured in important ways since 2016. This lesson examines the three major streams as adherents would themselves describe them — and then lets each stream prosecute its case against the other two.

## 1. The traditionalist stream

Core commitment: cultural and religious continuity. The institutions, customs, and beliefs inherited from prior generations are presumptively good and should be preserved unless shown to be substantially flawed. Change should be incremental and respectful of what's being changed.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Patrick Deneen, R. R. Reno, [[person:yuval-levin]] (in part), Rod Dreher, the *First Things* circle, the Front Porch Republic. Deneen's wing of the stream shades into outright [[ideology:post-liberalism]] — the view that the liberal experiment itself, and not merely its progressive variant, has failed.

Key claims:

- The progressive project of subjecting all inherited institutions to rational scrutiny tends to dissolve them rather than improve them. Marriage, family, religious community, and cultural traditions are particularly vulnerable. Once dissolved, they don't reconstitute easily.
- Classical liberalism — even in its conservative variant — tends to atomize society. Its emphasis on individual rights, market exchange, and procedural neutrality hollows out the civic and religious institutions that give life meaning. Deneen's *Why Liberalism Failed* (2018) is the canonical statement.
- Civic and religious institutions have been weakening for decades; the trend is not natural drift but the consequence of policy choices that prioritized individual mobility over community continuity.
- The right response is not state-driven cultural traditionalism (which is mostly impossible) but conservation of remaining institutions and the patient work of rebuilding civil society. Levin's *A Time to Build* (2020) makes this explicit.

## 2. The classical-liberal (free-market) stream

Core commitment: limited government, individual liberty, free markets, the rule of law. Economic freedom is a foundation of political freedom; state power is the central threat to both. Government's job is to protect property, enforce contracts, defend against violence, and otherwise let the productive activities of free people produce broad prosperity.

Contemporary intellectual figures: [[person:george-will]], Charles Cooke, Kevin Williamson, the libertarian wing of the Cato Institute (though that's largely separate from conservatism), the Reason Foundation (similar caveat), and the older National Review and Wall Street Journal editorial-page tradition.

Key claims:

- Free markets have been the most powerful force for poverty reduction in human history. Distorting them through regulation, subsidies, or industrial policy generally produces less wealth, not more.
- [[term:federalism]] and constitutional structure are the right framework for cultural disagreements. Different states and communities can adopt different policies; people can sort themselves; no national majority needs to impose its cultural preferences on everyone.
- Free trade has produced enormous gains for American consumers and for the global poor. Its concentrated losses for some industries are real but addressable through transition assistance, not through tariff-led re-industrialization.
- Immigration is on net a positive for the U.S. economy and for American dynamism. Restriction-leaning immigration policy is, on this view, in tension with conservative economic principles.
- The state should not promote particular religious or cultural frameworks. Will's *The Conservative Sensibility* (2019) argues that classical liberalism is genuinely compatible with conservative virtue — perhaps more so than traditionalism.

## 3. The civic-republican / national-conservative stream

Core commitment: the political community as a unit. The nation-state — its borders, its culture, its families, its industrial base — has legitimate claims that markets and abstract principles don't trump. Government action in service of national solidarity, family, and productive industry is legitimate and sometimes necessary.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Yoram Hazony, Sohrab Ahmari, Gladden Pappin, Adrian Vermeule (in some moods), the *American Affairs* circle, [[person:jd-vance]] (politically), Marco Rubio (in some speeches), Reihan Salam.

Key claims:

- The nation is the natural unit of political life. Hazony's *Conservatism: A Rediscovery* (2022) makes this case at book length: liberalism's universalism — including conservative classical liberalism — disregards the particular bonds of nation, language, and shared history that make political life possible.
- Government should support the family, industry, and the working class. Free-market orthodoxy has produced a generation of stagnant working-class wages, declining family formation, and de-industrialization. Tariffs, [[issue:industrial-policy]], [[issue:child-tax-credit]] expansion, and skills-based immigration are appropriate state actions.
- Cultural conservatism requires political success. Pure cultural traditionalism, in retreat from politics, leaves the institutions of state to progressive capture. The right response is to win political contests and use government legitimately to support traditional families, civic education, and productive industry.
- The post-1990s conservative coalition's commitment to economic-liberty orthodoxy was a strategic error. It cost the working class and produced the populist insurgency that the older establishment blames on others. National conservatism is the necessary correction.

## The argument, joined

Summaries flatten. The fastest way to feel the disagreements is to let each stream make its case against the other two — steel-manned, as its own writers would put it.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Traditionalist">
    **Against the classical liberals.** You are too focused on economic and political
    liberty and too comfortable with the cultural change markets bring. A community
    hollowed out by a closed factory or an attention economy is just as hollowed out
    as one regulated into dependency — yet your framework can only see the second
    harm. Markets are a good servant and a bad master.

    **Against the national conservatives.** The state is too blunt an instrument for
    cultural renewal and too easily captured — whatever cultural machinery you build
    in Washington, the next administration inherits. The real work is closer to the
    parish, the family, and the local school than to any federal department, and it
    cannot be won in an election because it was not lost in one.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Classical liberal">
    **Against the traditionalists.** You too easily accept government action when it
    serves cultural ends you favor — but the precedent does not stay yours. The
    conservation of institutions is best done by free people in free markets and free
    associations; a tradition that needs the state's scaffolding has already died.

    **Against the national conservatives.** You have traded the movement's core
    commitments — free markets, free trade, a generally welcoming immigration
    policy — for short-term political alignment. Tariffs and industrial policy will
    be captured by the well-connected, as they always are, and the result is a
    "conservatism" that conserves nothing of conservative substance.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="National conservative">
    **Against the traditionalists.** Withdrawal is surrender. Cultural preservation
    requires political power; political power requires winning elections; winning
    requires a coalition that includes the working class your localism cannot reach.
    While you rebuild one parish, the institutions of state educate everyone's
    children.

    **Against the classical liberals.** You confuse one historical variant — the
    post-WWII fusionist settlement — with conservatism as such. The older tradition,
    Burke included in his economic moments, was far more comfortable with state
    action in service of national interests. Your orthodoxy cost the working class
    its towns and wages, and the populist insurgency you lament is the bill arriving.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## Where each stream lands on specific issues

| Issue | Traditionalist | Classical liberal | National conservative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tariffs | Skeptical (state action) | Strongly opposed | Generally support |
| Immigration | Restrictionist on cultural grounds | Generally liberal | Skills-based + restrictionist |
| Industrial policy | Skeptical | Strongly opposed | Generally support |
| Same-sex marriage | Opposed; would prefer state-by-state | Mostly accept; state-by-state preferred | Opposed; willing to use federal power |
| Foreign policy | Selective restraint | Selective engagement | Selective realism |
| Welfare state | Pro-family; supports family-supporting transfers | Generally skeptical of expansion | Pro-worker; supports family-supporting transfers |
| School choice | Strongly support | Generally support | Strongly support |
| Federal regulation | Skeptical | Strongly opposed | Mixed (pro-industrial-policy regulation) |
| Constitutional originalism | Strong support | Strong support | Generally support but qualified |

This is a flattened summary; reality is more complicated. But the rough contours are:

- Classical liberals are most comfortable with markets, trade, and immigration.
- National conservatives are most comfortable with state action on industry and family.
- Traditionalists are uncomfortable with both extremes and emphasize institutional rebuilding.

  bills like this are built to split the old coalition.

## What this means for political coalitions

The fusionist synthesis held the streams together by minimizing the stakes of their disagreements. When the unifying enemy was Communism, when free trade was producing visible economic gains, and when cultural change was slow, the streams could coexist within a single party.

The post-2008 economic shocks, the post-2016 political realignment, and the post-2020 cultural intensification have made the disagreements more visible. The current Republican coalition is not fusionist — it's a contested mixture of all three streams plus populist elements that don't fit cleanly in any of them.

A defensible position within the conservative tradition now requires:

1. A view on **which stream best fits current circumstances** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **what to do about specific issues** where the streams diverge (trade, immigration, industrial policy).
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

What this lesson did *not* settle: whether any new synthesis can do for these streams what Meyer's fusionism did for their predecessors — or whether one stream simply wins. The next lesson widens the lens, examining how the conservative tradition relates to the other major American political traditions.
