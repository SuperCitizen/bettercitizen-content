# Internal disagreements within the tradition

Conservatism is a family of positions. The post-WWII fusionist synthesis held them together for decades but has fractured in important ways since 2016. This lesson examines the three major streams as adherents would themselves describe them.

## 1. The traditionalist stream

Core commitment: cultural and religious continuity. The institutions, customs, and beliefs inherited from prior generations are presumptively good and should be preserved unless shown to be substantially flawed. Change should be incremental and respectful of what's being changed.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Patrick Deneen, R. R. Reno, Yuval Levin (in part), Rod Dreher, the *First Things* circle, the Front Porch Republic.

Key claims:
- The progressive project of subjecting all inherited institutions to rational scrutiny tends to dissolve them rather than improve them. Marriage, family, religious community, and cultural traditions are particularly vulnerable. Once dissolved, they don't reconstitute easily.
- Classical liberalism — even in its conservative variant — tends to atomize society. Its emphasis on individual rights, market exchange, and procedural neutrality hollows out the civic and religious institutions that give life meaning. Deneen's *Why Liberalism Failed* is the canonical statement.
- Civic and religious institutions have been weakening for decades; the trend is not natural drift but the consequence of policy choices that prioritized individual mobility over community continuity.
- The right response is not state-driven cultural traditionalism (which is mostly impossible) but conservation of remaining institutions and the patient work of rebuilding civil society. Levin's *A Time to Build* makes this explicit.

Disagreements with classical liberals: classical liberals are too focused on economic and political liberty and too comfortable with cultural change. They tend to support markets even when markets erode community.

Disagreements with national conservatives: national conservatives are too willing to use state power for cultural ends; the state is too blunt an instrument and too easily captured. The traditionalist work is closer to the parish, the family, the local school — not the federal Department of Education.

## 2. The classical-liberal (free-market) stream

Core commitment: limited government, individual liberty, free markets, the rule of law. Economic freedom is a foundation of political freedom; state power is the central threat to both. Government's job is to protect property, enforce contracts, defend against violence, and otherwise let the productive activities of free people produce broad prosperity.

Contemporary intellectual figures: George Will, Charles Cooke, Kevin Williamson, the libertarian wing of the Cato Institute (though that's largely separate from conservatism), the Reason Foundation (similar caveat), and the older National Review and Wall Street Journal editorial-page tradition.

Key claims:
- Free markets have been the most powerful force for poverty reduction in human history. Distorting them through regulation, subsidies, or industrial policy generally produces less wealth, not more.
- Federalism and constitutional structure are the right framework for cultural disagreements. Different states and communities can adopt different policies; people can sort themselves; no national majority needs to impose its cultural preferences on everyone.
- Free trade has produced enormous gains for American consumers and for the global poor. Its concentrated losses for some industries are real but addressable through transition assistance, not through tariff-led re-industrialization.
- Immigration is on net a positive for the U.S. economy and for American dynamism. Restriction-leaning immigration policy is, on this view, in tension with conservative economic principles.
- The state should not promote particular religious or cultural frameworks. Will's *The Conservative Sensibility* argues that classical liberalism is genuinely compatible with conservative virtue — perhaps more so than traditionalism.

Disagreements with traditionalists: traditionalists too easily accept government action when it serves cultural ends. The conservation of institutions is best done by free people in free markets and free associations, not by state intervention.

Disagreements with national conservatives: national conservatives have abandoned core conservative commitments (free markets, free trade, immigration generally welcoming) for short-term political alignment. The result is a "conservatism" that conserves nothing of conservative substance.

## 3. The civic-republican / national-conservative stream

Core commitment: the political community as a unit. The nation-state — its borders, its culture, its families, its industrial base — has legitimate claims that markets and abstract principles don't trump. Government action in service of national solidarity, family, and productive industry is legitimate and sometimes necessary.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Yoram Hazony, Sohrab Ahmari, Gladden Pappin, Adrian Vermeule (in some moods), the *American Affairs* circle, J. D. Vance (politically), Marco Rubio (in some speeches), Reihan Salam.

Key claims:
- The nation is the natural unit of political life. Hazony's *Conservatism: A Rediscovery* makes this case at book length: liberalism's universalism — including conservative classical liberalism — disregards the particular bonds of nation, language, and shared history that make political life possible.
- Government should support the family, industry, and the working class. Free-market orthodoxy has produced a generation of stagnant working-class wages, declining family formation, and de-industrialization. Tariffs, industrial policy, child-tax-credit expansion, and skills-based immigration are appropriate state actions.
- Cultural conservatism requires political success. Pure cultural traditionalism, in retreat from politics, leaves the institutions of state to progressive capture. The right response is to win political contests and use government legitimately to support traditional families, civic education, and productive industry.
- The post-1990s conservative coalition's commitment to economic-liberty orthodoxy was a strategic error. It cost the working class and produced the populist insurgency that the older establishment blames on others. National conservatism is the necessary correction.

Disagreements with traditionalists: traditionalists are too withdrawn from political contest. The work of cultural preservation requires political power, which requires engagement, which requires a coalition that includes the working class. Pure traditionalism is a form of surrender.

Disagreements with classical liberals: classical liberals confuse one variant of conservatism (the post-WWII fusionist) with conservatism as such. The older conservative tradition — including Burke himself in his economic moments — was much more comfortable with state action in service of national interests than postwar American conservatism became.

## Where each stream lands on specific issues

| Issue | Traditionalist | Classical liberal | National conservative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tariffs | Skeptical (state action) | Strongly opposed | Generally support |
| Immigration | Restrictionist on cultural grounds | Generally liberal | Skills-based + restrictionist |
| Industrial policy | Skeptical | Strongly opposed | Generally support |
| Same-sex marriage | Opposed; would prefer state-by-state | Mostly accept; state-by-state preferred | Opposed; willing to use federal power |
| Foreign policy | Selective restraint | Selective engagement | Selective realism |
| Welfare state | Pro-family; supports family-supporting transfers | Generally skeptical of expansion | Pro-worker; supports family-supporting transfers |
| School choice | Strongly support | Generally support | Strongly support |
| Federal regulation | Skeptical | Strongly opposed | Mixed (pro-industrial-policy regulation) |
| Constitutional originalism | Strong support | Strong support | Generally support but qualified |

This is a flattened summary; reality is more complicated. But the rough contours are:
- Classical liberals are most comfortable with markets, trade, and immigration.
- National conservatives are most comfortable with state action on industry and family.
- Traditionalists are uncomfortable with both extremes and emphasize institutional rebuilding.

## What this means for political coalitions

The fusionist synthesis held the streams together by minimizing the stakes of their disagreements. When the unifying enemy was Communism, when free trade was producing visible economic gains, and when cultural change was slow, the streams could coexist within a single party.

The post-2008 economic shocks, the post-2016 political realignment, and the post-2020 cultural intensification have made the disagreements more visible. The current Republican coalition is not fusionist — it's a contested mixture of all three streams plus populist elements that don't fit cleanly in any of them.

A defensible position within the conservative tradition now requires:

1. A view on **which stream best fits current circumstances** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **what to do about specific issues** where the streams diverge (trade, immigration, industrial policy).
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
