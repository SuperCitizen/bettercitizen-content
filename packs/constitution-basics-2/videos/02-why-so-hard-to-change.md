## The paper that got a C

In 1982, a college student in Texas named Gregory Watson found an amendment that Congress had sent to the states in 1789. It carried no deadline, and he argued in a paper that it could still be ratified. He received a C.

[beat]

He then spent ten years writing to state legislators. On May 7, 1992, the proposal became the Twenty-seventh Amendment.

- Proposed 1789, ratified 1992

> The story is charming, and it is a warning: successful amendments are rare.

## Two supermajorities in a row

The rule is Article Five of the Constitution. An ordinary law needs a majority in each chamber of Congress. An amendment needs two-thirds of each chamber merely to be proposed, and then the agreement of three-fourths of the states, which today means 38 of 50.

- Propose: two-thirds of each chamber
- Ratify: 38 of 50 states

The President neither signs nor vetoes a proposed amendment. Article Five gives the President no role.

- No role for the President

## Thirteen states can say no

Turn the requirement around. If 38 states must agree, how many are enough to block?

[pause]

Thirteen, however many people live in the other thirty-seven.

> Thirteen states are enough to block any amendment.

Members of Congress have introduced about 12,000 proposals to amend the Constitution since 1789, and 27 have been ratified.

- About 12,000 proposed, 27 ratified

## Too hard, or hard enough

The founders themselves disagreed about whether this is right. James Madison's worry was that a constitution reopened too often would lose the respect that makes people obey it even when they lose an argument. Thomas Jefferson's worry was the opposite, that a constitution placed beyond reach would bind the living to the judgments of the dead.

- Madison: reopened too often, respect is lost
- Jefferson: the living bound by the dead

> Which danger do you take more seriously, Madison's or Jefferson's?
