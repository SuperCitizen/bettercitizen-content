# Why is the Constitution so hard to change?

In 1982, a college student in Texas named Gregory Watson was researching a paper when he came across an amendment that Congress had sent to the states in 1789. It barred any pay raise for members of Congress from taking effect until after the next election. Too few states had ratified it, and it had been forgotten for most of two centuries.

Watson noticed that the proposal carried no deadline, and he argued in his paper that it could still be ratified. He received a C. He then spent ten years writing to state legislators, and on May 7, 1992, the proposal became the Twenty-seventh Amendment, more than 202 years after Congress had approved it. His university changed the grade to an A in 2017.

The story is charming, and it is also a warning about how unusual a successful amendment is. To see why, start with the rule itself.

## The text: Article V

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Article V">

"The Congress, whenever two thirds of both Houses shall deem it necessary, shall propose Amendments to this Constitution, or, on the Application of the Legislatures of two thirds of the several States, shall call a Convention for proposing Amendments, which, in either Case, shall be valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of this Constitution, when ratified by the Legislatures of three fourths of the several States, or by Conventions in three fourths thereof, as the one or the other Mode of Ratification may be proposed by the Congress..."

</Callout>

That single sentence sets up two ways to propose an amendment and two ways to ratify one. Congress can propose by a two-thirds vote of each chamber, or two-thirds of the state legislatures can require Congress to call a convention for the purpose. Either kind of proposal must then be ratified by three-fourths of the states, acting through their legislatures or through conventions, as Congress chooses.

In practice, nearly all the traffic has used one route. No amendment has ever been proposed by a convention, and only the Twenty-first Amendment, which ended Prohibition in 1933, was ratified by state conventions.

<Checkpoint question="Read Article V again and list everyone who has a part in amending the Constitution. Who is missing that you might have expected to find?">

The text names Congress, the state legislatures, and conventions. It does not mention the President, who neither signs nor vetoes a proposed amendment, and it does not mention the courts. Voters are missing too, at least directly: there is no national referendum, and citizens act only through the legislators and delegates they elect.

</Checkpoint>

## Two supermajorities in a row

The difficulty lies in the fractions. [[term:supermajority]] is the term for any requirement above a simple majority, and Article V imposes two of them. An ordinary law needs a majority in each chamber. An amendment needs two-thirds of each chamber merely to be proposed, and then the agreement of three-fourths of the states, which today means 38 of 50.

The framers chose these fractions with a failure in mind. Under the Articles of Confederation, no change could be made unless every state legislature agreed to it, and that rule had left the Articles impossible to repair. Article V was meant to sit between two dangers, as Madison explained in Federalist No. 43: "It guards equally against that extreme facility, which would render the Constitution too mutable; and that extreme difficulty, which might perpetuate its discovered faults."

Whether the framers found the middle is the question the rest of this lesson turns on. Turn the requirement around and its force becomes clearer. Thirteen states are enough to block any amendment, however many people live in the other thirty-seven. A proposal therefore needs support that is broad across regions and across parties, and support of that kind is rare for anything people are still arguing about.

The record bears this out. Members of Congress have introduced about 12,000 proposals to amend the Constitution since 1789, and 27 have been ratified.

When agreement is broad, the process can move quickly. The Twenty-sixth Amendment, which lowered the voting age to 18, was approved by Congress on March 23, 1971, and ratified on July 1, a span of one hundred days. When agreement is missing, nothing moves at all.

## A live dispute: the Equal Rights Amendment

One amendment shows how much can turn on a detail of procedure. In 1972 Congress proposed the Equal Rights Amendment, which would bar the denial of equal rights under the law on account of sex, and it gave the states seven years to ratify. Thirty-five states did so by 1977, three short of the number required. Congress extended the deadline to 1982, and no further state acted before it passed. Five states, meanwhile, had voted to take back their earlier ratifications.

Decades later the count began to move again. Nevada ratified in 2017, Illinois followed in 2018, and Virginia became the thirty-eighth state in January 2020.

Supporters of the amendment argue that it has now met the test the Constitution sets. Article V says an amendment is valid "when ratified" by three-fourths of the states, and it contains no deadline. In their view the time limit, which appeared in the introduction to the proposal and not in the amendment's own text, could not override the Constitution.

Opponents argue that Congress has long had the power to attach a deadline, that this one expired in 1982, and that the three late ratifications came too late to count. In December 2024 the Archivist of the United States, the official who publishes ratified amendments, took that position, stating that the Archivist "cannot legally publish the Equal Rights Amendment."

The dispute is unusual because both sides claim to be following the same text. It also shows why the Twenty-seventh Amendment could wait two centuries: Congress attached no deadline to it at all.

## Should a constitution be hard to change?

The founders themselves disagreed about this, and their disagreement has not been improved upon.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the documents: Madison and Jefferson">

**Madison, Federalist No. 49 (1788):** "as every appeal to the people would carry an implication of some defect in the government, frequent appeals would, in a great measure, deprive the government of that veneration which time bestows on every thing, and without which perhaps the wisest and freest governments would not possess the requisite stability."

**Jefferson, letter to Samuel Kercheval (1816):** "laws and institutions must go hand in hand with the progress of the human mind... we might as well require a man to wear still the coat which fitted him when a boy, as civilized society to remain ever under the regimen of their barbarous ancestors."

</Callout>

Madison's worry was that a constitution reopened too often would lose the respect that makes people obey it even when they lose an argument. Jefferson's worry was the opposite, that a constitution placed beyond reach would bind the living to the judgments of the dead. Jefferson was no advocate of constant revision, and wrote in the same letter that he opposed "frequent & untried changes," but he thought each generation was entitled to repair what experience had shown to be broken.

The political scientist Donald Lutz put numbers to the question in 1994. Comparing constitutions around the world, he found the American one among the most difficult to amend. He also argued that a constitution which cannot easily be amended does not stop changing. The change moves elsewhere, chiefly into the courts, where judges reinterpret words such as "equal protection" as circumstances shift.

## What this lesson does not settle

Whether the shift that Lutz described is a defect or a strength is the open question. On one view, judges end up making choices that supermajorities of citizens were supposed to make. On the other, the arrangement keeps the fundamental rules steady while allowing their application to develop.

Your answer will depend on which danger you take more seriously, Madison's or Jefferson's. The text of Article V has stayed the same since 1787, and so has the argument over whether it asks too much.
