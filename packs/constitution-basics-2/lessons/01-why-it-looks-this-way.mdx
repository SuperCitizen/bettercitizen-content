# Why does the Constitution look the way it does?

In May of 1787, delegates from twelve states shuttered the windows of the Pennsylvania State House and swore one another to secrecy. Their instructions were to revise the Articles of Confederation. By the middle of June they had decided to design a different government altogether, and when they opened the doors in September they carried out a document that few of them considered perfect.

That is the most useful thing to know before reading the Constitution. It was not handed down by people who agreed with one another. It is a record of bargains among delegates who disagreed about nearly everything that mattered, and many of its strangest features mark the places where a bargain was struck.

## The problem on neutral ground

Imagine thirteen schools that want to form a league governed by a single student council. The largest school has 3,000 students and the smallest has 300, and the first question the league must settle is how many votes each school will have.

The large schools propose one vote for every hundred students, so that every student counts the same. The small schools refuse, because under that plan a few large schools could outvote all the rest. They propose one vote for each school instead, and now the large schools refuse, because a school of 300 would have as much say as a school ten times its size.

Neither side is being unreasonable. Each is defending a real principle, equal people on one side and equal members on the other, and the league will not exist at all unless both sides agree to join it.

## The bargain over representation

Replace the schools with states and you have the hardest fight of the convention. Large states such as Virginia wanted seats in Congress distributed by population. Small states such as Delaware wanted every state to have an equal vote, which was the rule under the Articles of Confederation.

The solution was to do both. In the House of Representatives, seats would follow population, and in the Senate, each state would have two members. James Madison, who kept the most complete record of the debates, noted how narrowly the plan was adopted.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Madison's notes, July 16, 1787">

"On the question for agreeing to the whole Report as amended & including the equality of votes in the 2d. branch. it passed in the Affirmative."

Voting aye: Connecticut, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland and North Carolina. Voting no: Pennsylvania, Virginia, South Carolina and Georgia. Massachusetts was divided.

</Callout>

<Checkpoint question="In Madison's tally, Virginia and Pennsylvania vote no, while Delaware and New Jersey vote aye. What does that pattern tell you about who gained from equal votes in the Senate?">

Two of the largest states opposed the plan, and two of the smallest supported it. Equal votes in the Senate were the price the small states demanded for joining a government in which the House would be dominated by the large ones. The bargain is still in force: the least populous state has the same two senators as the most populous.

</Checkpoint>

## The bargains over slavery

Search the Constitution of 1787 for the word "slavery" and you will not find it. The delegates avoided the word, and then wrote three clauses that protected the institution.

<Panels title="Slavery in the text of 1787">
<Panel title="Counting for seats" tone="ink">

*"three fifths of all other Persons"*

Three-fifths of a state's enslaved population counted toward its seats in the House, and so toward its votes for President.

</Panel>
<Panel title="The slave trade" tone="ink">

*"shall not be prohibited by the Congress prior to the Year one thousand eight hundred and eight"*

Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved people for twenty years.

</Panel>
<Panel title="People who escaped" tone="ink">

*"shall be delivered up"*

A person "held to Service or Labour" who fled to another state had to be returned.

</Panel>
</Panels>

</Figure>

The first of these clauses is the one most often misread. It is tempting to assume that opponents of slavery wanted enslaved people counted in full, as a matter of dignity. The record shows the reverse. Delegates from South Carolina moved that enslaved people be counted "equally with the Whites," because a larger count meant more seats in Congress for states that gave those same people no vote and no rights. Several northern delegates argued that they should not be counted at all. Three-fifths was the figure the two sides settled on.

These clauses lasted until the Civil War ended them. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in 1865, and the Fourteenth replaced the three-fifths rule in 1868.

## What the finished document contains

The text that emerged from these bargains is short and plainly organized. A one-sentence Preamble names "We the People" as the source of the government's authority and lists its purposes, without granting any power of its own.

Seven articles follow. The first three create Congress, the presidency and the federal courts. Article IV governs relations among the states, Article V provides for amendments, and Article VI makes the Constitution "the supreme Law of the Land." Article VII, the shortest, states the terms on which the whole design would take effect: approval by conventions in nine states. [[term:supremacy-clause]] is the usual name for the provision in Article VI, and it is the reason a state law that conflicts with the Constitution cannot stand.

## The fight to ratify

[[term:ratification]] by nine states was a real hurdle, and it meant the argument was only beginning. The framers sent the document to specially elected conventions in each state, and for the better part of a year Americans debated it in newspapers, taverns and meeting halls.

The opponents, known as Anti-Federalists, made a serious case. One of the ablest wrote under the name Brutus.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the documents: Brutus No. 1 and Federalist No. 10">

**Brutus (October 1787):** "a free republic cannot succeed over a country of such immense extent... In so extensive a republic, the great officers of government would soon become above the controul of the people, and abuse their power to the purpose of aggrandizing themselves, and oppressing them."

**Madison (November 1787):** "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens."

</Callout>

The two writers begin from the same fact, the size of the country, and reach opposite conclusions. For Brutus, distance is the danger: representatives who govern a continent cannot know the people they govern, and officials that far away escape their control. For Madison, size is the remedy: in a small republic one faction can seize the whole government, while in a large one so many interests compete that none can easily dominate the others.

<Checkpoint question="State the reasoning of Brutus and of Madison in one sentence each. Then name one fact about the United States today that each could offer as evidence.">

Brutus argues that a government far from its citizens slips out of their control, and he could point to the common complaint that Washington is out of touch with ordinary people. Madison argues that a large and varied country keeps any single faction from taking over, and he could point to how hard it is for one group to win everything it wants. Both arguments are still made, which is a sign that neither was simply wrong.

</Checkpoint>

The Federalists won the vote. New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify on June 21, 1788, and the Constitution took effect. The Anti-Federalists won something lasting as well. Several states ratified on the understanding that amendments would follow, and in 1791 the first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, were added to meet the objection that the document protected the government's powers more carefully than the people's liberties.

## What this lesson does not settle

Two arguments remain open. Historians disagree about whether the bargains over slavery were the price of having a union at all, or a moral failure that the delegates could have refused. Citizens disagree about whether equal votes for unequal states, a deal made among thirteen states in 1787, is still a fair rule for fifty.

Both questions lead to the same place, since a bargain can be reopened only if the document allows it. The next lesson examines the rule for changing the Constitution, and why it has been used so rarely.
