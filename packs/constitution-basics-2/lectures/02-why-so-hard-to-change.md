In 1982, a college student in Texas named Gregory Watson was researching a paper. He came across an amendment that Congress had sent to the states in 1789.

It barred any pay raise for members of Congress from taking effect until after the next election. Too few states had ratified it, and it had been forgotten for most of two centuries.

Watson noticed that the proposal carried no deadline. So he argued, in his paper, that it could still be ratified.

[beat]

He received a C.

[pause]

Then he spent ten years writing to state legislators. And on May 7, 1992, the proposal became the Twenty-seventh Amendment. That was more than 202 years after Congress had approved it.

His university did change the grade to an A. That took until 2017.

[pause]

The story is charming. It is also a warning, about how unusual a successful amendment is.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to explain why amendments are so rare. You should also be able to argue both sides of whether they ought to be.

We will make four stops. First, the rule itself, which is Article Five of the Constitution. Second, the arithmetic inside that rule. Third, a dispute going on right now, which turns on one detail of procedure. And last, an argument that began with the founders: should a constitution be hard to change?

## The rule: Article Five

Article Five is a single sentence, and its text has stayed the same since 1787. It is a long sentence, so you will hear it in three pieces. As you listen, notice everyone who is given a part.

It begins: "The Congress, whenever two thirds of both Houses shall deem it necessary, shall propose Amendments to this Constitution."

[beat]

So Congress can propose an amendment, by a two-thirds vote of each chamber. That is the first way to propose.

The second way comes next. Congress, "on the Application of the Legislatures of two thirds of the several States, shall call a Convention for proposing Amendments."

In plainer words, two-thirds of the state legislatures can require Congress to call a convention for the purpose.

Then comes approval. Either kind of proposal becomes part of the Constitution "when ratified by the Legislatures of three fourths of the several States, or by Conventions in three fourths thereof."

[pause]

To ratify is to approve. So three-fourths of the states must agree, acting through their legislatures or through conventions, as Congress chooses.

> Article Five sets up two ways to propose, and two ways to ratify.

In practice, nearly all the traffic has used one route. No amendment has ever been proposed by a convention. And only one has been ratified by state conventions: the Twenty-first Amendment, which ended Prohibition in 1933.

Now go back over what you heard. Congress. [beat] The state legislatures. [beat] Conventions. Who is missing that you might have expected to find?

[long pause]

The president is missing. A president neither signs nor vetoes a proposed amendment.

The courts are missing. No court approves the result.

And the voters are missing too, at least directly. There is no national referendum, which is to say no national vote. Citizens act only through the legislators and delegates they elect.

## Two supermajorities in a row

That is the rule. Now the arithmetic, because the difficulty lies in the fractions.

Any requirement above a simple majority is called a supermajority. Article Five imposes two supermajorities, one after the other.

Compare an ordinary law. A law needs a majority in each chamber. [beat] An amendment needs two-thirds of each chamber merely to be proposed. Then it needs the agreement of three-fourths of the states, which today means 38 of 50.

> An amendment must clear two supermajorities in a row.

The framers chose those fractions with a failure in mind. Under the Articles of Confederation, no change could be made unless every state legislature agreed to it. That rule had left the Articles impossible to repair.

So Article Five was meant to sit between two dangers. Madison named both of them in 1788, in Federalist 43. Listen for the two extremes.

"It guards equally against that extreme facility, which would render the Constitution too mutable; [beat] and that extreme difficulty, which might perpetuate its discovered faults."

[pause]

Extreme facility, and extreme difficulty. In plainer words, change that comes too easily, and change that comes too hard. Too easy, and the Constitution never holds still. [beat] Too hard, and its faults stay in place after they are discovered.

Whether the framers found the middle is the question the rest of this lecture turns on.

Now turn the requirement around. If an amendment needs 38 of the 50 states, how many states are enough to block it?

[long pause]

Thirteen. If thirteen states say no, only thirty-seven are left, and thirty-seven is one short.

> Thirteen states are enough to block any amendment.

It does not matter how many people live in the other thirty-seven. So a proposal needs support that is broad across regions and across parties. And support of that kind is rare for anything people are still arguing about.

The record bears this out. Members of Congress have introduced about 12,000 proposals to amend the Constitution since 1789. [beat] Only 27 have been ratified.

When agreement is broad, the process can move quickly. Take the Twenty-sixth Amendment, which lowered the voting age to 18. Congress approved it on March 23, 1971. It was ratified on July 1. That is a span of one hundred days.

[beat]

When agreement is missing, nothing moves at all.

## A dispute still open: the Equal Rights Amendment

One amendment shows how much can turn on a detail of procedure.

In 1972, Congress proposed the Equal Rights Amendment. It would bar the denial of equal rights under the law on account of sex. And Congress gave the states seven years to ratify.

By 1977, thirty-five states had done so. That was three short of the number required. Congress extended the deadline to 1982, and no further state acted before it passed. Five states, meanwhile, had voted to take back their earlier ratifications.

[pause]

Decades later, the count began to move again. Nevada ratified in 2017. Illinois followed in 2018. And in January 2020, [beat] Virginia became the thirty-eighth state.

Thirty-eight is the number Article Five requires. So is the amendment now part of the Constitution? There are two answers. Listen to how each one is built on the same text.

Supporters of the amendment argue that it has now met the test the Constitution sets. Article Five says an amendment is valid "when ratified" by three-fourths of the states, and it contains no deadline. The time limit appeared in the introduction to the proposal, and not in the amendment's own text. So in their view, it could not override the Constitution.

> The supporters' case: Article Five says "when ratified," and contains no deadline.

Opponents argue that Congress has long had the power to attach a deadline. In their view, this one expired in 1982, and the three late ratifications came too late to count. In December 2024, the Archivist of the United States took that position. The Archivist is the official who publishes ratified amendments, and stated that the Archivist "cannot legally publish the Equal Rights Amendment."

> The opponents' case: Congress may attach a deadline, and this one expired in 1982.

The dispute is unusual, because both sides claim to be following the same text.

[pause]

And it sends us back to Gregory Watson. His amendment could wait two centuries. Why was that possible? Think back to what he noticed in 1982.

[long pause]

The proposal carried no deadline. Congress attached no deadline to the Twenty-seventh Amendment at all.

## Should a constitution be hard to change?

Underneath all of this sits one question. Should a constitution be hard to change?

The founders themselves disagreed about that, and their disagreement has not been improved upon. So let's hear two of them, in their own words.

Madison first. In Federalist 49, in 1788, he warned against going back to the people too often. Every such appeal, he reasoned, would imply some defect in the government. And so, in his words, "frequent appeals would, in a great measure, deprive the government of that veneration which time bestows on every thing."

[pause]

That veneration which time bestows. Veneration is deep respect, and for Madison only time can supply it. Without it, he added, perhaps even the wisest and freest governments would not have the stability they need.

Madison's worry, then, was that a constitution reopened too often would lose the respect that makes people obey it, even when they lose an argument.

> Madison's danger: a constitution reopened too often loses its respect.

Now Thomas Jefferson, in a letter from 1816. He starts from a principle: "laws and institutions must go hand in hand with the progress of the human mind."

And then he gives a picture. Listen for the man, and for the coat.

Otherwise, he wrote, "we might as well require a man to wear still the coat which fitted him when a boy, [beat] as civilized society to remain ever under the regimen of their barbarous ancestors."

[pause]

Here is a question that only that sentence can answer. In Jefferson's comparison, who is the man? And what is the coat?

[long pause]

The man is civilized society, which has grown. [beat] The coat is the regimen of its ancestors: the laws and institutions that fitted an earlier time.

Jefferson's worry was the opposite of Madison's. A constitution placed beyond reach would bind the living to the judgments of the dead.

> Jefferson's danger: a constitution beyond reach binds the living to the dead.

One caution, to be fair to Jefferson. He was no advocate of constant revision. In the same letter, he wrote that he opposed frequent and untried changes. But he thought each generation was entitled to repair what experience had shown to be broken.

There is one more voice to add, and this one brought numbers. In 1994, the political scientist Donald Lutz compared constitutions around the world. He found the American one among the most difficult to amend.

He also argued something less expected. A constitution that cannot easily be amended does not stop changing. The change moves elsewhere, chiefly into the courts. There, judges reinterpret words such as "equal protection" as circumstances shift.

> Lutz argued that when amendment is hard, change moves into the courts.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Article Five sets up two ways to propose, and two ways to ratify. Congress proposes by two-thirds of each chamber, or two-thirds of the state legislatures require a convention. Then three-fourths of the states must ratify. The president, the courts and a national vote have no part.

[beat]

Two. An amendment must clear two supermajorities in a row. Turn that around, and thirteen states are enough to block one. Members of Congress have introduced about 12,000 proposals. Only 27 have been ratified.

[beat]

Three. The Equal Rights Amendment shows how much can turn on a deadline. Supporters argue that Article Five says "when ratified," and contains no deadline. Opponents argue that Congress may attach one, and that this one expired in 1982.

[beat]

Four. Madison's danger is a constitution reopened too often, which loses its respect. Jefferson's danger is a constitution beyond reach, which binds the living to the judgments of the dead.

[beat]

And five. Lutz argued that when amendment is hard, change moves into the courts.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Is the shift that Lutz described a defect, [beat] or a strength?

On one view, judges end up making choices that supermajorities of citizens were supposed to make. On the other, the arrangement keeps the fundamental rules steady, while allowing their application to develop.

Your answer will depend on which danger you take more seriously. Madison's, [beat] or Jefferson's.

[pause]

Think once more of Gregory Watson, and his ten years of letters. One amendment, more than two centuries in the making. [beat] The text of Article Five has stayed the same since 1787. And so has the argument over whether it asks too much.
