In May of 1787, delegates from twelve states shuttered the windows of the Pennsylvania State House. [beat] Then they swore one another to secrecy.

Their instructions were to revise the Articles of Confederation. By the middle of June, they had decided to design a different government altogether.

And when they opened the doors in September, they carried out a document that few of them considered perfect.

[pause]

Few of them considered it perfect. That is the most useful thing to know before you read the Constitution.

It was not handed down by people who agreed with one another. The delegates disagreed about nearly everything that mattered.

> The Constitution is a record of bargains.

And many of its strangest features mark the places where a bargain was struck.

So we will read it the way it was made, bargain by bargain. By the end, you should be able to explain why the Constitution looks the way it does.

There are four stops. First, the bargain over representation, which means how many votes each state would have in Congress. Second, the bargains over slavery. Third, the document those bargains produced. And last, the fight to get that document approved.

## Equal people, or equal members?

Begin on neutral ground, with a problem that has nothing to do with 1787.

Imagine thirteen schools that want to form a league, governed by a single student council. The largest school has 3,000 students. The smallest has 300. And the first question the league must settle is how many votes each school will have.

The large schools propose one vote for every hundred students, so that every student counts the same.

The small schools refuse. Under that plan, a few large schools could outvote all the rest. They propose one vote for each school instead.

And now the large schools refuse. Under that plan, a school of 300 would have as much say as a school ten times its size.

[pause]

Notice that neither side is being unreasonable. Each is defending a real principle. On one side, equal people. [beat] On the other, equal members. And the league will not exist at all unless both sides agree to join it.

Now replace the schools with states, and you have the hardest fight of the convention.

Large states such as Virginia wanted seats in Congress distributed by population. Small states such as Delaware wanted every state to have an equal vote, which was already the rule under the Articles of Confederation.

The solution was to do both.

> In the House, seats follow population. In the Senate, each state has two members.

Equal people in one chamber. [beat] Equal members in the other.

The plan was adopted narrowly. We know how narrowly because of James Madison, who kept the most complete record of the debates.

Here is his entry for July 16, 1787. The question before the delegates included, in his words, "the equality of votes" in the second branch. The second branch is the Senate. And Madison's note of the result is five words long: [beat] "it passed in the Affirmative."

[pause]

It passed. But listen to the tally behind those five words.

Five states voted aye: Connecticut, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland and North Carolina.

Four states voted no: Pennsylvania, Virginia, South Carolina and Georgia.

And Massachusetts was divided.

[pause]

Here is a question that only that tally can answer. Virginia and Pennsylvania vote no. Delaware and New Jersey vote aye. What does that pattern tell you about who gained from equal votes in the Senate?

[long pause]

Two of the largest states opposed the plan, and two of the smallest supported it. The small states were the ones who gained.

Equal votes in the Senate were the price the small states demanded. It was their price for joining a government in which the House would be dominated by the large ones.

And that bargain is still in force. [beat] The least populous state has the same two senators as the most populous.

## The word that is not there

The second set of bargains concerned slavery.

Search the Constitution of 1787 for the word slavery, [beat] and you will not find it. The delegates avoided the word. And then they wrote three clauses that protected the institution.

> The delegates avoided the word slavery, and wrote three clauses that protected it.

Take the three in order. For each one, you will hear the Constitution's own words, and then what those words did.

The first clause is about counting. Its words are "three fifths of all other Persons." Three-fifths of a state's enslaved population counted toward its seats in the House, and so toward its votes for president.

The second clause is about the slave trade. That trade, it says, "shall not be prohibited by the Congress prior to the Year one thousand eight hundred and eight." In plainer words, Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved people for twenty years.

The third clause is about people who escaped. It speaks of a person "held to Service or Labour" who fled to another state. That person, it says, "shall be delivered up." In plainer words, the person had to be returned.

[pause]

Counting. [beat] The slave trade. [beat] People who escaped. Three clauses, and none of them uses the word.

The first of those clauses is the one most often misunderstood, so let's slow down on it.

You already have the rule for the House: seats follow population. Now apply it. Who at the convention would want enslaved people counted in full? And who would want them not counted at all?

[long pause]

It is tempting to assume that the opponents of slavery wanted enslaved people counted in full, as a matter of dignity. [beat] The record shows the reverse.

Delegates from South Carolina moved that enslaved people be counted "equally with the Whites." Why? [beat] Because a larger count meant more seats in Congress, for states that gave those same people no vote and no rights.

Several northern delegates argued that they should not be counted at all. And three-fifths was the figure the two sides settled on.

So which states gained seats under the three-fifths clause? The states with slavery, because part of their enslaved population was counted. The people being counted could not vote. So the added power went to those who held them in slavery.

> The three-fifths count gave more House seats to the states with slavery.

These clauses lasted until the Civil War ended them. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in 1865. The Fourteenth replaced the three-fifths rule in 1868.

## What the finished document contains

Those were the bargains. Now the text that came out of them. It is short, and it is plainly organized.

It opens with a Preamble, one sentence long. The Preamble names "We the People" as the source of the government's authority, and it lists the government's purposes. It does not grant any power of its own.

Seven articles follow. The first three create Congress, the presidency and the federal courts. Article Four governs relations among the states. Article Five provides for amendments. And Article Six makes the Constitution "the supreme Law of the Land."

[beat]

The supreme law of the land. That provision is usually called the supremacy clause, and it is the reason a state law that conflicts with the Constitution cannot stand.

> Article Six makes the Constitution the supreme law of the land.

Article Seven is the shortest. It states the terms on which the whole design would take effect: approval by conventions in nine states.

## The fight to ratify

Approval by nine states has a name: ratification. It was a real hurdle, and it meant the argument was only beginning.

The framers sent the document to specially elected conventions in each state. And for the better part of a year, Americans debated it, in newspapers, in taverns and in meeting halls.

The opponents were known as Anti-Federalists, and they made a serious case. One of the ablest wrote under the name Brutus. In October of 1787, Brutus wrote that "a free republic cannot succeed over a country of such immense extent."

[pause]

A country of such immense extent. In so large a republic, Brutus went on, the great officers of government would soon rise above the control of the people. They would abuse their power, and oppress the people they governed.

The other side of the argument came in November, from James Madison, in the essay known as Federalist 10. As you listen, notice what Madison does with the size of the country.

"Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; [beat] you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens."

[pause]

Extend the sphere. Make the republic larger, and a majority finds it harder to unite against the rights of others.

Now a question that only those two passages can answer. Brutus and Madison begin from the same fact about the country. What is that fact? And where does each of them take it?

[long pause]

The fact is the size of the country. And from it, they reach opposite conclusions.

For Brutus, distance is the danger. Representatives who govern a continent cannot know the people they govern, and officials that far away escape the people's control.

For Madison, size is the remedy. In a small republic, one faction can seize the whole government. In a large one, so many interests compete that none can easily dominate the others.

> For Brutus, distance is the danger. For Madison, size is the remedy.

Carry that argument forward, because each of them could find evidence in the United States today.

Brutus could point to a common complaint: that Washington is out of touch with ordinary people.

Madison could point to how hard it is for one group to win everything it wants.

Both arguments are still made. And that is a sign that neither was simply wrong.

[pause]

So who won? [beat] The Federalists won the vote. New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify on June 21, 1788, and the Constitution took effect.

But the Anti-Federalists won something lasting as well. Several states ratified on the understanding that amendments would follow. And in 1791, the first ten amendments were added. They are the Bill of Rights.

They were added to meet an objection: that the document protected the government's powers more carefully than the people's liberties.

> The Federalists won the vote. The Anti-Federalists won the Bill of Rights.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Constitution is a record of bargains, struck by delegates who disagreed about nearly everything that mattered.

[beat]

Two. In the fight over representation, the choice was equal people or equal members, and the answer was both. In the House, seats follow population. In the Senate, each state has two members. The small states demanded that price, and the bargain is still in force.

[beat]

Three. The delegates avoided the word slavery, and wrote three clauses that protected it. Can you still say what each of the three was about?

[long pause]

Counting. The slave trade. People who escaped. And the three-fifths count gave more House seats to the states with slavery.

[beat]

Four. The finished document is a one-sentence Preamble and seven articles. The Preamble names We the People, and grants no power. Article Six makes the Constitution the supreme law of the land.

[beat]

And five. In the fight to ratify, Brutus saw distance as the danger, and Madison saw size as the remedy. The Federalists won the vote. The Anti-Federalists won the Bill of Rights.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Two arguments remain open.

Historians disagree about the bargains over slavery. Were they the price of having a union at all? Or were they a moral failure that the delegates could have refused?

And citizens disagree about the Senate. Equal votes for unequal states was a deal made among thirteen states, in 1787. Is it still a fair rule for fifty?

[pause]

Both questions lead to the same place. A bargain can be reopened only if the document allows it. So the next lecture examines the rule for changing the Constitution, and why it has been used so rarely.

For now, go back to those shuttered windows. The delegates carried out a document that few of them considered perfect. [beat] Now you know why. It is a record of bargains. And two of them are still being argued over.
