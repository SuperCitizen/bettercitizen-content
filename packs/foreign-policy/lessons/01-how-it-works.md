# How U.S. foreign policy actually works

Just before 7 p.m. Washington time on June 21, 2025, fourteen 30,000-pound GBU-57 "bunker-buster" bombs struck Iran's nuclear-enrichment sites at Fordow and Natanz, and submarine-launched Tomahawk missiles hit a third facility at Isfahan. Seven B-2 bombers had flown a roughly thirty-seven-hour round trip from Missouri, screened by more than 125 supporting aircraft and a decoy flight over the Pacific. The President announced the operation on social media that evening; most members of Congress learned of it the way everyone else did. The formal notification arrived two days later — a letter citing the President's "constitutional authority as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive." No congressional vote preceded the strikes, and none followed: six days later the Senate declined, 47–53, to advance a resolution requiring one.

Now hold that scene against the text. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress — not the President — the power "to declare War." Congress last used it in June 1942. Edward Corwin's verdict has aged well: the Constitution is "an invitation to struggle for the privilege of directing American foreign policy." This lesson maps the machine — who holds which lever and what each one actually does. Whether the machine is pointed at the right things is Lesson 2's argument.

## Two branches, one steering wheel

The Constitution splits foreign policy down the middle. Congress holds the powers in Article I §8:

- declare war; raise and support armies; provide and maintain a navy
- regulate commerce with foreign nations — which is why tariffs are constitutionally a *congressional* tax
- appropriate every dollar (the power of the purse)

The President holds the powers in Article II: command of the armed forces, the making of treaties (with two-thirds Senate consent), the appointment of ambassadors (with Senate consent), and the receiving of foreign ambassadors — a clause the Supreme Court read in *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015) to give the President *exclusive* power to recognize foreign governments.

The text gives Congress the decision to start a war and the President the conduct of it. Practice has run the other way for eighty years. Congress has declared war eleven times across five conflicts — the War of 1812, the Mexican-American War, the Spanish-American War, and the two World Wars — and not once since June 1942. Korea proceeded on a UN Security Council resolution and no congressional vote at all (Truman called it a "police action"); Vietnam ran on the 1964 Gulf of Tonkin Resolution; the Gulf War, Afghanistan, and Iraq ran on statutory authorizations that stopped short of the word "war." Presidents of both parties have meanwhile used force on their own authority hundreds of times.

## The War Powers Resolution and the sixty-day clock

Vietnam convinced Congress that the drift had gone too far, and in November 1973 it passed the [[term:war-powers-act]] (formally the War Powers Resolution) over Nixon's veto. The statute's core machinery has three parts: the President must consult Congress "in every possible instance" before introducing forces into hostilities; must report any such introduction within 48 hours; and must terminate the operation within 60 days — plus a 30-day withdrawal window — unless Congress authorizes it.

The compliance record since is a case study in the gap between a statute's text and its life. Every administration, of both parties, has filed the reports — well over a hundred — while maintaining that the Resolution's binding provisions are unconstitutional. The reports arrive "consistent with," never "pursuant to," the statute. Only one report in fifty years (Ford's, after the 1975 *Mayaguez* rescue) has cited the clause that actually starts the 60-day clock, and it was filed after the operation had ended. In 2011, the Obama administration argued that seven months of air operations over Libya were not "hostilities" at all, so no clock was running. Congress has never once used the Resolution to force a withdrawal.

The June 2025 Iran strikes ran the playbook again. The 48-hour letter arrived on June 23, resting on Article II rather than any statute. Senator Tim Kaine forced a vote on a resolution barring further offensive operations against Iran absent authorization; it failed 47–53 on June 27, nearly on party lines. And because Israel and Iran reached a ceasefire on June 24 — ending the twelve-day war the strikes had joined — the administration took the position that hostilities had "terminated," mooting the 60-day question entirely. The episode is the modern war-powers debate in miniature: the statute produced a report, a floor vote, and no constraint.

> **The scholar's lens:** This argument is older than the statute by 180 years. In the 1793 *Pacificus–Helvidius* debates, Hamilton argued that executive power is the natural residual home of foreign affairs; Madison answered that "in no part of the constitution is more wisdom to be found, than in the clause which confides the question of war or peace to the legislature, and not to the executive department." Every AUMF fight since is a footnote to that exchange.

## What Congress repealed — and what it didn't

Two authorizations did most of the post-9/11 legal work. The 2001 AUMF — a single sentence of about 60 words — authorizes force against the "nations, organizations, or persons" behind September 11. Successive administrations stretched it across operations in Afghanistan, Yemen, Somalia, Libya, Syria, Niger, and elsewhere, including against ISIS, an organization that did not exist in 2001. The 2002 Iraq AUMF outlived its war by two decades as makeweight authority — cited, alongside Article II, for the 2020 strike that killed Iranian general Qasem Soleimani.

That afterlife is now over. After years of near-misses (the Senate passed a standalone repeal in 2023 that the House never took up), Congress folded repeal of both the 1991 and 2002 Iraq authorizations into the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act, signed December 18, 2025 — the first clawback of a war authorization since the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution's repeal in 1971. The 2001 AUMF, by contrast, remains in force as of mid-2026, a quarter-century old and still the statutory basis for counterterrorism operations on three continents.

## Treaties, executive agreements, and the disappearing Senate

The Constitution's other famous foreign-policy supermajority is the [[term:treaty]] power: two-thirds of the Senate must consent. Read that and you would expect the Senate to be the gatekeeper of America's international commitments. It mostly isn't. By CRS's count, treaties have made up less than 7 percent of U.S. international agreements since the World War II era — only about 6 percent since 1990. Between 1940 and 1989 the United States entered 759 treaties and more than 13,000 executive agreements. The modern default never meets the two-thirds bar at all, and the differences are worth a table:

| Instrument | Approval required | Durability | Real examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Article II treaty | Two-thirds of the Senate | Highest — binding law, politically costly to exit | North Atlantic Treaty (1949, ratified 82–13); New START (2010, 71–26) |
| Congressional-executive agreement | Ordinary statute — majorities of both chambers | High — it *is* a statute | USMCA (2020); WTO membership (1994) |
| Sole executive agreement / political commitment | None — the President's signature | Lowest — the next President can walk away | Paris climate agreement (joined 2016; exited 2020; rejoined 2021; exit ordered again January 2025); the Iran nuclear deal (JCPOA, 2015; exited 2018) |

The table's bottom row explains a decade of diplomatic whiplash. Paris and the JCPOA were built as executive commitments precisely because two-thirds of the Senate was unreachable — and each was unwound by a successor with a signature. NATO sits at the other pole: the Senate consented 82–13 in 1949, and Finland's and Sweden's accession protocols cleared it 95–1 in 2022. The trade-off — speed versus durability — is the real content of the distinction, and foreign capitals price it in.

## The alliance system

Washington's farewell advice to "steer clear of permanent alliances" and Jefferson's "entangling alliances with none" held, with brief exceptions, for a century and a half: until the late 1940s the United States had no peacetime military allies. It now has mutual-defense commitments to more than fifty countries.

[[term:nato]] is the core. Founded with 12 members in 1949, it reached **32** when Finland joined in April 2023 and Sweden in March 2024 — both abandoning decades of nonalignment after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. [[term:nato-article-5]] — an attack on one is an attack on all — has been invoked exactly once: by the allies on behalf of the *United States*, the day after September 11. At the June 2025 Hague summit, the allies replaced the old 2-percent-of-GDP spending guideline (which NATO's 2024 estimates showed only 23 of 32 members meeting) with a far larger pledge: **5 percent of GDP by 2035** — at least 3.5 percent for core defense requirements plus up to 1.5 percent for defense-related investment like infrastructure, cyber, and industrial capacity — with annual plans and a 2029 review of the trajectory.

In the Pacific there is no NATO equivalent — instead a "hub-and-spokes" set of bilateral treaties from the early Cold War: the Philippines (1951), Australia (ANZUS, 1951), South Korea (1953), Thailand (via the 1954 Manila Pact), and Japan (1960). Layered on top: Five Eyes intelligence sharing (with the U.K., Canada, Australia, New Zealand); AUKUS (2021), the submarine-technology partnership with Australia and Britain; and the **Major Non-NATO Ally** designation, which gives about twenty partners — Israel, Jordan, Qatar, Taiwan (by statutory equivalence), and others — privileged defense cooperation *without* any defense commitment.

> **Common misconception:** NATO's spending targets are not dues; no ally "owes" the United States money. The 2 percent guideline — and now the Hague 5 percent pledge — measures what each member invests in *its own* defense. The real burden-sharing argument (Lesson 2) is about free-riding on American capabilities, not unpaid bills.

## The rest of the toolkit — and what 2025 did to it

Military force is the loudest instrument, but most foreign policy is conducted with quieter ones, and 2025 rearranged several of them more abruptly than any year since the 1940s.

**[[term:diplomacy]].** The State Department staffs more than 270 embassies, consulates, and missions worldwide. In July 2025 it executed its largest reorganization in decades: about 1,350 U.S.-based employees received reduction-in-force notices, part of a roughly 3,000-person (about 18 percent) cut to the domestic workforce, with bureaus covering refugees, democracy promotion, and human rights hit hardest.

**[[term:foreign-aid]].** USAID, the lead development agency since 1961, was dismantled in 2025: Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced in March that 83 percent of its roughly 6,200 programs were being terminated, and the agency formally closed on July 1, 2025, its surviving programs absorbed into State. Foreign aid has historically run at about 1 percent of federal spending — though polls consistently find Americans guessing 20 percent or more. The fight then moved to the budget: the administration requested $31.2 billion for FY2026 international affairs (a cut approaching half), and Congress answered in the appropriation signed February 3, 2026 with roughly $50 billion — below recent years, far above the request.

**[[term:sanctions]].** Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control administers dozens of sanctions programs, and the dollar's centrality in global finance gives them extraterritorial reach: *secondary* sanctions threaten third-country banks with loss of access to the U.S. financial system, which is why a Treasury designation can rearrange trade between two countries that never touch America.

**Defense dollars.** The FY2026 budget crossed a symbolic line: the administration requested about $1.0 trillion for national defense — roughly $890 billion in ordinary appropriations plus about $113 billion in one-time funds from the 2025 reconciliation act — and the FY2026 NDAA authorized $890.6 billion of it. Lesson 3 puts the trillion in context. Intelligence (a disclosed budget above $90 billion), trade policy (tariffs, export controls, investment screening — source of 2026's biggest separation-of-powers case, in Lesson 3), and information programs round out the kit.

## What this lesson is not

It is not yet a position on anything. You now know where the levers are: a war power split between the branches and operated mostly by one of them; a treaty power so demanding it pushed commitments into less durable forms; an alliance network of unprecedented peacetime scope, re-priced at The Hague; and civilian instruments reorganized — supporters say rationalized, critics say gutted — in a single year. Whether the United States *should* run an alliance-heavy, forward-deployed foreign policy is the genuinely contested question. Lesson 2 steel-mans the three serious answers: the **liberal internationalist**, who sees the post-1945 order as the buy of the century; the **restrainer**, who sees overextension and moral hazard; and the **conservative realist**, who would spend the same power on fewer, harder priorities.
