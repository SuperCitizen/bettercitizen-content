# How U.S. foreign policy actually works

The constitutional architecture for U.S. foreign policy is divided between Congress and the President, with the actual practice tilted heavily toward the executive over the past century. Understanding the structure — and the gap between text and practice — is the prerequisite to thinking about policy.

## The constitutional division

**Congressional authorities (Art. I §8):**
- Declare war.
- Raise and support armies; provide and maintain a Navy.
- Make rules concerning captures on land and water.
- Define and punish offenses against the law of nations.
- Regulate commerce with foreign nations.
- Appropriate funds (the power of the purse).

**Presidential authorities (Art. II §§1-2):**
- Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces.
- Make treaties (with two-thirds Senate consent).
- Appoint ambassadors (with Senate consent).
- Receive ambassadors (which has been read to include recognition of foreign governments).
- Take care that the laws are faithfully executed.

The text gives Congress the war-making power but the President operational command. The Founders intended this as a check; the practice since World War II has tilted heavily toward executive initiative. The last formal U.S. declaration of war was World War II (1942 against Bulgaria/Hungary/Romania). Korea, Vietnam, and the Gulf, Afghanistan, and Iraq wars all proceeded under various forms of authorization that fell short of formal declaration.

## The War Powers Resolution

In response to Vietnam, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution of 1973 over Nixon's veto. The Act:
- Requires the President to consult Congress "in every possible instance" before introducing armed forces into hostilities.
- Requires reporting within 48 hours of any introduction of forces.
- Requires withdrawal of forces within 60 days (extendable by 30) absent congressional authorization.

In practice, every administration since 1973 has reported under the WPR while denying its constitutionality. The "60-day clock" has rarely been enforced; Congress has not used the WPR's withdrawal mechanism. Recent debates (Yemen, Iran, Syria) have reopened the question without producing a definitive answer.

## The post-9/11 AUMF and its expansion

The 2001 Authorization for Use of Military Force authorized the President to use force against "those nations, organizations, or persons" responsible for 9/11 and "to prevent any future acts of international terrorism." This is one statute, ~60 words long.

It has been used as the legal basis for U.S. military action in:
- Afghanistan (the original scope).
- Iraq (separate 2002 AUMF, but 2001 cited as supplementary authority).
- Yemen, Somalia, Libya, Pakistan, Syria, Niger, Mali, Cameroon, Kenya, Mauritania (drone operations and special operations forces).
- Against ISIS (an organization that didn't exist in 2001).

The reach of the AUMF is itself contested. Multiple administrations have argued for expansive readings; Congress has periodically debated repeal or replacement. As of 2025, the 2001 AUMF remains in force.

## The alliance system

The U.S. is the formal ally of approximately 30 countries through mutual defense treaties. The major frameworks:

- **NATO** (32 members as of 2024): mutual-defense alliance founded in 1949. Article 5 (an attack on one is an attack on all) has been invoked once — by the U.S. after 9/11. Most NATO operations since have been collective security, not Article 5.
- **Bilateral mutual defense treaties** with Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, Thailand, Australia (ANZUS, with NZ partially out).
- **Major non-NATO Allies (MNNA)** designation — confers some defense-cooperation benefits without full treaty status: Israel, Egypt, Jordan, Argentina, Brazil, Bahrain, Kuwait, Morocco, Pakistan, the Philippines, Taiwan, Thailand, Tunisia, etc.
- **Five Eyes intelligence sharing**: U.S., U.K., Canada, Australia, NZ.
- **AUKUS** (2021): trilateral defense partnership U.S./U.K./Australia, focused on submarine technology and Indo-Pacific posture.

The alliance system is a defining feature of U.S. foreign policy since 1945 and is one of the core areas of disagreement among the perspectives we'll examine in Lesson 2. Liberal internationalists treat it as essential; restrainers view it as overextended; conservative realists support some alliances and oppose others on a case-by-case basis.

## Foreign-policy instruments beyond the military

**Diplomacy.** State Department, ambassadors, treaty negotiation, multilateral fora (UN, G7, G20, NATO, OECD).

**Trade policy.** Tariffs, free-trade agreements (USMCA, IPEF), trade-enforcement (Section 301 China investigation, Section 232 national-security tariffs), trade-promotion (Export-Import Bank).

**Sanctions.** Targeted financial sanctions through OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control), banking restrictions, technology export controls (CFIUS reviews, BIS export controls). The U.S. dollar's role in international finance gives sanctions a unique reach — secondary sanctions (against third countries doing business with sanctioned entities) extend U.S. enforcement globally.

**Foreign aid.** Bilateral aid (USAID, State Department PRM), multilateral aid (IMF, World Bank contributions), military aid (foreign military financing, training programs). Total foreign aid is ~$60B/year, less than 1% of federal spending despite popular perception.

**Intelligence.** CIA, NSA, DIA, ODNI, and other agencies. Foreign-intelligence collection, covert action, liaison with allied services. Most operations are classified; the budget is partially public ($90B+ total intelligence community, FY 2024).

**Information operations.** Voice of America, Radio Free Europe, USAGM (U.S. Agency for Global Media), various State Department public-diplomacy programs.

## The recent shift to "great-power competition"

The 2017 National Security Strategy and the 2018 National Defense Strategy formalized a shift in U.S. strategic framing: from "global war on terror" to "long-term strategic competition with China and Russia." This framing has been continued under both Republican and Democratic administrations since.

The shift has practical consequences:
- **Indo-Pacific reorientation.** AUKUS, expanded U.S.-Japan-South Korea cooperation, expanded port-call frequency in the South China Sea, increased Taiwan-strait transits.
- **Defense procurement.** Shift toward longer-range strike, hypersonic weapons, undersea capabilities, space and cyber capacity — away from counter-insurgency platforms.
- **Trade policy.** Section 301 tariffs on China, semiconductor export controls, CFIUS expansion to cover technology investments.
- **NATO expansion and reinforcement.** Finland and Sweden joined NATO 2023-2024; U.S. troop levels in Europe increased post-2022 Ukraine invasion.

The framing is itself contested. Liberal internationalists generally accept it but emphasize multilateral coordination. Restrainers worry about its tendency toward indefinite confrontation. Conservative realists emphasize discrimination in commitments — opposing some commitments while accepting others.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Liberal internationalists** argue that the post-1945 U.S.-led order has produced unprecedented peace and prosperity, that U.S. global engagement is the foundation of that order, and that the right policy combines forward defense, multilateral diplomacy, and rule-based international institutions.
- **Restrainers** argue that the U.S. is overextended, that the alliance system creates moral-hazard problems and entangles the U.S. in others' wars, and that the right policy is a more limited military posture combined with stronger diplomatic engagement and economic statecraft.
- **Conservative realists** argue both internationalists and restrainers are too categorical, that U.S. foreign policy should be guided by hard-headed assessment of national interest, and that the right policy is selective engagement based on the strategic stakes of each region or crisis.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
