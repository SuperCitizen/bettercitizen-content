# The current policy landscape

On February 20, 2026, Chief Justice John Roberts announced the judgment in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*: the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — the 1977 sanctions statute under which the President had been imposing tariffs on much of the world's goods since early 2025 — does not authorize tariffs at all. Six justices agreed that a statutory power to "regulate … importation" is not the power to tax, which Article I gives to Congress. With that, duties importers had been paying for a year became refundable overpayments — by industry estimates, as much as $175 billion. Lesson 1 made the classroom point that tariffs are constitutionally a *congressional* tax; *Learning Resources* is what that point looks like with money attached.

The case is the right door into the 2026 landscape, because every front on the board is, underneath, a story about instruments and who controls them. Four fronts dominate as of mid-2026 — China, Russia/Ukraine, the Middle East, and trade — with the war-powers fight from Lesson 1 running beneath all four. What follows is a snapshot of a moving target: the facts will age, but the frameworks from Lesson 2 are the durable part. For each front, watch which school's prescription current policy most resembles — the answer is rarely a purebred.

## Trade: the tariff power goes home to Congress

The post-1990s consensus on trade liberalization fractured a decade ago and has not re-formed. The United States has concluded no comprehensive trade agreement since the Trans-Pacific Partnership (negotiated through 2015, signed in 2016, never ratified, abandoned in January 2017). USMCA (2020) replaced NAFTA with more protective terms — and faces its first scheduled six-year "joint review" in July 2026. The [[term:tariff]], which 1990s economics declared politically dead, returned as the lead instrument: Section 301 duties on China from 2018, Section 232 duties on steel and aluminum, and then the 2025 escalation — IEEPA tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China in February, followed by a global "reciprocal" schedule in April.

The courts pushed back almost immediately. The Court of International Trade ruled against the IEEPA tariffs in May 2025 (*V.O.S. Selections*), the en banc Federal Circuit affirmed in August, and the Supreme Court closed the question that February morning in 2026. What the ruling did *not* do matters as much as what it did: it was statutory, not constitutional. Tariffs resting on express delegations survived — Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 and Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 delegate tariff authority explicitly, with procedural prerequisites (investigations, findings) that IEEPA's emergency framework had let the executive skip. The decision pushed the question back to Article I's first branch: Congress can re-delegate broad tariff power by statute, claw back the delegations that remain, or do what it has mostly done — nothing, leaving tariff policy to the narrower statutes.

The schools split unusually here, crossing their usual lines. Liberal internationalists want the U.S. back at the multilateral table but concede the old free-trade coalition is gone. Restrainers divide between free-trade traditionalists and economic nationalists. Conservative realists support strategic restrictions on China — chips, critical minerals, dual-use technology — while preferring open trade with allies, on the theory that the point of trade policy is power, not protection.

## China: the pacing challenge

China remains what successive strategy documents have called the "pacing challenge" — the one competitor with the scale to contest American power across every dimension at once. The strategic-competition frame, formalized in the 2017 National Security Strategy and 2018 National Defense Strategy, has now outlasted three administrations of both parties, which makes it the closest thing the era has to a consensus. The current toolkit layers four instruments:

- **Technology controls.** Export controls on advanced semiconductors and AI hardware (Commerce Department rules, October 2022 onward, tightened repeatedly since), plus CFIUS screening of inbound investment and a growing outbound-investment regime.
- **Forward posture.** Expanded rotational access in Australia, Japan, and the Philippines; the AUKUS submarine partnership (2021); and the alliance lattice from Lesson 1.
- **Taiwan.** Arms sales under the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979, periodic Taiwan Strait transits, and official **strategic ambiguity** — a deliberate refusal to say whether the United States would fight for the island, intended to deter Beijing without emboldening Taipei. ([[issue:taiwan-and-china]] tracks the live debate over whether ambiguity still deters.)
- **Industrial policy.** The CHIPS and Science Act's roughly $52 billion for domestic semiconductor manufacturing — though the clean-energy leg of the industrial strategy was largely repealed in July 2025, a reminder that subsidies are the easiest instrument to pass and the easiest to undo.

The schools' positions track Lesson 2 exactly. Internationalists endorse the posture but insist the controls only work multilaterally — chips flow through Dutch lithography and Japanese chemicals, so unilateral walls leak. Restrainers warn that treating rivalry as inevitable helps make it so, and that decoupling taxes Americans for security it may not buy. Realists want everything above, plus more shipbuilding and deeper munitions stocks — the FY2026 defense budget's symbolic crossing of the $1 trillion line (Lesson 1) is, on their reading, the minimum price of deterring a peer ([[issue:trade-with-china]] collects the economic side of the argument).

## Russia / Ukraine: the war meets the deadline

Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine — the largest European war since 1945 — drove the responses Lesson 1 cataloged: more than $60 billion in U.S. security assistance committed from 2022 through 2024, the most extensive [[term:sanctions]] architecture ever coordinated (banking restrictions, central-bank reserve freezes, energy-price caps, built with G7 partners), a U.S. troop presence in Europe that surged from roughly 65,000 to about 100,000, and the accession of Finland and Sweden to NATO.

By mid-2026 the policy has changed shape. Congressional funding became contested ground in 2024, when the last major aid supplemental stalled for months before passing. The current administration has announced no new security-assistance packages, continuing only previously committed deliveries, and has put its weight behind diplomacy instead: U.S.-brokered negotiations under an American-imposed deadline of June 2026 for the parties to reach a settlement. Europe, meanwhile, is re-arming under the Hague 5-percent pledge — which all three schools claim as vindication: the internationalist as proof alliances can be renegotiated, the restrainer as proof allies spend only when Washington credibly steps back, the realist as the precondition for handing Europe the European problem. The fight over continued support is tracked at [[issue:ukraine-support]]; the alliance-architecture question at [[issue:nato-and-alliances]].

<Checkpoint question="Washington presses both sides toward a settlement under a deadline while announcing no new aid. Run the move through the three schools: whose prescription does it most resemble, and what would each school watch to judge it?">
  The *form* is the restrainer's — a negotiated settlement because cost and escalation risk outran the interest — executed with a realist's motive: shift the burden to Europe and free resources for Asia. The internationalist dissents on the stakes, not the mechanics: a settlement that ratifies conquest re-prices every border the order protects, so they watch whether the terms reward aggression. The restrainer watches whether the U.S. actually retrenches or just rebrands. The realist watches one number — whether what's freed up actually moves to the Pacific.
</Checkpoint>

## The Middle East: two Iran campaigns in twelve months

Lesson 1 told the June 2025 story — the Fordow strikes, the twelve-day war, the ceasefire that mooted the 60-day clock. It turned out to be the prologue. On February 28, 2026, U.S. and Israeli forces opened a far larger campaign — nearly 900 strikes in the first twelve hours — against Iranian missiles, air defenses, and leadership, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Iran retaliated across the region, against U.S. installations and Gulf oil infrastructure, and hostilities spread to Lebanon. The Strait of Hormuz became the war's economic center: commercial transit collapsed by more than 90 percent and oil jumped from about $70 to over $100 a barrel — a reminder that a fifth of the world's oil passes a chokepoint Iran can hold at risk. A ceasefire brokered in early April — with Pakistan as intermediary, and involving the highest-level direct U.S.–Iran contact since 1979 — has held unevenly since, punctuated by renewed Iran–Israel exchanges in early June. As of mid-2026 the war is paused, not settled.

The rest of the regional file is continuity: the post-October 7 commitment to Israel; the Saudi security relationship, still complicated by the Khashoggi murder, Yemen, and OPEC+ coordination with Russia; counter-ISIS operations in Syria and Iraq running on the 2001 AUMF. The restrainer position has more traction here than on any other front — this is the region where the post-9/11 ledger was written — while internationalists and realists divide over whether offshore deterrence can hold what forward presence held.

## The war-powers scoreboard

Underneath the Iran war sits the constitutional story from Lesson 1, now with new entries. Neither the June 2025 strikes nor the February 2026 campaign was preceded by any congressional vote; both rested on Article II claims. Congress's responses, as of mid-2026: the repeal of the 1991 and 2002 Iraq AUMFs (December 2025) — real, but irrelevant to Iran operations that never cited them; the 2001 AUMF still standing, sunset proposals still stalled; and, on June 3, 2026, a House vote of 215–208 directing an end to hostilities with Iran — the first time a chamber has voted to halt this war.

<Checkpoint question="The February 2026 campaign began without any congressional vote. Under the War Powers Resolution as written, what should have happened by the end of April — and what does the June House vote tell you about what actually governs?">
  As written: a report within 48 hours, then termination within 60 days absent congressional authorization — a clock expiring in late April — plus 30 days to withdraw. In practice the clock has never been enforced (Lesson 1's compliance record), and the April ceasefire let the executive call hostilities "terminated" on its own schedule, as in 2025. The June 3 vote — a month after the clock would have run — shows where the real constraint lives: not in self-executing statutory machinery but in political mathematics, and a one-chamber 215–208 majority is not enough math. To bind, a measure must clear both chambers and survive a veto — which is why no war-powers resolution ever has.
</Checkpoint>

## What to watch

Each front reduces to a lever, an owner, and a test. The table is the lesson's map of the next two years:

| Front | The lever in play | Who actually holds it | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trade | Tariff authority after *Learning Resources* | Congress — unless it re-delegates; executive keeps Sections 301/232 | The July 2026 USMCA review; any statute restoring or restraining broad tariff power |
| China | Export controls, CFIUS, Taiwan arms sales | Executive, under broad statutory delegations | Whether strategic ambiguity survives a Strait crisis; allied alignment on chip controls |
| Russia/Ukraine | Aid appropriations and sanctions relief | Congress appropriates; the executive negotiates | Whether the June 2026 deadline produces a settlement — and on what terms, funded by whom |
| Middle East | Force, on Article II claims | Executive in practice; Congress on paper | Whether the ceasefire holds; whether any authorization vote ever occurs |

Read the right-hand columns together and the pattern from Lesson 1 reappears: the instruments that require Congress (money, statutes, treaties) move slowly and bind durably; the instruments the executive holds alone (force, recognition, controls, agreements) move fast and reverse fast. Where a front sits on that spectrum predicts its volatility better than any school's theory does.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. foreign policy requires:

1. A view on **what U.S. interests actually are** (the framework question — Lesson 2's three schools).
2. A view on **how those interests should be pursued** — force, alliances, diplomacy, sanctions, trade — and in what proportion.
3. A view on **where current commitments are right, overextended, or insufficient** (the calibration question), tested against the four fronts above.
4. An honest accounting of **what evidence would change the position** — the discipline every school in Lesson 2 accepted.

What this lesson did not settle: whether a Ukraine settlement holds or merely pauses; whether the Iran ceasefire becomes a relationship or a countdown; whether deterrence over Taiwan is strengthening or eroding; and whether Congress reclaims any of the powers practice has ceded. Those are the live questions — which makes them exactly what the Coach will press you on.
