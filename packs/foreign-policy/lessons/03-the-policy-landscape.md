# The current policy landscape

Four foreign-policy fronts dominate U.S. policy attention as of 2025: China, Russia/Ukraine, the Middle East, and the global trading system. Each is a contested question, and U.S. positioning is in flux.

## China

The dominant U.S. foreign-policy challenge of the next generation. Current U.S. China policy combines:

- **Trade restrictions.** Section 301 tariffs (imposed 2018, expanded periodically), export controls on semiconductors and AI hardware (BIS rules, 2022 onward), CFIUS reviews of investment.
- **Indo-Pacific deployment.** Increased rotational presence in Australia, Japan, the Philippines; expanded port-call frequency; AUKUS submarine partnership.
- **Taiwan.** Continued arms sales, periodic transits of the Taiwan Strait, official "strategic ambiguity" about defending Taiwan in case of PRC invasion.
- **Domestic industrial policy.** CHIPS and Science Act ($52B for domestic semiconductor manufacturing), IRA's clean-energy provisions (designed partly to reduce Chinese supply-chain dependence).

The three perspectives diverge sharply on China:
- Liberal internationalists support the current posture, with emphasis on multilateral coordination (working with allies on export controls, building Indo-Pacific frameworks like IPEF).
- Restrainers worry the framing is too confrontational, that decoupling is economically costly, and that strategic competition tips into conflict if not managed.
- Conservative realists support the posture and want to deepen it — more shipbuilding, more semiconductors, more deterrence in the Indo-Pacific.

## Russia / Ukraine

The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine triggered the largest European security event since the Cold War. Current U.S. policy includes:

- **Military aid to Ukraine.** ~$60B in security assistance through 2024; congressional approval became contested in 2024.
- **Sanctions on Russia.** Extensive financial sanctions (banking restrictions, central-bank reserve freezes, energy-sector restrictions) coordinated with G7 partners.
- **NATO reinforcement.** Increased U.S. troop levels in Europe (~100,000 currently, vs. ~65,000 pre-2022); Finland and Sweden joined NATO 2023-2024.
- **Energy diplomacy.** Coordinated with allies to reduce European dependence on Russian gas (LNG export expansion, accelerated renewable buildout).

The three perspectives:
- Liberal internationalists support sustained Ukraine support and treat it as central to defending the post-1945 order.
- Restrainers split — some support Ukraine aid as defense of a victim of aggression; others worry it draws the U.S. closer to direct conflict with Russia and that the cost-benefit doesn't justify open-ended commitment.
- Conservative realists generally support Ukraine aid as a means of weakening a U.S. competitor at relatively low cost (Ukraine fights, U.S. supplies).

## The Middle East

The U.S. footprint in the Middle East is being slowly reduced post-Iraq/post-Afghanistan, but the region remains central to several issues:

- **Iran.** Sanctions, intermittent diplomatic engagement, periodic military strikes against Iranian-backed proxies.
- **Israel.** Strong defense commitment, post-Oct. 7, 2023 sustained engagement.
- **Saudi Arabia.** Long-standing security relationship, complicated by Khashoggi murder, Yemen war, and OPEC+ coordination with Russia.
- **Counter-terrorism.** Continued operations against ISIS remnants in Syria and Iraq; periodic strikes elsewhere under the 2001 AUMF.

Restrainer position has more traction here than on China or Russia. Liberal internationalists generally want sustained engagement; conservative realists are mixed — some support sustained presence, others advocate retrenchment to focus on China.

## Trade

The post-1990s U.S. consensus on free-trade agreements has fractured. Current state:

- **No new comprehensive trade deals** since the 2015 Trans-Pacific Partnership (which the U.S. signed but did not ratify).
- **USMCA** (2020) replaced NAFTA with more protective provisions.
- **IPEF (Indo-Pacific Economic Framework)** launched 2022 — non-binding economic cooperation, not a tariff-reducing trade agreement.
- **Tariffs as policy.** Section 301 (China), Section 232 (steel/aluminum), reciprocal-tariff threats. The 1990s-2010s assumption that tariffs were politically dead has been reversed.

The three perspectives align less neatly here. Liberal internationalists want resumption of multilateral trade liberalization but recognize the political coalition has shifted. Restrainers are split between free-trade traditionalists and protectionist factions. Conservative realists generally support strategic-trade restrictions on China while remaining open to trade with allies.

## The AUMF and war-powers debate

The 2001 AUMF remains the legal basis for ongoing U.S. military operations in multiple countries. Reform efforts:

- **Repeal the 2002 Iraq AUMF.** Has bipartisan support; passed Senate but not enacted.
- **Sunset and rewrite the 2001 AUMF.** Various proposals; none has passed.
- **War Powers Resolution amendments.** Periodic proposals to strengthen reporting and consultation requirements.
- **Specific authorizations.** Ad-hoc votes on Yemen, Iran, Syria — usually procedural rather than substantive.

The three perspectives diverge on this:
- Liberal internationalists prefer specific updated authorizations rather than open-ended ones.
- Restrainers want more aggressive constraint of executive war-making.
- Conservative realists generally accept executive flexibility as necessary for credible deterrence.

## What's likely next

- **A 2024-2028 administration test of the China posture.** Whether semiconductor controls expand or contract; whether Taiwan ambiguity holds; whether trade-restriction or trade-engagement dominates.
- **Ukraine support continuation.** Funding and weapons-transfer cycles; the political coalition for continued aid is more contested in 2025 than it was in 2022.
- **AUMF reform.** Periodic congressional votes, with the 2002 AUMF repeal most likely to pass first.
- **Industrial-policy expansion.** CHIPS, IRA, defense procurement reform, Defense Production Act invocations. The line between foreign policy and industrial policy has blurred.
- **Crisis-response tests.** Specific events (a Taiwan-strait crisis, a Russian provocation, a Middle Eastern flare-up) will force position-taking that articulates which framework U.S. policy is operating under.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on U.S. foreign policy requires:

1. A view on **what U.S. interests actually are** (the framework question).
2. A view on **how those interests should be pursued** — military force, alliances, diplomacy, sanctions, trade — and in what proportion.
3. A view on **where current commitments are right, overextended, or insufficient** (the calibration question).
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
