## The author who argued back

In July 1947 George Kennan, writing as "X," argued for a patient but firm containment of Russian expansive tendencies. It became the most influential piece of strategy writing in American history. Kennan spent the next fifty years protesting what Washington built from it.

- George Kennan, 1947

## What is being argued about

The argument is not over whether the United States should have a foreign policy. It is over what its enormous power is for. Three serious schools answer differently, and none maps cleanly onto party.

One fact is shared ground. There has been no war between great powers since 1945, the longest such stretch in modern history. The schools disagree about what caused it.

> No great-power war since 1945. The fight is over the cause.

## The liberal internationalist

The liberal internationalist argues that the order the United States built, of alliances, institutions and open trade, produced the peace. G. John Ikenberry is the school's leading theorist. It points to NATO: no member has been attacked by a great power in the alliance's 75-plus years.

- Ikenberry: easy to join, hard to overturn

What would change its mind is evidence that the order no longer pays: allies that consume security without producing it, and costs that swamp the dividend.

> The internationalist case: deterrence is cheaper than the wars it prevents.

[check q4]

## The restrainer

The restrainer argues that nuclear deterrence and luck did the heavy lifting, and that the order claims credit it cannot prove. Barry Posen wrote the school's canonical text. It points to the wars since September 11: roughly 8 trillion dollars and more than 900,000 direct deaths.

- Posen: restraint is not isolationism

The school wants vigorous trade and diplomacy. What would change its mind is evidence that retrenchment invites catastrophe.

> The restrainer case: defend genuinely vital interests, and only those.

[check q2]

## The conservative realist

The conservative realist argues that American power, and the regional balances it enforced, kept the peace. John Mearsheimer's offensive realism is the school's spine. It points to China as the only plausible peer, so the Indo-Pacific gets the ships, the chips and the alliances.

- Mearsheimer: China is the central case

What would change its mind is evidence that China is not a true peer, or that selective retrenchment unravels.

> The realist case: the portfolio is not too big, but undiscriminated.

## Settled, and contested

What remains contested is everything that matters: whether American engagement caused the peace or merely attended it, whether specific commitments pass a cost-benefit test, and whether values belong among the aims.

- Caused the peace, or attended it?
- Do the commitments pass the test?
- Do values belong among the aims?

The lesson gives each school its full brief and its evidence, sets the Thucydides Trap against its critics, and shows where the three land on NATO, Ukraine and China.
