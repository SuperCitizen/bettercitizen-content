July 1947. Foreign Affairs publishes an article called "The Sources of Soviet Conduct." Its byline is a single letter: X.

The author is soon unmasked. He is George Kennan, the State Department's top Soviet hand. In it, he argues for "a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies."

[beat]

Containment. The article became the most influential piece of strategy writing in American history.

And Kennan spent the next fifty years protesting what Washington built from it. The militarization of containment. Vietnam. And finally NATO's enlargement, which he called, in 1997, "the most fateful error of American policy in the entire post-cold-war era."

[pause]

The man who wrote the strategy never stopped arguing with the people who ran it.

That is the permanent condition of American grand strategy. Nobody is arguing over whether the United States should have a foreign policy.

> The argument is over what America's enormous power is for.

Last time, the machinery: who holds which lever. This time, the argument over where to point it.

Three serious schools answer differently. None of them maps cleanly onto a party, and each has a wing in both. By the end, you should be able to state each case as its smartest advocate would, and to say what evidence would change its mind.

We will take it in three steps. First, the three questions that divide the schools. Second, the three cases, each built the same way. And last, one debate, over China, that runs through the whole field.

## Three questions

Start on ground that nobody owns.

There has been no war between great powers since 1945. The historian John Lewis Gaddis named that stretch "the Long Peace." No school disputes the fact. They divide over three questions.

The first is a question of cause. What kept the peace? Was it American hegemony, the dominance of one power? Was it nuclear deterrence? Or was it luck?

The second is a question of accounting. Do specific commitments pass a cost-benefit test? The enlargement of NATO. The American presence in the Middle East. Taiwan.

The third is a question of values. Should American power serve liberal ends? Or should it serve only security and prosperity, narrowly defined?

[pause]

The peace. The ledger. Values.

> No great-power war since 1945. The schools disagree about why.

Hold on to those three questions, because every school answers all three. Each case gets the same treatment: what the school protects, how it answers, the evidence it points to, and what would change its mind.

## The liberal internationalist case

Begin with the liberal internationalist. What this school protects is the order itself: the alliances, the institutions and the open trade that the United States built after 1945.

Its leading theorist is G. John Ikenberry. He argues that after 1945 the United States did something new. It did not rule as empires had ruled. It built an order of binding institutions, NATO among them, that restrained American power on purpose. That made it safe for weaker states to join, when they might have balanced against it.

In his signature formulation, the order is easy to join and hard to overturn. And its pillars hold each other up. Pull one out, and the rest sag.

So, its three answers.

On the peace: the order produced it. It coincided with the longest great-power peace in modern history, with the largest reduction of poverty ever recorded, and with the spread of democratic government to dozens of countries. And the alternative on offer from history is a world of many powers, with no underwriter. That world produced 1914. Then it produced 1939.

On the ledger: the commitments mostly pay, because deterrence is cheaper than the wars it prevents.

And on values: promoting democracy is an interest, not a charity.

> The liberal internationalist case: the order produced the peace, and its commitments mostly pay.

Now the evidence this school points to. Four things.

First, alliances deter. In more than 75 years, no member of NATO has been attacked by a great power. The counterfactual cannot be observed. But the two newest members, Finland and Sweden, had the best view of Russia, and they applied. On this reading, that is a market signal about what deters.

Second, democracies rarely fight each other. That finding is called the democratic peace, and it is among the most robust in political science.

Third, soft power is real power. The term is Joseph Nye's. It names the ability to get what you want through attraction, and not through coercion or payment. Universities. Culture. The perceived legitimacy of the system you run. It is cheap, it compounds, and it is exactly what unilateralism burns.

Fourth, the economic dividend. The primacy of the dollar rests on the credibility of the system the United States anchors. So does the unique reach of American sanctions.

And what would change this position? Evidence that the order no longer pays. Allies that consume security without producing it. Institutions captured by rivals. Maintenance costs that swamp the dividend.

Internationalists point here to The Hague, in June 2025, where NATO's members committed 5 percent of GDP to defense by 2035. On their reading, that pledge is proof the model can be renegotiated, and need not be abandoned. Whether the pledges are kept, they concede, is the test.

## The restrainer case

Now the restrainer, built the same way. What this school protects is the republic itself. On its view, armed crusades corrupt both the means and the republic.

Its canonical text is a book called Restraint, by Barry Posen. And its starting move is to split a claim that internationalists run together. Some American engagement is good: nobody disputes that. The current amount is necessary: that is very much disputed.

Posen argues that the present strategy, which he calls liberal hegemony, treats every region as vital. It converts allies into dependents. And it generates the wars it claims to prevent. A restrained strategy would defend what is truly vital: the homeland, command of the commons, and the balance of power in Asia.

So, its three answers.

On the peace: nuclear deterrence and luck did the heavy lifting. The order claims credit it cannot prove.

On the ledger: the accounting since September 11 is what unexamined commitments cost. And allies under an American umbrella rationally under-invest.

And on values: trade and diplomacy, yes. Armed crusades, no.

> The restrainer case: defend what is vital, and count what the rest has cost.

Now the evidence this school points to. Four things.

First, the ledger since September 11. Brown University's Costs of War project tallies the wars that followed at roughly 8 trillion dollars. [beat] And at more than 900,000 direct deaths. Afghanistan ended in 2021, with the Taliban back in Kabul. What theory of the national interest, the restrainer asks, survives that accounting?

Second, moral hazard. When Washington underwrites a region's security, the locals under-invest and over-reach. Before 2022, most NATO members sat below even the 2 percent guideline. And restrainers look at the pledge from The Hague with an arched eyebrow. The deadline is 2035. The enforcement is a review, in 2029. And pledges made under pressure have historically outrun delivery.

Third, escalation. Forces stationed against a rival's border can provoke the very spiral they are meant to deter. Restrainers note that Kennan himself predicted, in 1997, that expanding NATO would inflame Russian nationalism. The careful version of that claim is not that expansion caused the invasion of 2022. Putin's choices did. It is that the risks were named in advance, and the costs landed as predicted, while the question went largely undebated at home.

Fourth, the threat is measured wrong. A study by Michael Beckley finds that gross indicators like GDP overstate challengers. Inflated threats, on this view, produce inflated commitments.

One clarification, the one this school repeats most: restraint is not isolationism. Restrainers want vigorous trade and diplomacy.

And what would change this position? Evidence that retrenchment invites catastrophe. A Chinese move on Taiwan, or Russian opportunism, that costs more than the commitments did. Restrainers accept the burden of showing that their strategy can fail gracefully. The serious ones build in hedges for exactly that reason: naval power offshore, and the alliance with Japan.

## The conservative realist case

Now the conservative realist, built the same way. What this school protects is America's position: its power, and the interests that power serves.

This case is routinely confused with restraint, and the confusion is instructive. The realist agrees with the internationalist that American power organizes the world. The realist agrees with the restrainer that much of the current portfolio fails a cost-benefit test. The disagreement is the frame. Foreign policy is about power and interest. Not the upkeep of an order. Not thrift. And never values for their own sake.

Its intellectual spine is the work of John Mearsheimer, and a theory called offensive realism. It argues that among states there is anarchy: no world government, no nine-one-one to call. So no state can ever be certain of another's intentions. So great powers rationally seek to dominate their own regions, and to prevent any peer from dominating another.

So, its three answers.

On the peace: American power kept it, along with the regional balances that power enforced.

On the ledger: the portfolio fails, not because it is too big, but because it does not discriminate. China is the only problem on the scale of a peer, and secondary theaters drain the primary one.

And on values: they are instruments, never ends.

> The conservative realist case: power and interest, concentrated on the one peer, China.

In practice, that means discrimination. The Indo-Pacific gets the ships, the chips and the alliances. Russia is a regional problem, for Europeans rich enough to handle it. And the Middle East merits deterrence from offshore, not occupation.

Now the evidence this school points to. Four things.

First, the American tradition itself. Dominate the Western Hemisphere. Then fight or out-build every aspiring hegemon elsewhere: Imperial Japan, Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union.

Second, the record since the Cold War. Mearsheimer argues that Washington squandered its position on liberal hegemony: democracy promotion, NATO expansion, nation-building. Those projects ignored how power works, he argues, and they bought twenty years of failed wars.

Third, the industrial base. Hard power is the currency that matters: industrial capacity, munitions stocks, shipbuilding. Realists note that demand from the war in Ukraine forced the United States to multiply its production of artillery shells severalfold, from a near-cold start. On this reading, that is a warning about what a Pacific war would ask.

Fourth, the partners America courts. Saudi Arabia and Vietnam, realists note, are courted because geography beats ideology.

And what would change this position? Evidence that China is not a true peer. Beckley's measurements cut both ways. Or evidence that selective retrenchment unravels: that abandoning secondary theaters emboldens rivals in the primary one, because a reputation for resolve does not stay inside one region.

## Side by side

Three cases. Hold them side by side on one question: when to use force.

The liberal internationalist: rarely, lawfully, multilaterally.

[beat]

The restrainer: almost never, and only in defense of vital interests.

[beat]

The conservative realist: decisively, when concrete interests are threatened.

[pause]

Let's test whether you can tell them apart. After 2022, a liberal internationalist and a conservative realist both supported arming Ukraine. Same policy. What is the tell that distinguishes their reasons? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Listen for the why. The internationalist defends Ukraine as a defense of the order itself. Borders cannot be changed by force, so the norm holds everywhere. The realist defends it as cheap attrition of a rival: Russia's military degraded, without American casualties.

The difference surfaces when costs rise. The realist's support is contingent on the price, and on China remaining the priority. The internationalist's is contingent on the norm still being defensible.

And the restrainer stands apart from both, pushing for a negotiated settlement, on the argument that the risk of escalation and the cost outrun the interest.

You have now heard all three at full strength, and one of them convinced you least. Take that one. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## Thucydides in Washington

One debate remains, and the whole field is in it.

The question is what a rising China confronts. In Mearsheimer's telling, it confronts the United States. That is a problem of power transition. In Ikenberry's telling, it confronts the order. That is a problem of institutions. And a China grown rich inside that order has reasons not to wreck it.

The pessimistic half of that debate has a brand name. In a book called Destined for War, Graham Allison called it the Thucydides Trap. The name comes from the historian's line that the growth of Athenian power, and the fear it inspired in Sparta, made war inevitable.

Allison's project at Harvard coded 16 cases of a rising power challenging a ruling one. [beat] Of those, 12 ended in war.

> Allison: the danger is a rising power meeting a ruling one.

The critics are worth equal time. Historians have disputed both the selection of cases and the coding. Squeeze 2,400 years into so few data points, and the trap starts to look assembled, not discovered. And political scientists counter that talk of inevitability can become self-fulfilling, in both capitals.

The sharpest rejoinder inverts the thesis. In a book called Danger Zone, Hal Brands and Michael Beckley argue that the danger is not a rising China. It is a peaking one. Slowing growth. Shrinking demographics. Closing windows. A power that believes its moment is passing has reasons to move early.

> Brands and Beckley: the danger is a China that is peaking.

Put that to work. One analyst says war with China is most dangerous in the 2030s, when its economy may pass America's. Another says the danger is this decade, while Beijing still thinks it can win. Whose framework is each one using? And what does each imply for the defense budget?

[long pause]

The first is running Allison's logic. Danger peaks at the transition, so build patiently, for a long competition. The second is running the logic of Brands and Beckley. Danger peaks while a slowing China still believes its window is open, so surge deterrence now.

The same data, and opposite schedules for buying weapons. That is why a dispute from the seminar room shows up in the markup of appropriations bills.

## Settled, and contested

Before the gather, pull something back yourself. Near the start I gave you the three questions that divide the schools. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

What kept the peace. Whether the commitments pass the test. Whether values belong.

Some of the ground under those questions is firm. There has been no great-power war since 1945, the longest such stretch in modern history. American defense spending was 954 billion dollars in 2025, by SIPRI's count. That is about a third of the world total. It buys structural overmatch against any single rival, though munitions depth and industrial capacity are acknowledged gaps.

What remains contested is everything that matters. Whether American engagement caused the peace, or merely attended it. Whether specific commitments pass the test. Whether values belong in the objective at all.

Those are contested questions, not settled ones.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The argument is over what America's enormous power is for. Three questions divide the schools: the peace, the ledger, and values.

[beat]

Two. The liberal internationalist case: the order produced the peace, and its commitments mostly pay. Its evidence: alliances deter, democracies rarely fight each other, soft power, and the dollar. It would change on evidence that the order no longer pays.

[beat]

Three. The restrainer case: defend what is vital, and count what the rest has cost. Its evidence: the ledger since September 11, moral hazard, escalation, and a threat measured wrong. It would change on evidence that retrenchment invites catastrophe.

[beat]

Four. The conservative realist case: power and interest, concentrated on the one peer, China. Its evidence: the American tradition, twenty years of failed wars, and the industrial base. It would change on evidence that China is not a true peer, or that selective retrenchment unravels.

[beat]

And five. The debate over China. Does a rising China confront the United States, or the order? And is the danger a rising power, or a peaking one?

[pause]

Notice what each school did. It picked a frame. It said why. And it named the evidence that would change it.

> A defensible position names the evidence that would change it.

The next lecture takes the three frames to the fights of the moment: China, Ukraine, Iran, and the tariff wars.

For now, go back to Kennan. The man who wrote the strategy never stopped arguing with the people who ran it. [beat] You can now join that argument, in any of its three voices.
