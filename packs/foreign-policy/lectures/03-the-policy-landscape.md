February 20, 2026. At the Supreme Court, Chief Justice John Roberts announces the judgment in a case called Learning Resources v. Trump.

The case turns on one statute: the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, a sanctions law from 1977. Since early 2025, the president had been using it to impose tariffs on much of the world's goods.

The Court's answer is that the statute does not authorize tariffs at all.

[pause]

Six justices agreed on the reason. A statutory power to regulate importation is not the power to tax. And the power to tax is one that Article One gives to Congress.

With that, a year of duties became refundable overpayments. [beat] As much as 175 billion dollars, by the projection of the Penn Wharton Budget Model.

Two lectures ago, it was a classroom point: constitutionally, a tariff is a congressional tax. This is what that point looks like with money attached.

And the case is the right door into the landscape of 2026.

> Every front is a story about instruments, and who controls them.

Four fronts dominate, as of mid-2026. Trade. China. Russia and Ukraine. And the Middle East. Under all four runs the fight over war powers.

One caution before we walk them. This is a snapshot of a moving target. The facts will age. The durable part is the three frameworks from last time: the liberal internationalist, the restrainer, and the conservative realist.

So on each front, ask three things. What is the lever in play? Who holds it? And which school's prescription does current policy most resemble? [beat] The answer to that last one is rarely a purebred.

We will walk the four fronts in order, and then step back for the pattern underneath.

## Trade: the tariff power goes home

Start with trade, where that February morning belongs.

The old consensus on freer trade fractured a decade ago, and it has not re-formed. The United States has concluded no comprehensive trade agreement since the Trans-Pacific Partnership. And that one was signed, never ratified, and abandoned in January 2017.

The tariff, which the economics of the 1990s had declared politically dead, came back as the leading instrument. First came duties on China, from 2018, under a provision called Section 301. Then duties on steel and aluminum, under Section 232. And then the escalation of 2025, under the emergency statute: tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China in February, and a global schedule in April.

The courts pushed back almost at once. The Court of International Trade ruled against the emergency tariffs in May 2025. An appeals court affirmed in August. And the Supreme Court closed the question, that February morning.

Now, what the ruling did not do matters as much as what it did.

It was statutory, not constitutional. Tariffs that rest on an express delegation survived. Section 301 comes from the Trade Act of 1974. Section 232 comes from the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. Both delegate tariff authority explicitly. And both set steps to complete first: investigations, findings. The emergency framework had let the executive skip those steps.

> The Court held that one statute does not authorize tariffs. Nothing broader.

Let's test that. It is the morning after the ruling. Which American tariffs have to come down? All of them? Only the ones on China? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the tariffs that rested on the emergency statute. The duties under Sections 301 and 232 stand on different legal ground, and they were untouched.

Notice the near-miss. It is tempting to hear the ruling as "the president can never tariff." That overreads the case. Congress can delegate tariff power, and it has. The Court found only that this statute didn't. That is the whole case, in one distinction.

So the question went back to Congress, which has three choices. It can delegate broad tariff power again, by statute. It can claw back the delegations that remain. Or it can do what it has mostly done: nothing, and leave tariff policy to the narrower statutes.

On trade, the schools split unusually, and they cross their usual lines.

Liberal internationalists want the United States back at the multilateral table. They concede that the old free-trade coalition is gone.

Restrainers divide, between free-trade traditionalists and economic nationalists.

Conservative realists support strategic restrictions on China: chips, critical minerals, dual-use technology. They prefer open trade with allies. Their theory is that the point of trade policy is power, not protection.

What to watch on this front: the first scheduled review of USMCA, the agreement that replaced NAFTA, in July 2026. And any statute that restores or restrains broad tariff power.

## China: the pacing challenge

Second front: China.

Successive strategy documents have called China the "pacing challenge." That means the one competitor with the scale to challenge American power across every dimension at once.

The frame is called strategic competition. It was formalized in 2017, and it has now outlasted three administrations, of both parties. That makes it the closest thing this era has to a consensus.

The toolkit has four layers.

Technology controls. Export controls on advanced semiconductors and AI hardware, tightened repeatedly since October 2022, along with the screening of investment.

Forward posture. Expanded access for rotating forces in Australia, Japan and the Philippines, and the submarine partnership called AUKUS.

Taiwan. Arms sales under the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979. And an official policy called strategic ambiguity: a refusal, by design, to say whether the United States would fight for the island. It is intended to deter Beijing without emboldening Taipei.

And industrial policy. Roughly 52 billion dollars for domestic semiconductor manufacturing, under the CHIPS and Science Act. But the clean-energy leg of that strategy was largely repealed in July 2025. Subsidies are the easiest instrument to pass, and the easiest to undo.

Who holds these levers? The executive, under broad delegations from Congress.

And here the schools sit exactly where the last lecture left them.

Internationalists endorse the posture, and argue that the controls work only multilaterally. Chips flow through Dutch lithography and Japanese chemicals, so a wall built by one country leaks.

Restrainers argue that treating rivalry as inevitable helps make it so. And they argue that decoupling taxes Americans for security it may not buy.

Realists argue for all of it, and more: more shipbuilding, and deeper stocks of munitions. On their reading, a defense budget of 1 trillion dollars is the minimum price of deterring a peer.

What to watch: whether strategic ambiguity survives a crisis in the Taiwan Strait. And whether the allies align on chip controls.

## Russia and Ukraine: the war meets the deadline

Third front: Russia and Ukraine.

Russia's invasion, in February 2022, began the largest European war since 1945. The response had four parts. More than 60 billion dollars in American security assistance, committed from 2022 through 2024. The most extensive sanctions ever coordinated, built with the G7 partners. An American troop presence in Europe that surged to about 100,000. And Finland and Sweden, joining NATO.

By mid-2026, that policy has changed shape.

Funding became contested ground in 2024, when the last major aid package stalled for months before passing. The current administration has announced no new packages of security assistance. It continues only the deliveries already committed. And it has put its weight behind diplomacy: negotiations brokered by the United States, under an American deadline of June 2026 for the parties to reach a settlement.

Europe, meanwhile, is re-arming, under NATO's pledge at The Hague to spend 5 percent of GDP on defense.

And here is something worth slowing down for. All three schools claim that pledge as vindication.

> The internationalist reading: proof that alliances can be renegotiated.

> The restrainer reading: proof that allies spend only when Washington credibly steps back.

> The realist reading: the precondition for handing Europe the European problem.

One pledge. Three readings.

Now put the schools to work on the policy itself. Washington presses both sides toward a settlement, under a deadline, while announcing no new aid. Whose prescription does that most resemble? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The form is the restrainer's: a negotiated settlement, because cost and the risk of escalation outran the interest.

The motive is a realist's: shift the burden to Europe, and free resources for Asia.

And the internationalist dissents, on the stakes and not on the mechanics. A settlement that ratifies conquest re-prices every border the order protects.

So each school watches something different. The internationalist watches whether the terms reward aggression. The restrainer watches whether the United States actually retrenches, or just rebrands. The realist watches one number: whether what is freed up actually moves to the Pacific.

On this front the lever is split. Congress appropriates. The executive negotiates. What to watch: whether the deadline produces a settlement, on what terms, and funded by whom.

## The Middle East: two Iran campaigns

Fourth front: the Middle East.

This series opened in June 2025, with the strikes on Fordow, a twelve-day war, and a ceasefire. That turned out to be the prologue.

On February 28, 2026, American and Israeli forces opened a far larger campaign. Nearly 900 strikes in the first twelve hours, against Iranian missiles, air defenses and leadership. The strikes killed Iran's Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei.

Iran retaliated across the region, against American installations and against oil infrastructure in the Gulf. The fighting spread to Lebanon.

And the war's economic center became a strip of water: the Strait of Hormuz. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through it, and Iran can hold it at risk. Commercial transit collapsed, at the worst point to near zero. Oil had been at about 70 dollars a barrel. It jumped to peaks above 120.

A ceasefire was brokered in early April, with Pakistan as intermediary. It has held unevenly since, punctuated by renewed exchanges between Iran and Israel in early June.

> As of mid-2026, the Iran war is paused, not settled.

The rest of the regional file is continuity: the commitment to Israel, the Saudi security relationship, and counter-ISIS operations in Syria and Iraq, still running on the 2001 authorization.

And the schools? The restrainer position has more traction here than on any other front. This is the region where the ledger since September 11 was written. Internationalists and realists divide over another question: whether deterrence from offshore can hold what forward presence held.

## The war-powers scoreboard

Under the Iran war sits the constitutional story, with new entries.

Neither campaign was preceded by any vote in Congress. Not the strikes of June 2025. Not the campaign of February 2026. Both rested on claims under Article Two.

Congress has answered in three ways, as of mid-2026.

First, it repealed the Iraq war authorizations of 1991 and 2002. That is real. But it is irrelevant to operations against Iran, which never cited them.

Second, it has left the 2001 authorization standing. Proposals to sunset it are still stalled.

Third, on June 3, 2026, the House voted 215 to 208 to direct an end to hostilities with Iran. It was the first time a chamber has voted to halt this war.

Now set that against the War Powers Resolution, as written. A report within 48 hours. Then the operation ends within 60 days, unless Congress authorizes it, with 30 more days to withdraw.

So work it out. The campaign began on February 28. When does a 60-day clock run out? And what does a House vote in June tell you about what actually governs?

[pause 5s]

The clock runs out in late April. In practice, it has never been enforced. And the April ceasefire let the executive call hostilities "terminated" on its own schedule, as it did in 2025.

The House vote came a month after the clock would have run. So the real constraint does not sit in statutory machinery that enforces itself. It sits in political mathematics. And a majority of 215 to 208, in one chamber, is not enough math.

> To bind a president, a measure must clear both chambers and survive a veto.

No war-powers resolution ever has.

## A lever, an owner, a test

Four fronts. Now step back, and pull something back from memory. On each front, who holds the lever in play? Trade. China. Ukraine. The Middle East.

[long pause]

On trade: Congress, unless it delegates again. The executive keeps Sections 301 and 232.

On China: the executive, under broad delegations.

On Russia and Ukraine: Congress appropriates, and the executive negotiates.

On the Middle East: the executive in practice, and Congress on paper.

Hear the pattern in that. The instruments that require Congress are money, statutes and treaties. The instruments the executive holds alone are force, recognition, controls and agreements.

> Congress's instruments move slowly and bind. The executive's move fast and reverse fast.

Where a front sits on that spectrum predicts its volatility, better than any school's theory does.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Every front is a story about instruments, and who controls them. Congress's instruments move slowly and bind. The executive's move fast and reverse fast.

[beat]

Two. Trade. The Court held that one statute does not authorize tariffs. Nothing broader. Tariffs under Sections 301 and 232 survived, and the next move belongs to Congress.

[beat]

Three. China. The pacing challenge, and the closest thing this era has to a consensus. Four layers: technology controls, forward posture, Taiwan, and industrial policy.

[beat]

Four. Russia and Ukraine. No new aid, and a deadline to settle. The form is the restrainer's, the motive is a realist's, and the internationalist dissents on the stakes. And all three schools claim Europe's 5 percent pledge as vindication.

[beat]

And five. The Middle East. Two Iran campaigns in twelve months, neither preceded by a vote in Congress. The war is paused, not settled. And to bind a president, a measure must clear both chambers and survive a veto.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Whether a settlement in Ukraine holds, or merely pauses. Whether the ceasefire with Iran becomes a relationship, or a countdown. Whether deterrence over Taiwan is strengthening, or eroding. And whether Congress reclaims any of the powers that practice has ceded.

There is no next lecture in this series. From here, the argument goes to you. And a defensible position on foreign policy requires four things.

A view on what American interests actually are. That is the question of frameworks: the three schools.

A view on how those interests should be pursued, and in what proportion: force, alliances, diplomacy, sanctions, trade.

A view on where current commitments are right, where they are overextended, and where they are insufficient, tested against the four fronts.

And an honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position, and then to run a drill. You can begin that drill now. Of the three frameworks, take the one you find least persuasive. State its best case on one of these fronts, as its advocates would.

[pause 5s]

That is the drill this series exists for.

Now go back to the February morning where we began. One judgment. A year of tariffs, undone. [beat] An instrument, and an argument over who controls it. You can now find that argument on every front.
