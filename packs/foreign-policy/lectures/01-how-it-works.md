June 21, 2025. Just before seven in the evening, Washington time.

Fourteen bunker-buster bombs strike Iran's nuclear-enrichment sites at Fordow and Natanz. Each bomb weighs 30,000 pounds.

Seven B-2 bombers flew the mission from Missouri: a round trip of roughly thirty-seven hours. A decoy flight over the Pacific masked their approach.

[pause]

That evening, the president announces the operation on social media. Most members of Congress learn of it the way everyone else does.

The formal notice arrives two days later. It is a letter, and it cites the president's "constitutional authority as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive."

No vote in Congress came before the strikes. And none came after. Six days later the Senate declined, 47 to 53, to advance a resolution requiring one.

[pause]

Now hold that scene against the text.

Article One, section eight, gives Congress, not the president, the power "to declare War." [beat] Congress last used that power in June 1942.

Edward Corwin, writing on the presidency, gave a verdict that has aged well. The Constitution, he wrote, is "an invitation to struggle for the privilege of directing American foreign policy."

[beat]

An invitation to struggle.

This lecture maps the machine that struggle has produced: who holds which lever, and what each lever does. Whether the machine is pointed at the right things is an argument, and it waits for next time.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the war power: what the text says, and what practice has done with it.

Second, the laws Congress has written about war since.

Third, promises: treaties, agreements and alliances.

And last, the quieter tools, which do most of the work.

## Two branches, one steering wheel

Start with the text. The Constitution splits foreign policy down the middle.

Congress holds its share in Article One. It declares war. It raises and supports armies, and it provides and maintains a navy. It regulates commerce with foreign nations. And it appropriates every dollar, which is the power of the purse.

Stop on commerce for a moment, because a tariff is a tax on foreign commerce. So under the Constitution, a tariff is a congressional tax. Hold on to that. It comes back in the third lecture.

The president holds the other share, in Article Two. Command of the armed forces. The making of treaties, with the consent of two-thirds of the Senate. The appointment of ambassadors, also with the Senate's consent. And the receiving of ambassadors from abroad.

That last power sounds like ceremony. It is more. In 2015 the Supreme Court interpreted it to give the president exclusive power to recognize foreign governments.

[pause]

So the text gives Congress the decision to start a war, and gives the president the job of running it.

For eighty years, practice has run the other way.

Congress has declared war eleven times, across five conflicts: the War of 1812, the Mexican-American War, the Spanish-American War, and the two World Wars. [beat] And not once since June 1942.

Korea went ahead on a resolution of the United Nations Security Council, with no vote in Congress at all. President Truman called it a "police action." Vietnam ran on the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. The Gulf War, Afghanistan and Iraq ran on statutes that authorized force, and that stopped short of the word "war."

And all the while, presidents of both parties have used force on their own authority. Hundreds of times.

> The text gives Congress the war decision. Practice has run the other way.

Let's test the two lists. A president wants to make three moves. One: recognize a new government in a foreign capital. Two: put a tariff on that country's exports. Three: cut off aid that Congress has appropriated for its neighbor.

Which branch controls each move? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Recognition belongs to the president, and to the president alone. It flows from the power to receive ambassadors.

The tariff belongs to Congress. A tariff is a tax on commerce, so a president can impose one only where a statute delegates the power.

And the aid belongs to Congress too. An appropriation is law. A president who refuses to spend it collides with the power of the purse, and with a statute called the Impoundment Control Act.

## The sixty-day clock

That is the split, and the drift. Now Congress's attempt to stop the drift.

Vietnam convinced Congress that things had gone too far. So in November 1973 it passed the War Powers Resolution, over President Nixon's veto.

The statute's machinery has three parts. First, the president must consult Congress "in every possible instance" before introducing forces into hostilities. Second, the president must report any such move within 48 hours. Third, the president must end the operation within 60 days, unless Congress authorizes it. A 30-day window to withdraw follows.

Consult. Report in 48 hours. End in 60 days.

[pause]

That is the statute's text. Its life has been something else.

Every administration since, of both parties, has filed the reports. Well over a hundred of them. And each has maintained, all the while, that the Resolution's binding provisions are unconstitutional. So the reports arrive "consistent with" the statute. Never "pursuant to" it.

In 2011, the Obama administration argued that seven months of air operations over Libya were not "hostilities" at all. No hostilities, so no clock.

> Congress has never used the War Powers Resolution to force a withdrawal.

Now go back to the night we began with. The strikes on Iran ran the same playbook.

The 48-hour letter arrived. It rested on Article Two, and not on any statute.

Then came the Senate vote, on a resolution barring further offensive operations against Iran without authorization. It failed, by 47 to 53, nearly on party lines.

And the clock? [beat] On June 24, Israel and Iran reached a ceasefire. That ended the twelve-day war the strikes had joined. So the administration took the position that hostilities had "terminated." The 60-day question was moot.

The statute produced a report, a floor vote, and no constraint.

[pause]

The argument underneath is older than the statute, by 180 years.

In 1793, Hamilton and Madison argued it out, in what are called the Pacificus–Helvidius debates.

Hamilton argued that executive power is the natural residual home of foreign affairs. In plainer words: foreign affairs belong with the executive by default.

> Hamilton: foreign affairs belong, by nature, with the executive.

Madison argued the opposite. Here are his words. "In no part of the constitution is more wisdom to be found, than in the clause which confides the question of war or peace to the legislature, [beat] and not to the executive department."

> Madison: the question of war or peace belongs to the legislature.

Every fight over a war authorization since is a footnote to that exchange.

You may already lean toward one of those two. So take the other. State the best case for it, the way its author would.

[pause 5s]

## What Congress repealed, and what it did not

One more piece of the war power. If declarations stopped in 1942, what has carried the wars since?

Authorizations. The formal name is an Authorization for Use of Military Force, or AUMF. Two of them did most of the legal work after September 11.

The first is the 2001 authorization. It is a single sentence, of about 60 words. It authorizes force against the "nations, organizations, or persons" behind September 11.

Successive administrations stretched that sentence across operations in Afghanistan, Yemen, Somalia, Syria and elsewhere. They stretched it to cover ISIS, an organization that did not exist in 2001.

The second is the 2002 authorization, for Iraq. It outlived its war by two decades, as makeweight authority. In 2020 it was cited, alongside Article Two, for the strike that killed the Iranian general Qasem Soleimani.

That afterlife is now over. Congress folded a repeal into the National Defense Authorization Act, signed in December 2025. It repealed both Iraq authorizations: the one from 1991, and the one from 2002.

No war authorization had been clawed back since 1971, when Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution.

But the 2001 authorization was left alone. As of mid-2026 it remains in force: a quarter-century old, and still the statutory basis for counterterrorism operations on three continents.

> Congress repealed the Iraq authorizations. The 2001 authorization still stands.

Let's test that. It is 2027. A president orders strikes on an offshoot of ISIS, in a country where the 2001 authorization has never been applied, and cites that statute. A senator answers that Congress repealed "the AUMF" in 2025.

Who has the better factual ground? Think it through.

[long pause]

The president does, on the facts, whatever one thinks of the merits.

The repeal of 2025 covered the Iraq authorizations. The 2001 authorization was left standing, and it has no geographic limit and no sunset.

The senator's real remedy is the one Congress has debated and not passed: repealing or rewriting the 2001 statute itself.

Here is the shape of the whole story. Declarations. Then authorizations. Then claims under Article Two alone. Each step moves the war decision further from the floor of Congress. The repeal is the partial exception.

## A treaty, a statute, a signature

So much for force. Now promises.

The Constitution's other famous supermajority in foreign policy is the treaty power. Two-thirds of the Senate must consent. Hear that, and you would expect the Senate to be the gatekeeper of America's commitments abroad.

Mostly, it isn't.

Between 1940 and 1989, the United States entered 759 treaties. [beat] And more than 13,000 executive agreements.

So the modern default never meets the two-thirds bar at all. There are three ways to make a promise to another country, and what separates them is who must approve.

The first is the treaty itself, under Article Two. It needs two-thirds of the Senate. It is binding law, and it is politically costly to exit.

The second is an agreement passed as an ordinary statute, by majorities of both chambers. It is called a congressional-executive agreement. It is durable, because it is a statute. The trade agreement USMCA was made this way.

The third needs no approval at all, only the president's signature. It is called a sole executive agreement, or a political commitment. And it is the least durable of the three, because the next president can walk away.

[pause]

That third kind explains a decade of diplomatic whiplash.

Take the Paris climate agreement. The United States joined in 2016. It exited in 2020. It rejoined in 2021. And in January 2025, an exit was ordered again.

Take the Iran nuclear deal. Made in 2015. Exited in 2018.

Both were built as executive commitments for one reason: two-thirds of the Senate was unreachable. And each was unwound by a successor, with a signature.

NATO sits at the other pole. The Senate consented to its treaty in 1949, by 82 to 13.

> Durability tracks the approval hurdle.

Put another way: the harder a promise is to make, the harder it is to break. The trade is speed against durability. And foreign capitals price it in.

## The alliance system

One kind of promise deserves a stop of its own: the promise to defend another country.

For a century and a half, with brief exceptions, the United States would not make it. Washington's farewell advice was to "steer clear of permanent alliances." Until the late 1940s, the United States had no peacetime military allies.

It now has mutual-defense commitments to more than fifty countries.

[pause]

NATO is the core. It was founded in 1949, with 12 members. It now has 32. The two newest, Finland and Sweden, abandoned decades of nonalignment after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

The heart of NATO is Article Five: an attack on one is an attack on all. It has been invoked exactly once. [beat] By the allies, on behalf of the United States, the day after September 11.

At a summit in The Hague, in June 2025, the allies replaced their old guideline for defense spending, which was 2 percent of GDP. The new pledge is far larger: 5 percent of GDP, by 2035.

One caution, because this is so often said wrong.

> NATO's spending targets are not dues.

No ally owes the United States money. The targets measure what each member invests in its own defense. The real argument over burden-sharing is about free-riding on American capabilities, and not about unpaid bills.

In the Pacific there is no equivalent of NATO. There is a set of two-country treaties from the early Cold War, in a pattern called hub-and-spokes: with the Philippines, Australia, South Korea, Thailand and Japan.

Layered on top are looser ties. One is AUKUS, a submarine-technology partnership with Australia and Britain. Another is a label that misleads: Major Non-NATO Ally. About twenty partners hold it, among them Israel, Jordan and Qatar. It gives them privileged defense cooperation. [beat] It gives them no defense commitment.

Let's test the labels. In the same week, two countries are attacked. One is a treaty ally that hosts American bases. The other is a Major Non-NATO Ally. What does each label legally oblige the United States to do?

[long pause]

For the treaty ally, there is a mutual-defense commitment, ratified by the Senate. The United States is obliged to treat the attack as engaging American security, and to respond. Each treaty specifies how. NATO's lets each ally choose "such action as it deems necessary."

For the Major Non-NATO Ally: nothing. The label confers cooperation, like financing and access to technology. It does not confer protection.

> A treaty ally has a defense commitment. A Major Non-NATO Ally has none.

The gap between those two labels is exactly where credibility gets tested in a crisis.

## The quieter tools

Force is the loudest instrument. But most foreign policy is conducted with quieter ones. And 2025 rearranged several of them, more abruptly than any year since the 1940s.

Four tools, briefly.

Diplomacy. In July 2025 the State Department carried out its largest reorganization in decades, cutting its domestic workforce by about 18 percent.

Foreign aid. USAID, the leading development agency since 1961, was dismantled in 2025. It formally closed on July 1, its surviving programs absorbed into the State Department.

One number here is worth keeping. Foreign aid has historically run at about 1 percent of federal spending. [beat] Polls consistently find Americans guessing 20 percent or more.

Sanctions. The dollar's central place in global finance gives American sanctions reach beyond America's borders. A secondary sanction threatens a bank in a third country with losing access to the American financial system. That is why a Treasury designation can rearrange trade between two countries that never touch America.

And defense dollars. For fiscal year 2026, the administration requested about 1 trillion dollars for national defense. A symbolic line, crossed.

So: civilian instruments, reorganized in a single year. Supporters say rationalized. Critics say gutted.

## What to keep

Before I gather this up, pull one thing back yourself. A few minutes ago I gave you three ways to make a promise to another country. Can you still rank them, from most durable to least?

[long pause]

A treaty. A statute. A signature.

Now let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The text gives Congress the war decision, and practice has run the other way. Eleven declarations, five conflicts, and none since June 1942.

[beat]

Two. The War Powers Resolution: consult, report in 48 hours, end in 60 days. Congress has never used it to force a withdrawal. Over Iran, in 2025, it produced a report, a floor vote, and no constraint. And the argument underneath is as old as Hamilton and Madison.

[beat]

Three. Congress repealed the Iraq authorizations. The 2001 authorization still stands.

[beat]

Four. Durability tracks the approval hurdle. A treaty binds hardest. A signature can be undone by a signature.

[beat]

And five. A treaty ally has a defense commitment. A Major Non-NATO Ally has none. NATO has 32 members, and a pledge of 5 percent by 2035. And its spending targets are not dues.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not yet a position on anything. You know where the levers are. Should the United States run a foreign policy that is heavy on alliances, with its forces deployed forward? That is the genuinely contested question.

The next lecture gives the three serious answers, each at full strength. The liberal internationalist, who sees the order built after 1945 as the buy of the century. The restrainer, who sees overextension and moral hazard. And the conservative realist, who would spend the same power on fewer, harder priorities.

For now, go back to that evening in June. Bombers over Iran. A letter two days later. A vote that failed. [beat] The Constitution issued an invitation to struggle. You can now say who holds each lever, and who has been using it.
