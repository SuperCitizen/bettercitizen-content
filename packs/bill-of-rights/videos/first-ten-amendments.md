## The silted wharf

In the 1820s, John Barron co-owned one of the most profitable wharves in Baltimore harbor. Then the city, grading its streets, filled the water around it with sand and silt. Deep-draft ships could no longer dock there.

Barron sued under the Fifth Amendment, which says private property shall not be taken for public use without just compensation. In 1833 the Supreme Court told him the Bill of Rights did not apply to his case at all.

- Barron v. Baltimore, 1833

> In 1833, the first ten amendments restrained only the federal government.

## A promise extracted

The Constitution signed in 1787 had no bill of rights. Alexander Hamilton argued that one was unnecessary, and even dangerous. The public was not persuaded. State conventions agreed to ratify only while recommending amendments. In 1789 Congress sent twelve articles to the states, and by December 15, 1791, ten were ratified.

- 1787: no bill of rights
- 1789: twelve articles proposed
- 1791: ten ratified

## What the ten say

The ten sort into four clusters. The First and Second say what government may not touch. The Third and Fourth cover your home and person: the Fourth bars unreasonable searches and seizures. The Fifth through the Eighth say how government must treat you when it accuses you. The Ninth and Tenth say how to read the whole list.

- 1–2: what government may not touch
- 3–4: home and person
- 5–8: when you are accused
- 9–10: how to read the list

> Every guarantee in the ten amendments runs against government.

## The wall around the states

Back to Barron. Chief Justice Marshall reasoned that where the framers meant to limit the states, they said so: "No State shall." The amendments contain no such words. So Baltimore owed Barron nothing under the Fifth Amendment. For nearly a century, people wronged by state and local governments had no federal claim under the first ten amendments.

- Limits on states say "No State shall"

[check q4-barron]

## The long fuse

The Fourteenth Amendment, in 1868, uses those words: "No State shall." Through its due process clause, the Court absorbed rights one at a time. That is selective incorporation. Just compensation crossed in 1897, and free speech in 1925. As of mid-2026, two guarantees have still not crossed: the grand jury and the civil jury. That is why many states charge felonies by a prosecutor's information instead.

- 1868: Fourteenth Amendment
- 1897: just compensation
- 1925: free speech
- Not incorporated: grand jury, civil jury

> The text says what a right protects. The case law says against whom.

## Still open

Whether the last two clauses should cross, and how far the Ninth Amendment's unenumerated rights reach, remain contested.
