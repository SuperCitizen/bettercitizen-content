# How progressivism relates to other traditions

Progressivism's borders with neighboring traditions are blurry. Substantive overlaps with conservatism, libertarianism, socialism, and communitarianism are larger than partisan rhetoric suggests.

## What progressivism shares with conservatism

A surprising amount, especially with the civic-republican and traditionalist conservative streams:

- **Civic-republican overlap.** Sandel's communitarian progressivism, Reich's "common good" progressivism, and Yuval Levin's traditionalist conservatism share substantial substantive commitments — civic institutions matter, individualism is corrosive, public-spirited deliberation requires shared identity. The vocabularies differ; the substantive overlap is real.
- **Concern about concentrated private power.** Progressives worry about corporate concentration; national conservatives worry about Big Tech and financial concentration. The substantive concerns overlap heavily even when the political vehicles differ.
- **Working-class concern.** New Deal progressives and national conservatives both center the working class in their political projects. The Frank-Vance overlap on certain policy questions (industrial policy, family supports, skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy) reflects this.
- **Skepticism of meritocratic individualism.** Sandel's progressive critique and Hazony's national-conservative critique both target the post-1990s liberal-meritocratic synthesis.

What divides them: progressives emphasize change of inherited institutions toward more inclusive and equal forms; conservatives emphasize conservation of inherited institutions against destabilizing change. The substantive question is which institutions, and what counts as conservation vs. abandonment.

## What progressivism shares with libertarianism

Less than it shares with conservatism, but real:

- **Civil liberties.** Progressives and libertarians substantially agree on speech protection, criminal-procedure reforms, drug-policy liberalization, surveillance limitation, religious liberty (procedurally — they sometimes diverge on substance).
- **Drug policy reform, criminal justice reform.** Strong overlap.
- **Some immigration questions.** Many libertarians are pro-immigration; many progressives are similarly. The disagreement is more with restrictionist conservatives than between progressives and libertarians.

What divides them: economic policy. Libertarians treat market exchange as the legitimate ordering principle; progressives treat market outcomes as politically constructed and politically correctable. The two visions are deeply incompatible on fiscal, regulatory, and redistributive policy.

## What progressivism shares with socialism

Substantial but contested overlap. American progressivism and democratic socialism share commitments to:

- Universal healthcare, universal education, robust social insurance.
- Strong labor rights and collective bargaining.
- Critique of unaccountable corporate power.
- Wealth concentration as a political problem.

What divides them: progressives generally accept market capitalism with strong regulation and social insurance; democratic socialists generally seek to transcend market capitalism through worker ownership, public provision of basic goods, or social democracy that crosses the threshold from "regulated market" to "post-market." The Sanders/Warren wing of progressivism overlaps significantly with Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) social democrats; the New Deal liberal mainstream is closer to FDR's "save capitalism from itself" framing.

## What progressivism shares with communitarianism

Heavy overlap with the civic-republican stream, less with identity-justice. Sandel, Walzer, and Etzioni's communitarianism:

- Treats community membership as constitutive of identity.
- Supports civic institutions and shared deliberation.
- Worries about pure rights-based liberalism's atomizing effects.
- Supports public policy that strengthens family, neighborhood, religious community.

The civic-republican progressive stream is essentially a left-leaning version of communitarianism. Reich's *The Common Good* and Sandel's recent work fit comfortably in either category.

## "American" progressivism vs. European social democracy

European social-democratic parties (SPD in Germany, Labour in U.K., Socialists in France, etc.) differ from American progressives in several ways:

- **Welfare state.** European social democrats inherited and run substantial welfare states; American progressives have been building toward one against more institutional resistance.
- **Labor movement.** European labor parties are formally tied to trade unions; American progressivism's labor relationship has weakened with declining unionization.
- **Coalition.** European social-democratic parties traditionally drew on industrial labor + intellectual middle class. American progressivism's coalition includes professionals, urban voters, racial/ethnic minorities, and unions — a different composition.
- **Constitutional structure.** European parliamentary systems make social-democratic programs easier to enact than U.S. federalism + bicameralism + presidentialism + filibuster.

Bernie Sanders is sometimes described as a "democratic socialist" but his policy program looks much more like European social democracy (Medicare for All, free college, expanded Social Security) than like classic socialism (worker ownership, public production). The terminology has shifted in the U.S. context.

## Current points of cross-ideological convergence

Several areas where partisan alignment is misleading:

**Antitrust and Big Tech.** Bipartisan support for antitrust action against major platforms — Senator Klobuchar (D), Senator Hawley (R), and the Biden FTC under Lina Khan all converged on substantively similar positions, even when disagreeing about implementation details.

**Industrial policy.** CHIPS Act, IRA's manufacturing provisions, defense-industrial-base reform — bipartisan support across center-left progressives, center-right conservatives, and national conservatives. The free-trade orthodoxy of the 1990s no longer dominates either party.

**Universal child-tax-credit-style family policy.** Mitt Romney's Family Security Act, Senator Brown's CTC expansion, the ARPA temporary expansion — broad bipartisan substantive support, even when the political vehicles fail.

**Working-class economic policy.** The substantive policy positions of New Deal progressives and national conservatives have converged on labor rights, manufacturing investment, and skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy. Different framing, similar policies.

**Reform of "the administrative state."** Progressive concern about regulatory capture and conservative concern about administrative overreach overlap on issues like agency accountability, FDA reform, and environmental review streamlining (Klein-Thompson's *Abundance* makes the supply-side progressive version of this case).

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position about progressivism — whether you identify with the tradition or not — requires:

1. A view on **which stream of the tradition best fits current conditions** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **how the tradition relates to its critics** — where the strongest critiques bite and where they miss.
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

The Coach in this app will engage you on each of these. The Mirror will save your declared position; the steel-man drill will push you to construct the strongest version of the views you don't hold.

The discipline of having to construct the strongest version of a view you reject is where defended positions become refined ones.
