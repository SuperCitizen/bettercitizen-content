# Internal disagreements within the tradition

Progressivism's internal divisions are at least as sharp as conservatism's. Each stream has a coherent vision; they overlap on many policies but disagree on framing, priorities, and political coalition.

## 1. The New Deal liberal stream

Core commitment: economic security as the foundation of democratic citizenship. The state's primary obligation is to ensure that working people have decent wages, retirement security, healthcare, and protection from concentrated economic power. Race, gender, and other identity dimensions matter, but they are best addressed through a universal economic program that unites the working class across difference.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Bernie Sanders (politically), Elizabeth Warren (in part), Robert Reich, Joseph Stiglitz, Adolph Reed Jr. (more pointedly than the others), the *Jacobin* / *Dissent* circle, the Roosevelt Institute.

Key claims:
- The post-1980 economic restructuring (financialization, free-trade orthodoxy, declining unionization, tax cuts at the top) produced the rising inequality and political fracture we now see. Reversing those trends is the central progressive task.
- Universal programs are politically more durable than targeted programs. Social Security and Medicare retain massive support; targeted welfare programs are politically vulnerable. The progressive policy agenda should center on universal economic-security programs.
- Identity-based policy framing has costs — it fragments the political coalition that economic-justice progressivism requires, and it allows opponents to portray the movement as serving particular groups rather than the broader public.
- Universal economic programs (Medicare for All, expanded Social Security, free public college, robust labor rights) deliver disproportionate benefits to historically-excluded groups *because* those groups are over-represented in working-class positions. This is a way to combine universal framing with substantive equity.

Disagreements with identity-justice progressives: identity-justice framing prioritizes the wrong axis. Class is the more powerful organizing principle and the more politically tractable one. Identity politics, while addressing real injustices, has produced a coalition that loses elections it should win.

Disagreements with civic-republican progressives: civic-republicanism is too philosophical, too disconnected from the bread-and-butter issues that organize working-class politics. Important academically; less useful politically.

## 2. The identity-justice stream

Core commitment: the historical and continuing exclusion of Black, Indigenous, women, LGBTQ, and other marginalized groups is central to American political life and cannot be subsumed under economic-justice frames. Genuine equity requires direct address of these specific exclusions, not just general economic uplift that benefits these groups indirectly.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Ta-Nehisi Coates, Ibram X. Kendi, Kimberlé Crenshaw, the *Dissent* circle (with overlap with Reed), the Movement for Black Lives' policy platform, the Black Lives Matter movement's intellectuals.

Key claims:
- The New Deal coalition required compromises with Southern white Democrats that excluded Black Americans from much of its benefits — Social Security initially excluded agricultural and domestic workers (overwhelmingly Black), federal mortgage policy underwrote redlining, the GI Bill was administered with massive racial disparities. Treating these as accidents misreads what the New Deal actually did.
- Race-conscious policy is sometimes necessary to address race-specific harms. Reparations for slavery and Jim Crow, affirmative action in higher education, race-equity audits of housing and lending markets — these address specific injuries that universal economic policy doesn't.
- Coalition politics can't paper over the cultural questions that movements like Black Lives Matter, the LGBTQ rights movement, and feminist organizing have placed at the center of American politics. The post-2008 progressive surge was driven in significant part by these movements; pretending they're peripheral misreads the political coalition.
- Universal-economic framing's "downstream benefit to marginalized groups" claim has empirical limits. Some inequalities (residential segregation, criminal-justice disparities, mass incarceration) require direct policy attention that economic-citizenship programs alone don't address.

Disagreements with New Deal liberals: economic-justice framing alone is insufficient. The civil rights movement and the women's and LGBTQ movements are not subsidiary to economic justice — they are central to what progressivism is.

Disagreements with civic-republican progressives: civic-republicanism's emphasis on shared civic identity sometimes elides the specific exclusions that have shaped — and continue to shape — American civic life.

## 3. The civic-republican stream

Core commitment: democratic citizenship is a shared activity, not just a bundle of individual rights. Progressivism should be in service of public-spirited democratic deliberation, civic participation, and a shared sense of common purpose. Both pure individualism (libertarian) and pure identity-fragmentation (some forms of identity-justice) undermine the civic foundation that progressive policy depends on.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Michael Sandel, Robert Reich, Mark Lilla (in some moods, though he's also identity-critical), Danielle Allen, the *American Affairs* circle (which crosses left-right boundaries), the *Niskanen Center* (more centrist).

Key claims:
- Progressivism is at its strongest when it speaks in the language of shared citizenship, common-good, and democratic participation. The New Deal's success and the civil rights movement's moral authority both rested on appeals to American national identity and shared civic purpose.
- Modern progressivism has too often been framed as a coalition of grievances rather than a positive vision of shared life. This both alienates potential allies and undersells what progressivism actually offers.
- Meritocratic individualism — which has become the operating ideology of much elite-liberal politics — is itself a problem. Sandel's *The Tyranny of Merit* argues that the post-1990s "liberal" emphasis on credentials, mobility, and individual achievement disregards the dignity of work and the value of solidarity, and contributes to the polarization it then bemoans.
- Progressive policy should be designed to strengthen civic institutions — democratic participation, public spaces, public-service careers, civic education — not just to redistribute resources. Reich's *The Common Good* makes this explicit.

Disagreements with New Deal liberals: economic-justice programs without civic institutions atomize. We need both.

Disagreements with identity-justice progressives: the language of grievance, while pointing at real injustices, is poor at building the political coalitions and shared civic identity that progressive change actually requires.

## Where each stream lands on specific issues

| Issue | New Deal liberal | Identity-justice | Civic-republican |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal healthcare | Strong support; framed as economic security | Strong support; framed in part as racial equity | Strong support; framed as common-good |
| Reparations for slavery | Skeptical (universal vs. targeted) | Strong support | Mixed |
| Affirmative action | Mixed (class-based often preferred) | Strong support | Mixed |
| Wealth tax | Strong support | Generally support | Generally support |
| Carbon pricing | Generally support | Generally support but emphasize environmental justice | Generally support |
| Free public college | Strong support | Strong support | Generally support |
| Open vs. restricted immigration | Mixed (labor concerns) | Generally welcoming | Mixed (civic-integration concerns) |
| Cultural identity programs (DEI, etc.) | Skeptical | Strong support | Mixed |
| Universal child allowance | Strong support | Strong support | Strong support |
| Antitrust | Strong support | Generally support | Strong support |
| Trade with China | Mixed (labor concerns) | Mixed | Mixed (alliance and democracy concerns) |

The streams differ in framing more than in raw policy positions, but the framing matters for political coalition-building and for which trade-offs each is willing to accept.

## What this means for political coalitions

The post-2008 progressive movement has been a contested mixture of all three streams plus various smaller voices. The Sanders campaign drew heavily on the New Deal liberal stream; the post-Floyd reckoning drew heavily on the identity-justice stream; books like Sandel's and Reich's pull toward the civic-republican stream.

The current Democratic coalition includes all three. Their tensions are visible — debates over whether to call particular policies "racial equity" or "working-class," debates over how much to emphasize cultural questions vs. economic ones, debates over whether to lead with civic-republican framing or with movement-particularist framing.

A defensible position within the progressive tradition now requires:

1. A view on **which stream best fits current circumstances** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **what to do about specific issues** where the streams diverge (reparations, affirmative action, immigration, DEI policy).
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
