# How progressivism relates to other traditions

In 2024, Senator J.D. Vance — months away from becoming the Republican vice-presidential nominee — called Lina Khan, the Biden FTC chair whom progressive antitrust reformers had championed, one of the few people in that administration doing a pretty good job. A national-conservative Republican praising the left's favorite trustbuster is the kind of data point partisan maps cannot explain. Ideological maps can.

Progressivism's borders with neighboring traditions are blurry. Substantive overlaps with conservatism, libertarianism, socialism, and communitarianism are larger than partisan rhetoric suggests.

## What progressivism shares with conservatism

A surprising amount, especially with the civic-republican and traditionalist conservative streams:

- **Civic-republican overlap.** Sandel's communitarian progressivism, Reich's "common good" progressivism, and [[person:yuval-levin]]'s traditionalist conservatism share substantial substantive commitments — civic institutions matter, individualism is corrosive, public-spirited deliberation requires shared identity. The vocabularies differ; the substantive overlap is real.
- **Concern about concentrated private power.** Progressives worry about corporate concentration; national conservatives worry about Big Tech and financial concentration. The substantive concerns overlap heavily even when the political vehicles differ.
- **Working-class concern.** New Deal progressives and national conservatives both center the working class in their political projects. The overlap between labor-left writers like Thomas Frank and national conservatives like Vance on certain policy questions (industrial policy, family supports, skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy) reflects this.
- **Skepticism of meritocratic individualism.** Sandel's progressive critique and Yoram Hazony's national-conservative critique both target the post-1990s liberal-meritocratic synthesis.

What divides them: progressives emphasize change of inherited institutions toward more inclusive and equal forms; conservatives emphasize conservation of inherited institutions against destabilizing change. The substantive question is which institutions, and what counts as conservation vs. abandonment.

  The **New Deal liberal** stream: family supports, industrial policy, and skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy sit squarely in its working-class economic program. The predicted fracture is cultural, not economic — immigration restriction and a traditionalist cultural agenda are usually part of the national-conservative package, and that is where the economic overlap stops carrying the coalition.

## What progressivism shares with libertarianism

Less than it shares with conservatism, but real (see [[ideology:libertarianism]] for the full tradition):

- **Civil liberties.** Progressives and libertarians substantially agree on speech protection, criminal-procedure reforms, drug-policy liberalization, surveillance limitation, religious liberty (procedurally — they sometimes diverge on substance).
- **Drug policy reform, criminal justice reform.** Strong overlap.
- **Some immigration questions.** Many libertarians are pro-immigration; many progressives are similarly. The disagreement is more with restrictionist conservatives than between progressives and libertarians.

What divides them: economic policy. Libertarians treat market exchange as the legitimate ordering principle; progressives treat market outcomes as politically constructed and politically correctable. The two visions are deeply incompatible on fiscal, regulatory, and redistributive policy.

## What progressivism shares with socialism — and where European social democracy fits

Substantial but contested overlap. American progressivism and [[ideology:democratic-socialism]] share commitments to:

- Universal healthcare, universal education, robust social insurance.
- Strong labor rights and collective bargaining.
- Critique of unaccountable corporate power.
- Wealth concentration as a political problem.

What divides them: progressives generally accept market capitalism with strong regulation and social insurance; democratic socialists generally seek to transcend market capitalism through worker ownership, public provision of basic goods, or social democracy that crosses the threshold from "regulated market" to "post-market." The Sanders/Warren wing of progressivism overlaps significantly with Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) social democrats; the New Deal liberal mainstream is closer to FDR's "save capitalism from itself" framing.

A related boundary runs between American progressivism and [[ideology:social-democracy]] as practiced in Europe. The social-democratic parties there (the SPD in Germany, Labour in the U.K., the Socialists in France) differ from American progressives in several ways:

- **Welfare state.** European social democrats inherited and run substantial welfare states; American progressives have been building toward one against more institutional resistance.
- **Labor movement.** European labor parties are formally tied to trade unions; American progressivism's labor relationship has weakened with declining unionization.
- **Coalition.** European social-democratic parties traditionally drew on industrial labor + intellectual middle class. American progressivism's coalition includes professionals, urban voters, racial/ethnic minorities, and unions — a different composition.
- **Constitutional structure.** European parliamentary systems make social-democratic programs easier to enact than U.S. federalism + bicameralism + presidentialism + filibuster.

## What progressivism shares with communitarianism

Heavy overlap with the civic-republican stream, less with identity-justice. The [[ideology:communitarianism]] of Sandel, Michael Walzer, and Amitai Etzioni:

- Treats community membership as constitutive of identity.
- Supports civic institutions and shared deliberation.
- Worries about pure rights-based liberalism's atomizing effects.
- Supports public policy that strengthens family, neighborhood, religious community.

The civic-republican progressive stream is essentially a left-leaning version of communitarianism. Reich's *The Common Good* and Sandel's recent work fit comfortably in either category.

Communitarianism is the last of the four neighbors, so the borders can now be laid side by side:

| Neighbor | Strongest shared ground | The dividing line |
|---|---|---|
| Conservatism | Civic institutions, working-class economics, suspicion of concentrated private power and of meritocratic individualism | Change inherited institutions toward inclusion vs. conserve them against destabilizing change |
| Libertarianism | Civil liberties, criminal-justice and drug-policy reform, surveillance limits, (often) immigration | Whether market outcomes are a legitimate spontaneous order or political constructions to be corrected |
| Democratic socialism | Universal social insurance, labor rights, critique of corporate power | Regulate and insure within market capitalism vs. transcend it (worker ownership, public production) |
| Communitarianism | Community as constitutive of identity; civic institutions against atomization | Communitarianism is a method shared across left and right; progressives apply it to egalitarian ends |

Notice that the dividing lines are not interchangeable. With conservatism the argument is about *change*; with libertarianism, about *markets*; with socialism, about *how far correction goes*. Knowing which argument you are actually in is most of the work of arguing well across traditions.

## Current points of cross-ideological convergence

Several areas where partisan alignment is misleading:

**Antitrust and Big Tech.** Bipartisan support for [[issue:antitrust-enforcement]] against major platforms — Senator Klobuchar (D), Senator Hawley (R), and the Biden FTC under Lina Khan all converged on substantively similar positions, even when disagreeing about implementation details.

**Industrial policy.** CHIPS Act, IRA's manufacturing provisions, defense-industrial-base reform — bipartisan support for [[issue:industrial-policy]] across center-left progressives, center-right conservatives, and national conservatives. The free-trade orthodoxy of the 1990s no longer dominates either party.

**Universal child-tax-credit-style family policy.** Mitt Romney's Family Security Act, Sherrod Brown's CTC-expansion bills, the ARPA temporary expansion — broad bipartisan substantive support, even when the political vehicles fail.

**Working-class economic policy.** The substantive policy positions of New Deal progressives and national conservatives have converged on labor rights, manufacturing investment, and skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy. Different framing, similar policies.

**Reform of "the administrative state."** Progressive concern about regulatory capture and conservative concern about administrative overreach overlap on issues like agency accountability, FDA reform, and environmental review streamlining (Klein-Thompson's *Abundance* makes the supply-side progressive version of this case).

<Checkpoint question="Antitrust, industrial policy, and family policy all show cross-ideological convergence right now. Using this lesson's logic, name an issue area where convergence is least likely — and say why.">
  Pick an issue that sits directly *on* one of the dividing lines rather than beside it: broad redistribution (a wealth tax) lands on the libertarian market divide; immigration and cultural-identity policy land on the conservative change-vs-conservation divide. Convergence happens where traditions' *diagnoses* overlap (concentrated power, working-class decline) while their first principles are not at stake. Where the foundational disagreement is itself the question, coalition arithmetic stops working.
</Checkpoint>

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position about progressivism — whether you identify with the tradition or not — requires:

1. A view on **which stream of the tradition best fits current conditions** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **how the tradition relates to its critics** — where the strongest critiques bite and where they miss.
3. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

The Coach in this app will engage you on each of these. The Mirror will save your declared position; the steel-man drill will push you to construct the strongest version of the views you don't hold.

A useful next step: declare a position at `/me/positions/new` with a slug like `progressivism-stream` (which stream best fits current conditions?) or `progressivism-vs-x` (where does progressivism best engage with another tradition?). The discipline of having to construct the strongest version of a view you reject is where defended positions become refined ones.

What this lesson did *not* settle: whether the convergences above are durable realignments or marriages of convenience — and whether progressivism can absorb the strongest critiques from its neighbors (Burkean caution, libertarian price signals, socialist ownership questions, communitarian belonging) without dissolving into them. That is the live argument, and it is being conducted in public, in real time.
