# What American progressivism actually believes

Like conservatism, progressivism is a family of positions, not a single creed. This lesson presents the core commitments most progressives across the family share, in language they would themselves recognize.

## A core that holds across the family

Most American progressives, regardless of more specific orientation, share commitments roughly as follows:

**Active government as a tool of public purpose.** Progressives generally accept (and welcome) a substantial federal role in addressing collective problems — economic inequality, public health, education, environmental protection, civil rights enforcement. The state is not primarily a threat to liberty; it is one of the instruments through which a democratic public pursues its values. Pure laissez-faire produces concentrations of private power that are themselves threats to liberty.

**Equality as a substantive, not just procedural, commitment.** Procedural equality (equal treatment under law) is necessary but not sufficient. Substantive equality — equal opportunity, equal political voice, equal capacity to participate in democratic life — requires positive action. Programs that redistribute, that regulate concentrated power, and that ensure access to basic goods (education, healthcare, housing) are part of a democratic society's obligations to its members.

**Belief in expert judgment and evidence-based policy.** The Progressive Era emerged in part from the rationalization of governance — civil service reform, regulatory agencies, the social-science disciplines as policy resources. Modern progressivism continues to treat expertise (medical, scientific, economic, social-scientific) as central to good governance. This sits in tension with populist suspicion of expert authority but is a defining commitment.

**Inclusion as a central political value.** The expansion of political and civil rights — to women, to non-white Americans, to LGBTQ individuals, to immigrants and naturalized citizens — is central to the progressive project. The trajectory of American history, on this view, is the progressive widening of the circle of "we, the people."

**Climate, public health, and structural injustice as urgent.** Progressives generally treat climate change, public-health threats, and structural inequalities (racial, economic, gender-based) as urgent collective challenges that require active government response — and as moral, not just political, questions.

**Skepticism of inherited hierarchies.** Inherited authority — class, race, gender, religious — is presumptively suspect. Existing arrangements may be defensible on their merits, but they don't get the benefit of the doubt that comes from longevity. This is a temperamental disagreement with conservatives' Burkean presumption-of-continuity.

## The foundational thinkers

Herbert Croly (1869-1930) — *The Promise of American Life* (1909). Argued that the Hamiltonian tradition of strong federal government should be put in service of Jeffersonian-democratic ends — i.e., that the federal government should be active in pursuing equality and shared prosperity. Founder of *The New Republic* magazine. Foundational for the early-1900s progressive movement.

John Dewey (1859-1952) — pragmatist philosopher whose *The Public and Its Problems* (1927) argued that democratic governance requires an informed public capable of deliberation, and that public-spirited intelligence is the central work of democracy. Influential on educational reform and on the institutional design of the New Deal era.

Franklin Roosevelt (1882-1945) — not a theorist but the architect of the New Deal coalition. Roosevelt's framing in the 1932 Commonwealth Club Address treated economic security as a precondition for democratic citizenship. The New Deal — Social Security, NLRB, FDIC, securities regulation, public works — was both a response to crisis and a positive vision of economic citizenship.

John Rawls (1921-2002) — *A Theory of Justice* (1971). Provided the canonical philosophical framework for modern liberal political thought. The "veil of ignorance" thought experiment grounded a vision of justice as fairness, where principles are chosen by hypothetical contractors who don't know their own social position. Foundational for late-20th-century progressive political philosophy.

Cornel West, Bell Hooks, Toni Morrison, and others — voices that emerged from the civil rights and women's movements and that have shaped the identity-justice stream of contemporary progressivism. Their contribution: insisting that any account of American history and political life must grapple substantively with race, gender, sexuality, and class — and that abstract universalism that ignores these dimensions misses what's actually at stake.

## The historical phases

**Progressive Era (~1890-1920).** Civil service reform, anti-trust legislation (Sherman, Clayton), Federal Reserve, women's suffrage (19th Amendment), direct election of senators (17th Amendment), income tax (16th Amendment), Pure Food and Drug Act, Federal Trade Commission. Both Republican (Theodore Roosevelt, Hiram Johnson) and Democratic (Woodrow Wilson, William Jennings Bryan) progressives drove these reforms.

**New Deal (1933-1945).** Social Security, Securities Acts, Wagner Act / National Labor Relations Board, FDIC, public works programs, agricultural reform, the Tennessee Valley Authority. Built the modern American regulatory state and the political coalition (industrial labor, urban ethnics, African Americans, intellectual elites, Southern Democrats — with the latter exiting later) that dominated U.S. politics for ~40 years.

**Postwar liberalism (1945-1968).** Maintained and extended New Deal commitments. GI Bill, expansion of higher education, Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting Rights Act (1965), Medicare and Medicaid (1965), Immigration and Nationality Act (1965). The Great Society explicitly extended the New Deal's economic-citizenship framework to civil rights and to elderly and low-income Americans.

**Post-1968 fragmentation.** The civil rights movement's success, the Vietnam War's political destruction of the Democratic foreign-policy consensus, and the rise of the Reagan coalition broke the New Deal majority. Progressivism since has lacked a single dominant political vehicle and has fractured into multiple streams (next lesson examines this).

**Post-2008 surge.** The financial crisis, the Sanders/Warren economic-justice surge, the Black Lives Matter movement, and the climate-policy reorientation produced a more energetic progressive movement than had existed in decades. The IRA of 2022, the CHIPS Act, the American Rescue Plan, and expansive child-tax-credit proposals all reflect this energy.

## What progressives think the conservative critique gets wrong

A defining feature of the tradition is the belief that the conservative case for inherited institutions is too quick. The argument:

- Many "inherited" institutions were not the product of accumulated wisdom but of accumulated power — slavery, gender exclusion, racial caste, employer dominance over labor. Defending them as "tradition" means defending arrangements that benefited some at the expense of others.
- The Burkean presumption against root-and-branch change, when applied to such institutions, reads as a defense of injustice. Progressivism's willingness to challenge inherited arrangements is not naïve revolutionarism but moral seriousness.
- Conservative concerns about the state often translate into defenses of private power that is itself coercive. A worker without a union, an unchecked employer, a tenant without legal protection, a consumer facing concentrated market power — these are not free in any meaningful sense. Progressive use of state power is, on this view, a tool to balance private-sector power, not to displace freedom.

Conservatives, of course, contest each of these claims. The disagreement is philosophical (about what counts as freedom, what counts as legitimate authority) and empirical (about how specific institutions actually operate).

## Why this lesson is contested

This is an ideology pack, not an issue pack. The contestation is internal — within the tradition, how should the inheritance be understood, and how should it engage current questions?

Three perspectives the next lesson sharpens:

- **New Deal liberals** emphasize economic security, labor, and the New Deal regulatory state as central. They see the post-2008 progressive surge as continuous with the FDR tradition.
- **Identity-justice progressives** emphasize that the New Deal coalition required Southern white compromises that excluded Black Americans, women, and LGBTQ individuals from much of its benefits. The progressive task is to extend the New Deal's universalism without those exclusions.
- **Civic-republican progressives** emphasize the public-spirited dimension of the tradition — citizenship, civic participation, deliberation, public-spiritedness. Sandel's recent work and Reich's *The Common Good* are in this register.

The next lesson examines these in detail.
