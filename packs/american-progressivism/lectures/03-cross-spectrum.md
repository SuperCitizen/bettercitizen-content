It is 2024. J.D. Vance is a senator, a Republican, and a national conservative. He is months away from becoming his party's nominee for vice president.

And he has praise for someone in the Biden administration. [beat] Her name is Lina Khan. She chairs the FTC, and progressive antitrust reformers championed her. Vance calls her one of the few people in that administration doing a pretty good job.

[pause]

A national-conservative Republican, praising the left's favorite trustbuster. A partisan map cannot explain that. [beat] An ideological map can.

Last time we stayed inside the progressive family. This time we walk its borders.

Those borders are blurry. Progressivism overlaps with conservatism, with libertarianism, with socialism, and with communitarianism. And the overlaps are substantive.

> The overlaps between traditions are larger than partisan rhetoric suggests.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to say what progressivism shares with each neighbor, and where the dividing line falls. And you should be able to predict which alliances across those lines will hold, and which will fracture.

We will take it in three steps.

First, the four neighbors, one at a time. For each, two questions: what is shared, and what divides.

Second, the borders laid side by side.

And last, the places where the traditions are converging right now.

## Conservatism

Start with the neighbor you might expect to share the least. In fact, progressivism shares a surprising amount with conservatism, especially with the civic-republican and traditionalist conservative streams. Four overlaps.

First, civic institutions. Take Sandel's communitarian progressivism, and Reich's progressivism of the common good. Set beside them the traditionalist conservatism of Yuval Levin. All three hold that civic institutions matter, that individualism is corrosive, and that public-spirited deliberation requires a shared identity. The vocabularies differ. The substantive overlap is real.

[beat]

Second, concentrated private power. Progressives worry about corporate concentration. National conservatives worry about Big Tech, and about financial concentration. Those concerns overlap heavily, even when the political vehicles differ.

[beat]

Third, the working class. New Deal progressives and national conservatives both center the working class in their political projects. That is why a labor-left writer like Thomas Frank and a national conservative like Vance overlap on certain questions: industrial policy, family supports, skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy.

[beat]

Fourth, skepticism of meritocratic individualism. Sandel's progressive critique and Yoram Hazony's national-conservative critique share a target: the post-1990s liberal-meritocratic synthesis.

[pause]

So what divides them? Listen for the verb, because both sides get the same one. Progressives emphasize changing inherited institutions, toward more inclusive and more equal forms. Conservatives emphasize conserving inherited institutions, against destabilizing change.

> Progressives emphasize changing inherited institutions. Conservatives emphasize conserving them.

Underneath sits the substantive question. Which institutions? And what counts as conserving one, as opposed to abandoning it?

Let's test that border.

A national-conservative senator introduces a bill. It expands the child tax credit. And it penalizes companies that offshore manufacturing.

Last time we met three progressive streams: the New Deal liberal, the identity-justice, and the civic-republican. Which stream finds this bill easiest to support? And where will the alliance fracture? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The New Deal liberal stream. Family supports, industrial policy, and skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy sit squarely inside its working-class economic program.

And the fracture is cultural, not economic. Immigration restriction and a traditionalist cultural agenda are usually part of the national-conservative package. That is where the economic overlap stops carrying the coalition.

> New Deal liberals and national conservatives converge on economics. The predicted fracture is cultural.

## Libertarianism

The second neighbor is libertarianism. Progressivism shares less with it than with conservatism. But what it shares is real.

The shared ground is civil liberties. Progressives and libertarians substantially agree on protecting speech, on reforming criminal procedure, on liberalizing drug policy, and on limiting surveillance. They agree on religious liberty as a matter of procedure, though they sometimes diverge on substance.

There is overlap on some immigration questions too. Many libertarians are pro-immigration, and so are many progressives. On that issue, the disagreement is more with restrictionist conservatives than with each other.

What divides them is economic policy. And again, both sides get the same verb.

Libertarians treat market exchange as the legitimate ordering principle. Progressives treat market outcomes as politically constructed, and politically correctable.

> Libertarians treat markets as the legitimate order. Progressives treat market outcomes as correctable.

In plainer words: for the libertarian, what the market produces is a legitimate, spontaneous order. For the progressive, a market outcome is something politics constructed, and something politics can correct.

On fiscal policy, on regulation, and on redistribution, those two visions are deeply incompatible.

## Socialism, and social democracy

The third neighbor is socialism. Here the overlap is substantial, and it is contested.

American progressivism and democratic socialism share four commitments. Universal healthcare, universal education, and robust social insurance. Strong labor rights, and collective bargaining. A critique of unaccountable corporate power. And the view that wealth concentration is a political problem.

So where is the line? Progressives generally accept market capitalism, with strong regulation and social insurance. Democratic socialists generally seek to transcend market capitalism: through worker ownership, through public provision of basic goods, or through a social democracy that crosses the threshold from a regulated market to something beyond the market.

> Progressives generally accept market capitalism. Democratic socialists generally seek to transcend it.

And the streams inside progressivism stand at different distances from that line. The Sanders and Warren wing overlaps significantly with the social democrats in the Democratic Socialists of America. The New Deal liberal mainstream is closer to FDR's framing: "save capitalism from itself."

Let's test that line on a well-known case.

Bernie Sanders calls himself a democratic socialist. Now judge by his actual policy program: Medicare for All, free public college, expanded Social Security. Which tradition does that program most resemble? Classic socialism, with worker ownership and public production? Or something else? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It most resembles European social democracy: universal social insurance, atop a market economy. The program expands social insurance and public provision within a market economy. It does not transfer ownership of production to workers, or to the state.

So in the American context, the label and the program have drifted apart. The dividing line that matters is the one you just heard: does a program regulate and insure market capitalism, or does it seek to transcend it?

[pause]

That answer raises one more border, between American progressivism and social democracy as it is practiced in Europe. Think of the SPD in Germany, Labour in the U.K., the Socialists in France. They differ from American progressives in four ways.

The welfare state. European social democrats inherited substantial welfare states, and they run them. American progressives have been building toward one, against more institutional resistance.

[beat]

The labor movement. European labor parties are formally tied to trade unions. American progressivism's relationship with labor has weakened, as unionization has declined.

[beat]

The coalition. European social-democratic parties traditionally drew on industrial labor and the intellectual middle class. The American progressive coalition has a different composition: professionals, urban voters, racial and ethnic minorities, and unions.

[beat]

And the constitutional structure. European parliamentary systems make social-democratic programs easier to enact. The American system sets four things in the way: federalism, bicameralism, presidentialism, and the filibuster.

## Communitarianism, and the borders side by side

The fourth neighbor is communitarianism: the thought of Sandel, Michael Walzer, and Amitai Etzioni.

Communitarians treat membership in a community as constitutive of identity. They support civic institutions and shared deliberation. They worry about the atomizing effects of a liberalism built purely on rights. And they support public policy that strengthens family, neighborhood, and religious community.

How much of that does progressivism share? It depends on the stream.

> Communitarianism overlaps heavily with the civic-republican stream, less with identity-justice.

In fact, the civic-republican stream is in substance a left-leaning version of communitarianism. Reich's book The Common Good, and Sandel's recent work, fit comfortably in either category.

So here the dividing line is of a different kind. Communitarianism is a method, shared across left and right. Progressives apply it to egalitarian ends.

[pause]

That is all four neighbors. Now lay the borders side by side, and notice something. The dividing lines are not interchangeable.

> With conservatism, change. With libertarianism, markets. With socialism, how far correction goes.

Knowing which argument you are actually in is most of the work of arguing well across traditions.

So here is an exercise in exactly that. Choose one of those three lines. Take the side of it that you find less convincing. And state the best case for that side, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Where the traditions converge

Now the present. There are several areas today where partisan alignment is misleading. Here are five, quickly.

One: antitrust and Big Tech. There is bipartisan support for antitrust enforcement against the major platforms. Senator Klobuchar, a Democrat, Senator Hawley, a Republican, and the Biden FTC under Lina Khan all converged on substantively similar positions, even while disagreeing about the details of implementation.

[beat]

Two: industrial policy. Think of the CHIPS Act. Support runs across center-left progressives, center-right conservatives, and national conservatives. The free-trade orthodoxy of the 1990s no longer dominates either party.

[beat]

Three: family policy, in the style of a universal child tax credit. Think of Mitt Romney's Family Security Act, and Sherrod Brown's bills to expand the credit. There is broad bipartisan support on substance, even when the political vehicles fail.

[beat]

Four: working-class economic policy. The positions of New Deal progressives and national conservatives have converged on labor rights, on manufacturing investment, and on skepticism of free-trade orthodoxy. Different framing, similar policies.

[beat]

Five: reform of the administrative state. Progressives are concerned about regulatory capture. Conservatives are concerned about administrative overreach. Those concerns overlap on agency accountability, on FDA reform, and on streamlining environmental review.

[pause]

Now put the whole lecture to work. Antitrust, industrial policy and family policy all show convergence right now. Name an issue area where convergence is least likely. And say why.

To answer, you will need the three dividing lines. Bring them back. Take a moment.

[long pause]

The lines were change, markets, and how far correction goes. So pick an issue that sits directly on one of those lines, rather than beside it.

Broad redistribution, such as a wealth tax, lands on the libertarian divide over markets. Immigration and cultural-identity policy land on the conservative divide, between change and conservation.

And that gives us the rule.

> Convergence happens where diagnoses overlap, and first principles are not at stake.

The diagnoses that overlap are concentrated power, and working-class decline. But where the foundational disagreement is itself the question, coalition arithmetic stops working.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The overlaps between traditions are larger than partisan rhetoric suggests. Progressivism's borders are blurry.

[beat]

Two. Each border has its own dividing line. With conservatism, change. With libertarianism, markets. With socialism, how far correction goes. And communitarianism is a method shared across left and right, which progressives apply to egalitarian ends.

[beat]

Three. The streams sit differently along those borders. New Deal liberals and national conservatives converge on economics, and the predicted fracture is cultural. Communitarianism overlaps heavily with the civic-republican stream, less with identity-justice.

[beat]

Four. The Sanders program most resembles European social democracy, not classic socialism. And American progressives differ from Europe's social democrats in four ways: the welfare state, the labor movement, the coalition, and the constitutional structure.

[beat]

And five. Convergence happens where diagnoses overlap, and first principles are not at stake. Where an issue sits on a dividing line, coalition arithmetic stops working.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Are those convergences durable realignments, or marriages of convenience? And can progressivism absorb the strongest critiques from its neighbors without dissolving into them? From conservatism, Burkean caution. From libertarianism, price signals. From socialism, questions about ownership. From communitarianism, belonging.

That argument is open. It is being conducted in public, in real time.

A defensible position about progressivism, whether you identify with the tradition or not, requires three things. A view on which stream best fits current conditions, or what synthesis is achievable. A view on how the tradition relates to its critics: where the strongest critiques bite, and where they miss. And an honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position of your own, and to construct the strongest version of a view you reject.

This is the last lecture in this series. From here, the argument continues in the series on socialism, which examines the line between progressive and socialist in more detail.

For now, go back to where we started. A national-conservative senator, praising the left's favorite trustbuster. [beat] On a partisan map, that was a puzzle. On the map you now carry, it is a shared diagnosis, with a dividing line running beside it. You know what the two of them share. [beat] And you know where it stops.
