The year is 1912. A former president is running again. He served as a Republican. And the man he is running against is his own party's sitting president.

So he runs on the ticket of a brand-new party, the Progressive Party. [beat] And he takes 27 percent of the popular vote. That is still the strongest third-party showing in American presidential history.

His name was Theodore Roosevelt, and he lost. The winner was Woodrow Wilson, a Democrat, who claimed the progressive mantle just as insistently as Roosevelt did.

[pause]

A Republican ex-president and a Democratic former professor spent an entire campaign fighting over the same word. [beat] When that happens, the word is plainly bigger than any party.

The word names a family of positions. It is not a single creed, and it is not a party platform.

> Progressivism is a set of convictions. Parties are its temporary vehicles.

So set one mistake aside right away. Progressivism is not another name for the Democratic Party.

The reforms of the Progressive Era were driven by Republicans as much as by Democrats. Today's Democratic coalition contains moderates, and progressives regularly run primary challengers against them. And the commitments outlast any one policy program.

What are those commitments? By the end of this lecture, you should be able to state them the way progressives state them, in language they would themselves recognize. That is the standard all the way through: the tradition in its own terms.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the core: six commitments that most progressives share.

Second, the thinkers who gave those commitments their shape.

Third, the history: five waves.

And last, two arguments. The one progressives have with conservatives, and the one they have among themselves.

## Six commitments

Six is a lot to carry by ear, so take them in three pairs. Two about government. Two about equality. Two about change.

[beat]

Start with government.

The first commitment is active government, as a tool of public purpose. Progressives generally accept, and embrace, a substantial federal role in addressing collective problems: economic inequality, public health, education, environmental protection, the enforcement of civil rights.

Why embrace it? Because on this view, the state is not primarily a threat to liberty. It is one of the instruments through which a democratic public pursues its values.

And the thought has a second half. Pure laissez-faire, progressives hold, produces concentrations of private power, and those concentrations are themselves threats to liberty. [beat] Take the state away, on this view, and power does not disappear. It gathers in private hands.

The second commitment is a belief in expert judgment, and in policy built on evidence.

That belief goes back to the beginning. The Progressive Era emerged, in part, from an effort to make governing more rational, with the social sciences as a resource for policy. And modern progressivism still treats expertise as central to good governance.

One caution, and hold on to it. That trust sits in tension with the populist suspicion of expert authority. It is a defining commitment all the same. [beat] We will come back to the tension.

[pause]

Now the second pair: equality.

The third commitment is that equality must be substantive, not just procedural. Two terms of art, so let's take them slowly.

Procedural equality means equal treatment under the law: the same rules, applied to everyone. Progressives hold that this is necessary. [beat] They also hold that it is not sufficient.

Substantive equality asks for more: equal opportunity, an equal political voice, an equal capacity to take part in democratic life. And that, on this view, requires positive action. It requires programs that redistribute, rules that regulate concentrated power, and access to basic goods like education, healthcare and housing. Progressives count all of that among a democratic society's obligations to its members.

> For progressives, equal treatment under law is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Let's test that with a case.

Two states both guarantee every citizen equal treatment under law. In one of them, school funding follows local property wealth. So poor districts spend half as much per pupil as rich ones.

Which progressive commitment says that something is still wrong there? And what does it demand? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The commitment to substantive equality. The law in that state treats every citizen alike, and progressives call that necessary. But a child's actual capacity to take part in democratic life now depends on an accident: the tax base of a district. So on the progressive view, equal opportunity requires positive action. Funding reform. Redistribution. [beat] Not merely neutral rules.

The fourth commitment is inclusion. The expansion of political and civil rights is central to the progressive project: to women, to non-white Americans, to LGBTQ individuals, to immigrants and naturalized citizens.

Progressives have an image for it. The trajectory of American history, on this view, is the widening of a circle. [beat] The circle of "we, the people."

[pause]

And the third pair: change.

The fifth commitment is urgency. Progressives generally treat climate change, public-health threats, and structural inequalities as urgent collective challenges, which require an active response from government. And progressives treat these as moral questions, not just political ones.

The sixth commitment is skepticism of inherited hierarchies. Authority that is inherited, whether through class, race, gender or religion, is presumptively suspect. An existing arrangement may well be defensible on its merits. But it gets no benefit of the doubt for having lasted.

That marks a disagreement of temperament with conservatives, who begin from the opposite presumption: that long-standing arrangements should continue. It is called the Burkean presumption of continuity. Keep it in mind, because the last part of this lecture turns on it.

[pause]

So. Six commitments, in three pairs.

An active state, guided by expert judgment. [beat] Equality that is substantive, inside a circle that widens. [beat] Urgent problems, and no benefit of the doubt for inherited hierarchy.

## The thinkers

Those are the commitments. Now, who gave them their shape? Four names, and then a group.

[beat]

First, Herbert Croly. In 1909 he published The Promise of American Life, a founding text of the early Progressive Era.

Croly took the Hamiltonian tradition, the tradition of strong federal government, and argued that it should be put in the service of Jeffersonian, democratic ends. In plainer words: the federal government should be active in pursuing equality and shared prosperity.

> Croly's formula: Hamiltonian means, Jeffersonian ends.

Second, John Dewey, the pragmatist philosopher. In 1927, in a book called The Public and Its Problems, Dewey argued that democratic governance requires an informed public, one capable of deliberation.

[beat]

Third, Franklin Roosevelt: not a theorist, but the architect of the New Deal coalition. In 1932, in his Commonwealth Club Address, Roosevelt treated economic security as a precondition for democratic citizenship.

Hold on to that phrase. [beat] On Roosevelt's framing, economic security has to be in place before democratic citizenship can be. So the New Deal was a response to a crisis, and it was also a positive vision: economic citizenship.

[beat]

Fourth, John Rawls. In 1971 he published A Theory of Justice, which gave modern liberal political thought its canonical philosophical framework.

At its center is a thought experiment, called the veil of ignorance. Imagine people choosing the principles of justice for a society. [beat] But they choose from behind a veil. None of them knows their own social position.

Principles chosen that way are what Rawls meant by justice as fairness.

[pause]

And then the group: Cornel West, bell hooks, Toni Morrison, and others. These voices emerged from the civil rights movement and the women's movement, and they have shaped the identity-justice stream of today's progressivism.

Their contribution is an insistence. Any account of American history and political life must grapple, substantively, with race, gender, sexuality and class. And an abstract universalism that ignores those dimensions, they argue, misses what is actually at stake.

## Five waves

So much for ideas. Now institutions. The family's commitments were forged in distinct waves, five of them. Let's walk them in order.

The first wave is the Progressive Era, from 1890 to 1920. It brought civil service reform, the antitrust acts, the Federal Reserve and the Federal Trade Commission. It also brought three constitutional amendments: the income tax, the direct election of senators, and women's suffrage. Republican progressives and Democratic progressives drove those reforms together.

Before the second wave, a question that reaches back. A progressive and a populist both want to break up concentrated corporate power.

Where are the two most likely to part ways? Think back to the six commitments, and to a tension I asked you to hold on to.

[long pause]

They part over expertise. Both traditions attack concentrated private power, both accept federal action, and both treat inequality as a political question. The fault line is who should do the enforcing. A progressive would entrust it to regulatory agencies, staffed by credentialed experts. A populist is suspicious of expert authority.

[beat]

The second wave is the New Deal, from 1933 to 1945. Social Security. The National Labor Relations Board. The FDIC. It built the modern American regulatory state.

And it built a coalition: industrial labor, urban ethnics, African Americans, intellectuals, and Southern Democrats. That coalition dominated American politics for roughly forty years. Notice the Southern Democrats on that list. [beat] They would later exit.

The third wave runs from 1945 to 1968: postwar liberalism, and the Great Society. First the Civil Rights Act, in 1964. Then, in 1965, the Voting Rights Act, and Medicare and Medicaid.

Here Roosevelt's phrase comes back. The Great Society explicitly extended the New Deal's framework of economic citizenship, to civil rights, and to elderly and low-income Americans.

[beat]

The fourth wave is a break. It runs from 1968 to 2008: fragmentation, and the Third Way. Three things broke the New Deal majority. The success of the civil rights movement. Vietnam, which destroyed the Democratic consensus on foreign policy. And the rise of the Reagan coalition.

Then came the New Democrats of the Clinton era, who pulled the party toward markets, trade, and deficit discipline.

[beat]

The fifth wave began in 2008, and it is still under way. The financial crisis. The economic-justice campaigns of Sanders and Warren. Black Lives Matter. A reorientation of climate policy. Together they produced the most energetic progressive movement in decades.

[pause]

Two things to notice in that sequence.

First, each wave institutionalizes. The first left constitutional amendments and regulatory agencies. The second left social insurance. The third left the rights and healthcare statutes of the Great Society.

> Each wave left institutions that the next wave built on, and fought over.

Second, the breaks matter as much as the building. The very success of the civil rights movement helped fracture the coalition that produced it. And progressives still argue over the Third Way turn of the 1990s: whether that decade was a betrayal of the tradition, or an adaptation of it. The next lecture takes that argument up.

## The argument with conservatism

That is the tradition's past. Now its arguments, beginning with the one it has with conservatism.

A defining feature of the tradition is a belief that the conservative case for inherited institutions is too quick. Here is the progressive argument, in three steps.

Step one. Many inherited institutions, progressives argue, were not the product of accumulated wisdom. They were the product of accumulated power. Slavery. Gender exclusion. Racial caste. The dominance of employers over labor. To defend those as tradition is to defend arrangements that benefited some at the expense of others.

[beat]

Step two. Conservatism carries a Burkean presumption against root-and-branch change. Applied to institutions like those, progressives argue, that presumption reads as a defense of injustice. So the willingness to challenge inherited arrangements is not naive. On this view, it is moral seriousness.

[beat]

Step three. Conservative concerns about the state, progressives argue, often translate into defenses of private power, and private power can itself be coercive. A worker without a union. A tenant without legal protection. A consumer facing concentrated market power. On this view, none of them is free in any meaningful sense. So the progressive use of state power is a tool to balance private power, not to displace freedom.

[pause]

Let's put that argument to work.

A senator defends the filibuster on Burkean grounds. It has survived for two centuries, the senator says, so it must embody accumulated wisdom. Reconstruct the progressive reply. And there is one rule: you may not use the word "unfair." Take a moment.

[long pause]

The reply starts from a distinction. Longevity is evidence of survival. It is not evidence of wisdom. An institution can persist because it serves accumulated power.

Then progressives point to the record. The filibuster's most consequential use in the twentieth century, they point out, was blocking anti-lynching and civil-rights legislation.

So the progressive move is to ask two questions of any arrangement. Whom has it actually benefited? And would it be chosen on its merits today? Inheritance earns scrutiny, not the benefit of the doubt.

> The progressive reply: longevity is evidence of survival, not of wisdom.

Now the other side of the table. Conservatives contest each of those three claims. And the disagreement is of two kinds. Part of it is philosophical: what counts as freedom, and what counts as legitimate authority. Part of it is empirical: how specific institutions actually operate.

So here is a harder exercise. Two presumptions are on the table. One holds that an arrangement which has lasted deserves the benefit of the doubt. The other holds that inheritance earns scrutiny instead. Choose the one you find less convincing. Then state the best case for it, the way its smartest defender would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The argument inside the family

One argument remains, and it is internal. Progressivism is an ideology, not a single issue. So the contest is over how the inheritance should be understood, and how it should engage the questions of the present.

Three perspectives answer differently. Here they are in brief, each in its own terms.

New Deal liberals emphasize economic security, labor, and the regulatory state that the New Deal built. They see the progressive surge since 2008 as continuous with the tradition of Franklin Roosevelt.

> New Deal liberals emphasize economic security, labor, and the regulatory state.

Identity-justice progressives emphasize what the New Deal coalition required. It required compromises with Southern whites, and those compromises excluded Black Americans, women, and LGBTQ individuals from much of its benefits. So the progressive task, on this view, is to extend the New Deal's universalism without those exclusions.

> Identity-justice progressives emphasize the New Deal's exclusions, and a universalism without them.

Civic-republican progressives emphasize the public-spirited dimension of the tradition: citizenship, civic participation, deliberation. The recent work of Sandel is in this register, and so is Reich's book, The Common Good.

> Civic-republican progressives emphasize citizenship, participation, deliberation and public spirit.

Three emphases, and one inheritance.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Progressivism is a set of convictions, and parties are its temporary vehicles. In 1912, a Republican and a Democrat fought over the word.

[beat]

Two. Six commitments, in three pairs. Government: an active state, and trust in expert judgment. Equality: substantive, not just procedural, and inclusion. Change: urgency, and skepticism of inherited hierarchies.

[beat]

Three. Four thinkers, and a group. Croly: Hamiltonian means, Jeffersonian ends. Dewey: an informed public, capable of deliberation. Roosevelt: economic security as a precondition for democratic citizenship. Rawls: justice as fairness, chosen behind a veil of ignorance. And the voices of the identity-justice stream, who insist on race, gender, sexuality and class.

[beat]

Four. Five waves: the Progressive Era, the New Deal, the Great Society, the Third Way break, and the surge since 2008. Each wave left institutions that the next wave built on, and fought over.

[beat]

And five. The progressive reply to conservatism: longevity is evidence of survival, not of wisdom. Conservatives contest it. And inside the family, three emphases: economic security, a universalism without exclusions, and shared citizenship.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. The argument with conservatism is partly philosophical and partly empirical, and neither part is closed. And within the tradition, the inheritance itself is in dispute: how to understand it, and how it should engage the present.

The next lecture goes inside that dispute. Three streams, each stated the way its smartest advocate would state it. And each with a case against the other two.

For now, go back to 1912. Two candidates, two parties, one word. [beat] More than a century on, that word still belongs to no party. And the people who claim it are still arguing over what it demands.
