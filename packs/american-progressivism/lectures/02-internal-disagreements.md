A rally in Nevada, in February 2016. Hillary Clinton is speaking, and she puts a pointed question to her supporters.

If we broke up the big banks tomorrow, would that end racism? [beat] Would it end sexism?

And the crowd shouts back: no.

[pause]

The jab was aimed at Bernie Sanders, and at a campaign centered on the banks. But the argument it crystallized is far older than either candidate. It is the internal argument of American progressivism, and it has two halves. Which axis of injustice organizes the others? And which programs actually deliver?

> The family's argument: which injustice organizes the others, and which programs deliver?

Last time, what the family shares. This time, where it divides.

And it divides sharply. The divisions inside progressivism are at least as sharp as the divisions inside conservatism. Three streams run through the tradition. Each has a coherent vision. They overlap on many policies, and they disagree on framing, on priorities, and on political coalition.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to state each stream's case the way its smartest advocate would. And you should be able to say where the three truly part company.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the three streams, one at a time. Each gets the same treatment: what it is protecting, who speaks for it, what it argues, and what it points to.

Second, each stream's case against the other two.

Third, where the three land on actual policy.

And last, a newer critique, aimed at all three at once.

## The New Deal liberal stream

Begin with the New Deal liberal stream, and with what it is protecting: economic security, as the foundation of democratic citizenship.

On this view, the state's primary obligation is to working people. It should ensure decent wages, retirement security, healthcare, and protection from concentrated economic power.

What about race, and gender, and the other dimensions of identity? They matter, this stream holds. But they are best addressed through a universal economic program, one that unites the working class across difference.

Who speaks for it? In politics, Bernie Sanders, and in part Elizabeth Warren. Robert Reich and Joseph Stiglitz. Adolph Reed Jr., more pointedly than the others. And the circle around Jacobin and Dissent.

Now the argument, in the stream's own terms. It has three parts.

One: a diagnosis. Since 1980, this stream argues, the economy has been restructured: financialization, free-trade orthodoxy, declining unionization, tax cuts at the top. That restructuring produced the rising inequality and the political fracture we now see. Reversing those trends is the central progressive task.

[beat]

Two: universal programs are politically more durable than targeted ones. Here the stream points to the record. Social Security and Medicare retain massive support. Targeted welfare programs are politically vulnerable.

[beat]

Three, and this is the hinge of the whole case. Universal economic programs deliver disproportionate benefits to historically excluded groups. [beat] Why? Because those groups are over-represented in working-class positions.

So take Medicare for All, expanded Social Security, free public college, robust labor rights. Each one is universal in its framing. And each one, this stream argues, delivers substantive equity.

> The New Deal liberal case: universal programs endure, and benefit the excluded most.

Put another way: on this view, a program does not have to name a group in order to reach it.

## The identity-justice stream

Now the identity-justice stream, built the same way.

What it is protecting is a recognition. The exclusion of Black, Indigenous, women, LGBTQ and other marginalized groups is central to American political life. That exclusion is historical, and it is continuing. And it cannot be subsumed under the frames of economic justice.

In plainer words: on this view, genuine equity requires addressing those specific exclusions directly. A general economic uplift, which benefits these groups indirectly, is not enough.

Who speaks for it? Ta-Nehisi Coates. Ibram X. Kendi. Kimberlé Crenshaw. And the intellectuals around the policy platform of the Movement for Black Lives.

Now the argument, in the stream's own terms. It too has three parts.

One: the record of the New Deal itself. The New Deal coalition required compromises with Southern white Democrats, and those compromises excluded Black Americans from much of its benefits. Here the stream points to the record. Social Security at first excluded agricultural and domestic workers, who were overwhelmingly Black. Federal mortgage policy underwrote redlining. The GI Bill was administered with massive racial disparities. To treat those as accidents, this stream argues, is to misread what the New Deal actually did.

[beat]

Two: race-conscious policy is sometimes necessary, to address race-specific harms. Reparations for slavery and Jim Crow. Affirmative action in higher education. Race-equity audits of housing and lending markets. Each addresses a specific injury that universal economic policy does not.

[beat]

Three: the promise of downstream benefit has empirical limits. Residential segregation. Criminal-justice disparities. Mass incarceration. These, this stream argues, require direct policy attention, which programs of economic citizenship alone do not address.

> The identity-justice case: some harms cannot be reached by class-wide programs.

Put another way: on this view, where the injury was race-specific, a race-blind remedy misses the point.

Let's set those two streams side by side, in one concrete decision.

A city council is designing a program to help with down payments on homes. One faction wants it open to all first-generation homebuyers. Another wants it limited to residents of formerly redlined neighborhoods.

How would a New Deal liberal argue? And an identity-justice progressive? Use each stream's own logic, not a caricature. Take a moment.

[long pause]

The New Deal liberal backs the universal version. Universal programs are politically durable. And Black families, with other historically excluded families, are over-represented among first-generation buyers. So the universal design delivers substantive equity, without handing opponents a wedge.

The identity-justice progressive replies that redlining was a race-specific injury, so a race-blind remedy misses the point. It will leak benefits to people who were never injured, while under-compensating the actual victims.

Notice what the two share. [beat] The goal is the same: closing the racial wealth gap. The disagreement is genuine all the same. It is about whether universal means can reach harms that are specific to an injury.

## The civic-republican stream

Third, the civic-republican stream. Same treatment.

What it is protecting is democratic citizenship as a shared activity, not just a bundle of individual rights. On this view, progressivism should be in service of public-spirited deliberation, civic participation, and a shared sense of common purpose.

And this stream names two threats to that civic foundation. One is pure individualism, of the libertarian kind. The other is pure identity-fragmentation, which it finds in some forms of identity-justice. Both, it argues, undermine the foundation that progressive policy depends on.

Who speaks for it? Michael Sandel. Danielle Allen. Robert Reich, whose name you have now heard twice. And Mark Lilla, in some moods, though he is also a critic of identity politics.

Now the argument, in the stream's own terms. Three parts again.

One: progressivism is at its strongest when it speaks the language of shared citizenship, the common good, and democratic participation. Here the stream points to the record. The success of the New Deal, and the moral authority of the civil rights movement, both rested on appeals to American national identity and to shared civic purpose.

[beat]

Two: meritocratic individualism is itself a problem. It has become the operating ideology of much elite-liberal politics. Here the stream points to Sandel's book, The Tyranny of Merit. Its target is the post-1990s liberal emphasis on credentials, mobility, and individual achievement. That emphasis, Sandel argues, disregards the dignity of work and the value of solidarity. And it contributes to the polarization it then bemoans.

[beat]

Three: progressive policy should be designed to strengthen civic institutions, not just to redistribute resources. Democratic participation. Public spaces. Public-service careers. Civic education. Reich's book The Common Good makes that explicit.

> The civic-republican case: citizenship is a shared activity, not just a bundle of rights.

Put another way: on this view, policy should build what citizens share, and not only move resources among them.

[pause]

Three streams. Before they turn on each other, let's see what has stayed with you.

Here are four claims. Three belong to one stream each. One belongs to all three. Sort them as you hear them.

Universal programs are politically more durable than targeted ones.

[beat]

Race-conscious policy is sometimes necessary to address race-specific harms.

[beat]

The meritocratic ideal corrodes solidarity: it tells winners they earned everything, and losers they deserve their lot.

[beat]

Wealth concentration is a political problem, not merely an economic one.

Which of the four is shared ground? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The last one. All three streams treat wealth concentration as a political problem. The first claim is the New Deal liberal argument from durability. The second is the signature claim of identity-justice. And the third could only come from the civic-republican stream: it is Sandel's critique of meritocratic individualism, as a solvent of solidarity.

## Each against the others

So far, each stream has made its own case. The disagreements are sharpest when each one states its case against the other two. Take each as its smartest advocate would make it.

The New Deal liberal first.

Against identity-justice, the New Deal liberal argues that it prioritizes the wrong axis. Class is the more powerful organizing principle, and the more politically tractable one. Identity politics addresses real injustices. But it has produced a coalition that loses elections it should win. And universal programs deliver disproportionate benefits to marginalized groups anyway.

Against civic-republicanism, the New Deal liberal argues that it is too philosophical: too disconnected from the bread-and-butter issues that actually organize working-class politics. Important academically. Less useful politically.

[pause]

Now the identity-justice progressive.

Against New Deal liberalism, the identity-justice progressive argues that economic-justice framing alone is insufficient, and that the New Deal's own record proves it. A program called universal, and built on Southern compromises, excluded most Black workers by design. The civil rights movement, the women's movement and the LGBTQ movement are not subsidiary to economic justice. They are central to what progressivism is.

Against civic-republicanism, the identity-justice progressive argues that the language of shared civic identity sometimes elides the specific exclusions that have shaped American civic life, and that continue to shape it. Whose shared identity, exactly?

[pause]

And the civic republican.

Against New Deal liberalism, the civic republican argues that economic-justice programs without civic institutions atomize. Redistribution alone does not build the solidarity that sustains redistribution. We need both.

Against identity-justice, the civic republican argues that the language of grievance points at real injustices, but is poor at building the coalitions, and the shared civic identity, that progressive change actually requires. The civil rights movement won by claiming the American promise, not by standing outside it.

[pause]

Three streams, six criticisms, all from inside one family.

Now choose the stream you find least convincing. State its case against the other two, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Where they land

So much for argument. Where do the three streams land on actual policy?

Lay them against a list of current issues, and the first thing you notice is agreement.

Take universal healthcare. All three give it strong support. Only the frame changes. For the New Deal liberal, it is economic security. For the identity-justice progressive, it is in part racial equity. For the civic republican, it is the common good.

A universal child allowance draws strong support from all three. A wealth tax, antitrust, free public college, carbon pricing: support across the board.

So the streams differ in framing more than in raw policy positions. But the framing matters. It matters for building a political coalition, and for which trade-offs each stream is willing to accept.

[pause]

Now the exceptions. On three issues, the streams disagree about the policy itself, and not just the story told about it. Reparations for slavery. Affirmative action. And cultural identity programs, such as DEI.

On each of the three, the identity-justice stream gives strong support. The New Deal liberal is skeptical, or mixed. The civic republican is mixed.

What do those three issues have in common? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Each one is a targeted policy, conscious of race or identity. So each one sits directly on a single fault line: universal versus targeted.

> The family's deepest divide: universal programs, or targeted ones.

Immigration is contested too, for partly different reasons: labor concerns in one stream, concerns about civic integration in another. But on healthcare, the child allowance, and antitrust, the streams converge on substance. They argue only about which story to tell.

## A critique aimed at all three

One more voice, and it is the newest.

Since 2008, the progressive movement has been a contested mixture of all three streams, plus various smaller voices. The Sanders campaign drew heavily on the New Deal liberal stream. The post-Floyd reckoning drew heavily on the identity-justice stream. And books like Sandel's and Reich's pull toward the civic-republican stream.

Then, since the early 2020s, a fourth current has been gathering: the supply-side progressives, or abundance progressives. They are associated with a book called Abundance, by Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson, published in 2025.

Here is their argument. The binding constraint on progressive goals is no longer money. It is the government's capacity to build: housing, clean energy, transmission lines. And part of what blocks the building, they argue, is procedural rules that progressives themselves authored.

It is less a fourth stream than a critique aimed at all three.

> The abundance critique: whatever your framing, the state has to be able to deliver.

Today's Democratic coalition includes all of these, and the tensions are visible. There are debates over whether to call a policy "racial equity" or "working-class." And there are debates over how much to emphasize cultural questions against economic ones.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The family's argument: which injustice organizes the others, and which programs deliver?

[beat]

Two. The New Deal liberal case: universal programs endure, and benefit the excluded most. It is protecting economic security, as the foundation of democratic citizenship. It points to Social Security and Medicare.

[beat]

Three. The identity-justice case: some harms cannot be reached by class-wide programs. It is protecting the recognition that specific exclusions are central to American political life. It points to the New Deal's own record.

[beat]

Four. The civic-republican case: citizenship is a shared activity, not just a bundle of rights. It points to the New Deal and the civil rights movement, and their appeals to shared civic purpose. And it alone treats meritocratic individualism as a problem in itself.

[beat]

And five. The streams differ in framing more than in policy. The exceptions sit on the family's deepest divide: universal programs, or targeted ones. And the abundance critique is aimed at all three: the state has to be able to deliver.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not said which stream best fits current circumstances, or what synthesis is achievable. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

A defensible position within the tradition now requires three things. A view on the streams. A view on the issues where they diverge: reparations, affirmative action, immigration, DEI. And an honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.

The next lecture steps outside the family, and asks how progressivism relates to the other major American political traditions.

For now, go back to that rally in Nevada. Break up the banks, and would it end racism? The crowd shouted no. [beat] Inside the progressive family, the answer has never been one word long. You can now give it in three voices.
