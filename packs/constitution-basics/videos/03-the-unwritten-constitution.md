## The first rejection

On August 5, 1789, the Senate rejected a presidential nominee for the first time. Benjamin Fishbourn was George Washington's choice for naval officer of the port of Savannah. The Senate turned him down at the urging of a Georgia senator who held a personal grudge against him.

The next day Washington submitted a replacement, whom the Senate promptly confirmed.

- August 5, 1789: Fishbourn rejected

No clause commanded any of this.

## Two constitutions

The practice that rejection founded is senatorial courtesy: deference to home-state senators on home-state appointments. The text says only "Advice and Consent". The practice has operated for more than two centuries. It persists today in the blue-slip practice, under which the Judiciary Committee asks for home-state senators' approval before moving judicial nominees. Nothing but custom defines it.

- Senatorial courtesy: custom, not text

> American government runs on two constitutions: one written, one unwritten.

## What the text never says

Article Three never says courts may strike down statutes. That power is an inference drawn in Marbury v. Madison in 1803, and settled by two centuries of acceptance.

- Judicial review: inferred in Marbury, 1803

The Constitution says nothing about political parties. And the word cabinet appears nowhere in the original Constitution. The text lets the President require a written opinion from each department head. It was Washington who began consulting them as a group.

- Parties: nothing in the text
- In the text: written opinions
- The cabinet: Washington's practice

> A precedent hardens into an expectation, then into something close to law.

## When a norm breaks

Washington retired after two terms, and that convention held for nearly a century and a half. Then Franklin Roosevelt won a third term in 1940, and a fourth in 1944. Nothing illegal occurred. The only enforcement a convention has is political, and the voters declined to enforce.

In 1951 the Twenty-second Amendment converted the broken norm into text.

- Set by Washington
- Broken in 1940
- Written down in 1951

> A norm's penalty is paid in politics. A law's is paid in legal force.

## What stays open

Which present-day norms should be written down, and which would be damaged by writing them down, is a contested question.

- Which norms to write down: contested

The lesson has how political parties rewired checks and balances, a norm repaired by statute in 2022, and the scholars who argue that democracies are guarded less by their texts than by unwritten habits.
