## Ten votes in Concord

On June 21, 1788, a convention in Concord, New Hampshire voted 57 to 47. New Hampshire was the ninth state to ratify, and nine were enough. Inside that ten-vote margin, the United States government came into legal existence.

The document those delegates fought over runs about 4,400 words. Almost everything Americans argue about in politics eventually routes back to some clause of it.

- About 4,400 words

> One Preamble, seven Articles, and so far 27 amendments.

## A mission statement

The Preamble opens with "We the People of the United States", and states six purposes, from a more perfect Union to the Blessings of Liberty. It grants no power and confers no rights.

- Six purposes, no powers

> The Preamble is the mission statement. The machinery comes next.

## Seven articles

The first three articles create the three branches, one each. Article One creates Congress, and it is the longest by far. Article Two creates the Presidency. Article Three creates the federal judiciary. The last four govern how the pieces relate: the states, amendment, supremacy and ratification.

- Article I: Congress
- Article II: the Presidency
- Article III: the federal judiciary
- Articles IV to VII: how the pieces relate

## The keystone

Article Six makes the Constitution, and the federal laws made under it, "the supreme Law of the Land". It does not make Washington supreme in all things. But where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields, and the clause binds the judges in every state to enforce that result.

- State judges are bound to apply it

> Where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields.

## Hard, but not impossible

Article Five is the update mechanism. Two-thirds of both the House and the Senate can propose an amendment. Then three-fourths of the states must ratify it, 38 of 50, almost always through their legislatures. The President has no formal role in any of it.

- Propose: 2/3 of House and Senate
- Ratify: 38 of 50 states
- The President: no formal role

Members of Congress have introduced more than 11,000 amendment proposals since 1789. Thirty-three went to the states. Twenty-seven were ratified, and one of those took 202 years.

> More than 11,000 proposals. Twenty-seven amendments.

## The map, not the dispute

Knowing what the seven Articles do does not settle how to read them. Whether the Constitution's meaning is fixed at ratification or develops over time is contested.

- How to read it: contested
