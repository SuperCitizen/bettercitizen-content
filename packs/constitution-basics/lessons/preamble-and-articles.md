# What's actually in the Constitution?

On June 21, 1788, a convention in Concord, New Hampshire voted 57 to 47 — and inside that ten-vote margin, the United States government as we know it came into legal existence. Article VII of the proposed Constitution said ratification by nine states would be "sufficient for the Establishment of this Constitution," and New Hampshire was the ninth. The document those delegates fought over runs about 4,400 words — shorter than the terms-of-service agreements you accept without reading — and it is the oldest written national constitution still in operation. Almost everything Americans argue about in politics eventually routes back to some clause of it. So it is worth knowing, with precision, what is actually in there: one Preamble, seven Articles, and (so far) 27 amendments.

## The gamble of 1787

The Constitution was written in Philadelphia between May 25 and September 17, 1787, by delegates who had been sent to do something much smaller. Their formal assignment was to propose revisions to the Articles of Confederation, the weak first charter under which Congress could not effectively tax, regulate commerce, or compel the states to do much of anything. Instead, the 55 delegates who attended (Rhode Island boycotted entirely) drafted a replacement government. Of the 42 still present on the final day, 39 signed. Three refused — George Mason and Edmund Randolph of Virginia, and Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts — Mason in part because the document contained no bill of rights, an objection that would shape the first decade of the new republic and is the subject of this curriculum's [[term:bill-of-rights]] pack.

Then came the harder fight: ratification. The text went to popularly elected conventions in each state, not to state legislatures — a deliberate choice to root the new government in the people rather than in the state governments it would partially displace. The public argument was ferocious. Supporters, writing as "Publius" in what we now call the [[term:federalist-papers]], published 85 essays defending the design; opponents — the Anti-Federalists — warned of a distant, consolidated power. Delaware ratified first, on December 7, 1787. New Hampshire's vote in June 1788 crossed the nine-state threshold; Virginia and New York, the states that actually made the union viable, followed within weeks. Rhode Island, true to form, held out until May 29, 1790, by which point the new government had been running for over a year.

## The Preamble: a mission statement, not a power source

The Preamble was drafted at the very end of the convention by the Committee of Style, in the hand of Gouverneur Morris of Pennsylvania. Morris made one edit with enormous consequences: an earlier draft opened "We, the People of the States of New-Hampshire, Massachusetts…" and named all thirteen. Morris cut the list and wrote "We the People of the United States." Since no one knew which states would ratify, listing them was impractical — but the effect was to declare that the Constitution's authority flows from a single national people, not from a compact of states. Anti-Federalist Patrick Henry saw the implication immediately and demanded to know what right the framers had to say "We, the People" instead of "We, the States."

The Preamble then states six purposes: to form a more perfect Union, establish Justice, insure domestic Tranquility, provide for the common defence, promote the general Welfare, and secure the Blessings of Liberty to ourselves and our Posterity. Note what it does *not* do: it grants no power and confers no rights. The Supreme Court said so directly in *Jacobson v. Massachusetts* (1905) — the Preamble indicates the Constitution's general purposes but "has never been regarded as the source of any substantive power." It is the mission statement; the machinery comes next.

## Seven articles, one architecture

The body of the Constitution is organized with almost diagrammatic logic. The first three articles create the three branches — one each — in what political scientists call the [[term:separation-of-powers]]. The last four govern how the pieces relate.

| Article | What it does | Details worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Creates Congress | The longest article by far. Section 8 lists Congress's enumerated powers; Section 9 lists what Congress may not do; Section 10 limits the states. |
| II | Creates the Presidency | Vests "the executive Power" in one person; sets the elector system, the oath, the commander-in-chief role, and the duty to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." |
| III | Creates the federal judiciary | Establishes one Supreme Court — and leaves every lower federal court for Congress to create by statute. |
| IV | States' relations | Full faith and credit, privileges and immunities, admission of new states, and a guarantee to every state of "a Republican Form of Government." |
| V | Amendment | The two-track procedure covered below. |
| VI | Supremacy | Federal debts honored; the Supremacy Clause; oaths of office; no religious test for office. |
| VII | Ratification | Nine state conventions sufficed to launch the Constitution. |

Read down that table and the framers' priorities show. Article I is first and longest because the legislature was expected to dominate — and to be the most dangerous branch, which is why it alone is split in two ([[term:bicameral]]: House and Senate must separately agree to everything). The articles also interlock: the President can veto Congress's bills, Congress can override and can impeach, the Senate confirms judges and ratifies treaties, courts decide cases under all of it. That lattice of mutual interference is the system of [[term:checks-and-balances]] — a phrase that appears nowhere in the document but describes everything about it.

Article VI's second clause deserves its own sentence, because it is the keystone: the Constitution, federal laws "made in Pursuance thereof," and treaties are "the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding." That is the [[term:supremacy-clause]]. It does not make Washington supreme in all things — federal law prevails only where the federal government is acting within its granted powers — but where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields, and the clause conscripts *state judges* specifically to enforce that result.

Article VI's Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law "the supreme Law of the Land" and binds "the Judges in every State" — explicitly overriding anything in a state constitution or state law to the contrary. The judge's state oath doesn't change the answer; Article VI also requires state officers to swear support for the federal Constitution. The one real escape hatch is upstream: arguing the federal statute isn't a valid exercise of federal power in the first place.</p>

## Article V: the amendment machine

The framers knew they were shipping version 1.0. Article V is the update mechanism, and it was tuned to a deliberate setting: hard, but not impossible. The Articles of Confederation had required unanimity to amend — which meant, in practice, never. The Constitution lowered the bar to supermajorities at two stages.

**Proposal** can happen two ways: two-thirds of both the House and the Senate can propose an amendment, or two-thirds of the state legislatures (34 of 50) can apply to Congress, which "shall call a Convention for proposing Amendments." **Ratification** then requires three-fourths of the states — 38 of 50 — acting either through their legislatures or through one-time state conventions, whichever mode Congress designates.

The numbers tell you how steep the climb is. Members of Congress have introduced more than 11,000 amendment proposals since 1789, by the Senate's own count. Thirty-three cleared the two-thirds hurdle and went to the states. Twenty-seven made it through [[term:ratification]]. The convention route — the states' emergency bypass around a Congress that refuses to act — has never once been used, though campaigns for a balanced-budget-amendment convention have come within a handful of states of the 34 needed, and the procedure's unsettled rules (Who picks delegates? Can a convention be limited to one topic?) are among the liveliest open questions in constitutional law.

The 21st Amendment shows the machine's flexibility. When Congress proposed repealing [[term:prohibition]] in 1933, it knew many rural, dry-leaning state legislatures would balk, so it designated the *convention* ratification mode — the only time that has ever happened. Wet delegates were elected directly by voters, and repeal was ratified in under ten months, on December 5, 1933. At the other end of the speed scale, the 26th Amendment (lowering the voting age to 18) went from congressional proposal to ratification in about 100 days in 1971 — the fastest ever.

<details class="checkpoint">
<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> An amendment proposal passes the House 300–130 and the Senate 60–40. The President announces she will veto it. Then 37 state legislatures ratify. Is it part of the Constitution?</summary>
<p><strong>No — and the veto is a red herring.</strong> The Senate vote failed: 60 of 100 is short of the required two-thirds, so the proposal never validly left Congress (the House's 300–130 cleared the bar). The President is irrelevant either way — Article V gives the executive no role, as the Supreme Court confirmed back in 1798 in <em>Hollingsworth v. Virginia</em>. And even with a valid proposal, 37 states is one short of the 38 (three-fourths) required. Three independent reasons, one answer.</p>
</details>

## The 202-year amendment

Nothing illustrates Article V's strange physics better than the 27th Amendment. In September 1789, the First Congress sent twelve amendments to the states. Articles three through twelve were ratified in 1791 and became the Bill of Rights. Article two — barring any congressional pay raise from taking effect until after the next House election — stalled at six states and was forgotten.

In 1982, a University of Texas sophomore named Gregory Watson, digging through a book for a government paper, noticed that the proposal carried no ratification deadline. He argued in his paper that it was still legally pending and could still be ratified. He got a C. Instead of letting it go, Watson spent the next decade writing to state legislatures — and the politics of the 1980s, thick with public anger over congressional pay raises, did the rest. State after state ratified, and on May 7, 1992, Michigan became the 38th. The amendment proposed by James Madison's Congress was certified as part of the Constitution 202 years and seven months after it left the building. (In 2017, the University of Texas formally changed Watson's grade to an A.)

The episode settled, in practice, a real legal question: an amendment proposal without a deadline stays alive indefinitely. Congress only began attaching deadlines in the 20th century — which is why the Equal Rights Amendment, proposed in 1972 with a ratification deadline in its proposing resolution, remains the subject of live legal argument, while Madison's deadline-free pay amendment simply waited out two centuries.

<details class="checkpoint">
<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> Four of the twelve amendments Congress proposed in 1789 ended up with different fates: ten ratified by 1791, one ratified in 1992, one still pending. What does the 1992 ratification imply about the one still pending?</summary>
<p>The remaining proposal — the never-ratified congressional apportionment amendment — is, by the 27th Amendment's logic, <strong>still legally pending before the states</strong>, because the First Congress attached no deadline. The 27th's certification in 1992 established that age alone doesn't kill a proposal. Whether ratifications spread across centuries reflect any coherent popular consent is a separate, much-debated question — but the mechanism stays open.</p>
</details>

## The scholar's lens: parchment, ambition, and motive

Why all this redundancy — two chambers, three branches, two amendment tracks, supermajorities everywhere? Madison gave the canonical answer in Federalist No. 51 (1788): "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Paper guarantees alone — "parchment barriers," he called them elsewhere — would never restrain officials; the structure had to make each power-holder's self-interest a check on the others'. The essay's most quoted line compresses the whole theory of the document: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."

A century and a quarter later, historian Charles Beard's *An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution* (1913) offered a colder reading: the framers, he argued, were creditors and security-holders designing a government to protect their own asset class, and the document is better explained by portfolio than by philosophy. Beard's thesis dominated textbooks for four decades — until Robert E. Brown (1956) and Forrest McDonald's *We the People* (1958) re-ran his evidence delegate by delegate and found the economic alignments didn't hold; McDonald concluded the interpretation simply was "not in accordance with the facts." The modern consensus splits the difference: interests were everywhere in Philadelphia, but they cut across, not along, the lines Beard drew. Keep both lenses; the document rewards each.

## What this lesson is not

This lesson is the map, not the territory dispute. Knowing what the seven Articles do does not settle how to *read* them — whether the Constitution's meaning is fixed at ratification or develops over time is the originalism–living constitutionalism argument, and it is genuinely contested terrain this pack does not adjudicate. Nor have we covered the first ten amendments (the Bill of Rights pack) or the way Articles I, VI, and the Tenth Amendment divide power between Washington and the states (the Federalism pack). And Article V itself remains half-mapped: the convention route's procedures have never been tested, and scholars disagree sharply about what would happen if 34 states ever pulled that lever. For a quick video companion to the basics, see [Crash Course US History #8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0pGNrIfcwV0); for the primary text itself — all 4,400 words — the [National Archives transcript](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript) takes about twenty minutes to read. Few documents repay the time better.
