# Amending the Constitution: how Article V works, and why it almost never does

Thirty-eight states — exactly the number Article V requires — have voted at some point to ratify the Equal Rights Amendment. It is not in the Constitution. Meanwhile, an amendment that sat ignored for two centuries, ratified by a trickle of legislatures spread across ten presidencies, *is* — certified in 1992 without anyone seriously disputing it. Same article, same machinery, opposite outcomes. The difference comes down to a few sentences Congress did or didn't attach in the proposing resolution, and to questions the Supreme Court has deliberately refused to answer. This lesson takes the amendment machine apart piece by piece: the two ways to propose, the two ways to ratify, the deadline doctrine that decides which proposals die, and the one clause the machine itself cannot touch.

## The machine, piece by piece

Article V is a single dense paragraph, but it specifies a complete pipeline. **Proposal** comes first, by either of two tracks: two-thirds of both chambers of Congress (290 of 435 representatives and 67 of 100 senators, if everyone votes), or a national convention that Congress "shall call" when two-thirds of the state legislatures — 34 of 50 — apply for one. **Ratification** follows, again by either of two tracks: approval by three-fourths of the states, 38 of 50, acting through their legislatures or through one-time ratifying conventions. Congress, not the states, picks the [[term:ratification]] mode when it sends a proposal out. And when the 38th state's paperwork arrives, the Archivist of the United States certifies the [[term:amendment]] and publishes it — a purely ministerial act under federal statute (1 U.S.C. 106b): the Archivist verifies that the documents are facially sufficient and "does not make any substantive determinations as to the validity of State ratification actions."

Two features of the design deserve a second look. First, the supermajorities compound: a proposal needs the agreement of two-thirds of two congressional chambers *and* of legislative majorities (or conventions) in 38 separate states. A [[term:supermajority]] stacked on a supermajority is why blocking an amendment requires only 13 states — in principle, the 13 least populous, containing under 5 percent of the U.S. population. Second, Article V ends with an entrenchment clause, the Constitution's only permanent one: "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate." An amendment giving California more senators than Wyoming would need not 38 states but the consent of every state losing ground — which is to say, it is effectively unamendable. (Two other entrenchments, shielding the slave trade and direct-tax rules, expired by their own terms in 1808.)

<Checkpoint question="A reform group drafts an amendment to replace the Senate's two-senators-per-state rule with seats allocated by population, and it somehow wins two-thirds of Congress and 38 ratifications — every state except the 12 smallest. Is it valid?">
  **No.** Article V's final clause says no state may be "deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate" *without its consent*. The 12 smallest states didn't consent, so the amendment fails as to them no matter how many other states ratify — which makes the change practically impossible rather than merely hard. It is the one substantive rule the amendment machine cannot reach by ordinary supermajorities.
</Checkpoint>

## The convention nobody has called

The second proposal track was the Founders' insurance policy: if Congress itself ever became the obstacle — refusing, say, to propose term limits on its own members — the states could route around it. George Mason insisted on it at Philadelphia precisely because, he argued, Congress would never propose amendments curbing its own power. The trigger has never been pulled, but it has been *approached*. The campaign for a federal [[issue:balanced-budget-amendment]] brought state applications within a handful of the 34 needed in the early 1980s, and organized drives on both the right and the left have kept counting applications since.

What keeps even sympathetic legislators wary is that Article V says almost nothing about how a convention would work. Who picks the delegates, and how are votes allocated — one state one vote, or by population? Must Congress call a convention when 34 applications address different subjects, or only when they match? And the hardest one: could a convention called for a single topic propose anything it liked? The only precedent is not reassuring to either side — the Philadelphia convention of 1787 was itself convened merely to revise the Articles of Confederation and instead wrote a new government. Whatever a runaway convention proposed would still need 38 states to ratify, which is the real safety net; but "the safety net has never been load-tested" is exactly the argument opponents make.

## Deadlines, and where they live

Nothing in Article V mentions time limits. For the Constitution's first 128 years, proposals simply went out and stayed out — which is how Madison's pay amendment could lie dormant from 1789 to 1992. Congress first attached a deadline in 1917, writing a seven-year limit directly into the text of the 18th Amendment ([[term:prohibition]]). A man arrested under the new prohibition law challenged the practice, and in *Dillon v. Gloss* (1921) the Supreme Court upheld it, reasoning that ratification ought to be "sufficiently contemporaneous" to reflect the will of one generation rather than an accident of accumulation. Eighteen years later the Court stepped back from policing time at all: *Coleman v. Miller* (1939), a dispute over Kansas's ratification of the child labor amendment 13 years after its proposal, held that the timeliness of a ratification is a political question for Congress, not the courts.

That pair of holdings left a live wire in the system, because *where* Congress puts the deadline turns out to matter enormously. Six of the 33 proposals Congress has sent out were never ratified, and their fates diverge on exactly this point:

| Proposal | Sent to the states | Deadline | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Congressional apportionment | 1789 | None | Still pending |
| Titles of nobility | 1810 | None | Still pending |
| Corwin Amendment (shielding slavery from federal interference) | 1861 | None | Still pending — a dead letter morally and politically, but never withdrawn |
| Child labor | 1924 | None | Still pending; stalled at 28 states |
| Equal Rights Amendment | 1972 | Seven years, in the **proposing resolution** | Contested — see below |
| D.C. voting rights | 1978 | Seven years, in the **amendment's own text** | Expired 1985; indisputably dead |

Read the last two rows against each other. The D.C. voting rights amendment placed its time limit inside the amendment text itself — ratification within seven years was made a condition of the amendment's own validity, so when the clock ran out in 1985 with only 16 states, there was nothing left to argue about. The ERA's seven-year limit sat in the proposing resolution's introductory clause, *outside* the text the states actually ratified. Whether that kind of deadline binds — and whether Congress can extend or repeal it after the fact — is the unresolved question on which the ERA's entire modern story turns.

## Case study: the fifty-year amendment

The Equal Rights Amendment is one sentence — "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex" — and it has been in motion for over a century. Alice Paul's National Woman's Party first had it introduced in 1923, three years after the 19th Amendment secured [[term:suffrage]]. It was reintroduced in every Congress for half a century before passing the House 354–24 in October 1971 and the Senate 84–8 in March 1972 — margins that made ratification look like a formality. It was anything but.

Walk through what the timeline forces you to confront. If Congress's deadline was valid, were the 2017–2020 ratifications legal nullities — and was the 1978 extension, passed by simple majorities, valid in the first place? If late ratifications count because *Coleman* makes timing a political question for Congress, then do the four rescissions count too, by the same logic of deference to states' latest word? The 27th Amendment is precedent for counting very old ratifications — but Madison's proposal had no deadline at all, which is precisely the distinction the Archivist's office has drawn. As of mid-2026 the ERA sits in a constitutional superposition: ratified by 38 states on one accounting, expired since 1982 on another, uncertified on any — and the dispute remains live in Congress, in the courts, and in the scholarship.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose a future Congress passes a joint resolution, by simple majority, declaring the ERA's deadline repealed and directing the Archivist to certify it. Does that settle the matter?">
  **It would force the question, not settle it.** *Coleman v. Miller* says ratification timeliness is a political question committed to Congress — which supporters read as authorizing exactly this move. But opponents would answer that a deadline attached by a two-thirds proposing majority can't be stripped by a simple majority fifty years later, and that four states validly rescinded — leaving the count short of 38 regardless. Courts would then have to decide whether *Coleman* requires them to stay out entirely. The honest answer is that Article V's text resolves none of this, which is the deepest lesson of the ERA case.
</Checkpoint>

## The scholar's lens: the hardest constitution to amend

Political scientist Donald Lutz put the difficulty in comparative perspective in "Toward a Theory of Constitutional Amendment" (*American Political Science Review*, 1994), measuring amendment procedures across the 50 American states and dozens of national constitutions. The U.S. Constitution came out among the very hardest to amend in the world, with one of the lowest amendment rates — and Lutz found that a *moderate* amendment rate, not a minimal one, is what correlates with a constitution's survival. The state constitutions make the contrast vivid: they are amended constantly, often by simple ballot measure, and Alabama's 1901 charter alone accumulated close to a thousand amendments before the state adopted a recompiled version in 2022.

Lutz's framework also names the consequence of setting the dial this stiff: constitutional change does not stop, it *relocates*. When formal amendment is blocked, the updating work migrates to judicial interpretation — the meaning of "equal protection" or "commerce" stretches and shifts through litigation instead of through Article V. Whether that migration is a pathology (judges doing what supermajorities of voters should) or a feature (insulating fundamentals from transient passions while letting application evolve) is one of the standing arguments of American constitutional theory, and it shadows every fight in this lesson — including the ERA's, where skeptics argue the courts have already delivered much of what the amendment promised, and supporters answer that rights resting on precedent can be unsettled in a way that text cannot.

## What this lesson is not

This lesson explains the machine; it does not referee the machine's hardest open cases. Whether the ERA is validly ratified is, as of mid-2026, genuinely undecided — actively litigated and politically contested — and you should treat anyone who tells you the answer is obvious, in either direction, as advocating rather than reporting. Nor does this lesson settle whether Article V's difficulty is set correctly: Lutz gives you the comparative data, but the judgment — durable text versus democratic responsiveness — is yours to argue. And one whole category of constitutional change has gone unmentioned here: the kind that happens with no amendment at all, through practice, precedent, and unwritten convention. That is the next lesson.
