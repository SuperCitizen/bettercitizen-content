# The unwritten constitution: what runs on convention, not text

On August 5, 1789 — before the Supreme Court had heard a single case, before the Bill of Rights existed — the United States Senate rejected a presidential nominee for the first time. Benjamin Fishbourn, George Washington's choice for naval officer of the port of Savannah, was a Revolutionary War veteran with sterling qualifications, and the Senate turned him down anyway, at the urging of Georgia senator James Gunn, who had a candidate of his own. Washington, stung, withdrew the nomination and submitted the man Georgia's senators wanted. No clause commanded any of this. Yet the practice it founded — *senatorial courtesy*, deference to home-state senators on home-state appointments — has now operated for more than two centuries, longer than most of the written Constitution's amendments. The American government runs on two constitutions: the 4,400-word document the last two lessons mapped, and a second, unwritten one made of precedents, practices, and norms. This lesson maps the second.

## The rules behind the rules

The British constitutional scholar A. V. Dicey gave the phenomenon its classic name in *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885): "the conventions of the constitution" — rules of political behavior that are followed as if binding, that everyone in the system treats as obligatory, but that no court will enforce. Britain, famously, runs almost entirely on such conventions; Dicey's point was that even a system *with* a written charter cannot escape them, because no text can specify everything. A document that tried to script every contingency of governance would be obsolete before the ink dried — which the framers understood. They wrote a short framework and left the operating manual to be worked out in practice.

The constitutional scholar Akhil Reed Amar argues in *America's Unwritten Constitution* (2012) that the written and unwritten constitutions are not rivals but partners: the text itself presupposes unwritten rules (it never defines "jury" or "due process," borrowing centuries of inherited practice), and the practices in turn draw their legitimacy from the text's silences and structures. The test of whether something belongs to the unwritten constitution is not whether it is *important* — plenty of statutes are important — but whether it does *constitutional* work: structuring who holds power and how they may use it, while resting on nothing but precedent and expectation.

Five examples carry this lesson, and it is worth seeing them side by side before examining each:

| --- | --- | --- |

Read the middle column and notice how little load the text bears. Now read the right column and notice what bears it instead: in every case, an early precedent that hardened into an expectation that hardened into something close to law.

## Judicial review: the power the text never grants

Start with the biggest entry on the list. The Supreme Court's power to strike down an Act of Congress — [[term:judicial-review]] — structures all of American politics; confirmation battles are wars precisely because everyone knows what the Court can do. Yet Article III never says courts may invalidate statutes. It vests "the judicial Power" in the courts and extends it to cases "arising under this Constitution" — and stops there.

The argument that judicial review was nonetheless *implied* predates the document's ratification. Hamilton made it in Federalist No. 78 (1788), reasoning that a constitution is law — superior law — and that judges, whose whole office is applying law, must prefer it when a statute conflicts; the judiciary could be trusted with the power, he added, because it held "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment." Chief Justice John Marshall converted the argument into practice in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), declining to enforce a section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 that he found exceeded Article III: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The genius of the opinion was that it asserted the power while wielding it *against the Court's own jurisdiction* — handing Marshall's political rival Jefferson the immediate result he wanted, so there was nothing to defy.

What made *Marbury*'s inference into a pillar was not the opinion but the two centuries of acceptance that followed — Congresses that rewrote struck-down statutes instead of ignoring the Court, presidents who enforced judgments they hated. That acceptance, not any clause, is why judicial review now *feels* textual. It is the unwritten constitution's greatest success: an inference so settled that most Americans would guess it is written down.

## Parties: the machine the framers dreaded

If judicial review is the unwritten constitution's proudest feature, parties are its most ironic. The Constitution says nothing about them, and the omission was contemptuous, not careless: the framers associated organized political combination with corruption and instability. Washington devoted much of his Farewell Address (1796) to warning the country "in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party." By the time he published it, the warning was already obsolete — Hamilton's Federalists and Jefferson's Republicans had organized around the cabinet table itself, and within four years the election of 1800 was a straight two-party fight. Its aftermath produced both halves of a pattern this lesson keeps returning to: the [[term:federalist-papers]]' generation absorbed parties into the *text* (the 12th Amendment, separating presidential and vice-presidential ballots after the Jefferson–Burr tie) and into *convention* (the inauguration of March 4, 1801 — the first peaceful transfer of national power between opposing parties, with the defeated Federalists simply handing over the machinery of state).

The deepest consequence took two centuries to name. In "Separation of Parties, Not Powers" (*Harvard Law Review*, 2006), Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes argued that Madison's design assumed branches would check branches — Congress jealous of the President, whoever held either office. Parties rewired that circuit: ambition now runs through party, not branch, so the Madisonian machine of [[term:checks-and-balances]] engages vigorously under divided government and idles under unified government. The written separation of powers still stands; the unwritten party system determines whether it actually operates.

<Checkpoint question="The President's party holds majorities in both chambers, and Congress declines to subpoena executive-branch officials over a scandal it would have investigated furiously under an opposition president. Is the constitutional system malfunctioning — or working exactly as it now works?">
  By the *written* design, it is a malfunction: Madison expected Congress as an institution to guard its prerogatives against any executive. By the *operating* design Levinson and Pildes describe, it is normal: the real competition is between parties, so inter-branch checking switches on with divided government and off without it. Nothing in the text changed — the unwritten machinery that runs the text did. That is why "the Constitution provides checks and balances" is true on paper and contingent in practice.
</Checkpoint>

## The cabinet and the courtesies

The word "cabinet" appears nowhere in the original Constitution. The closest the text comes is the Opinion Clause of Article II, Section 2: the President "may require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments" — a power to demand memos, one officer at a time. It was Washington who, in his first term, began summoning his principal officers — Jefferson, Hamilton, Knox, and Attorney General Randolph — as a deliberative body, and the practice stuck so completely that the cabinet became the recognizable core of every administration since. The text caught up only obliquely and only in 1967, when the 25th Amendment gave "the principal officers of the executive departments" a role in determining presidential incapacity — still without using the word.

The Senate's internal life is even more convention-built. Senatorial courtesy, born with Fishbourn in 1789, persists today in the blue-slip practice, under which the Judiciary Committee solicits home-state senators' approval before moving judicial nominees — a custom whose strictness expands and contracts at the committee chair's discretion, because nothing but custom defines it. The [[term:filibuster]], often mistaken for a constitutional feature, is an artifact of Senate rules with no textual basis at all; the Constitution names its supermajorities explicitly (treaties, veto overrides, expulsions, amendments, conviction on impeachment) and passing ordinary legislation is not among them. Each of these practices does real constitutional work — allocating appointment power, setting an effective vote threshold for legislation — while remaining, formally, just a habit the chamber could drop tomorrow.

## When norms break, text grows

What happens when someone simply ignores an unwritten rule? The two-term presidency is the cleanest natural experiment. Washington's retirement after two terms, reinforced by Jefferson's, set a convention that held for nearly a century and a half — challengers (Grant, Theodore Roosevelt) tested it and lost. Then Franklin Roosevelt, with a world war looming, ran for and won a third term in 1940 and a fourth in 1944. Nothing illegal occurred; the only enforcement mechanism a convention has is political, and the voters declined to enforce. So the system reached for its other tool: in 1951 the 22nd Amendment converted the broken norm into text, and a third term went from unthinkable to void.

The timeline's last entry shows the same repair running through statute rather than amendment: after the count of January 6, 2021 was disputed, Congress rewrote the Electoral Count Act to state expressly that the Vice President's role in the count is ministerial — converting an assumption nobody had thought needed writing down into black-letter law. The pattern is general, and it is the unwritten constitution's signature dynamic: conventions are cheap, flexible, and adaptive while they hold, and when one breaks badly enough, the system pays the cost of codification.

Two recent works give this dynamic its theory. Daphna Renan's "Presidential Norms and Article II" (*Harvard Law Review*, 2018) maps how much of the presidency — from Justice Department independence in criminal matters to financial self-dealing rules — runs on norms that both empower and constrain, and shows why such norms are most fragile exactly when most needed: their enforcement depends on political actors valuing the long game over the immediate win. Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt's *How Democracies Die* (2018) generalizes the point across countries: constitutional democracies, they argue, are guarded less by their texts than by two "soft guardrails" — *mutual toleration* (treating opponents as legitimate rivals, not enemies) and *institutional forbearance* (declining to exploit every power the rules technically allow). Written constitutions survive, on their account, only where those unwritten habits do.

<Checkpoint question="A norm and a law can forbid the same conduct. Using the third-term story, name the practical difference between the two when someone decides to defy them.">
  When FDR defied the two-term **norm** in 1940, the only available enforcement was political — opponents could campaign against the breach, and voters chose to ratify it instead. After 1951, a third term violates **text**: it is legally void, courts and officials are bound to treat it as a nullity, and no election result can cure it. A norm's penalty is paid in politics; a law's penalty is paid in legal force. That asymmetry is why norms are flexible in ordinary times, fragile under determined pressure — and why badly broken ones tend to get written down.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson is not

This lesson establishes that an unwritten constitution exists and does heavy work; it does not settle the fights about its content. Whether judicial review's modern *scope* is right — judicial supremacy versus the view that each branch interprets the Constitution for itself — is a live argument this pack does not adjudicate, and *Marbury* is claimed by both sides. Which present-day norms deserve the 22nd Amendment treatment, and which would be damaged by writing them down (codification can invite line-hunting at the new boundary, and forbearance resists drafting), is a genuinely contested question of institutional design rather than a knowable fact. And the unwritten constitution keeps being written *by practice* right now, in disputes this lesson is too early to score. What you should take from the pack as a whole is the full inventory: a short text (lesson one), a deliberately stiff mechanism for changing it (lesson two), and a thick layer of convention that does much of the daily governing (this lesson) — three layers, one constitutional order.
