It is August 5, 1789. The Supreme Court has not heard a single case. The Bill of Rights does not exist yet. And the United States Senate is about to do something for the first time.

It rejects a presidential nominee.

[beat]

The nominee is Benjamin Fishbourn, George Washington's choice for naval officer of the port of Savannah. He is a Revolutionary War veteran with sterling qualifications.

The Senate turns him down anyway, at the urging of a Georgia senator, James Gunn, who nursed a personal grudge against him.

Washington is stung. He sends the Senate a written protest. And the next day he submits a replacement, whom the Senate promptly confirms.

[pause]

No clause of the Constitution commanded any of this. Yet the practice it founded has now operated for more than two centuries. That is longer than most of the written Constitution's amendments.

The practice has a name: senatorial courtesy. It means deference to home-state senators on home-state appointments. Keep that name, because I will ask you for it later.

And keep the larger point it stands for.

> The American government runs on two constitutions: one written, one unwritten.

The written one is the document of 4,400 words that the last two lectures mapped. The unwritten one is made of precedents, practices and norms.

This lecture maps the second, and we will take it in four steps.

First, what an unwritten rule is, and why no written text can govern without them.

Second, the largest of them all: the power of courts to strike down laws.

Third, what the text never set up: political parties, the cabinet, and the courtesies of the Senate.

And last, what happens when someone breaks an unwritten rule.

## The rules behind the rules

First, a caution about a word. In the last lecture, a convention was a meeting, called to propose amendments. Here, a convention is something else. It is a rule of behavior.

The British constitutional scholar A. V. Dicey gave such rules their classic name, in 1885. He called them "the conventions of the constitution."

They are rules of political behavior that are followed as if binding. Everyone in the system treats them as obligatory. [beat] And no court will enforce them.

> A convention is a rule everyone treats as binding and no court will enforce.

Britain, famously, runs almost entirely on such conventions. But Dicey's point was broader than Britain. Even a system with a written charter cannot escape them, because no text can specify everything.

The framers understood that. A document that tried to script every contingency of governance would be obsolete before the ink dried. So they wrote a short framework, and left the operating manual to be worked out in practice.

Does that make the two constitutions rivals? The constitutional scholar Akhil Reed Amar argues that they are partners.

The text itself presupposes unwritten rules. It never defines a jury, or due process. It borrows centuries of inherited practice. And the practices, in turn, draw their legitimacy from the text's silences and structures.

[pause]

How do you tell what belongs to the unwritten constitution? The test is not whether a practice is important. Plenty of statutes are important.

The test is whether it does constitutional work. Does it structure who holds power, and how they may use it, while resting on nothing but precedent and expectation?

Five examples will carry us: judicial review, political parties, the cabinet, senatorial courtesy, and the two-term presidency.

Set them side by side, and you notice how little load the text bears. Then you notice what bears the load instead. In every case, an early precedent hardened into an expectation, and the expectation hardened into something very like law.

## Judicial review

Start with the biggest of the five. The Supreme Court can strike down an Act of Congress. That power is called judicial review, and it structures all of American politics.

Confirmation battles are wars precisely because everyone knows what the Court can do.

Now go looking for that power in the text. Article Three vests "the judicial Power" in the courts. It extends that power to cases "arising under this Constitution." [beat] And it stops there.

It never says that courts may invalidate statutes.

So where did the power come from? From an argument, and the argument is older than the ratified document.

Hamilton made it in 1788, in Federalist 78. A constitution is law, he reasoned, and superior law. Judges, whose whole office is applying law, must prefer it when a statute conflicts with it.

And the judiciary could be trusted with that power, he added, because it held "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."

In 1803, Chief Justice John Marshall turned the argument into practice. The case was Marbury v. Madison. Marshall declined to enforce a section of a statute, the Judiciary Act of 1789, which he found exceeded Article Three.

And he wrote the sentence everyone remembers.

"It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

[pause]

To say what the law is.

The genius of the opinion lay in how it used the power. Marshall asserted judicial review while wielding it against the Court's own jurisdiction. That handed his political rival, Jefferson, the immediate result Jefferson wanted. [beat] So there was nothing to defy.

But an opinion does not make a pillar. What made Marbury's inference into one was the two centuries of acceptance that followed.

Congresses rewrote struck-down statutes instead of ignoring the Court. Presidents enforced judgments they hated.

> No text grants judicial review. It rests on an inference, and on acceptance.

That acceptance, and not any clause, is why judicial review now feels textual. It is the unwritten constitution's greatest success: an inference so settled that most Americans would guess it is written down.

Let's test that. Three friends are arguing about where the Court gets its power to strike down an Act of Congress.

The first says Article Three expressly authorizes it. The second says the Supremacy Clause names the Supreme Court the final arbiter of all law. The third says Congress assigned the power by statute, in the Judiciary Act of 1789.

Which of them is right? Take a moment.

[long pause]

None of them.

Article Three vests the judicial power without defining it. The Supremacy Clause binds state judges, and never mentions reviewing Congress. And the Judiciary Act is the statute that Marbury partially struck down, not the source of the power.

The source is an inference. Marshall drew it from the judges' duty to apply the Constitution as superior law. And its durability rests on acquiescence, which is exactly what makes it part of the unwritten constitution.

## Parties

If judicial review is the unwritten constitution's proudest feature, political parties are its most ironic.

The Constitution says nothing about them. And the omission was contemptuous, not careless. The framers associated organized political combination with corruption and instability.

Washington devoted much of his Farewell Address, in 1796, to the danger. He warned the country "in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party."

[pause]

By the time he published it, the warning was already obsolete. Hamilton's Federalists and Jefferson's Republicans had organized around the cabinet table itself. And within four years, the election of 1800 was a straight two-party fight.

That generation then absorbed parties in two ways at once.

It absorbed them into the text. The Twelfth Amendment separated the ballots for president and vice president, after a tie between Jefferson and Burr.

And it absorbed them into convention. The inauguration of March 4, 1801, was the first peaceful transfer of national power between opposing parties. The defeated Federalists simply handed over the machinery of state.

[pause]

The deepest consequence took two centuries to name. In 2006, Daryl Levinson and Richard Pildes published an article in the Harvard Law Review. Its title is the argument: "Separation of Parties, Not Powers."

Madison's design, they argued, assumed that branches would check branches. Congress would be jealous of the president, whoever held either office.

Parties rewired that circuit. Ambition now runs through party, not through branch. So the machine of checks and balances engages vigorously under divided government, and idles under unified government.

In plainer words: when different parties hold Congress and the presidency, the checks switch on. When one party holds both, they switch off.

> The written checks still stand. The party system determines whether they operate.

Here is a case to try that on. The president's party holds majorities in both chambers of Congress. A scandal breaks, and Congress declines to subpoena officials of the executive branch. Under a president of the other party, it would have investigated furiously.

Is the constitutional system malfunctioning? Or is it working exactly as it now works? What would you say?

[long pause]

Each answer is right, about a different constitution.

By the written design, it is a malfunction. Madison expected Congress, as an institution, to guard its prerogatives against any executive.

By the operating design that Levinson and Pildes describe, it is normal. The real competition is between parties, so checking between the branches switches on with divided government, and off without it.

Nothing in the text changed. The unwritten machinery that runs the text did. And that is why the sentence "the Constitution provides checks and balances" is true on paper, and contingent in practice.

## The cabinet and the courtesies

Now two quieter cases. One sits in the executive branch, and one in the Senate.

The word cabinet appears nowhere in the original Constitution. The closest the text comes is the Opinion Clause, in Article Two. The president, it says, "may require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments."

[beat]

Listen to how small that is. It is a power to demand memos, one officer at a time.

It was Washington who built something larger. In his first term, he began summoning his principal officers as a deliberative body: Jefferson, Hamilton, Knox, and the attorney general, Randolph.

The practice stuck so completely that the cabinet became the recognizable core of every administration since.

The text caught up only obliquely, and only in 1967. That year, the Twenty-Fifth Amendment gave "the principal officers of the executive departments" a role in determining presidential incapacity. [beat] Still without using the word.

[pause]

The Senate's internal life is even more convention-built. And here I owe you a question.

When this lecture began, the Senate turned down Washington's nominee, at the urging of a senator from Georgia. What is the name of the practice that began that day?

[long pause]

Senatorial courtesy. Deference to home-state senators on home-state appointments.

It was born with Fishbourn, and it persists today in the blue-slip practice. Under that practice, the Judiciary Committee solicits the approval of home-state senators before it moves judicial nominees.

How strict is the custom? That expands and contracts at the committee chair's discretion, because nothing but custom defines it.

Then there is the filibuster, which is often mistaken for a constitutional feature. It is an artifact of Senate rules, with no textual basis at all.

The Constitution names its supermajorities explicitly: treaties, veto overrides, expulsions, amendments, and conviction on impeachment. Passing ordinary legislation is not among them.

Each of these practices does real constitutional work. One allocates appointment power. Another sets an effective vote threshold for legislation. And each remains, formally, just a habit the chamber could drop tomorrow.

> A habit can do constitutional work, and still be only a habit.

## When norms break

That brings us to the last step. What happens when someone simply ignores an unwritten rule?

The two-term presidency is the cleanest natural experiment. Washington retired after two terms, and Jefferson's retirement reinforced the example.

The convention held for nearly a century and a half. Two challengers, Grant and Theodore Roosevelt, tested it and lost.

Then came Franklin Roosevelt. With a world war looming, he ran for a third term in 1940, and won. In 1944 he won a fourth.

[beat]

Nothing illegal occurred. The only enforcement mechanism a convention has is political, and the voters declined to enforce.

So the system reached for its other tool. In 1951, the Twenty-Second Amendment converted the broken norm into text. A third term went from unthinkable to void.

[pause]

The same repair can run through a statute. The electoral count of January 6, 2021, was disputed. So in 2022, Congress passed the Electoral Count Reform Act.

It spells out in law that the vice president's role in counting electoral votes is purely ministerial. An assumption nobody had thought needed writing down became black-letter law.

The pattern is general. Conventions are cheap, flexible and adaptive while they hold. And when one breaks badly enough, the system pays the cost of codification.

> Badly broken norms tend to get written down.

Two recent works give that pattern its theory.

The first is an article by Daphna Renan, on presidential norms. It maps how much of the presidency runs on them, from the Justice Department's independence in criminal matters to the rules on financial self-dealing.

And it shows why such norms are most fragile exactly when they are most needed. Their enforcement depends on political actors valuing the long game over the immediate win.

The second is a book, How Democracies Die, by Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt. It carries the point across countries. Constitutional democracies, they argue, are guarded less by their texts than by two soft guardrails.

One is mutual toleration: treating opponents as legitimate rivals, not enemies. The other is institutional forbearance: declining to exploit every power the rules technically allow.

On their account, written constitutions survive only where those unwritten habits do.

[pause]

One last test. A norm and a law can forbid the same behavior. Think back over the third-term story.

What is the practical difference between the two, when someone decides to defy them?

[long pause]

When Franklin Roosevelt defied the two-term norm, the only enforcement available was political. Opponents could campaign against the breach, and voters chose to ratify it instead.

After 1951, a third term violates text. It is legally void. Courts and officials are bound to treat it as a nullity, and no election result can cure it.

> A norm's penalty is paid in politics. A law's is paid in legal force.

That asymmetry is why norms are flexible in ordinary times, and fragile under determined pressure. And it is why badly broken ones tend to get written down.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The American government runs on two constitutions: one written, one unwritten. The unwritten one is made of conventions: rules everyone treats as binding, and no court will enforce.

[beat]

Two. No text grants judicial review. It rests on an inference, drawn in Marbury v. Madison in 1803, and on two centuries of acceptance.

[beat]

Three. The Constitution says nothing about parties. Yet the party system determines whether the written checks operate. They engage under divided government, and idle under unified government.

[beat]

Four. The cabinet, senatorial courtesy, the blue slip, the filibuster. Each is a habit doing constitutional work.

[beat]

Five. A norm's penalty is paid in politics. A law's is paid in legal force. So badly broken norms tend to get written down, as the two-term limit was in 1951.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has shown that an unwritten constitution exists, and that it does heavy work. It has not settled the fights over what it contains.

Is the modern scope of judicial review right? One view is judicial supremacy. The other holds that each branch interprets the Constitution for itself. That argument is still open, and Marbury is claimed by both sides.

And which of today's norms deserve the treatment the two-term limit got? Which would be damaged by writing them down? Codification can invite line-hunting at the new boundary, and forbearance resists drafting.

That is a contested question of institutional design, not a knowable fact.

Meanwhile, the unwritten constitution keeps being written, by practice, right now, in disputes it is too early to score.

There is no next lecture in this series, so take the full inventory with you. A short text. A deliberately stiff mechanism for changing it. And a thick layer of convention that does much of the daily governing. [beat] Three layers, one constitutional order.

And go back, one last time, to that day in August of 1789. A president nominated. A senator objected. The Senate went along. [beat] No clause required it. And more than two centuries later, the practice it began is still at work.
