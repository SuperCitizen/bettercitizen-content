Start with a number. Thirty-eight.

[beat]

That is how many states it takes to ratify an amendment to the Constitution. And 38 states have voted, at some point, to ratify the Equal Rights Amendment.

It is not in the Constitution.

[pause]

Now set a second amendment beside it. Last time, we followed Madison's pay amendment, which sat ignored for two centuries. A trickle of state legislatures ratified it. And in 1992 it was certified, without anyone seriously disputing it.

Same article. Same machinery. [beat] Opposite outcomes.

Why? The difference comes down to a few sentences that Congress did, or did not, attach when it proposed each one. And it comes down to questions the Supreme Court has deliberately refused to answer.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to explain how those two amendments landed where they did. We will take the amendment machine apart in four steps.

First, the pipeline: how an amendment is proposed, ratified and certified, and the one rule the pipeline cannot touch.

Second, the convention that nobody has ever called.

Third, deadlines: where they come from, and why it matters so much where they are written.

And last, the case that tests all of it, the Equal Rights Amendment. Then we will step back, and ask what happens to a constitution this hard to change.

## The pipeline

The whole amendment procedure sits in Article Five. It is a single dense paragraph, but it lays out a complete pipeline, in three stages.

Stage one is proposal, and there are two tracks.

On the first, two-thirds of both chambers of Congress vote to propose. If everyone votes, that means 290 representatives. And it means 67 senators.

The second track goes around Congress. When two-thirds of the state legislatures apply for one, Congress "shall call" a national convention to propose amendments. Two-thirds of the states is 34.

Stage two is ratification, and again there are two tracks. Three-fourths of the states must approve, which is 38 of 50. They act through their legislatures, or through one-time ratifying conventions.

And it is Congress, not the states, that picks the mode when it sends a proposal out.

Stage three is certification. When the 38th state's paperwork arrives, the Archivist of the United States certifies the amendment and publishes it.

Under federal statute, that is a purely ministerial act: a duty to carry out, not a judgment call. The Archivist checks that the documents are sufficient on their face. The Archivist does not decide whether a state's ratification was valid.

Propose. Ratify. Certify.

[pause]

Notice who never appears. No governor signs anything, because ratification is the act of a legislature or a convention. And the president has no role at any stage.

Two features of this design deserve a second look.

The first is that the supermajorities compound. A proposal needs two-thirds of two chambers of Congress. Then it needs the agreement of 38 separate states. That is a supermajority stacked on a supermajority.

Now turn the arithmetic around. If it takes 38 states to ratify, how many does it take to block? [beat] Thirteen.

In principle, those could be the 13 least populous states, which together hold under 5 percent of the country's population.

> It takes 38 states to ratify. It takes only 13 to block.

The second feature is the way Article Five ends. Its last clause is an entrenchment clause: a rule placed beyond the ordinary reach of amendment. Here are the words.

"No State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate."

[pause]

Think about what that does. An amendment giving California more senators than Wyoming would not need 38 states. It would need the consent of every state losing ground. Which is to say, it is effectively unamendable.

> No state loses its equal vote in the Senate without its consent.

It is the only permanent entrenchment in the Constitution.

Let's test that. A reform group drafts an amendment. It would replace the rule of two senators for each state with Senate seats allocated by population.

Somehow it wins two-thirds of Congress. And it wins 38 ratifications: every state except the 12 smallest.

Is it valid? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No. The 12 smallest states did not consent. So the amendment fails as to them, however many other states ratify.

That makes the change practically impossible, and not merely hard. It is the one substantive rule the amendment machine cannot reach by ordinary supermajorities.

## The convention nobody has called

Now the second proposal track, the convention.

It was the Founders' insurance policy. Suppose Congress itself became the obstacle. Suppose it refused, say, to propose term limits on its own members. The states could route around it.

George Mason insisted on this track at Philadelphia, and he said why. Congress, he argued, would never propose amendments curbing its own power.

The trigger has never been pulled. But it has been approached. In the early 1980s, the campaign for a federal balanced budget amendment brought state applications within a handful of the 34 needed.

So why are even sympathetic legislators wary? Because Article Five says almost nothing about how a convention would work.

Who picks the delegates? How are votes allocated: one state, one vote, or by population? Must Congress call a convention when the 34 applications address different subjects, or only when they match?

And then the hardest one. Could a convention called for a single topic propose anything it liked?

[pause]

The only precedent is not reassuring to either side. The Philadelphia convention of 1787 was itself convened merely to revise the Articles of Confederation. [beat] It wrote a new government instead.

There is a safety net. Whatever a runaway convention proposed would still need 38 states to ratify.

But that safety net has never been load-tested, and that is exactly the argument opponents make.

> Article Five creates the convention route, and says almost nothing about how it works.

## Deadlines, and where they live

Now, time. How long does a proposal stay open?

Nothing in Article Five mentions time limits. For the Constitution's first 128 years, proposals simply went out and stayed out. That is how Madison's pay amendment could lie dormant from 1789 to 1992.

Congress first attached a deadline in 1917. It wrote a seven-year limit directly into the text of the Eighteenth Amendment, the Prohibition amendment.

A man arrested under the new prohibition law challenged the practice. In 1921, in a case called Dillon v. Gloss, the Supreme Court upheld it. Ratification, the Court reasoned, ought to be "sufficiently contemporaneous" to reflect the will of one generation, and not an accident of accumulation.

Eighteen years later, the Court stepped back from policing time at all. The case was Coleman v. Miller, in 1939. Kansas had ratified a child labor amendment 13 years after its proposal.

The Court held that the timeliness of a ratification is a political question. It is for Congress, not for the courts.

So hold those two holdings together. Congress may set a deadline. And whether a ratification came in time is a question for Congress.

[pause]

That pair left one thing unsettled, and it turns out to matter enormously. Where does Congress put the deadline?

Here is the evidence. Congress has sent 33 proposals to the states. Six were never ratified. And their fates divide on exactly this point.

Four of the six went out with no deadline at all.

One on congressional apportionment, in 1789. One on titles of nobility, in 1810. The Corwin Amendment of 1861, which would have shielded slavery from federal interference. And a child labor amendment, in 1924, which stalled at 28 states.

All four are still pending. The Corwin Amendment is a dead letter, morally and politically. [beat] But it was never withdrawn.

The other two each carried a seven-year deadline. And they carried it in different places.

The D.C. voting rights amendment, proposed in 1978, put the limit inside the amendment's own text. Ratification within seven years was made a condition of the amendment's own validity.

The clock ran out in 1985, with only 16 states. After that, there was nothing left to argue about. It is indisputably dead.

[pause]

The Equal Rights Amendment, proposed in 1972, put its seven-year limit somewhere else: in the introductory clause of the proposing resolution. That is outside the text the states actually ratified.

Does that kind of deadline bind? Can Congress extend it, or repeal it, after the fact? Those questions are unresolved, and the amendment's entire modern story turns on them.

> A deadline in the amendment's text binds. A deadline in the resolution is contested.

Let's put that to work. Congress proposed the Corwin Amendment in 1861, and the D.C. voting rights amendment in 1978.

Which of the two could a state legislature still validly ratify today? Think it through.

[long pause]

The older one. Only the Corwin Amendment.

It carries no deadline, and a proposal without a deadline stays legally pending indefinitely. That is the rule that the 1992 certification of the Twenty-Seventh Amendment confirmed in practice.

The D.C. amendment is the opposite case. Its limit was written into its own text, so it expired in 1985.

## The fifty-year amendment

Now the case that tests all of it.

Here is what the Equal Rights Amendment says.

"Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex."

[pause]

It has been in motion for over a century. Alice Paul's National Woman's Party first had it introduced in 1923. It was reintroduced in every Congress for half a century.

Then it passed the House, 354 to 24. And it passed the Senate, 84 to 8. Margins like those made ratification look like a formality.

It was anything but. Follow the count.

In 1972, Congress proposes, and the proposing resolution sets a seven-year deadline. Hawaii ratifies within hours. About thirty states ratify within two years.

In 1977, Indiana becomes the 35th state. That is three short. [beat] And there the count freezes, because Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign has turned ratification into a contested fight in the remaining legislatures.

In 1978, with the deadline approaching, Congress extends it, to the middle of 1982. Notice how. It acts by simple majorities, not by the two-thirds it used to propose.

By now, four state legislatures have voted to rescind their ratifications.

In 1982, the extended deadline passes. No state has ratified during the extension.

A federal district court holds that the rescissions were valid, and that the extension was unconstitutional. The Supreme Court takes the appeal, and then vacates everything as moot once the deadline expires.

The ERA is widely declared dead.

[pause]

Then, decades later, three more states ratify. Nevada in 2017. Illinois in 2018. And Virginia, in January of 2020, claiming the 38th slot. [beat] Every dormant legal question is forced at once.

The fight moves to certification. In 2020, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel concludes that the deadline was valid, and that the ERA expired.

In December of 2024, the Archivist states that she "cannot legally publish" the amendment. A month later, President Biden voices his view that the ERA has cleared every hurdle. [beat] But he orders nothing, and nothing is certified.

So where does that leave it? As of mid-2026, on one accounting, the ERA was ratified by 38 states. On another, it expired in 1982. On any accounting, it is uncertified.

And the dispute is still open: in Congress, in the courts, and in the scholarship.

> Thirty-eight states have voted to ratify the ERA. It is not in the Constitution.

Look at the questions that story forces. If Congress's deadline was valid, were the late ratifications legal nullities? Was the 1978 extension, passed by simple majorities, valid in the first place?

Or suppose the late ratifications count, because Coleman makes timing a political question for Congress. Then do the four rescissions count too, as those states' latest word?

And the Twenty-Seventh Amendment only goes so far as a precedent. It supports counting very old ratifications. But Madison's proposal had no deadline at all, and that is precisely the distinction the Archivist's office has drawn.

Let's try one more move. Suppose a future Congress passes a joint resolution, by simple majority. It declares the ERA's deadline repealed, and it directs the Archivist to certify the amendment.

Does that settle the matter? What would you say?

[long pause]

It would force the question. [beat] It would not settle it.

Supporters would point to Coleman v. Miller. Timeliness is a political question committed to Congress, and they take that as authorizing exactly this move.

Opponents would answer with two points. A deadline attached by a two-thirds proposing majority cannot be stripped by a simple majority fifty years later. And four states validly rescinded, which leaves the count short of 38 regardless.

Then the courts would have to decide whether Coleman requires them to stay out entirely.

The honest answer is that the text of Article Five resolves none of this. And that is the deepest lesson of the ERA case.

## The hardest constitution to amend

Step back from the cases now. Is the American Constitution unusually hard to amend?

The political scientist Donald Lutz measured it. In a study published in 1994, he compared amendment procedures across the 50 American states and dozens of national constitutions.

The U.S. Constitution came out among the very hardest to amend in the world, with one of the lowest amendment rates.

You already hold the reason. Earlier I gave you the number of states it takes to block an amendment. Can you still name it?

[long pause]

Thirteen. A supermajority stacked on a supermajority.

Lutz found something else as well. What correlates with a constitution's survival is a moderate amendment rate, not a minimal one.

The state constitutions make the contrast vivid. They are amended constantly, often by a simple ballot measure. Alabama's charter of 1901 alone accumulated nearly a thousand amendments.

Lutz's framework also names what follows from setting the dial this stiff.

> When formal amendment is blocked, constitutional change does not stop. It relocates.

Where does it go? To judicial interpretation. The meaning of a phrase like equal protection, or of a word like commerce, stretches and shifts through litigation, instead of through Article Five.

Is that migration a pathology, or a feature? On one view, judges are doing what supermajorities of voters should do. On the other, it insulates fundamentals from transient passions, while letting their application evolve.

That is one of the standing arguments of American constitutional theory.

And it shadows the ERA fight too. Skeptics argue that the courts have already delivered much of what the amendment promised. Supporters answer that rights resting on precedent can be unsettled in a way that text cannot.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The pipeline. Two-thirds of both chambers propose, or a convention called at the request of 34 states. Then 38 states ratify, and the Archivist certifies. The president has no role.

[beat]

Two. It takes 38 states to ratify, and only 13 to block. And one rule sits beyond the machine: no state loses its equal vote in the Senate without its consent.

[beat]

Three. Article Five says nothing about time. Deadlines began in 1917, and it matters where they are written. A deadline in the amendment's text binds. A deadline in the resolution is contested.

[beat]

Four. Six proposals were never ratified. Four had no deadline, and are still pending. The D.C. amendment is dead. And the Equal Rights Amendment is contested: 38 states have voted to ratify it, and it is not in the Constitution.

[beat]

Five. The Constitution is among the hardest in the world to amend. And when formal amendment is blocked, constitutional change does not stop. It relocates, to the courts.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled, and has not tried to settle.

Is the ERA validly ratified? As of mid-2026, that is genuinely undecided. It is actively litigated, and politically contested. Anyone who tells you the answer is obvious, in either direction, is advocating, not reporting.

Nor have we settled whether Article Five's difficulty is set correctly. Lutz gives you the comparative data. The judgment, durable text against democratic responsiveness, is yours to argue.

And one whole category of constitutional change has gone unmentioned: the kind that happens with no amendment at all. Through practice. Through precedent. Through unwritten convention. That is the next lecture.

For now, go back to the number we started with. Thirty-eight states have voted yes. Article Five tells you that 38 is the number. [beat] It does not tell you whether all of those votes still count.
