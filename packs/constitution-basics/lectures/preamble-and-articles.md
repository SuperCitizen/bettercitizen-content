It is June 21, 1788, in Concord, New Hampshire. A state convention is voting on a proposed Constitution.

[beat]

The count comes in at 57 to 47.

Ten votes. And inside that ten-vote margin, the United States government as we know it came into legal existence.

Why that vote? Because Article Seven of the proposed Constitution said that ratification by nine states would be "sufficient for the Establishment of this Constitution." [beat] New Hampshire was the ninth.

[pause]

Now consider what those delegates had been fighting over. It runs about 4,400 words. That is shorter than the terms-of-service agreements you accept without reading.

It is the oldest written national constitution still in operation. And almost everything Americans argue about in politics eventually routes back to some clause of it.

So it is worth knowing, with precision, what is actually in there. Here is the whole inventory.

> One Preamble. Seven Articles. And, so far, 27 amendments.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to say what each of those parts does, and why the amendments are so few. We will take it in four steps.

First, where the document came from: the gamble of 1787.

Second, the Preamble, and what it says the Constitution is for.

Third, the seven articles, and the one clause that holds them together.

And last, the machine for changing it, which once took 202 years to finish a single job.

## The gamble of 1787

Start with the men who wrote the Constitution, because they were not sent to write one.

The drafting happened in Philadelphia, between May and September of 1787. The delegates' formal assignment was much smaller. They were to propose revisions to the Articles of Confederation, the country's weak first charter.

Under that charter, Congress could not effectively tax, could not regulate commerce, and could not compel the states to do much of anything.

The delegates did not revise it. [beat] They drafted a replacement government.

> Sent to revise the old charter, the delegates wrote a new government.

The convention also struck bargains, and three of them protected slavery without ever naming it.

One clause counted "three fifths of all other Persons" toward a state's seats in the House. That enlarged the slaveholding states' weight in Congress and, through the electors, in the choice of presidents.

A second clause barred Congress from ending the importation of enslaved people before 1808.

And a third required that a person "held to Service or Labour," who escaped into another state, be "delivered up."

[pause]

The words slave and slavery appear nowhere in the document of 1787. They entered the Constitution only in 1865, when the Thirteenth Amendment abolished the institution.

[pause]

Then came the harder fight, which was ratification.

The text did not go to the state legislatures. It went to conventions in each state, elected by the people. That was a deliberate choice, to root the new government in the people, and not in the state governments it would partially displace.

The public argument was ferocious. Supporters published 85 essays defending the design, which we now call the Federalist Papers. Opponents, the Anti-Federalists, warned of a distant, consolidated power.

So the Constitution was a gamble twice. The delegates wrote far more than they were sent to write. And then the people's conventions, state by state, had to say yes.

## The Preamble

That is where the document came from. Now open it, and listen to its first three words.

"We the People."

[beat]

Those words were a late edit. The Preamble was drafted at the very end of the convention, by the Committee of Style, in the hand of Gouverneur Morris of Pennsylvania.

An earlier draft had opened another way. It began "We, the People of the States of New-Hampshire, Massachusetts," and it went on to name all thirteen.

Morris cut the list. He wrote "We the People of the United States."

There was a practical reason. No one knew which states would ratify, so listing them was impractical.

But the Anti-Federalist Patrick Henry saw an implication in the change, and he saw it immediately. He demanded to know what right the framers had to say "We, the People" instead of "We, the States."

What had Henry seen? Take a moment.

[long pause]

He had seen a claim about where authority comes from. With the list of states gone, the Constitution declared that its authority flows from a single national people, and not from a compact of states.

[pause]

After those opening words, the Preamble states six purposes. Hear them as a list of jobs.

To form a more perfect union. To establish justice. To insure domestic tranquility. To provide for the common defense. To promote the general welfare. And to secure the blessings of liberty to ourselves and our posterity.

In plainer words, the Constitution exists to hold the country together, to make it just, and to keep the peace at home. It exists to defend the country, to look after the common good, and to keep it free, for that generation and for the ones after it.

Now notice what the Preamble does not do. It grants no power, and it confers no rights.

The Supreme Court said so directly in 1905, in a case called Jacobson v. Massachusetts. The Preamble indicates the Constitution's general purposes, the Court said, but it "has never been regarded as the source of any substantive power."

> The Preamble is a mission statement. It grants no power and confers no rights.

Put another way, the Preamble tells you what the government is for. It does not tell you what the government may do. For that you need the machinery, and the machinery comes next.

## Seven articles

The body of the Constitution is organized with almost diagrammatic logic. You can hold the whole plan in two short sentences.

> Three articles create the branches. Four govern how the pieces relate.

Take the first three.

Article One creates Congress, and it is the longest article by far. One of its sections lists the powers Congress has. Another lists what Congress may not do. A third limits the states.

Article Two creates the presidency. It vests "the executive Power" in one person. It also sets up the system of electors, the oath, the role of commander in chief, and a duty to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed."

Article Three creates the federal judiciary. It establishes one Supreme Court, and it leaves every lower federal court for Congress to create by statute.

Congress, the presidency, the courts. One branch to an article. Political scientists call that arrangement the separation of powers.

[pause]

Now the last four.

Article Four governs relations among the states, in clauses known as full faith and credit, and privileges and immunities. It provides for admitting new states. And it guarantees to every state "a Republican Form of Government."

Article Five is amendment: the procedure for changing the document. We will spend the last part of this lecture inside it.

Article Six is supremacy. It promises that federal debts will be honored. It requires oaths of office. It bars any religious test for office. And it contains the clause that is the keystone of the whole structure. Hold that thought for a moment.

Article Seven is ratification. You met it in Concord. Nine state conventions were enough to launch the Constitution.

[pause]

Run back through that list, and the framers' priorities show.

Article One is first and longest because the legislature was expected to dominate. It was also expected to be the most dangerous branch. That is why it alone is split in two, a House and a Senate, which must separately agree to everything. The word for that arrangement is bicameral.

The articles also interlock. The president can veto Congress's bills. Congress can override, and can impeach. The Senate confirms judges and must consent to treaties. And the courts decide cases under all of it.

That lattice of mutual interference is the system of checks and balances. The phrase appears nowhere in the document, and yet it describes everything about it.

Why build a government to get in its own way? Madison gave the classic answer in Federalist 51.

"Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

[beat]

Paper guarantees alone would never restrain officials. So the structure had to make each power-holder's self-interest a check on the others.

[pause]

Now, the keystone I asked you to hold. It is the second clause of Article Six.

It says that the Constitution, federal laws "made in Pursuance thereof," and treaties are "the supreme Law of the Land."

Then it says who is bound. "The Judges in every State shall be bound thereby." [beat] And that holds whatever a state's own constitution or laws say to the contrary.

That is the Supremacy Clause.

One caution before we use it. The clause does not make Washington supreme in all things. Federal law prevails only where the federal government is acting within its granted powers.

But where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields. And the clause conscripts state judges, specifically, to enforce that result.

> Where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields.

Let's test that. Here is a case.

Your state's constitution guarantees a right. That right directly conflicts with a valid federal statute. The case lands in front of a state judge, who took an oath to uphold the state constitution.

What must the judge do? And which article says so? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The judge must apply the federal statute, and Article Six says so. Its Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law the supreme law of the land, and it binds the judges in every state.

The state oath does not change the answer, because Article Six also requires state officers to swear support for the federal Constitution.

There is one real escape hatch, and it sits upstream. It is to argue that the federal statute was never a valid exercise of federal power in the first place.

## The amendment machine

That is the structure. Now, how do you change it?

The framers knew they were shipping a first version. Article Five is the update mechanism, and it was tuned to a deliberate setting.

> Amending the Constitution was built to be hard, but not impossible.

The framers had seen the other extreme. The Articles of Confederation required unanimity to amend, which meant, in practice, never. So the Constitution lowered the bar to supermajorities, at two stages.

The first stage is proposal, and it can happen two ways. Two-thirds of both the House and the Senate can propose an amendment. Or two-thirds of the state legislatures, 34 of 50, can apply to Congress, which "shall call a Convention for proposing Amendments."

The second stage is ratification, and it also has two modes. Three-fourths of the states must approve, which is 38 of 50. They act through their legislatures, or through one-time state conventions, whichever mode Congress designates.

> Two-thirds to propose. Three-fourths to ratify.

And keep one number with you. It takes 38 states to ratify.

Notice, too, who is missing. The president has no formal role in any of it. The Supreme Court confirmed that as far back as 1798, in a case called Hollingsworth v. Virginia.

Two ways to propose, and two ways to ratify. That makes four possible routes.

Yet in over two centuries of practice, one route has carried every amendment but one: Congress proposes, and state legislatures ratify. The convention route for proposing has never once been used. And the convention mode of ratifying has been used exactly once, in 1933, to repeal Prohibition.

[pause]

So how steep is the climb? The numbers tell you.

Members of Congress have introduced more than 11,000 amendment proposals since 1789, by the Senate's own count. [beat] Only 33 cleared the two-thirds hurdle and went to the states. [beat] And 27 made it through ratification.

Let's put the machine to work. This one takes some working out, so I will go slowly.

An amendment proposal passes the House, 300 to 130. It passes the Senate, 60 to 40. The president announces that she will veto it. Then 37 state legislatures ratify.

Is it part of the Constitution?

[pause 5s]

No. And there are three independent reasons.

First, the Senate vote failed. 60 of 100 is short of the required two-thirds, so the proposal never validly left Congress. The House vote did clear the bar.

Second, the veto is a red herring. The president is irrelevant either way, because Article Five gives the executive no role.

Third, even with a valid proposal, 37 states is one short of the 38 required.

Three reasons, one answer.

## The 202-year amendment

How long can the machine take? [beat] In one case, 202 years.

Here is the story. In September of 1789, the First Congress sent twelve amendments to the states. Ten of them were ratified in 1791, and became the Bill of Rights.

One of the other two was about congressional pay. It barred any pay raise for Congress from taking effect until after the next House election. It stalled at six states, and it was forgotten.

[pause]

Now jump to 1982. A sophomore at the University of Texas, Gregory Watson, is digging through a book for a government paper. He notices that the old pay proposal carried no ratification deadline.

So he argues, in his paper, that it is still legally pending and could still be ratified.

He gets a C.

[beat]

Watson does not let it go. He spends the next decade writing to state legislatures. The politics of the 1980s, thick with public anger over congressional pay raises, do the rest.

State after state ratifies. And on May 7, 1992, Michigan puts it over the line.

You already know which number Michigan had to be. Can you bring it back?

[long pause]

Michigan was the 38th. Three-fourths of fifty states is 38.

So an amendment proposed by James Madison's Congress was certified as part of the Constitution 202 years and seven months after it left the building. It is the Twenty-Seventh Amendment.

And in 2017, the University of Texas formally changed Watson's grade to an A.

[pause]

The episode settled, in practice, a real legal question.

> In practice, an amendment proposal without a deadline stays alive indefinitely.

Congress only began attaching deadlines in the twentieth century. That is why the Equal Rights Amendment is still the subject of legal argument. It was proposed in 1972, with a ratification deadline in its proposing resolution.

Madison's pay amendment had no deadline, so it simply waited out two centuries.

And one of its companions is waiting still. Of the twelve amendments of 1789, ten were ratified by 1791, and one in 1992. The last of the twelve, on congressional apportionment, has never been ratified.

But the First Congress attached no deadline to it either, and the certification in 1992 established that age alone does not kill a proposal. [beat] So it is still legally pending before the states.

Whether ratifications spread across centuries reflect any coherent popular consent is a separate question, and a much-debated one. But the mechanism stays open.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The shape of the document. One Preamble, seven articles, and so far 27 amendments, in about 4,400 words.

[beat]

Two. The Preamble is a mission statement. It speaks for one national people, and it names six purposes. It grants no power and confers no rights.

[beat]

Three. The seven articles. Three create the branches: Article One, Congress. Article Two, the presidency. Article Three, the courts. Four govern how the pieces relate: Article Four, the states. Article Five, amendment. Article Six, supremacy. Article Seven, ratification.

[beat]

Four. The keystone sits in Article Six. Where valid federal law and state law collide, state law yields, and the judges in every state are bound to say so.

[beat]

Five. Amending the Constitution was built to be hard, but not impossible. Two-thirds to propose. Three-fourths to ratify, which means 38 states. And a proposal without a deadline stays alive, as the 202-year amendment showed.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. This lecture has been the map, not the territory dispute. Knowing what the seven articles do does not settle how to interpret them.

Is the Constitution's meaning fixed at ratification, or does it develop over time? That is the argument between originalism and living constitutionalism. It is genuinely contested, and these lectures do not adjudicate it.

Article Five is only half-mapped, too. The next lecture takes the amendment machine apart in full: both tracks, the doctrine of deadlines, and the Equal Rights Amendment's fifty-year journey. The lecture after that maps everything the document does not say.

For now, go back to Concord, and to that ten-vote margin. What those ten votes put into force is about 4,400 words long, and it takes about twenty minutes to read. [beat] Few documents repay the time better.
