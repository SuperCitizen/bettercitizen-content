# What the First Amendment actually protects

The most quoted line in American free-speech argument — "you can't shout fire in a crowded theater" — is not the law, and has not been for more than half a century. It was an aside by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes in *Schenck v. United States* (1919), a case that sent a man to prison for mailing leaflets urging resistance to the World War I draft. The "clear and present danger" standard it illustrated was abandoned by the Supreme Court in 1969, and the result in *Schenck* itself is one almost no modern court would defend. Cite the fire-in-a-theater line today and you are quoting a repudiated test from a case that is now a cautionary tale. The actual law of the [[term:first-amendment]] is both more protective and far more precise — and the place to start is not *what* it protects but *whom it binds*.

## The first five words

The amendment begins: "Congress shall make no law." Through the Fourteenth Amendment, courts have extended that command to every state, county, city, public school, and police department — the incorporation story told in this catalog's [[term:bill-of-rights]] pack. But the actor must be a **government**. This is the **state-action doctrine**, and it is the single most common point of public confusion about free speech.

A private employer that fires you for a political post has not violated your First Amendment rights — you never had any against your employer. A social platform that removes your post or bans your account has not "censored" you in any constitutional sense; in *Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck* (2019) the Supreme Court held that a private entity does not become the state merely by opening a forum for other people's speech. A private university disciplining a student for an op-ed implicates contracts and policies, not the Constitution — while a *public* university doing the same thing raises a genuine First Amendment problem. The roiling debates over [[term:cancel-culture]] are, almost entirely, debates about *private* sanctions: socially consequential, constitutionally invisible.

Two refinements keep the doctrine honest. First, other law can fill the gap — several states protect employees' off-duty political activity by statute, and state constitutions can guarantee more than the federal floor (*PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins*, 1980, upheld California's requirement that a private mall tolerate leafleting under the *state* constitution). Second, a private actor can cross the line when it performs a traditional, exclusive public function or acts under government compulsion — which is exactly where Lesson 3's fight over platforms and government "jawboning" begins.

## A closed list, not a balancing act

Once a government actor is in the picture, the baseline rule is startlingly strong: content-based restrictions on speech are presumptively unconstitutional. The exceptions are a short, historically defined list of **unprotected categories** — and the Supreme Court treats the list as closed. In *United States v. Stevens* (2010), the government asked the Court to recognize a new category (depictions of animal cruelty) whenever the speech's social costs outweigh its value. Chief Justice Roberts, writing for eight justices, refused: the First Amendment does not permit the government to create fresh exceptions through "ad hoc" cost-benefit balancing. The categories exist because history defined them, not because judges weighed them — which is why each one comes with a hard-edged test.

| Category | The test | Source case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incitement | Directed to inciting *imminent* lawless action and *likely* to produce it | *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) |
| True threats | Serious expression of intent to commit violence; the speaker must be at least *reckless* about how the words would be understood | *Virginia v. Black* (2003); *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) |
| Fighting words | Face-to-face insults that tend to provoke an immediate violent response | *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire* (1942) |
| Obscenity | Prurient appeal + patently offensive depiction of sexual conduct defined by law + lacking serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value | *Miller v. California* (1973) |
| Defamation (public officials) | A false statement of fact made with "actual malice" — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth | *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan* (1964) |

The list runs a little longer in full — child sexual abuse material (*New York v. Ferber*, 1982), fraud, and speech integral to criminal conduct round it out — but the table above carries the load-bearing doctrine. Notice what is *not* on it: hate speech, misinformation, blasphemy, offensive opinion. Lesson 2 takes up why.

## Incitement: three words doing all the work

*Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) arose from a Ku Klux Klan rally on a farm in Hamilton County, Ohio — hooded figures, a burning cross, a local television crew invited to film, and a leader's speech threatening "revengeance" if the government continued to "suppress the white, Caucasian race." Ohio convicted him under a 1919 criminal-syndicalism statute. The Supreme Court reversed, unanimously, and replaced fifty years of drift since *Schenck* with a two-part rule: advocacy of force or lawbreaking is protected *unless* it is **directed to** inciting or producing **imminent** lawless action and is **likely** to incite or produce such action.

Each italicized word kills a category of prosecution. *Directed to* requires intent — reckless rhetoric is not enough. *Imminent* means now, not someday: in *Hess v. Indiana* (1973), an anti-war protester's shout that demonstrators would "take the street later" was protected, because "later" is not "imminent." *Likely* makes the state prove the audience was actually poised to act. Abstract advocacy of revolution, even enthusiastic praise of violence, stays protected — the line sits at the moment words become a triggering mechanism for lawless action that is about to happen.

## Threats and fighting words: two narrow doors

**True threats** — serious expressions of intent to commit violence against a person — were never protected; the open question was what the speaker had to be thinking. *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) answered it. Billy Counterman sent a Colorado musician hundreds of unsolicited Facebook messages over two years, some of them menacing; he was convicted under a stalking statute on a purely objective standard — how a reasonable recipient would read the messages. The Supreme Court vacated the conviction: the First Amendment requires proof that the speaker was at least **reckless** — that he consciously disregarded a substantial risk his words would be taken as threats. Justice Kagan's rationale was breathing space: a purely objective standard would chill speakers who fear their words might be misread. The dissenters' worry is worth stating fairly: a subjective filter protects some genuinely frightening stalkers whose self-awareness cannot be proven.

**Fighting words** is the oldest category and today the narrowest. Walter Chaplinsky, a Jehovah's Witness preaching in Rochester, New Hampshire, called the town marshal "a God damned racketeer" and "a damned Fascist"; the Supreme Court unanimously upheld his conviction in 1942, defining fighting words as those "which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace." The definition survives; the convictions do not. The Court has narrowed the doctrine to direct, face-to-face provocations — and it has not upheld a fighting-words conviction since *Chaplinsky* itself. The category is best understood as a museum piece with a live nameplate: cited often, applied almost never.

## Obscenity and defamation: tests with teeth

**Obscenity** is unprotected, but only as *Miller v. California* (1973) defines it, and all three prongs must be met: (1) the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find the work taken as a whole appeals to the prurient interest; (2) the work depicts sexual conduct, specifically defined by the applicable law, in a patently offensive way; and (3) the work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value. The third prong — judged by a reasonable-person standard, not local taste — is why serious art and serious argument stay protected however explicit, and why successful obscenity prosecutions are now rare.

**Defamation** shows the doctrine's civil-rights spine. In 1960, the *New York Times* ran a fundraising advertisement, "Heed Their Rising Voices," describing official violence against the Southern civil-rights movement. It contained minor errors — it said Dr. King had been arrested seven times in Alabama, for instance, when the number was four — and Montgomery commissioner L. B. Sullivan, though never named, won a $500,000 Alabama libel judgment. It was one of a wave of Southern libel suits aimed at pricing the national press out of covering the movement. The Supreme Court reversed, unanimously. *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan* (1964) holds that a public official suing over statements about official conduct must prove **actual malice** — that the statement was made with knowledge it was false or with reckless disregard for whether it was true — because, in Justice Brennan's words, debate on public issues "should be uninhibited, robust, and wide-open," and inevitable error needs breathing space if criticism of power is to survive. Later cases extended actual malice to public *figures*, while private individuals can recover on lesser showings. The rule has modern critics on the Court itself — Justices Thomas and Gorsuch have each called for reconsidering *Sullivan* — which is one reason this pack carries a contested banner.

## What this lesson did not settle

This lesson gave you the architecture: the First Amendment restrains governments, not private actors, and government restrictions on content must fit a short list of historically defined categories, each with a demanding test. It deliberately did not touch the questions that make free speech a live political argument rather than a settled doctrine. Why does the United States protect hate speech that every other major democracy punishes — and who has the better of that argument? Can the government make you *say* something? Is spending money on politics "speech"? Do students and public employees keep these rights on the clock? Those are Lesson 2. And the hardest modern question — what happens when the public square is privately owned, moderated by algorithm, and pressured by officials behind the scenes — is Lesson 3. The tests you just learned are the equipment; the arguments are still ahead.
