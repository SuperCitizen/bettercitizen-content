## The day the app went dark

On January 17, 2025, the Supreme Court unanimously upheld a federal law requiring TikTok's Chinese parent company to sell the platform or see it banned. Two days later the app went dark for its roughly 170 million American users. It came back on, and the law sat unenforced under presidential orders.

- About 170 million American users
- Upheld, then left unenforced

## A square with an owner

The First Amendment was written for governments. The public square is now privately owned.

In 2019 the Court held, five to four, that hosting other people's speech does not make a private entity a state actor. So when a platform labels or bans, no First Amendment claim lies against it.

> When a platform bans a user, no First Amendment claim lies against it.

## Twenty-six words

A 1996 statute, Section 230, built the moderated internet. Its core is twenty-six words: a platform is not to be treated as the publisher or speaker of what someone else posts. A second clause protects the good-faith removal of material a platform finds objectionable.

- Not liable for what users post
- Not liable for taking it down

The author of a post remains fully liable. Nothing in the statute conditions immunity on neutrality.

> Two shields: one for hosting, one for removing.

## Three positions on Section 230

The platform-editorial-rights view argues that without the statute, anyone hosting user speech must moderate and be liable, or host a cesspool. The giants can afford lawyers. Startups cannot.

- Editorial rights: the load-bearing wall

The common-carrier reformer argues that a handful of companies control the effective public square, and should serve all comers without viewpoint discrimination, as telegraphs and phone networks did.

- Common carrier: serve all comers

The accountability reformer argues that the statute was written for passive hosting, and should not cover recommendation engines that push content for engagement.

- Accountability: no immunity for amplification

## Force and pressure

May a state force a platform to carry speech? In Moody v. NetChoice, in 2024, the Court struck down nothing. It vacated the rulings on two state laws and remanded, nine to zero. But the majority said that curating a feed is an expressive choice, like a newspaper editor's.

- Moody, 2024: vacated and remanded
- Feed curation is editorial

And when officials lean on private companies to drop a speaker? In NRA v. Vullo, the same year, all nine justices said a regulator may criticize and persuade, but may not wield threats of legal sanction to suppress advocacy. In a third case, plaintiffs could not trace their suppressed posts to the government.

> Coercion is unconstitutional in principle, and hard to prove in practice.
