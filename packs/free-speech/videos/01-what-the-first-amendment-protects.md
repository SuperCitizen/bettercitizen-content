## The line that is not the law

"You can't shout fire in a crowded theater." It is the most quoted line in American free-speech argument, and it is not the law.

Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote it as an aside in 1919, in a case that sent a man to prison for mailing leaflets against the draft. The Supreme Court abandoned the standard it illustrated in 1969.

- An aside from 1919
- Abandoned in 1969

The actual law starts somewhere else. Not with what the First Amendment protects, but with whom it binds.

## The first five words

The amendment begins: "Congress shall make no law." Courts have extended that command to every state, city, public school and police department. But the actor must be a government.

> The First Amendment restrains governments, not private actors.

A private employer that fires you for a political post has not violated the First Amendment. Neither has a platform that removes your post. A protester arrested by city police because of the message on his sign is a different matter.

- Employer: no First Amendment claim
- Platform: no First Amendment claim
- City police: a potential claim

## A closed list

Once a government is in the picture, the baseline rule is strong. Restrictions based on the content of speech are presumptively unconstitutional. The exceptions are a short list of unprotected categories, each defined by history and each with its own test. Incitement, true threats, fighting words, obscenity and defamation carry most of the load.

- Incitement and true threats
- Fighting words and obscenity
- Defamation

In 2010, in United States v. Stevens, the government asked the Court to recognize a new category whenever the social costs of speech outweigh its value. Eight justices refused.

> The list is closed. Courts may not add to it by weighing costs.

[check q4]

## Three words

Take incitement. In Brandenburg v. Ohio, in 1969, a Ku Klux Klan leader had been convicted for a rally speech. The Supreme Court reversed, unanimously. Advocacy of force or lawbreaking is protected, unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action, and likely to produce it.

- Directed to: intent
- Imminent: now, not someday
- Likely: an audience poised to act

In a later case, a protester shouted that demonstrators would "take the street later." That was protected. Later is not imminent.

> Even praise of violence is protected, until words become a trigger.

## What is not on the list

Hate speech is not on the list. Neither are misinformation, blasphemy or offensive opinion. And the doctrine has critics on the Court itself: two justices have each called for reconsidering the 1964 rule that makes a public official prove actual malice in a defamation suit.

- Not listed: hate speech, misinformation
