March 2006. Albert Snyder is burying his son.

Matthew Snyder was a Marine, twenty years old, killed in Iraq.

A thousand feet from the funeral stand members of the Westboro Baptist Church. They picket military funerals to proclaim that American war deaths are God's punishment for tolerating homosexuality. Their signs say "Thank God for Dead Soldiers."

[pause]

A jury awarded the grieving father millions, for intentional infliction of emotional distress.

In 2011, in Snyder v. Phelps, the Supreme Court took that verdict away. [beat] Eight to one.

What Westboro said, Chief Justice Roberts wrote, "in the whole context of how and where it chose to say it," was entitled to special protection. And that protection, he wrote, "cannot be overcome by a jury finding that the picketing was outrageous."

[pause]

Last time, the doctrine looked clean: a closed list of categories, and a crisp test for each. This lecture is about the cases where that doctrine produces results many thoughtful people find very hard to accept. Each time, those people have included at least one sitting justice.

By the end, you should be able to state the rule in each hard case, and the best objection to it.

Four stops. Hate. Compulsion. Money. And rights on the clock.

## The category that is not there

First stop: hate. Search the closed list from last time, and you will not find hate speech on it.

That absence is a deliberate choice, and two cases show how it is built.

The first begins in June 1990, in St. Paul, Minnesota. Teenagers burned a cross inside the fenced yard of a Black family. The city charged one of them under an ordinance. It banned symbols that a person should know arouse "anger, alarm or resentment in others on the basis of race, color, creed, religion or gender."

The case is R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, decided in 1992. All nine justices agreed that the ordinance was unconstitutional, though they split sharply on why.

Justice Scalia's majority opinion conceded that the burning cross could be punished, as fighting words or as a threat. But not under a law that singled out racial and religious invective, while leaving every other insult legal.

The principle: even inside an unprotected category, government may not pick viewpoints.

Be clear about what fell that day. The teenager could have been prosecuted under ordinary threat and arson laws. What falls is the hate-specific law. [beat] Not the protection of the family.

The second case made the principle explicit. Simon Tam, an Asian-American musician, named his band The Slants, to reclaim the slur. The Patent and Trademark Office refused to register the trademark, under a statute barring trademarks that disparage any person.

In 2017, in Matal v. Tam, the Court ruled for Tam, unanimously. That clause, Justice Alito wrote, "offends a bedrock First Amendment principle: Speech may not be banned on the ground that it expresses ideas that offend."

[pause]

Now put those two cases beside the funeral, and the American rule emerges.

> Hateful speech is punished only when it meets one of the category tests.

Put another way, the law asks whether the speech is a threat, or incitement, or fighting words. It does not ask whether the speech is hateful. [beat] Hatefulness itself never qualifies.

So take a sentence you have heard: "Hate speech is not free speech." As American constitutional law, that is false. As a statement about Canada, Germany, or international human-rights law, it is roughly accurate.

Let's test the rule. Here are four acts. Under current American doctrine, which one can the government punish?

A rally speech praising political violence in the abstract. A viral post describing an ethnic group as vermin. Burning a cross on a specific family's lawn, to terrorize them. An op-ed arguing that a religious minority should be deported.

Take a moment.

[long pause]

The burning cross. Aimed at a particular family, it is intimidation, punishable as a true threat and under ordinary criminal law.

The other three are hateful viewpoints, protected unless they cross a category line. And that is the point of R.A.V. A prosecution must rest on threat or incitement doctrine, not on a law that singles out bigoted messages.

## Who has the better argument?

Which system has it right? That is a contested question, and we will treat it as one.

Start on ground nobody disputes. Nearly every other major democracy made the opposite choice. In 1990, Canada's Supreme Court upheld the crime of wilfully promoting hatred, four votes to three. Germany criminalizes incitement to hatred.

Three positions answer the question. Each gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, and what it points to.

First, the civil-libertarian absolutist. What this position protects is the dissenter and the minority.

Its argument, in its own terms. The fatal question for any hate-speech law is who decides. Definitions of hatred are written by whoever holds power. A government that can punish the Klan's ideas today can punish a liberation movement's tomorrow.

It points to the record. Speech restrictions, it argues, have landed first on dissenters and minorities: sedition prosecutions of antiwar socialists, and Southern officials wielding libel law against the civil-rights press. And it points to other tools: counter-speech, equality law aimed at conduct, and the threat and incitement categories, which already reach genuinely dangerous speech.

Its tradition answers in the words of Justice Brandeis, from 1927. Where there is time to answer falsehood, "the remedy to be applied is more speech, not enforced silence."

> The civil-libertarian absolutist: viewpoint neutrality is the minority's structural shield.

Second, the dignity-and-equality school. What this position protects is equal standing.

Its argument, in its own terms, comes from the political theorist Jeremy Waldron, in a book called The Harm in Hate Speech. A well-ordered society owes each member a visible, public assurance of equal standing. Hate propaganda attacks that assurance. The poster, the burning cross, the viral slur: each tells a target group that the guarantee of equal membership does not cover them. That harms dignity, not merely feelings.

Equal standing, this school argues, is as much a precondition of self-government as free speech is. Call the harm a price of liberty, and you load the cost of free speech onto its most vulnerable beneficiaries.

It points to the record abroad: Canada, Germany and the United Kingdom have punished the worst hate propaganda for decades, without sliding into general censorship. And it points to international human-rights law, which requires prohibiting advocacy of national, racial, or religious hatred. On this view, the American rule is the world's outlier, not its baseline.

> The dignity-and-equality school: hate propaganda attacks the public assurance of equal standing.

Third, the contextual balancer. What this position protects is the boundary: rules that reach harassment and threats without swallowing public debate.

Its argument, in its own terms. Both camps overstate. The real action is at boundaries the categories already draw. Targeted, repeated harassment is punishable. A captive audience, at a funeral or in a workplace, changes the analysis. Threats and incitement are crimes. So the hard question is not whether to create a hate-speech category. It is how existing doctrines apply, such as hostile-environment law and stalking statutes.

It points to outcomes, judged case by case. American law, it argues, protects far less hateful conduct than the absolutist framing suggests. And European law punishes far less opinion than American critics fear.

> The contextual balancer: the labels differ more than the outcomes.

[pause]

What is settled here, and what is not?

Settled: the American refusal of a hate-speech category is firm law.

Not settled: whether that refusal is right. Waldron's challenge stands. The answer on the table is a wager, the civil-libertarian wager that neutrality protects minorities better than dignity statutes would. Whether that wager is right is a contested question, not a settled one.

Now choose the position you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest adherent would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The right not to say it

Second stop: compulsion. The First Amendment has a mirror image, and it is just as strong.

> The government that may not silence you may not script you either.

In 1940, the Court let schools expel Jehovah's Witness children for refusing the flag salute. Expulsions followed, and mob violence against Witnesses.

Three years later, in the middle of a world war, the Court reversed itself. The case is called Barnette, handed down on Flag Day, June 14, 1943. By six votes to three, it held that no schoolchild can be forced to salute.

Justice Jackson's sentence remains the most quoted in the canon. Listen for the image it opens with.

"If there is any fixed star in our constitutional constellation, it is that no official, high or petty, [beat] can prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion."

[pause]

A fixed star. In plainer words: no official may force citizens to salute the flag, or to confess a belief.

The modern line runs straight from Barnette, and in 2023 it reached a case called 303 Creative. The question was whether Colorado's public-accommodations law could compel a website designer to create custom wedding sites, celebrating marriages she rejected on religious grounds. By six votes to three, the Court held that it could not.

Be precise about that case. It was decided under the Free Speech Clause: compelled speech, not religious liberty. And it turned on stipulations that the designer's products were expressive and customized.

States may still require businesses to serve all comers, and to make purely factual commercial disclosures. What they may not do is conscript the creation of a message.

Where does service end, and message begin? That is now the contested frontier.

A state public-accommodations law is applied to two wedding vendors, and both object to a particular wedding. One is a caterer, asked to deliver the same menu she sells everyone. The other is a freelance speechwriter, asked to compose a toast celebrating the couple.

After 303 Creative, who has the stronger compelled-speech claim? Think it through.

[long pause]

The speechwriter. A custom toast is the creation of an expressive message, and that is what 303 Creative says the state cannot compel. Catering a standard menu is non-expressive commercial service, and the Court left public-accommodations laws fully applicable to it.

The dissent's worry deserves a fair statement too. Markets are full of services that are arguably expressive. So the line invites every vendor to recast a refusal of service as a refusal of message.

## Money, briefly

Third stop, and a short one. Is spending money speech?

The doctrine's answer comes from a 1976 case, Buckley v. Valeo. Restricting political spending restricts speech, because mass communication costs money.

Then the opinion split the field. Limits on contributions to candidates were upheld, as guards against quid pro quo corruption. Limits on independent expenditures were struck down, as "direct and substantial restraints on the quantity of political speech."

In 2010, Citizens United extended the expenditure side to corporations and unions, five to four. And notice what it left alone. Direct corporate contributions to candidates stayed banned, as they have been since 1907. Disclosure requirements were upheld, eight to one.

Whether independent spending corrupts, and whether money should count as expression at all, is among the deepest fights in constitutional politics. The campaign-finance series in this catalog carries it in full. Carry only the kernel from here.

> Contributions may be limited. Independent expenditures, by anyone, may not.

## Rights on the clock

Last stop. Two groups hold First Amendment rights against a government that also supervises them every day: public-school students, and public employees.

Students first. Three cases set the boundaries.

Tinker, 1969. Students wore black armbands in school, mourning the Vietnam dead. The Court announced that students do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate."

> Student speech is protected, absent material and substantial disruption.

Morse, 2007. A banner at a school-supervised event. The Court added a narrow exception: a school may restrict speech reasonably viewed as promoting illegal drug use, disruption or not.

Mahanoy, 2021. Brandi Levy, fourteen years old, failed a cheerleading tryout. On a Saturday, from a convenience store, she profanely cursed her school's cheer program on Snapchat. The school suspended her from the team for a year. Eight to one, the Court held that the suspension violated her rights. School authority over off-campus speech is sharply diminished.

In school. At a school event. Off campus. [beat] Hear those three together, and you get a presumption with carve-outs, not a general power.

Public employees get a thinner version of the same settlement.

Pickering, 1968. The Court protected a teacher who was fired for a newspaper letter criticizing his school board's spending. When an employee speaks as a citizen, on a matter of public concern, courts balance the speech against the employer's interest in workplace function.

Garcetti, 2006, five to four. A deputy district attorney wrote an internal memo, flagging misrepresentations in a search-warrant affidavit. He alleged that retaliation followed. The Court held that he had no First Amendment claim. Statements made pursuant to official duties are not citizen speech at all.

Let's test that pair. A public-school teacher writes two documents criticizing her district's budget. One is a memo to her principal, required by her job, flagging budget irregularities. The other is a letter to the local newspaper about the same irregularities, written on her own time.

Which one does the First Amendment protect? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The letter. Under Garcetti, the memo is speech pursuant to official duties, and it gets no First Amendment protection. The letter is citizen speech on a matter of public concern. Pickering protects it, unless the district shows real workplace harm.

The subject matter is identical. The capacity in which she spoke decides the case.

> A public employee's statements pursuant to official duties are not protected citizen speech.

The dissenters in Garcetti called that result perverse. Report wrongdoing through official channels, and you are unprotected. Take it to the press, and Pickering balancing applies. Statutory whistleblower law now does work that the Constitution declines to do.

One closing word, on a category from last time: true threats. Before a conviction, Counterman v. Colorado requires the state to show that the defendant "consciously disregarded a substantial risk that his communications would be viewed as threatening violence." That is recklessness. It protects the rant a jury might misread. The price, which the dissenters named, is sheltering some frightening speakers whose awareness of the risk cannot be proven.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull one thing back. At the first stop, I gave you the American rule on hateful speech in a single sentence. When is hateful speech punished?

[long pause]

Only when it meets one of the category tests. Hatefulness itself never qualifies. In the words of Matal v. Tam, speech may not be banned on the ground that it expresses ideas that offend.

That is the first of five things to keep.

[beat]

Two. Whether that rule is right is contested, three ways. The civil-libertarian absolutist: viewpoint neutrality is the minority's structural shield. The dignity-and-equality school: hate propaganda attacks the public assurance of equal standing. The contextual balancer: the labels differ more than the outcomes.

[beat]

Three. The government that may not silence you may not script you either. Barnette: no official may force citizens to salute the flag or confess a belief. 303 Creative: a state may not compel a designer to create expressive work carrying a message she rejects.

[beat]

Four. Money. Contributions may be limited. Independent expenditures, by anyone, may not. After Citizens United, anyone includes corporations and unions.

[beat]

And five. Rights on the clock. Tinker: student speech is protected, absent material and substantial disruption. Garcetti: a public employee's statements pursuant to official duties are not protected citizen speech. And Counterman: no true-threats conviction without proof of recklessness.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. The argument over hate speech is open. The compelled-speech principle is a fixed star, but where expressive creation ends and ordinary commerce begins is being litigated right now. And money as speech is stable as doctrine, and radioactive as politics.

And every rule in this lecture assumed that the speech regulator was a government. The hardest modern problem inverts that assumption. The forums that now host most American speech are private companies. They are moderated by algorithm, immunized by statute, pressured by officials, and, in one case, ordered sold by Congress.

That is the next lecture.

For now, go back to the funeral. A father. A verdict. And a Court that took the verdict away, eight to one. [beat] You can now state the rule that did that. And you can state the best case on each side of it.
