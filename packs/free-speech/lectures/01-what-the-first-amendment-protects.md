You have heard the line. You may have said it yourself. "You can't shout fire in a crowded theater."

[beat]

It is the most quoted line in American free-speech argument. And it is not the law.

[pause]

It has not been the law for more than half a century.

Here is where the line came from. In 1919, in a case called Schenck v. United States, Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote it as an aside. The case sent a man to prison. What he had done was mail leaflets that urged resistance to the draft in World War One.

The line illustrated a standard called "clear and present danger." In 1969, the Supreme Court abandoned that standard. And the result in Schenck itself is one that almost no modern court would defend.

So anyone who cites fire in a theater today is quoting a repudiated test, from a case that is now a cautionary tale.

[pause]

The actual law of the First Amendment is more protective than that line, and far more precise. By the end of this lecture, you should be able to hear about a speech dispute and ask two questions of it. Who is doing the punishing? And which narrow test, if any, does the speech meet?

We will take it in four steps.

First, whom the amendment binds. That comes before what it protects.

Second, the short list of speech it does not protect, and why that list is closed.

Third, the test for incitement, which hangs on three words.

And last, the other tests on the list: threats, fighting words, obscenity, and defamation.

## The first five words

Start with the first five words of the amendment. "Congress shall make no law."

[beat]

Congress. The amendment names a government. Over time, through the Fourteenth Amendment, courts have extended that command to every state, county and city, to every public school and every police department.

But the actor must be a government. That requirement has a name: the state-action doctrine. And it is the single most common point of public confusion about free speech.

> The First Amendment restrains governments. It does not restrain private actors.

Here is what that means in practice.

A private employer fires you for a political post. Your First Amendment rights have not been violated, because you never had any against your employer.

A social platform removes your post, or bans your account. In any constitutional sense, you have not been censored. In 2019, in a case called Halleck, the Supreme Court said why. A private entity does not become the state merely by opening a forum for other people's speech.

A private university disciplines a student for an op-ed. That raises questions of contract and policy, not of the Constitution. But let a public university do the same thing, and there is a genuine First Amendment problem.

The same op-ed. The same discipline. [beat] What changes the answer is who is doing the punishing.

[pause]

Think about the debates over cancel culture in that light. They are, almost entirely, debates about private sanctions. Socially, those sanctions can be consequential. Constitutionally, they are invisible.

[pause]

Two refinements keep the doctrine honest.

First, other law can fill the gap. Several states protect an employee's off-duty political activity by statute. And a state constitution can guarantee more than the federal floor. A 1980 case called PruneYard upheld California's requirement that a private mall tolerate leafleting, under the state's own constitution.

Second, a private actor can cross the line. That happens when it performs a traditional, exclusive public function, or when it acts under government compulsion. Hold on to that second refinement. It is where the fight over platforms begins, two lectures from now.

Let's test the doctrine. Here are four people. Listen for the one who has a possible First Amendment claim.

An accountant is fired by her firm for a political bumper sticker. A user is banned from a social platform for posting conspiracy theories. A protester is arrested by city police because of the message on his sign. And a student is suspended by a private college for a controversial op-ed.

Which one? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The protester. Only he faces state action: city police, punishing him for the content of his message.

The other three may have claims under a contract, a state statute, or an institution's own policies. Under the Constitution, they have none.

## A closed list

So now a government is in the picture. What is the rule?

The baseline is startlingly strong. When a government restricts speech because of its content, the restriction is presumed to be unconstitutional.

There are exceptions. They form a short list of unprotected categories, each one defined by history. And the Supreme Court treats that list as closed.

Watch what happened when the government tried to open it. The case is United States v. Stevens, from 2010, and the speech at issue was depictions of animal cruelty. The government asked the Court to recognize a new unprotected category whenever the social costs of speech outweigh its value.

Chief Justice Roberts, writing for eight justices, refused. The First Amendment, he wrote, does not permit the government to create fresh exceptions through "ad hoc" balancing of costs and benefits.

> The unprotected categories exist because history defined them, not because judges weighed them.

Put another way, nobody gets to add a category by weighing what some kind of speech costs against what it is worth. The only question is whether the speech falls inside a category that history already defined. And because the categories are fixed, each one comes with a hard-edged test.

Five categories carry most of the load. Incitement. True threats. Fighting words. Obscenity. And defamation.

[pause]

The full list runs a little longer. It also holds child sexual abuse material, fraud, and speech that is integral to criminal conduct.

Now notice what is not on the list. Hate speech. Misinformation. Blasphemy. Offensive opinion. [beat] None of those is a category. The next lecture takes up why.

## Incitement: three words

Take the first category, incitement, and go back to 1969: the year the Court abandoned the standard behind the theater line.

The case is Brandenburg v. Ohio. Picture a farm in Hamilton County, Ohio, and a rally of the Ku Klux Klan. Hooded figures. A burning cross. A local television crew, invited to film. And a leader's speech, threatening what he called "revengeance" if the government continued to "suppress the white, Caucasian race."

Ohio convicted him under a criminal-syndicalism statute. The Supreme Court reversed. [beat] Unanimously.

Fifty years of drift had followed Schenck. In its place, the Court set a rule with two parts. Advocacy of force or lawbreaking is protected, unless it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action, and is likely to incite or produce such action.

[pause]

Three words do all the work there. Directed. Imminent. Likely.

> Incitement must be directed to imminent lawless action, and likely to produce it.

Take the three one at a time, because each of them kills a category of prosecution.

"Directed to" requires intent. Reckless rhetoric is not enough.

"Imminent" means now, not someday.

In a 1973 case, Hess v. Indiana, an anti-war protester shouted that demonstrators would "take the street later." That shout was protected. Later is not imminent.

And "likely" makes the state prove that the audience was actually poised to act.

So abstract advocacy of revolution stays protected, and so does even enthusiastic praise of violence. The line sits at the moment when words become a trigger for lawless action that is about to happen.

Let's put the test to work. It is evening, at a tense rally outside a county jail. A speaker tells the crowd: "Tomorrow night, we should burn this place to the ground."

Is that punishable incitement under Brandenburg? Think it through.

[long pause]

Almost certainly not. Tomorrow defeats imminence, just as later did in Hess. Advocacy of illegal action at some future time remains protected.

Now change the words to "right now," and put an angry crowd in front of the speaker, visibly ready to move. Then all three elements can snap into place: intent, imminence, likelihood.

The test is built so that prosecutors must prove the speech was a trigger. It is not enough to prove that the speech was ugly.

## Threats and fighting words

Two more categories now, and both are narrow doors.

The first is true threats: serious expressions of an intent to commit violence against a person. True threats were never protected. The open question was what the speaker had to be thinking.

In 2023, in Counterman v. Colorado, the Court answered it. Billy Counterman sent a Colorado musician hundreds of unsolicited Facebook messages, over two years, and some of them were menacing. He was convicted under a stalking statute, on a purely objective standard: how a reasonable recipient would understand the messages.

The Supreme Court vacated that conviction. The First Amendment, it held, requires proof that the speaker was at least reckless. Reckless has an exact meaning here. The speaker consciously disregarded a substantial risk that his words would be taken as threats.

> A true-threats conviction requires proof that the speaker was at least reckless.

The justices divided over that rule. Each side had a concern, and each concern deserves a fair statement.

Justice Kagan's rationale, for the Court, was breathing space. A purely objective standard, the Court reasoned, would chill speakers who fear that their words might be misread.

> The Court's concern: speakers chilled by the fear of being misread.

The dissenters saw the rule from the other end. A subjective filter, the dissenters reasoned, protects some genuinely frightening stalkers, whose self-awareness cannot be proven.

> The dissenters' concern: frightening stalkers whose self-awareness cannot be proven.

Decide which of those two concerns moves you less. Then state the best case for it, the way a justice who held it would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

The second narrow door is fighting words. It is the oldest category, and today the narrowest.

Walter Chaplinsky was a Jehovah's Witness, preaching in Rochester, New Hampshire. He called the town marshal "a God damned racketeer" and "a damned Fascist." In 1942, the Supreme Court unanimously upheld his conviction.

The Court defined fighting words as those "which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace."

The definition survives. [beat] The convictions do not. The Court has narrowed the doctrine to direct, face-to-face provocations, and it has not upheld a fighting-words conviction since Chaplinsky itself.

Think of the category as a museum piece. It is cited often, and applied almost never.

## Obscenity and defamation

The last pair. Both come with tests that have teeth.

Obscenity is unprotected, but only as a 1973 case, Miller v. California, defines it. The Miller test has three prongs, and all three must be met.

First, the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find that the work, taken as a whole, appeals to the prurient interest.

Second, the work depicts sexual conduct, specifically defined by the applicable law, in a patently offensive way.

Third, the work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

That is a lot to hold by ear, so here it is in three phrases. Prurient appeal. Patent offensiveness. No serious value.

[pause]

The third prong is judged by a reasonable-person standard, not by local taste. That is why serious art and serious argument stay protected, however explicit. And it is why successful obscenity prosecutions are now rare.

Now defamation, which shows the doctrine's civil-rights spine.

In 1960, the New York Times ran a fundraising advertisement. It was titled "Heed Their Rising Voices," and it described official violence against the civil-rights movement in the South.

The advertisement contained minor errors. It said that Dr. King had been arrested seven times in Alabama. [beat] The number was four.

A Montgomery commissioner, L. B. Sullivan, was never named in it. He won an Alabama libel judgment anyway. [beat] 500,000 dollars. It was one of a wave of Southern libel suits, aimed at pricing the national press out of covering the movement.

In 1964, the Supreme Court reversed, unanimously. The case is New York Times v. Sullivan, and here is its rule. A public official who sues over statements about official conduct must prove something called actual malice.

Actual malice means the statement was made with knowledge that it was false, or with reckless disregard for whether it was true.

> A public official must prove actual malice: a knowing or reckless falsehood.

Why set the bar that high? Justice Brennan gave the reason. Debate on public issues, he wrote, "should be uninhibited, robust, and wide-open." In debate like that, error is inevitable. And error needs breathing space, if criticism of power is to survive.

[pause]

Breathing space. You have heard that phrase once already in this lecture. Where?

[long pause]

In Counterman, the threats case. There, breathing space was for the speaker whose words might be misread. Here, it is for the critic of power who gets a fact wrong. The doctrine reaches for the same phrase both times.

Later cases extended actual malice to public figures. Private individuals can recover on lesser showings.

And the rule has modern critics, on the Court itself. Justices Thomas and Gorsuch have each called for reconsidering Sullivan. That is one reason this series of lectures is marked as contested.

One last test. A newspaper publishes a damaging front-page story about a mayor, and the story turns out to be false. The reporter had relied on a source the paper had used reliably for years, and had no reason to doubt. Can the mayor win a defamation suit? What would you say?

[long pause]

No. Under Sullivan, falsity alone is not enough when the plaintiff is a public official. She must prove actual malice: that the paper knew the story was false, or recklessly disregarded the truth. Good-faith reliance on a trusted source defeats that showing.

But do not overshoot. A false statement about a politician can still be the basis of a lawsuit. A paper that fabricates a story, or publishes one while seriously doubting it, can still lose.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The First Amendment restrains governments. It does not restrain private actors: not your employer, not a platform, not a private university.

[beat]

Two. The unprotected categories exist because history defined them, not because judges weighed them. The list is short, it is closed, and every category on it has a test.

[beat]

Three. Brandenburg, 1969. Incitement must be directed to imminent lawless action, and likely to produce it. Tomorrow is not imminent.

[beat]

Four. Two narrow doors. Counterman: a true-threats conviction requires proof that the speaker was at least reckless. Chaplinsky: fighting words are face-to-face insults that tend to provoke immediate violence, cited often and applied almost never.

[beat]

And five. Two tests with teeth. Miller: obscenity means prurient appeal, patent offensiveness, and no serious value. Sullivan: a public official must prove actual malice, a knowing or reckless falsehood.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. You have the architecture. You do not yet have the questions that make free speech a political argument, and not only a settled doctrine.

Why does the United States protect hate speech that every other major democracy punishes, and who has the better of that argument? That is the first of them. Three more follow it. Whether the government can make you say something. Whether spending money on politics is speech. And whether students and public employees keep these rights on the clock.

All four belong to the next lecture. The hardest modern question waits for the one after that. What happens when the public square is privately owned, moderated by algorithm, and pressured by officials behind the scenes?

For now, go back to the crowded theater. The next time you hear that line, you will know what it is: a repudiated test, from a cautionary tale. [beat] And you will know the two questions to ask in its place. Who is doing the punishing? And which test, on a short and closed list, does the speech meet?

The tests are the equipment. The arguments are still ahead.
