January 19, 2025. Roughly 170 million Americans use an app called TikTok. That day, the app goes dark.

[beat]

Two days earlier, the Supreme Court had unanimously upheld a federal law. The law required TikTok's Chinese parent company to sell the platform, or see it banned.

So the law took effect, and the app went dark. [beat] And then it came back on.

[pause]

The law sat there, unenforced, under successive presidential orders, until a brokered divestiture closed in January 2026.

Every thread of this lecture runs through that episode. A public square that is privately owned. A Congress that can reach it only on certain rationales. An executive with discretion over whether a law is enforced at all. And a First Amendment written for governments, now refereeing fights among governments, companies, and the people who post.

[pause]

Last time, every rule assumed that the speech regulator was a government. Now the square is privately owned. By the end, you should be able to take any fight over a platform and say who is acting on whom. And you should be able to state what each major case decided, and what it left open.

We will take it in four steps.

First, who owns the square, and the two hard questions that follow.

Second, the statute that built the moderated internet, and the three-cornered fight over it.

Third, the Court's answers to those two questions, so far.

And last, the newest cases: a ban, and an age gate.

## The square is privately owned

Every platform dispute begins with the state-action doctrine, from the first lecture. The First Amendment restrains governments, not private actors.

The case to know is Halleck, from 2019. A private nonprofit ran New York's public-access television channels: a forum that the city had created for public speech. Did that make the nonprofit a state actor?

[beat]

By five votes to four, the Court held that it did not.

A private body can count as the state when it performs a public function. Justice Kavanaugh's majority confined that route to functions traditionally and exclusively performed by government. And hosting other people's speech has never been one.

> Hosting other people's speech does not make a company the state.

That holding transfers directly to platforms. A platform may demonetize a post, derank it, label it, or ban the account, and no First Amendment claim lies against it. That is why the steady stream of "platform censorship" suits by suspended users has gone nowhere.

Justice Sotomayor's dissent marks the road not taken. An operator standing in the city's shoes, the dissent argued, should inherit the city's obligations.

[pause]

But Halleck settled only the easy direction. Two harder questions survived it, and the rest of this lecture hangs on them.

One. May the government force platforms to carry speech they want to remove?

Two. When officials pressure platforms to remove speech the government dislikes, does private moderation become state censorship in disguise?

Forced to carry. Pressured to remove. [beat] Hold on to that pair.

## Twenty-six words

Before either question, you need the statute that built the moderated internet.

Go back to the early 1990s, and to two companies. CompuServe did not police its forums. It was treated like a newsstand, and escaped liability for its users' posts.

Prodigy advertised family-friendly moderation. In 1995, a New York trial court held that Prodigy was the publisher of a user's defamatory post. It was exposed to ruinous damages, precisely because it moderated.

[pause]

Leave your forums alone, and you are safe. Clean them up, and you can be sued. [beat] That trap is called the moderator's dilemma.

Congress responded in 1996, with Section 230. Two of its provisions matter here.

The first is twenty-six words long. Listen for the word publisher.

"No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker [beat] of any information provided by another information content provider."

[pause]

In plainer words: a platform is not the publisher of what its users post.

The second provision adds that providers are not liable for removing, in good faith, material they consider objectionable, "whether or not such material is constitutionally protected."

Two shields, then, and one design.

> Platforms are not liable for what users post, or for taking it down.

The heading over those provisions speaks of "Good Samaritan" blocking and screening of offensive material. Congress was encouraging moderation. It was not asking for neutrality.

Now two things the statute is not.

It is not the First Amendment. Congress could amend it tomorrow.

And it is not unconditional. Federal criminal law and intellectual-property law sit outside it. And the Supreme Court has never decided how it applies to a platform's own algorithmic recommendations.

Let's test the two shields. A user posts a defamatory lie about you on a social network. Under Section 230, whom can you successfully sue for defamation? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The user who wrote it. The author remains fully liable. Your claim against the platform fails, because the first shield bars treating the platform as the publisher or speaker of another person's content.

And clear away the most common myth in this debate: that a platform which moderates unevenly loses its protection. Nothing in Section 230 conditions immunity on neutrality. The statute was written to encourage moderation, and its Good Samaritan clause protects takedowns of even constitutionally protected material.

## Three corners

Should those twenty-six words be changed? That is a contested question, and we will treat it as one.

Start on ground nobody owns. Section 230 is that rare statute attacked from opposite flanks at once, for opposite reasons. So the debate has three corners.

Each position gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, and what it points to.

First, the platform-editorial-rights view. What this position protects is the host: forums, review sites, small newspapers with comment sections.

Its argument, in its own terms. Section 230 is the internet's load-bearing wall. Without it, every host faces the moderator's dilemma again: moderate and be liable as a publisher, or host a cesspool. Repeal, it argues, would produce both over-removal of lawful speech, to manage legal risk, and unmoderated sludge. The giants can afford lawyers either way. Startups cannot.

It points past the statute, to the First Amendment. After a 2024 case called Moody, curation is protected editorial judgment. So most reforms aimed at forcing carriage, or punishing moderation choices, would fail even without the statute.

> The platform-editorial-rights view: Section 230 is the internet's load-bearing wall.

Second, the common-carrier reformer. What this position protects is the citizen, speaking in a square that a handful of companies control.

Its argument, in its own terms. Those companies moderate with an ideological lean that their critics consider demonstrable. The common law had an answer to private chokepoints such as telegraphs, railroads and phone networks. It was common carriage: serve all comers, without viewpoint discrimination. And Section 230, it argues, compounds the problem. It hands platforms a subsidy newspapers never had, immunity for what they carry, while they exercise a newspaper's editorial control over what citizens may say.

It points to a justice, and to a state. Justice Thomas floated the common-carrier analogy in a 2021 concurrence. And Texas wrote it into law.

> The common-carrier reformer: pick one. Editor with liability, or carrier with immunity.

Third, the accountability reformer. What this position protects is the person harmed by content that defames, defrauds or endangers.

Its argument, in its own terms. The problem is not that platforms remove too much. It is that the statute shields a business model. The first shield was written for passive hosting. Yet courts extended it to amplification: recommendation engines that select, rank and push content for engagement. No other industry, it argues, enjoys blanket immunity for harms its own design choices foreseeably cause.

It points to the statute's own bargain: immunity in exchange for cleanup. The duty side needs teeth. So the reform is recalibration, not repeal. Keep hosting immunity, and carve out algorithmic promotion and paid content. Then victims of fraud or coordinated harassment have someone to sue.

> The accountability reformer: keep immunity for hosting, and carve out amplification.

[pause]

What is settled, and what is not? Settled: what the statute says, and that Congress could amend it tomorrow. Not settled: whether Congress should, and in which direction. Reform bills keep arriving from both flanks, and they keep dying.

Now choose the position you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest adherent would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Forced to carry

Now the first of our two hard questions. May a government force a platform to carry speech?

In 2021, after major platforms banned President Trump following January 6, Florida and Texas passed laws restricting how large platforms moderate. The Texas law flatly barred removal based on viewpoint. That is common carriage, by statute. The platforms' trade group, NetChoice, challenged both laws on their face, and the Supreme Court took both.

The decision, in 2024, is Moody v. NetChoice. It is easy to overread, so let's state it carefully, in two parts.

Part one is what the Court did. It was unanimous only in this: it vacated both lower-court decisions, and it remanded. It sent the cases back. A challenge to a law on its face requires analyzing all of the law's applications, and no court had done that.

Part two is what the majority said. Justice Kagan's opinion laid down the framework that controls when the cases go back. When platforms decide which third-party content to display, and how to order it, they are making expressive choices, like a newspaper editor or a parade organizer. And a state's desire to balance the marketplace of ideas is not a legitimate interest that can override them.

> Under Moody, curating a feed is protected editorial activity.

So forcing speech onto a curated feed is presumptively unconstitutional. Yet the laws themselves are still in litigation, and the framework's edges are open. Direct messages, marketplaces, search, and feeds ranked purely by algorithm were all left for another day.

Say it the careful way. Moody remanded. [beat] It struck down nothing. Keeping that straight is the difference between citing the case and misciting it.

## Pressured to remove

Now the second question, the inverse of the first. Here, officials do not order a platform to remove speech. They lean on it. That is jawboning: pressure without process.

The case to know is NRA v. Vullo, decided unanimously in 2024. After the Parkland shooting, New York's chief financial regulator pressed banks and insurers to drop the National Rifle Association. The regulator paired public "guidance letters" with the quiet leverage of pending enforcement actions.

Justice Sotomayor wrote for all nine justices that the NRA had plausibly alleged a First Amendment violation. A regulator may criticize, advocate and persuade. A regulator may not wield threats of legal sanction against third parties "to punish or suppress" a speaker's advocacy.

> Officials may persuade. They may not coerce.

That is the principle. A second case from the same term shows how hard a violation is to prove.

In Murthy v. Missouri, two states and five individuals alleged that pressure from the White House, the CDC and the FBI, during the COVID era, got their posts suppressed. The Court never reached the merits: whether that pressure was coercion. The plaintiffs could not trace specific moderation decisions to government conduct, as opposed to the platforms' own independently developed policies. So, Justice Barrett wrote for six justices, they lacked standing.

[pause]

Put the pair together, and you have the current map. Coercion is unconstitutional in principle. It is nearly unreachable in practice, without proof of causation.

And where persuasion ends and coercion begins is decided case by case: tone, regulatory power, threats, consequences.

Let's test that line. Two messages reach the same platform.

A state attorney general writes: "Posts falsely claiming our election was rigged are spreading. Remove them within 30 days, or this office will open a consumer-fraud investigation into your company."

Separately, a city health department emails a list of viral posts it considers false, "flagged for whatever review you deem appropriate."

Which message crosses the constitutional line? Think it through.

[long pause]

The attorney general's. Under Vullo, three things combine there: regulatory authority, a demand aimed at specific viewpoints, and an explicit threat of legal consequences. That is coercion: the state punishing speech through an intermediary.

The health department's email is government speech. Flagging, informing and persuading are permitted, and the platform remains free to ignore it.

The hard cases sit between those poles. Repeated "requests" from an agency that regulates you carry implicit weight, even without a stated threat.

## A ban, and an age gate

Last stop: the newest cases. Each rewards precision about what was actually held.

Go back to TikTok, and to the law the Court upheld in January 2025. Here is the key move. The Court treated the law as content neutral on its face.

> The TikTok law targets foreign-adversary control and data collection, not any message.

Because the law was content neutral, the Court applied the less demanding of two tests. It is called intermediate scrutiny, and the more demanding one is called strict scrutiny. Under intermediate scrutiny, the interest in data security carried the law.

Notice what the Court declined to do. The government had a separate worry, about covert manipulation of content. The Court pointedly declined to rely on it, because that rationale would have raised content-based problems.

You know what followed. Successive executive orders directed non-enforcement of a law the Court had just upheld. That use of enforcement discretion was debated across the political spectrum.

Now the age gate. In June 2025, in Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton, the Court upheld a Texas law, six to three. It covers commercial websites whose content is more than one-third sexual material "harmful to minors," and it requires them to verify users' ages.

Justice Thomas's majority held that the law triggers only intermediate scrutiny. Minors have no right to the material. Adults retain access. And proving age is an incidental burden. Adults, the Court said, have no First Amendment right to avoid age verification.

Justice Kagan's dissent, for three justices, objected that a content-based burden on adults' protected speech has always demanded strict scrutiny.

The open question is the holding's reach. A wave of state age-gating laws is now being measured against it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The square is privately owned. Hosting other people's speech does not make a company the state. So a suspended user has no First Amendment claim against the platform.

[beat]

Two. Section 230. Pull this one back yourself. I called its two provisions two shields. What are the two things a platform is not liable for?

[long pause]

What its users post. And taking it down. There is no neutrality condition. And Congress could amend the statute tomorrow.

[beat]

Three. The fight over that statute has three corners. The platform-editorial-rights view: Section 230 is the internet's load-bearing wall. The common-carrier reformer: pick one, editor with liability, or carrier with immunity. The accountability reformer: keep immunity for hosting, and carve out amplification.

[beat]

Four. The two hard questions. Forced to carry: under Moody, curating a feed is protected editorial activity. But Moody remanded, and struck down nothing. Pressured to remove: officials may persuade, and they may not coerce. Vullo gives the principle. Murthy, decided on standing, shows how hard it is to prove.

[beat]

And five. The ban and the age gate were both upheld under intermediate scrutiny. The TikTok law targets foreign-adversary control and data collection, not any message. And under the Texas law, proving age is an incidental burden on adults.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. [beat] Almost everything. And that is the reason to study it now.

Nobody knows how Moody's framework treats feeds ranked by machine-learning systems that no human curates. Whether Section 230's first shield covers the recommendation engine itself remains open. The jawboning cases leave a clear principle, with no workable path for proving it. And a case now on appeal asks whether the First Amendment disciplines immigration enforcement at all.

[pause]

The first lecture gave you the doctrine's architecture. The second gave you its hardest trade-offs. This last one ends where the law actually is, in mid-2026: unfinished, and contested by serious people in every direction.

So go back to that January weekend. An app goes dark, and comes back on. A Court upholds a law, and presidential orders leave it unenforced. [beat] You can now say what each of them was doing, and on what ground.

The law of the modern square is being written in real time: by courts, by Congress, by platforms, [beat] and by the citizens who keep posting.
