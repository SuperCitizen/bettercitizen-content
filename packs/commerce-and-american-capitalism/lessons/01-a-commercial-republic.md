# A commercial republic

What kind of economy should the United States have? That question was contested at the founding and has echoed ever since. This lesson follows the early debate over commerce, the constitutional and banking institutions that knit a national market together, the market revolution that transformed everyday economic life, and the rise of giant corporations that prompted the first federal antitrust law.

## Hamilton versus Jefferson

Eighteenth-century thinking about commerce drew on Adam Smith, whose Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that prosperity grew from the division of labor, free exchange, and the "invisible hand" of self-interested actors coordinating through markets. Smith was an intellectual backdrop, not a blueprint, but the founders read him closely.

Two visions of the American economy emerged. Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton wanted an active, commercial, and increasingly industrial nation. In a series of reports to Congress — on public credit, on a national bank, and his Report on the Subject of Manufactures (1791) — he urged the federal government to fund the national debt, charter a bank, encourage manufacturing, and bind merchants and creditors to the new government's success. He believed commerce, credit, and manufacturing were the foundations of national strength.

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison feared this program. They worried that banks, public debt, and concentrated wealth would corrupt republican government and create a moneyed aristocracy. Jefferson idealized a republic of independent farmers — virtuous, self-sufficient, and tied to the land. The dispute was not only economic; it was about what sort of citizens and what sort of liberty the republic should cultivate. These clashing visions helped give rise to the first political parties.

## A national market under the Constitution

The Constitution itself reflected a commercial purpose. Under the Articles of Confederation, states had erected tariffs and trade barriers against one another and issued unstable currencies. The Constitution's Commerce Clause (Article I, Section 8) gave Congress power "to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States," while other clauses gave Congress authority over currency, bankruptcy, and weights and measures, and barred states from coining money or taxing interstate trade. Together these provisions aimed to create a single national market — a free-trade zone across the states.

The Supreme Court reinforced that design. In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), Chief Justice John Marshall read the commerce power broadly, striking down a state-granted steamboat monopoly and affirming federal authority over interstate trade. Over the next two centuries the Commerce Clause would become one of the most important sources of federal economic power.

## Banks of the United States

Hamilton's bank was central to his plan. Congress chartered the First Bank of the United States in 1791, over Jefferson's and Madison's objection that the Constitution granted no such power. Hamilton's "implied powers" argument — that a national bank was a proper means to carry out enumerated powers like taxing and borrowing — prevailed, and President Washington signed the charter. The Bank held federal deposits, made loans, and helped manage the currency. Its twenty-year charter lapsed in 1811.

A Second Bank of the United States was chartered in 1816. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), the Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality and the doctrine of implied powers. But the Bank became a political flashpoint. President Andrew Jackson, distrusting concentrated financial power, vetoed its recharter in 1832 and withdrew federal deposits. After its federal charter expired in 1836, the United States went without a central bank for nearly eighty years — a gap that left the country vulnerable to recurring financial panics.

## The market revolution

In the first half of the nineteenth century, the United States underwent what historians call the market revolution. Canals such as the Erie (1825), then steamboats and railroads, slashed the cost of moving goods and tied distant regions into integrated markets. Farmers who had grown food mainly for their own households increasingly produced cash crops for sale; artisans gave way to wage work; and banks, credit, and corporations spread. More Americans now bought and sold in markets that reached far beyond their localities. The change brought rising output and new opportunity, but also unfamiliar dependence on prices, creditors, and distant buyers.

## Big business, trusts, and the Sherman Act

After the Civil War, as historian Richard White chronicles in The Republic for Which It Stands, the scale of enterprise exploded. Railroads, steel, and oil gave rise to enormous corporations. Industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie in steel and John D. Rockefeller in oil built dominant firms, and Rockefeller's Standard Oil pioneered the "trust" — a device that placed competing companies under unified control to limit competition and stabilize prices.

These concentrations of economic power alarmed farmers, small businesses, and reformers, who feared that monopolies were squeezing consumers and corrupting politics. Congress responded with the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, which declared illegal "every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade" and barred monopolization. Enforcement was weak at first, and courts initially read the law narrowly, but the Sherman Act established a lasting principle: that the federal government could police the structure of markets in the name of competition. It set the stage for the more activist regulatory state examined in the next lesson.
