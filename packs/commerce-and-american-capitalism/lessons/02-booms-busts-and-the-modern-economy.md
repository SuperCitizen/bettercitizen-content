# Booms, busts, and the modern economy

Capitalist economies do not grow in a straight line. They expand and contract in a recurring pattern, and the United States has repeatedly built new institutions in response to the worst downturns. This lesson follows that history: the business cycle and the panics that prompted the Federal Reserve, the Great Depression and the New Deal, the postwar order, and the shifts of the late twentieth century that produced today's mixed economy.

## The business cycle and the panics

The business cycle is the alternation of expansion and contraction — booms followed by recessions or depressions. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the United States suffered severe financial panics roughly every couple of decades: 1819, 1837, 1857, 1873, 1893, and 1907. Banks failed, credit froze, businesses collapsed, and unemployment spiked. Without a central bank after 1836, there was no institution that could act as a "lender of last resort" to supply emergency cash and stop bank runs from cascading. The Panic of 1907 was halted only by the private intervention of financier J. P. Morgan and his allies — a vivid demonstration, to many in Congress, that the country could not safely rely on a private banker to rescue the system.

## Creating the Federal Reserve

In response, Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, creating the nation's third central bank and its first to endure. The Federal Reserve System was designed as a compromise: twelve regional Reserve Banks coordinated by a board in Washington, blending public and private features to ease fears of concentrated power. The Fed was meant to provide an "elastic currency," act as lender of last resort, and supervise banks. Its powers and goals would expand over the following century, but the Fed has stood ever since as the central manager of money and credit in the United States.

## The Crash and the Great Depression

The 1920s brought a roaring boom — rising stock prices, new consumer goods bought on installment credit, and widespread speculation. In October 1929 the stock market crashed. The crash was followed by the Great Depression, the deepest economic collapse in the nation's history: by 1933 roughly a quarter of the workforce was unemployed, thousands of banks had failed, and output had fallen by nearly a third. Economists still debate the precise causes, but bank failures, a contracting money supply, collapsing demand, and global financial strains all played roles.

## The New Deal regulatory state

President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal responded with an unprecedented wave of federal action that reshaped the relationship between government and the economy. Several landmark measures created lasting institutions:

- The Banking Act of 1933, known as Glass-Steagall, separated commercial banking from investment banking and created federal deposit insurance through the FDIC, which guaranteed ordinary bank deposits and largely ended panic-driven bank runs.
- The Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 required honest disclosure to investors and created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to regulate stock and bond markets.
- The Social Security Act of 1935 established old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, building a federal social-insurance system.

Together these reforms created a regulated, "managed" form of capitalism. They were contested in their day and remain debated, but they established a durable framework of financial regulation and social insurance.

## The postwar order and Bretton Woods

The United States emerged from World War II as the dominant economic power. In 1944, delegates from the Allied nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, and designed a postwar international monetary system: currencies pegged to the U.S. dollar, which was in turn convertible to gold, alongside new institutions, the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. The decades after the war brought a long boom — rising productivity, growing wages, expanding home ownership, and a large middle class. The Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates lasted until 1971, when the United States ended the dollar's convertibility to gold and the major currencies eventually moved to floating exchange rates.

## Stagflation, deregulation, and the mixed economy

The 1970s strained the postwar order. The economy suffered "stagflation" — high inflation and high unemployment together — which the prevailing economic theories had not expected. Oil shocks, the end of fixed exchange rates, and other pressures contributed. In response, the Federal Reserve under Chairman Paul Volcker raised interest rates sharply around 1979-81 to break inflation, at the cost of a severe recession.

The late 1970s and the 1980s also brought a wave of deregulation across industries such as airlines, trucking, and finance, and renewed emphasis among many policymakers on markets and tax reduction. Glass-Steagall's separation of banking activities was gradually loosened and then largely repealed in 1999. How far government should regulate markets, manage the money supply, tax, and provide social insurance remains genuinely debated, with serious arguments on multiple sides.

The result is what economists call a mixed economy: private markets and private enterprise remain the engine of activity, but they operate within a framework of central banking, financial regulation, antitrust law, taxation, and social-insurance programs. The institutions traced in these two lessons — the national market, the Federal Reserve, the New Deal regulatory agencies — continue to shape how American capitalism works today.
