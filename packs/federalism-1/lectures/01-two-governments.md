Picture two friends who are both fourteen years old. One of them lives in South Dakota, and the other lives in New Jersey.

The friend in South Dakota can already get a learner's permit. That is a card that lets a new driver practice, with an adult in the car.

The friend in New Jersey has to wait. [beat] Two more years, until the age of sixteen.

[pause]

Both friends are the same age, and both live in the same country. So why are their rules different?

[long pause]

The reason is that each state writes its own driving laws. Lawmakers in South Dakota chose one age, and lawmakers in New Jersey chose another.

Driving is only one example. Each state also decides how its schools are run, what it will tax, and how it punishes most crimes. When you cross a state line, some of the rules around you change.

Today we will find out why. We will take three steps. First, the two governments you live under. Second, why the country was set up this way. And third, a picture from your own school, and the one place where that picture goes wrong.

## Two governments at once

Some countries have a single government, and it makes the rules for everyone in the country. The United States works in a different way.

It has one national government, which meets in Washington, D.C. It also has fifty state governments, one for each state. And every one of them makes laws, collects taxes, and runs its own courts.

Think about what that means for you.

> Every American lives under two governments at the same time.

You are a citizen of the United States. You are also a citizen of the state where you live. [beat] Both, at once.

This way of sharing power has a name. It is called federalism. In a federal system, a national government and a group of smaller governments each have real power of their own.

Let me say that again, because everything else rests on it.

> Federalism means power is shared by a national government and the states.

You meet both governments every day, often without noticing which one is which. So let's walk through one ordinary day.

The money in your pocket is made by the national government. The mail is carried by the national government. And the army and the navy are run by the national government.

Now the other side. Your state government gives out licenses to drive. It sets up the public schools. And it writes most of the traffic laws.

[pause]

Let's check the new word. One person says that federalism means the national government holds all of the power. Another person says it means the fifty states hold all of the power. Which of them has it right?

[long pause]

Neither one. In a federal system, power is split. The national government has some jobs, and the states have others. Neither one holds all of the power.

## Why was it set up this way?

That is how it works. Now the why. And the why begins with a surprise.

The states are older than the national government.

[beat]

When Americans declared their independence, in 1776, there were thirteen states, and each one governed itself.

Their first plan for working together was called the Articles of Confederation. That plan left almost all of the power with the states. The national government could ask the states for money, [beat] but it could not make them pay.

The plan turned out to be too weak.

So in 1787, leaders from the states met in Philadelphia and wrote a new plan. We call that plan the Constitution. They wanted a national government that was strong enough to do its work.

They did not want to erase the states, though. Many Americans trusted their own state, and they feared a faraway government with too much power.

So what did the writers do?

[beat]

> The writers split the power in two.

They gave certain jobs to the new national government. And they left the rest with the states.

Let's put that to work. A family moves from South Dakota to New Jersey. Think of one rule that could change for them. Then think of one thing that will stay the same.

[long pause]

The age for a learner's permit will change, because each state sets that rule for itself. Other state rules could change too, such as taxes, and the rules for schools.

And what stays the same? The family will still use the same money, and they will get mail in the same way. They are still citizens of the United States. The national government's laws go with them.

## Is it like a school?

Here is a picture that may help. Think about your own school.

Some rules are the same in every room, such as where to walk during a fire drill. Other rules belong to one teacher, such as where to hand in your homework.

Federalism is a little like that. Some laws cover the whole country, and other laws cover only one state.

But the picture goes wrong in one important place.

A principal is the boss of the teachers, and a principal can change any classroom rule. The national government is not the boss of the states in that way.

[pause]

The Constitution gives each side powers of its own.

> Neither side can just take the other's powers away.

Put another way, a state is not a classroom in someone else's school. Its powers are its own. And the national government's powers are its own too.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. You live under two governments at the same time. One is the national government, and the other is your state. That is why the age for a learner's permit is a rule each state chooses for itself.

[beat]

Two. This way of sharing power has a name. Can you still say it?

[long pause]

Federalism. It means power is shared by a national government and the states.

[beat]

Three. The states came first. Their first plan, the Articles of Confederation, left the national government too weak. So in 1787, the Constitution split the power in two.

[beat]

Four. The national government is not the boss of the states, the way a principal is the boss of teachers. Neither side can just take the other's powers away.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. Splitting power sounds simple, but it leaves a hard question behind. For any one job, which government should be in charge?

Americans have argued about that question since 1787, and they are still arguing about it today.

The next lecture shows how the Constitution tries to answer it.

For now, think back to the two friends. Both are fourteen. One can practice driving, and one has to wait. [beat] They share one country. But each of their states wrote its own rule.
