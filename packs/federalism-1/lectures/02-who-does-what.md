Put a stamp on a letter in Maine. That letter can travel all the way to Hawaii.

Now, how many new stamps does it need along the way, as it crosses into one state after another?

[beat]

None. One stamp is enough.

[pause]

That works because the mail belongs to the national government. The Constitution gives Congress the power "To establish Post Offices." One government runs the mail for the whole country.

Money works the same way. A dollar is good in all fifty states. That is because the Constitution lets Congress "coin Money," and says that no state may make its own.

Imagine a trip across the country if every state used different money.

[pause]

Last time, we were left with a hard question. For any one job, which government should be in charge?

The mail and money are two jobs that were handed to the national government. [beat] So who has the rest?

We will sort the jobs, in four steps. First, jobs for the national government. Second, jobs for the states. Third, jobs they share. And last, what happens when two laws disagree.

## Jobs for the national government

The Constitution includes a list of the things Congress may do. Here are five jobs from that list.

Congress may declare war, and keep an army and a navy. It may coin money. It may set up post offices.

It may make rules for trade, between the states and with other countries. And it may decide how a person from another country becomes a citizen.

[pause]

These jobs have something in common. The writers thought each one would work best if the whole country did it in a single way. One kind of money. One stamp.

But the list does a second thing. [beat] It also sets a limit.

> The national government may use only the powers the Constitution gives it.

In plainer words, it cannot do whatever it likes. The list says what it may do. And so the list also says where it must stop.

Hold on to that limit. We will need it again.

## Jobs for the states

Now the states. You might expect a second list here. [beat] There is none.

For the states, the Constitution uses one sentence instead. That sentence is the Tenth Amendment.

> The national government has a list. The states have one sentence.

In plain words, here is what that sentence says. Any power that was not given to the national government, and was not taken away from the states, belongs to the states or to the people.

[pause]

Let me say it once more. Set aside the powers that were given to the national government. Set aside the powers that were taken away from the states. [beat] Whatever is left belongs to the states, or to the people.

Let's test that sentence. The Constitution says nothing about who runs the public schools. So under the Tenth Amendment, who has that power?

[long pause]

The states, or the people. Running schools is not on the national list, so it is left to the states.

That one sentence covers a great deal more. States give out licenses to drive, and they write most of the laws about crime. They decide who may work as a doctor or a nurse. And they create every city, town and county inside their borders.

That last job reaches your own street. You probably have a third government too, such as a city, a town or a county. It may run the library, fix the streets, and send out the fire trucks.

The Constitution does not mention cities or counties at all. They are created by the states, and they get their powers from the state. So your local government belongs to the states' side of the split.

Now for the other half of the Tenth Amendment. What is a power that was taken away from the states? You heard one at the very start, so think back to the dollar. A state decides to print its own dollar bills. Is that allowed?

[long pause]

No. The Constitution gives Congress the power to coin money, and it also says that no state may do so. The writers wanted one kind of money for the whole country.

This is one of the places where the Constitution tells the states exactly what they cannot do.

## Jobs they share

There is a third group. Some powers belong to both governments at once.

Both can collect taxes, and both can borrow money. Both build roads, and both set up courts. Both make laws, and both punish the people who break them.

That is why a grown-up may pay taxes to the national government, and to a state, in the same year.

That makes three groups.

> Some jobs are national. Some belong to the states. And some are shared.

Let's sort four jobs into those groups. Making a treaty with another country. Giving out a license to drive. Collecting taxes. And fixing a city street. Where does each one belong?

[pause 5s]

Making a treaty belongs only to the national government. Giving out a license to drive belongs to the states. Collecting taxes belongs to both. And fixing a city street belongs to a local government, one that a state created.

## When two laws disagree

One problem is left. Sometimes a national law and a state law say opposite things. Which one wins?

The Constitution has a rule for that problem. It calls itself, and the national laws made under it, "the supreme Law of the Land."

[pause]

The supreme law. In plainer words, the highest law. Judges in every state must follow it, and this rule is known as the Supremacy Clause.

So when the two laws truly clash, [beat] the national law wins.

There is a catch, though, and it takes us back to the list.

> A national law counts only if the Constitution gave the power to make it.

Remember the limit. The national government may use only the powers the Constitution gives it. So if Congress reaches past its list, a court can strike the law down. Then the law no longer counts, and it cannot win.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The national government has a list of jobs, such as the mail, money, war and treaties. It may use only the powers the Constitution gives it.

[beat]

Two. The states have one sentence, the Tenth Amendment. Any power not given to the national government, and not taken away from the states, belongs to the states or to the people. That covers schools, licenses to drive, and every city and county.

[beat]

Three. Some jobs are shared. Both governments collect taxes, build roads, and set up courts.

[beat]

Four. When a national law and a state law truly clash, the national law wins. That is the Supremacy Clause. But a national law counts only if the Constitution gave the power to make it.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. Our three groups sound tidy. In real life the edges are fuzzy, and people often disagree about where a job belongs.

The next lecture tells the story of one of those fights.

For now, think of that letter once more, on its way from Maine to Hawaii. [beat] One stamp is enough, because the mail is a job for one government. The Constitution put it on the list.
