You already know the difference between two things. Sometimes a person in charge tells you to do something. Other times, they say that if you do it, you will get something for it.

[beat]

The first is an order. The second is an offer. [beat] And with an offer, you can still say no.

[pause]

Hold on to those two words. A fight between one state and the national government turned on them.

Last time, we said that people often disagree about where a job belongs. Here is one of those fights.

In the early 1980s, the states did not agree about alcohol. In South Dakota, a person who was nineteen could buy a weak kind of beer. Other states made people wait until they were twenty-one.

That difference caused a problem on the roads. A group that studied drunk driving for the president found that young people were driving to states with a lower age. They drank there. [beat] And then they drove home.

[pause]

Congress wanted the age to be twenty-one everywhere. But choosing that age had always been a job for the states. It was not clear that Congress had the power to order the states to change.

So what could Congress do? We will follow the story in four steps. First, how Congress changed the drinking age without giving an order. Second, how an offer can go too far. Third, the good that can come when states differ. And last, the harm.

## An offer instead of an order

Congress found another way. It gives the states money to build highways. In 1984, it passed a law about that money. Any state that let people under twenty-one buy alcohol would lose part of it.

South Dakota took the national government to court. The state argued that Congress was using money to take over a job that belonged to the states.

In 1987, the Supreme Court ruled against South Dakota, by a vote of seven to two.

Why did the Court allow the law? Think back to our two words. Was this law an order, or was it an offer?

[long pause]

It was an offer, the Court said, and not an order. South Dakota was still free to keep its own rule. If it did, it would lose only five percent of that highway money. [beat] So it still had a real choice.

> Congress did not order the states to change. It tied a rule to money.

And the law from 1984 is still in force today.

## Can an offer go too far?

A real choice. Those words matter, because the Court has also said that an offer can turn into a threat.

In 2012, the Court looked at a law about a health program for people with little money. The law told each state to make the program much bigger. A state that refused could lose all of its money for the program, including money it already counted on.

The Court said that was no real choice. The chief justice called it [beat] "a gun to the head."

[pause]

> Congress may offer money. But a state must have a way to say no.

Now try that rule on a new case. Congress offers every state extra money for school buses, but only if the state makes its school year longer. Is that an offer, or an order? And what would make it unfair?

[long pause]

It is an offer, because a state can say no, and go without the extra money. It would become unfair if Congress threatened to take away money the state already depends on. Then the state would have no real choice. And the Court has said that goes too far.

## States can try new ideas

Disagreement is not always a bad thing. Because each state makes its own rules, one place can try an idea before the rest of the country is ready.

In 1869, lawmakers in Wyoming passed a law that let women vote. Wyoming was still a territory then, which means it was not yet a state. It kept the law when it became a state.

Then the idea spread. By 1912, women could vote in nine states in the West. [beat] And in 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment made it the rule for the whole country.

[pause]

A justice of the Supreme Court, Louis Brandeis, once described how this works. He wrote that a state may "serve as a laboratory," and try new things "without risk to the rest of the country."

A laboratory is a place for experiments. If an idea works in one state, other states can copy it. If the idea fails, only one state has to fix it.

> One state can try an idea before the whole country does.

## When a state's rule is unfair

Now the other side. The same freedom can be used badly.

For many years, some states had laws that sent Black children and white children to separate schools. In 1954, the Supreme Court ruled that those laws broke the Constitution, and every justice agreed.

Some state leaders refused to obey. In 1957, the governor of Arkansas sent soldiers from the state's National Guard to Central High School, in Little Rock. Their job was to keep nine Black students out.

[pause]

The next year, the Court answered, and again every justice agreed. It said that state leaders must follow the Constitution, even when they do not like what it requires.

> A state may not take away rights that the Constitution protects.

## So which is better?

So which is better, one rule for everyone, or a rule for each state? Americans have made both arguments since 1787. Each side has three reasons.

First, the reasons to let each state decide. People in different places want different things. A state is closer to its people than Washington is. And states can test new ideas.

Now, the reasons to make one rule for all. Your rights should not change at a state line. Some problems, such as dirty air, cross from one state into the next. And one rule is easier to understand.

[pause]

And notice one more thing. People often change sides, depending on which rule they like. Someone may want one rule for the whole country on one question, and want the states to choose on the next.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The story of the drinking age. Can you still tell it in order?

[long pause]

The states chose different ages. Congress passed a law about highway money. South Dakota went to court. And the Court ruled that the offer was allowed.

[beat]

Two. An offer can go too far. Congress may offer money, but a state must have a way to say no.

[beat]

Three. One state can try an idea before the whole country does. Wyoming went first, and the country followed.

[beat]

Four. A state may choose many things for itself. But it may not take away rights that the Constitution protects.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. One rule for everyone, or a rule for each state? Federalism does not end that argument.

> Federalism gives Americans two places to argue: in Washington, and in their own state.

The argument goes on from here, and now you can follow it. When Washington and a state disagree, listen for our two words. An offer leaves a state free to say no. [beat] An order does not.
