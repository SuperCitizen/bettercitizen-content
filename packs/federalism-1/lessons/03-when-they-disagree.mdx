# What happens when they disagree?

In the early 1980s, the states did not agree about alcohol. In South Dakota, a person who was nineteen could buy a weak kind of beer. Other states made people wait until they were twenty-one.

That difference caused a problem on the roads. A group that studied drunk driving for the President found that young people were driving to states with a lower age. They drank there, and then they drove home.

Congress wanted the age to be twenty-one everywhere. But choosing that age had always been a job for the states. It was not clear that Congress had the power to order the states to change.

## An offer instead of an order

Congress found another way. In 1984 it passed a law about the money it gives the states to build highways. Any state that let people under twenty-one buy alcohol would lose part of that money.

South Dakota took the national government to court. The state argued that Congress was using money to take over a job that belonged to the states.

In 1987 the Supreme Court ruled against South Dakota, by a vote of seven to two. The Court said that Congress had made an offer and had not given an order. South Dakota was still free to keep its own rule. If it did, it would lose only five percent of that highway money.

The law from 1984 is still in force today.

## Can an offer go too far?

The Court has also said that an offer can turn into a threat. In 2012 it looked at a law about a health program for people with little money. The law told each state to make the program much bigger. A state that refused could lose all of its money for the program, including money it already counted on.

The Court said that was no real choice. The chief justice called it "a gun to the head." So Congress may offer money to the states, but it may not leave them with no way to say no.

<Checkpoint question="Congress offers every state extra money for school buses, but only if the state makes its school year longer. Is that an offer or an order? What would make it unfair?">

It is an offer, because a state can say no and simply go without the extra money. It would become unfair if Congress threatened to take away money the state already depends on. Then the state would have no real choice, and the Court has said that goes too far.

</Checkpoint>

## States can try new ideas

Disagreement is not always a bad thing. Because each state makes its own rules, one place can try an idea before the rest of the country is ready.

In 1869, lawmakers in Wyoming passed a law that let women vote. Wyoming was still a territory then, which means it was not yet a state. It kept the law when it became a state in 1890. By 1912, women could vote in nine states in the West. In 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment made it the rule for the whole country.

A justice of the Supreme Court, Louis Brandeis, once described how this works. He wrote that a state may "serve as a laboratory" and try new things "without risk to the rest of the country." A laboratory is a place for experiments. If an idea works in one state, other states can copy it. If the idea fails, only one state has to fix it.

## When a state's rule is unfair

The same freedom can be used badly. For many years, some states had laws that sent Black children and white children to separate schools.

In 1954 the Supreme Court ruled that those laws broke the Constitution, and every justice agreed. Some state leaders refused to obey. In 1957 the governor of Arkansas sent soldiers from the state's National Guard to Central High School in Little Rock. Their job was to keep nine Black students out.

The next year the Court answered, and again every justice agreed. It said that state leaders must follow the Constitution, even when they do not like what it requires. A state may choose many things for itself, but it may not take away rights that the Constitution protects.

Notice something about this argument. People often change sides, depending on which rule they like. Someone may want one rule for the whole country on one question, and want the states to choose on the next.
