# Why do you live under two governments?

Picture two friends who are both fourteen years old. One of them lives in South Dakota, and the other lives in New Jersey.

The friend in South Dakota can already get a learner's permit. That is a card that lets a new driver practice with an adult in the car. The friend in New Jersey has to wait two more years, until the age of sixteen.

Both friends live in the same country, so why are their rules different? The reason is that each state writes its own driving laws. Lawmakers in South Dakota chose one age, and lawmakers in New Jersey chose another.

Driving is only one example. Each state also decides how its schools are run, what it will tax, and how it punishes most crimes. When you cross a state line, some of the rules around you change.

## Two governments at once

Some countries have a single government that makes the rules for everyone in the country. The United States works in a different way.

It has one national government, which meets in Washington, D.C. It also has fifty state governments, one for each state. Every one of them makes laws, collects taxes and runs its own courts.

This means that every American lives under two governments at the same time. You are a citizen of the United States, and you are also a citizen of the state where you live.

[[term:federalism]] is the name for this way of sharing power. In a federal system, a national government and a group of smaller governments each have real power of their own.

## Why was it set up this way?

The states are older than the national government. When Americans declared their independence in 1776, there were thirteen states, and each one governed itself.

Their first plan for working together was called the Articles of Confederation. That plan left almost all of the power with the states. The national government could ask the states for money, but it could not make them pay.

The plan turned out to be too weak. In 1787, leaders from the states met in Philadelphia and wrote a new plan, which we call the Constitution. They wanted a national government that was strong enough to do its work.

They did not want to erase the states, though. Many Americans trusted their own state and feared a faraway government with too much power. So the writers split the power in two. They gave certain jobs to the new national government, and they left the rest with the states.

<Checkpoint question="A family moves from South Dakota to New Jersey. Name one rule that could change for them, and one thing that will stay the same.">

The age for a learner's permit will change, because each state sets that rule for itself. Other state rules could change too, such as taxes and the rules for schools. The family will still use the same money and get mail in the same way. They are still citizens of the United States, and the national government's laws go with them.

</Checkpoint>

## Is it like a school?

Think about your own school. Some rules are the same in every room, such as where to walk during a fire drill. Other rules belong to one teacher, such as where to hand in your homework.

Federalism is a little like that. Some laws cover the whole country, and other laws cover only one state.

The comparison breaks down in one important place. A principal is the boss of the teachers and can change any classroom rule. The national government is not the boss of the states in that way. The Constitution gives each side powers of its own, and neither side can simply take the other's powers away.

## An argument that never ends

Splitting power sounds simple, but it leaves a hard question behind. For any one job, which government should be in charge?

Americans have argued about that question since 1787, and they are still arguing about it today. The next lesson shows how the Constitution tries to answer it.
