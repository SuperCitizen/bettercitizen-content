# Who is in charge of what?

Put a stamp on a letter in Maine, and the letter can travel all the way to Hawaii. It does not need a new stamp each time it crosses into another state.

That works because the mail belongs to the national government. The Constitution gives Congress the power "To establish Post Offices." One government runs the mail for the whole country, so one stamp is enough.

Money works the same way. A dollar is good in all fifty states, because the Constitution lets Congress "coin Money" and says that no state may make its own. Imagine a trip across the country if every state used different money.

The mail and money are two jobs that were handed to the national government. Other jobs were left with the states, and some jobs are shared.

## Jobs for the national government

The Constitution includes a list of the things Congress may do. Here are some of the jobs on that list:

- declare war, and keep an army and a navy
- coin money
- set up post offices
- make rules for trade between the states and with other countries
- decide how a person from another country becomes a citizen

These jobs have something in common. The writers thought each one would work best if the whole country did it in a single way.

The list also sets a limit. The national government may use only the powers that the Constitution gives it, so it cannot do whatever it likes.

## Jobs for the states

The Constitution has no list like that for the states. It uses one sentence instead, which is the Tenth Amendment.

In plain words, that sentence says this. Any power that was not given to the national government, and was not taken away from the states, belongs to the states or to the people.

That one sentence covers a great deal. States set up public schools, give out licenses to drive, and write most of the laws about crime. They decide who may work as a doctor or a nurse. They also create every city, town and county inside their borders.

## Jobs they share

Some powers belong to both governments at once. Both can collect taxes, and both can borrow money. Both build roads, and both set up courts. Both make laws and punish the people who break them.

That is why a grown-up may pay taxes to the national government and to a state in the same year.

<Checkpoint question="A state decides to print its own dollar bills. Is that allowed? Explain why or why not.">

No, it is not allowed. The Constitution gives Congress the power to coin money, and it also says that no state may do so. The writers wanted one kind of money for the whole country. This is one of the places where the Constitution tells the states exactly what they cannot do.

</Checkpoint>

## What about your city or town?

You probably have a third government too, such as a city, a town or a county. It may run the library, fix the streets and send out the fire trucks.

The Constitution does not mention cities or counties at all. They are created by the states, and they get their powers from the state. So your local government belongs to the states' side of the split.

## When two laws disagree

Sometimes a national law and a state law say opposite things. The Constitution has a rule for that problem. It calls itself, and the national laws made under it, "the supreme Law of the Land." Judges in every state must follow it. This rule is known as the Supremacy Clause.

So when the two laws truly clash, the national law wins. There is a catch, though. A national law counts only if the Constitution gave the national government the power to make it. If Congress reaches past its list, a court can strike the law down.

## The lines are not always clear

The three groups look tidy on a page. In real life the edges are fuzzy, and people often disagree about where a job belongs. The last lesson tells the story of one of those fights.
