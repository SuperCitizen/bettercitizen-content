## Two friends, two rules

Picture two friends who are both fourteen years old. One lives in South Dakota, and the other lives in New Jersey.

The friend in South Dakota can already get a learner's permit. That is a card that lets a new driver practice with an adult in the car. The friend in New Jersey has to wait until the age of sixteen.

- South Dakota: 14
- New Jersey: 16

Both friends live in the same country. So why are their rules different?

## Who chose the age?

Each state writes its own driving laws. Lawmakers in South Dakota chose one age, and lawmakers in New Jersey chose another.

> Each state writes its own driving laws.

Driving is only one example. Each state also decides how its schools are run, and what it will tax. When you cross a state line, some of the rules around you change.

- Schools, taxes, driving: state rules

## Two governments at once

The United States has one national government, which meets in Washington, D.C. It also has fifty state governments, one for each state. Every one of them makes laws, collects taxes and runs its own courts.

- 1 national government
- 50 state governments

So every American lives under two governments at the same time. This way of sharing power has a name. It is called federalism.

> Federalism: a national government and the states each hold real power.

[check q2]

## Why it was set up this way

The states are older than the national government. Their first plan for working together was called the Articles of Confederation. It left almost all of the power with the states. The national government could ask the states for money, but it could not make them pay.

- The first plan: too weak

In 1787, leaders from the states wrote a new plan, the Constitution. They wanted a national government strong enough to do its work. But many Americans feared a faraway government with too much power.

- 1787: the Constitution

> So the writers split the power in two.

[check q3]

## The question it left behind

Splitting power sounds simple, but it leaves a hard question. For any one job, which government should be in charge? Americans have argued about that since 1787, and they are still arguing today.

- Which government should be in charge?

The lesson shows both governments in one ordinary day. It also compares the country to a school, and shows where that comparison breaks down.
