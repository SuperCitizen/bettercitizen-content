## One stamp

Put a stamp on a letter in Maine, and the letter can travel all the way to Hawaii. It does not need a new stamp each time it crosses into another state.

[beat]

One stamp is enough, because one government runs the mail for the whole country.

- The mail: one government runs it

## A list for the nation

The Constitution includes a list of the things Congress may do. Setting up post offices is on that list. So is coining money, and so is declaring war.

- The mail, money, war

The writers thought each of these jobs would work best if the whole country did it in a single way.

The list also sets a limit. The national government may use only the powers that the Constitution gives it.

> The national government may use only the powers the Constitution gives it.

[check q1]

## A sentence for the states

The states have no list like that. They have one sentence instead, which is the Tenth Amendment.

- The Tenth Amendment

In plain words, it says this. Any power that was not given to the national government, and was not taken away from the states, belongs to the states or to the people.

> What the nation was not given, the states or the people keep.

That one sentence covers a great deal. States set up public schools, give out licenses to drive, and write most of the laws about crime.

- Schools, licenses, most crime laws

[check q2]

## Jobs they share

Some powers belong to both governments at once. Both can collect taxes. Both build roads, and both set up courts.

- Both: taxes, roads, courts

[check q3]

## When two laws disagree

Sometimes a national law and a state law say opposite things. The Constitution has a rule for that problem. It calls itself, and the national laws made under it, "the supreme Law of the Land." This rule is known as the Supremacy Clause.

- The Supremacy Clause

So when the two laws truly clash, the national law wins. There is a catch, though. A national law counts only if the Constitution gave the national government the power to make it.

> A national law wins only if Congress had the power to make it.

## The lines are not always clear

The three groups look tidy on a page. In real life the edges are fuzzy, and people often disagree about where a job belongs.

- Tidy on a page, fuzzy in life
