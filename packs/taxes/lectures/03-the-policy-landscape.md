For seven years, everyone who worked on tax policy knew exactly where the next war would be fought. They even knew the date. December 31, 2025.

That was the night the individual provisions of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act were scheduled to die. Think tanks modeled the cliff. Campaigns raised money off it.

[beat]

It never happened.

[pause]

On July 1, 2025, after an all-night marathon of amendments, the Senate passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 51 to 50. Vice President Vance broke the tie. Two days later the House cleared it, 218 to 214. President Trump signed it on July 4.

The cliff is gone. And the whole policy landscape has been rebuilt around three things: what that law did, what it deliberately scheduled to expire later, and what it cost.

Last time, we heard three cases at full strength: progressive expansion, supply-side reform, and consumption-tax restructuring. This lecture maps the field they are fighting on, as it stands in mid-2026. By the end, you should be able to say what is now law, what it costs, what is still open, and who has the power to change any of it.

We will take it in four steps.

First, what the law settled, and what it left ticking.

Second, the price tag, and three readings of one chart.

Third, the fights that are open in 2026, and who holds the pen.

And last, the clocks ahead.

## Settled, and ticking

Start with the durable core. These things are now permanent law, with no expiration, and indexed for inflation.

The seven-bracket rate structure, with its top rate of 37 percent. The enlarged standard deduction. The child tax credit of 2,200 dollars. The main business deductions. And an estate-tax exemption of 15 million dollars.

But Congress wrote a new set of clocks, even as it smashed the old one.

The first clock runs out after 2028. That is when four new deductions expire: for tips, for overtime, for seniors, and for interest on car loans.

The second clock runs out in 2030. That is when the cap on deducting state and local taxes, the SALT cap, reverts from 40,000 dollars to 10,000.

A built-in expiration date like those is called a sunset.

[pause]

Here is the puzzle. This Congress had the votes to make things permanent. Why would it sunset its own most advertised provisions? "No tax on tips" was a promise of the 2024 campaign.

There are two structural reasons, and they explain half of modern tax politics.

The first is the budget window. A tax bill can pass the Senate with only 51 votes, through a process called reconciliation. But a reconciliation bill must fit an agreed deficit number over ten years. And under what is called the Byrd Rule, it cannot raise deficits beyond that window without offsets.

Now do the arithmetic. A deduction that lasts four years costs a quarter as much, on paper, as a permanent one. So Congress buys the headline now, and books the cost as someone else's problem.

The second reason is leverage. Every expiration is a future bill that must pass, and both parties can campaign on it. That is exactly how the old cliff organized tax politics, from 2017 to 2025.

Last time, you met James Buchanan's idea of fiscal illusion: a bias toward taxes that citizens cannot see. It generalizes. The system is also biased toward giveaways that are visibly temporary, whose true permanent cost is never scored on the day of the vote.

The Congressional Budget Office, or CBO, has put a number on it. Making the temporary provisions permanent would add nearly another trillion dollars to the official price.

> The sunset was the price of passage, not an oversight.

Let's test that. It is 2028. A Senate candidate promises to make the overtime deduction permanent. Her argument: CBO says it has cost only a few billion dollars a year so far, so permanence is cheap.

What two things is she leaving out? Think it through.

[long pause]

First, the provision was cheap because it was temporary. A permanent version is scored over the full window and beyond, at many times the four-year cost.

Second, the Byrd Rule. A reconciliation bill cannot carry deficits past the budget window without offsets. So cheap permanence still requires offsetting revenue, or offsetting cuts, [beat] or 60 votes.

## The price tag, and three readings

Now the cost. CBO's final score is that the law adds about 3.4 trillion dollars to deficits, over the ten years from 2025 through 2034. Include the interest on the added debt, and it is about 4.1 trillion.

Two moves sit inside that number. Roughly 4.5 trillion dollars in revenue reductions. And about 1.1 trillion in net spending cuts, chiefly to Medicaid, SNAP and student loans, with about 10 million fewer people projected to have insurance.

All of that lands on a fiscal position that was already historically abnormal for peacetime.

The measure to watch is debt held by the public, as a share of GDP, the size of the whole economy. In 2007, that share was about 35 percent. In 2026, it is about 101 percent.

The all-time record is 106 percent, set in 1946, after World War II. On CBO's projection, the debt passes that record around 2030. By 2036, it reaches 120 percent.

[pause]

The deficit itself ran at 5.9 percent of GDP in fiscal 2025. A share like that has been reached before only in world wars, in the crisis of 2008 and 2009, and in the pandemic.

And net interest now costs about 1 trillion dollars a year. [beat] More than defense.

Every reform conversation happens in that shadow. And each of the three camps sees the same chart in its own way.

The progressive-expansion advocate sees a level of revenue, about 17.5 percent of GDP, that cannot fund an aging society.

> The progressive-expansion reading: revenue must rise, and rise at the top.

The supply-side reform advocate sees spending at 23.3 percent of GDP, against a historical norm of 21.2, and looks especially to the big entitlement programs.

> The supply-side reform reading: the spending path is the variable.

The consumption-tax restructuring advocate sees the gap between those two numbers, and the only instrument of sufficient size.

> The consumption-tax restructuring reading: neither can close the gap. It takes a VAT.

You may lean toward one of those readings. Take the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

One thing the chart will not do is choose among them.

> The national debt doesn't pick a side. It just compounds.

## Open fights

Four fights are open in 2026. We will take them one at a time.

First, the child tax credit. In 2021, a temporary expansion made the credit worth 3,000 to 3,600 dollars per child, fully refundable, and paid monthly. That coincided with the lowest child poverty ever recorded: 5.2 percent, on the Census Bureau's supplemental measure. When the expansion lapsed, the rate jumped to 12.4 percent in 2022, and rose again in 2023.

The 2025 law set the credit at 2,200 dollars per child. But it is refundable, meaning paid out as cash, only up to 1,700 dollars, and only for families with enough earnings. So the real dispute is full refundability, which is worth nothing to the highest earners, and everything to the poorest. There are proposals for it on both sides of the aisle.

[pause]

Second, taxing wealth. Some proposals would tax net worth directly. Others would make households worth more than 100 million dollars pay a minimum rate on their income, counting unrealized gains: gains on assets they have not sold. These proposals are dormant under unified Republican government, and they remain core planks of the Democratic platform.

Their constitutional cloud got a test in 2024, in a case called Moore v. United States. The Supreme Court upheld a one-time tax from the 2017 law, seven to two. But it did so on a narrow ground. The income in that case had been realized by the corporation, and merely attributed to its shareholders.

The majority expressly declined to decide whether the Sixteenth Amendment requires realization at all. And four justices signaled, in separate writings, that they think it does.

So let's test it. After Moore, one senator says: "the Supreme Court has now blessed taxing billionaires' unrealized gains." A colleague says: "the Court signaled wealth taxes are unconstitutional."

Who is right? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Neither. The Court upheld that tax because the income had been realized, and it explicitly reserved the question of whether realization is required. Four justices lean toward a requirement. But nothing was held.

> After Moore, the door to taxing unrealized gains is neither open nor closed.

The next such statute walks into a genuinely undecided question.

[pause]

Third, the global minimum tax. A 2021 agreement at the OECD aimed at a minimum corporate rate of 15 percent, worldwide. The United States never enacted the OECD version. It has its own 15 percent minimum tax, on companies with more than 1 billion dollars in profits.

Drafts of the 2025 law went further. They threatened a retaliatory tax on countries that applied the agreement's enforcement rules to American firms.

In June 2025, that standoff resolved. Under a G7 understanding called side-by-side, multinationals with American parents are exempt from those rules, in recognition of the American minimum tax. The retaliatory tax was dropped. Whether side-by-side is a stable equilibrium, or the start of the global deal's unraveling, is an open question that divides trade and tax scholars.

[pause]

Fourth, the IRS itself. In 2022, Congress gave the agency 79.4 billion dollars over a decade, mostly for enforcement. The target was the tax gap: 696 billion dollars a year, gross, in tax that is owed and not paid. Then Congress took the money back, in stages. By early 2026, roughly 26 billion dollars of it was left, and the enforcement account was nearly empty.

One side holds, on Treasury and CBO estimates, that each enforcement dollar returns several dollars of owed tax. The other side, the coalition that took the money back, holds that enforcement chiefly burdens taxpayers who already comply. That is now a natural experiment, running in the open.

## Who holds the pen

Now the question underneath all four: who can actually change tax policy.

In fiscal 2025, tariffs seemed to offer an answer. Customs duties rose two-and-a-half-fold, to about 195 billion dollars. Some administration figures floated tariffs as a structural pillar of revenue, which is how the federal government financed itself before 1913.

Then, on February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court decided a case called Learning Resources v. Trump. It held, six to three, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs. The broad emergency tariffs were vacated. Tariffs under other statutes, which are bound by procedure, survive.

For tax policy, the holding matters beyond trade.

> The taxing power belongs to Congress.

And revenue measures of vast "economic and political significance," the holding reaffirmed, cannot ride in on statutory silence.

Let's put that to work. Without any new act of Congress, which of these four things can a president actually do?

One. Lower the corporate rate from 21 percent to 15, by executive order.

Two. Impose broad import tariffs, by declaring an economic emergency under that same act.

Three. Direct the Treasury and the IRS to issue regulations interpreting the existing code, and to reset enforcement priorities.

Four. Suspend the SALT cap for high-cost states.

Which of the four? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the third. Rates, bases, caps and credits are statutes. Only Congress changes them, because Article One assigns the taxing power to the legislature. And the Learning Resources ruling closed the emergency-tariff route too.

What remains is the executive's real but bounded toolkit: regulations that interpret the code, guidance, and discretion over enforcement, all within the statute. Everything else requires legislation. In practice, that means either 60 votes in the Senate, or reconciliation, with all its Byrd Rule constraints.

## The clocks ahead

The old cliff is gone. But the calendar still legislates.

A reach back first. Near the start, I gave you two clocks that Congress wrote into the 2025 law. Can you still name the two years, and what runs out in each?

[long pause]

After 2028, the deductions for tips, overtime, seniors and car loans. That lands in a presidential election year, by design. Expect a re-run of the make-it-permanent fight.

In 2030, the SALT cap snaps back to 10,000 dollars for everyone. That re-arms the most regionally divisive issue in the code.

Congress set those clocks deliberately. The next one, the hardest, it inherited.

In 2033, on the projections of the 2025 Trustees, Social Security's retirement fund is depleted, and so is Medicare's hospital insurance fund. After that, current law pays 77 percent of scheduled retirement benefits. Merging the retirement and disability funds buys only one more year. And the automatic cuts are ones no Congress is expected to tolerate.

So the trust-fund dates make some major fiscal package nearly inevitable before the early 2030s. Every instrument from this series will be on that table: rates, bases, caps, a VAT, wealth levies, spending restraint.

So will a list that every Congress hunting for revenue rediscovers: tax expenditures. These are provisions that work like spending, delivered through the tax code. The largest is the exclusion of health insurance paid by employers. Together they approach 2.3 trillion dollars a year. Every serious plan to broaden the base, left and right, begins there. [beat] And each item's beneficiaries make sure it never ends there.

And today, no political coalition supports any combination of them at the necessary scale.

The argument among the three camps is, among other things, an argument about what that eventual package should look like.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The 2025 law made a core permanent, and wrote new clocks. Four deductions expire after 2028, and the SALT cap snaps back in 2030. The sunset was the price of passage, not an oversight.

[beat]

Two. The official price is about 3.4 trillion dollars over ten years. It lands on a public debt of about 101 percent of GDP, projected to pass the 1946 record around 2030.

[beat]

Three. One chart, three readings. Revenue must rise, and rise at the top. The spending path is the variable. The gap is too large for either, and it takes a VAT. [beat] The national debt doesn't pick a side. It just compounds.

[beat]

Four. The taxing power belongs to Congress. A president can interpret and enforce the code. Rates, bases, caps and credits take legislation.

[beat]

And five. Much is still open. Full refundability of the child credit. The funding of the IRS. And after Moore, the door to taxing unrealized gains is neither open nor closed.

[pause]

So here is what is settled, and what is not. Settled: the law and the arithmetic. The permanent core, the deliberate sunsets, the debt trajectory, the trust-fund clocks. Contested: everything those facts feed.

A defensible position on federal taxes in 2026 requires four commitments, stated honestly.

The level: what share of GDP the federal government should collect. Today's roughly 17.5 percent, something nearer the OECD's norms, or less, with a smaller state.

[beat]

The base: income, consumption, wealth, imports, or which mix.

[beat]

The distribution: how steeply burdens should rise with income or wealth, and measured against which evidence.

[beat]

And the falsifier: what evidence would change your answer to the first three.

[pause]

No lecture settles those four. That is where the argument goes from here. When you are back at a screen, the Coach in the app will press you on each one, and a steel-man drill will have you argue the camp you find least persuasive. After this series, you can do that with its actual best evidence, and not its caricature.

For now, go back to the date everyone was watching: December 31, 2025. The cliff never came. [beat] The clocks did. And the calendar still legislates.
