Washington, one evening in 1974. As the story goes, the economist Arthur Laffer is sitting in a restaurant called the Two Continents, with two young officials of the Ford administration: Dick Cheney and Donald Rumsfeld.

Laffer sketches a curve on a cocktail napkin.

[beat]

Tax rates run along one axis. Tax revenue runs along the other. At a rate of zero, the government collects nothing. At a rate of 100 percent, it collects nothing again. And somewhere in between, there is a peak.

[pause]

The journalist Jude Wanniski told that tale, and in 1978 he gave the sketch its name: the Laffer curve. The napkin became the founding relic of supply-side economics.

Fifty years on, American tax politics is still, in a sense, an argument about where the peak of that curve sits. And about whether the most revenue is even the right goal.

Last time, we built the machinery of federal taxes. This time, the argument. By the end, you should be able to state three serious positions the way their own advocates would, and to say what the evidence settles, and what it cannot.

We will take it in three steps.

First, a toolkit: two ideas that all three camps build with.

Second, the three positions, one at a time. Each gets the same treatment: where it starts, three arguments that carry it, its honest weak point, and the evidence that would change it.

And last, where the three stand: what is nearly settled, and what is still a fight.

## The toolkit

The first tool is a problem, posed in 1971 by the economist James Mirrlees.

A government wants revenue, and it cares about the worse-off. But it cannot observe anyone's underlying ability. It can observe only their income.

Tax income too steeply, and able people work less, invest less, and report less. Tax it too lightly, and the government gives up redistribution that would have cost little.

Where the balance falls turns on things that can be measured. One matters throughout: how strongly taxable income responds when rates change. Economists call that responsiveness the elasticity.

Every position you are about to hear is, in effect, a different empirical bet inside Mirrlees's framework.

The second tool is Laffer's curve, done seriously.

At its two ends, the curve is trivially true. Everything interesting is the location of the peak.

The benchmark calculation comes from the economists Peter Diamond and Emmanuel Saez, in 2011. Using a mid-range elasticity from the research, they put the revenue-maximizing top rate at about 73 percent.

That is an all-in rate: federal, state and payroll taxes combined, on a base purged of loopholes.

And where is the United States today? The top federal rate is 37 percent. Add Medicare and typical state taxes, and the all-in top rate sits in the mid-40s. [beat] On the rising side of every mainstream estimate.

In 2012, a University of Chicago panel put a question to top economists from across the political spectrum. Would a cut in the federal income tax raise revenue within five years? [beat] Not a single panelist said yes.

[pause]

Now the other half. The 73 percent figure is an estimate, not a law. Some economists model long-run responses that this static benchmark misses: careers chosen, skills built, ideas pursued. They derive much lower peaks, with published estimates running from the 20s to the 40s.

So the honest summary has two halves. The claim that "tax cuts from current U.S. rates pay for themselves" has almost no empirical support. [beat] And the claim that "rates near 70 percent are costless" does not follow from the evidence either.

> The curve is a constraint everyone shares. The peak is a fight.

Let's test that. A senator says: "It's basic Laffer-curve economics: cutting the top rate will increase federal revenue."

What does the curve actually establish? And what would the senator need to show? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The curve establishes only that a revenue-maximizing rate exists somewhere between zero and 100 percent. To predict that a cut raises revenue, the senator must show that today's rate sits above the peak. And the mainstream estimates place today's rate well below it.

There is a defensible supply-side claim, and it is a different one. Cuts lose less revenue than static scores imply, and they buy some growth. That is a claim about magnitudes, not about free lunches.

## The progressive-expansion case

Now the three positions. Start with the progressive-expansion advocate.

This position starts at the very top of the income distribution, and asks whether the tax system kept pace. What it wants to protect is a tax system that keeps pace with the top.

Its benchmark series comes from the economists Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman. Around 1980, in that series, the top 1 percent received roughly 12 percent of national income before taxes. In recent years, roughly 20 percent. Over the same decades, the top income-tax rate fell from 70 percent to 37.

Three arguments carry it.

First, top rates are far below the benchmark. Even if you discount the 73 percent figure heavily, this case argues, there is room above 37 percent before any mainstream estimate says a higher rate loses revenue.

Second, capital income is taxed below labor income, and the very top is mostly capital. Long-term capital gains top out at 23.8 percent. Wages top out at 37. And gains held until death escape the income tax entirely. This case points to an estimate by Saez and Zucman, counting all taxes at all levels of government. In 2018, by that estimate, the 400 richest American families paid a total rate of 23 percent. [beat] That is below the bottom half's rate, of roughly 24.

Third, enforcement collapsed where the money is. Audit rates on returns of a million dollars and more fell by roughly 70 percent during the 2010s. And the IRS estimates that 696 billion dollars of tax was legally owed but not paid, for 2022 alone. Rebuilding enforcement, on this view, raises revenue from the rates already on the books.

Wealth itself, this case adds, goes nearly untaxed. So it proposes a wealth tax.

> The progressive-expansion case: the top pulled away, and taxes did not keep pace.

Now the honest weak point. The strongest version of this case states it plainly. Its numbers are contested, and the dispute is serious scholarship, not spin.

Two economists, Gerald Auten and David Splinter, rebuilt the income series with different assumptions. And assumptions matter here, because roughly half the income in these fights never appears in the tax data. Each team has to assign that missing income to households. Piketty, Saez and Zucman assign more of it to the top. Auten and Splinter assign more of it to the middle.

On Auten and Splinter's numbers, the top 1 percent's share after taxes and transfers rose only about 1.4 percentage points since 1979. So where the unobserved half goes decides whether inequality looks explosive, or merely rising.

The strongest progressive position absorbs all of this. Even on Auten and Splinter's numbers, concentration rose. And the answer to avoidance is design, not surrender: tax gains at death, and fund the IRS.

What would change this position? A demonstration that the responses of top earners are large, and cannot be fixed by design. Or a showing that the measured concentration mostly dissolves under better measurement. The advocate would retreat if high earners genuinely produced less, rather than re-labeled their income.

## The supply-side reform case

Now the supply-side reform advocate, built the same way.

This position starts from behavior. Marginal rates change what people do: how much they work, save, invest, start businesses, and report. What it wants to protect is what a high rate quietly prevents: the careers not attempted, the firms not built, the capital not deployed.

Those costs compound, in ways static budget tables never show. At its strongest, this is a claim about magnitudes and growth.

Three arguments carry it.

First, the elasticity is real, and it rises at the top. The standard review of the research finds a response that is modest on average, but largest among high earners. They have the most flexibility over the timing, form and reporting of their income. And a higher elasticity, this case argues, mechanically lowers the best top rate.

Second, capital is the most distorting thing to tax. A tax on the return to saving is a tax on future consumption, at rates that compound. This case points to the research on optimal taxes, which repeatedly finds that capital income should be taxed lightly, compared with labor income. You heard the 23.8 percent top rate on capital gains a moment ago. On this view, it is not a loophole. [beat] It is rough policy wisdom.

Third, the deficit, on this view, is a problem of spending growth. Federal revenue in 2026 is about 17.5 percent of GDP, the size of the whole economy. That is almost exactly its 50-year average. Spending is 23.3 percent, against a 50-year average of 21.2. Revenue has returned to trend after every major rate change, in both directions. Spending has not.

> The supply-side reform case: rates change behavior, and the costs compound.

Now the honest weak point. The strongest version of this case states it plainly. The strong claim, that cuts pay for themselves, failed.

The best study of the 2017 corporate tax cut found a real response. Firms facing the average tax change raised their domestic investment by about 20 percent, relative to baseline. But the cut cost revenue equal to 41 percent of what the corporate tax had been collecting. And the growth it produced won back only about 2 of those 41 points.

Then there is Kansas. Its aggressive income-tax cuts, of 2012 and 2013, produced budget crises, not a growth boom. A Republican legislature reversed them, over a veto.

The strongest supply-side position owns both. The directional claim survived: investment responds. But rate cuts are not magic. They are a price worth paying only where elasticities are genuinely high.

What would change this position? Evidence that elasticities at the top are mostly avoidance, which a broader base can eliminate. Or accumulating evidence that rate differences within the American range have no detectable effect on long-run growth. The advocate would trade lower rates for a broader base, if the elasticities proved to be closable loopholes.

## The consumption-tax restructuring case

And the consumption-tax restructuring advocate, built the same way.

This position starts from the base itself. The other two camps, it says, are fighting over the wrong one. An income tax penalizes saving by design. Earn a dollar, pay tax. [beat] Save what is left, earn a return, and pay tax again.

What it wants to protect is thrift: the saving and investment that drive long-run growth. So tax consumption instead, meaning what people take out of the economy. Then the double tax disappears.

Three arguments carry it.

First, the rest of the rich world already runs on this base. Every OECD country except the United States levies a value-added tax, or VAT: a tax on consumption, collected inside prices. Total taxation in the United States, at all levels of government, is 25.6 percent of GDP. The OECD average is 34.1. And the difference is mostly consumption-tax revenue, not income-tax rates. A VAT, this case argues, is the one known instrument that raises revenue at the scale of the actual fiscal gap.

Second, a consumption tax need not be regressive. That is a design choice. This case points to a design called the X-tax, which puts graduated rates on wages, and lets businesses deduct investment immediately. It can be as progressive as today's income tax, on the standard distribution tables, without taxing saving.

Third, it resolves the wealth-tax fight sideways. A billionaire who reinvests everything pays little under any income-based system. Under a consumption base, the same billionaire pays precisely when the fortune is spent. On the yacht. On the compound. On the staff. At progressive rates, and with no need to appraise private companies every year.

> The consumption-tax restructuring case: tax what people spend, not what they save.

Now the honest weak point. The strongest version of this case states it plainly. It comes from the economist James Buchanan, and it is called fiscal illusion.

Governments, Buchanan argued, systematically prefer taxes that citizens cannot see. And a VAT is collected invisibly, inside every price, with no painful reckoning in April. That may be exactly why countries with high VATs grew their states so large.

The strongest consumption-tax position answers with design. Print the tax on every receipt. Cap the rate in statute. Pair adoption with repeal of the income tax, not addition to it. Whether any of those locks would hold against a determined future Congress is a political question, not an economic one.

What would change this position? Evidence that real-world VATs are regressive even after rebates and credits, and cannot be fixed politically. Or evidence that saving responds too little to justify the costs of the transition. The advocate would fold if saving turned out not to respond to its after-tax return.

## Where the three stand

Before we set the three side by side, a reach back. Each case named its own honest weak point. Can you still name all three?

[long pause]

For the progressive-expansion case: disputed measurements, with half the income unobserved.

[beat]

For the supply-side reform case: Kansas, and a corporate cut that won back 2 points of 41.

[beat]

For the consumption-tax restructuring case: fiscal illusion.

Now let two of them collide. Imagine a progressive X-tax, and two billionaires. Each has a return of 1 billion dollars this year. Billionaire A reinvests 900 million in her firms, and spends 100 million. Billionaire B cashes out, and spends the full billion.

Who pays more tax? And why would a progressive-expansion advocate still object? Take a moment.

[long pause]

B pays roughly ten times A's tax. Consumption is the base, and B consumed ten times more.

The progressive-expansion advocate objects that A's unspent 900 million still compounds, into economic and political power: control of firms, of media, of philanthropy used as influence. On this view, that is a reason to tax accumulation itself, and not only its spending.

The consumption-tax advocate replies that accumulation which is never consumed is deferred philanthropy, or investment working for everyone.

The two camps genuinely disagree about whether wealth as power, apart from wealth as future spending, belongs in the tax base. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

[pause]

Here is what the evidence does nearly settle.

Taxable income responds to rates: modestly on average, and most at the top.

[beat]

Current American rates sit below every mainstream estimate of the revenue peak.

[beat]

The United States collects substantially less of its GDP than its peers, mostly for lack of a consumption tax.

[beat]

And the federal system, all in, remains progressive.

[pause]

And here is what stays genuinely contested.

How much the concentration of income and wealth at the top actually grew.

[beat]

What rate changes within the American range do to long-run growth.

[beat]

Whether a wealth tax can be administered, and whether it is constitutional.

[beat]

And whether the political economy of a VAT is a feature, or a fatal bug.

## Your turn

You may already lean toward one of the three. So take the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own advocates would.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The curve is a constraint everyone shares. The peak is a fight. The claim that cuts from today's rates pay for themselves has almost no empirical support. And the claim that rates near 70 percent are costless does not follow from the evidence either.

[beat]

Two. The progressive-expansion case: the top pulled away, and taxes did not keep pace. Its bet: top-end responses are mostly avoidance, and fixable.

[beat]

Three. The supply-side reform case: rates change behavior, and the costs compound. Its bet: elasticities and growth effects are large and structural.

[beat]

Four. The consumption-tax restructuring case: tax what people spend, not what they save. Its bet: saving responds to its after-tax return.

[beat]

And five. Each case is an empirical bet.

> A defensible position states its bets, and names what would change its mind.

[pause]

This lecture gives no verdict among the three. The next one turns to the field where these positions are actually fighting in 2026, after the One Big Beautiful Bill Act redrew it.

For now, go back to that restaurant in 1974, and the curve on the napkin. Everyone accepts its two ends. [beat] Fifty years on, the fight is still over the middle: where the peak sits, and whether the peak is even the goal.
