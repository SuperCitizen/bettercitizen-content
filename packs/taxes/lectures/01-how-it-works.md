Ask around any office in December, and sooner or later you will meet this person.

They turned down a raise. Or they refused overtime, or asked payroll to hold back a bonus. And they will tell you why. "It would have pushed me into the next bracket, and I'd actually take home less."

[beat]

This person has just declined free money, to dodge a rule that does not exist.

[pause]

No raise, at any income, can lower your take-home pay through the bracket system. The United States does not tax your income at one rate. It taxes slices of your income, at rates that climb, like a staircase. Climb to a higher step, and you change the tax on the dollars on that step. You never change the tax on the dollars underneath.

> A higher bracket taxes only the dollars on that step.

The myth survives because almost nobody is ever shown the staircase. So let's walk it.

By the end, you should be able to work out a tax bill the way the law does, and say where the federal government's money comes from. And you will have the question that economists insist on, and politicians almost never ask: who actually bears a tax, whoever writes the check.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the staircase: how the income tax turns a salary into a bill.

Second, where the money comes from, including a tax that most debates leave out.

Third, the law as it stands in 2026, and the deadline that never arrived.

And last, who pays, and who really bears the burden.

One thing to carry with you. There are three questions about any tax system, and political shouting blurs them. How much revenue? That is the level. Who pays? That is the distribution. And what is taxed? That is the base.

Level, distribution, base. [beat] Three dials. We will come back to them.

## The staircase

Start with two rates that people mix up all the time.

The first is the rate on your next dollar of taxable income. It is the step you are standing on. That is called your marginal rate.

The second is your total tax, divided by your total income. It is the average over all the steps you have climbed. That is called your effective rate.

> Marginal is the rate on your next dollar. Effective is your average.

Those two can be wildly different numbers. And confusing them is the single most common arithmetic error in American political life.

Watch them come apart. Take a single filer who earns a salary of 100,000 dollars, in 2026.

Before any rate applies, the standard deduction comes off the top. For her, that is 16,100 dollars the income tax never touches. It leaves 83,900 dollars of taxable income.

Now walk that income up the stairs.

The bottom step is taxed at 10 percent. It holds her first 12,400 dollars.

The second step is taxed at 12 percent. It runs from there up to 50,400 dollars.

The third step is taxed at 22 percent. The rest of her income lands there, and runs out there. She never reaches the fourth step.

So she is in the 22 percent bracket. Does she owe 22 percent of her salary?

[beat]

Nowhere near. Add up the three steps, and her bill is 13,170 dollars. [beat] That is 13.2 percent of her salary.

[pause]

Her marginal rate is 22 percent. Her effective rate is 13.2 percent. One person, one year, one tax return.

And notice what the staircase cannot do. It cannot make her bill jump when a raise carries her across a line. Only the new dollars meet the new rate.

The lesson page has a simulator where you can slide an income up these stairs. Slide it slowly across a bracket line, and the marginal rate ticks up, while the dollars owed only glide. That smoothness is the answer to the colleague who refused the raise.

Let's test it. Your taxable income is exactly 50,400 dollars: the top of the 12 percent step. Then you earn one more dollar.

By how much does your income tax rise? And what happens to the tax on your first 50,400 dollars? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Your tax rises by 22 cents. [beat] The new dollar lands on the 22 percent step, and it is the only dollar there.

The tax on everything underneath is untouched. The first 12,400 dollars still pay 10 percent. The next 38,000 still pay 12.

> Brackets are never retroactive.

That is the entire logic of marginal taxation, in one dollar.

One caution, because the myth has a real cousin. Some benefits and tax credits shrink as income rises, like the Earned Income Tax Credit. So a raise can occasionally cost a household of low or middle income more in lost benefits than it adds in pay.

That is a phase-out cliff, not a bracket. And reformers across the spectrum agree that the distinction matters.

## Where the money comes from

That is the income tax. Now step back, and look at everything the federal government collects.

In fiscal year 2025, it collected about 5.2 trillion dollars. Here is where each dollar came from.

The individual income tax brought in 51 cents. [beat] About half.

Payroll taxes, the ones for Social Security and Medicare, brought in 33 cents. [beat] A third.

The corporate income tax brought in under 9 cents.

Tariffs, which are taxes on imports, brought in 4 cents.

Everything else brought in 3 cents: excise taxes, the estate and gift tax, and fees.

[pause]

Most first guesses put the corporate share too high, and the payroll share too low.

> About half is income tax. A third is payroll. Corporate, under nine cents.

Three things in that mix do real work.

First, payroll taxes are enormous, and invisible in most tax debates. They fund the two giant entitlement programs, Social Security and Medicare. And they obey different rules from the income tax.

The Social Security tax is 12.4 percent of wages. On paper it is split: half is withheld from the worker, and half is paid by the employer. But it applies only up to a cap, which in 2026 is 184,500 dollars of wages. Above the cap, the tax stops.

The Medicare tax is 2.9 percent, split the same way, and it has no cap.

Think about that shape. A worker earning 50,000 dollars pays the full rate on every dollar. An executive earning 2 million dollars pays the Social Security tax on less than a tenth of her wages.

The income tax takes a bigger share as income climbs. A tax shaped that way is called progressive. The payroll tax is flat, and then, above the cap, it takes a smaller share. A tax shaped that way is called regressive. So the payroll tax is the income tax's mirror image.

Let's see that on a pay stub. A single worker earns 60,000 dollars. Her stub shows about 4,590 dollars withheld for Social Security and Medicare. It shows about 5,000 dollars withheld for federal income tax. She concludes that the income tax is her biggest federal tax.

What is she missing? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The other half. Her employer sent in another 4,590 dollars of payroll tax on her behalf. And the broad consensus among economists is that the employer's half is really paid by workers, through lower wages.

Count both halves, and her payroll burden is roughly 9,200 dollars. [beat] Nearly double her income tax.

And payroll taxes start on the first dollar of wages. No standard deduction erases them, and no refundable credit either. For a large share of working households, they exceed the income tax entirely.

Second of the three, the corporate income tax, a flat 21 percent on corporate profits, is small. So fights over it, however fierce, move less money than the rhetoric implies.

And third, the strangest line in the mix: tariffs. For decades, customs duties supplied 1 to 2 percent of federal revenue. Then, in fiscal 2025, they jumped by 153 percent, as the second Trump administration imposed sweeping import taxes by executive order.

Then, in February 2026, the Supreme Court held that the emergency statute behind most of those tariffs does not authorize the president to impose tariffs at all. The Constitution gives the taxing power to Congress.

[pause]

One piece of history, because it returns later in this series. The income tax rests on the Sixteenth Amendment, from 1913, which lets Congress tax incomes "from whatever source derived." Whether a federal wealth tax is constitutional is still argued. And that argument turns on exactly what the amendment did, and did not, authorize.

## The law as it stands

So far, the machine. Now the settings Congress has chosen for it.

For seven years, every tax conversation in Washington ended with the same clock. In 2017, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act cut individual rates, roughly doubled the standard deduction, and cut the corporate rate from 35 percent to 21. But to fit the Senate's budget rules, it scheduled almost all of its individual provisions to die at the end of 2025.

[beat]

The great expiration never came.

[pause]

On July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act became law. It had passed the Senate 51 to 50, on a tie broken by Vice President Vance.

What did the 2025 law do? Four things are worth holding.

One. It made the individual structure of the 2017 law permanent. The seven rates, from 10 percent up to 37. The enlarged standard deduction. A child tax credit of 2,200 dollars per child.

[beat]

Two. It added brand-new deductions that are deliberately temporary. They cover tips, overtime premiums, taxpayers aged 65 and over, and interest on loans for new cars assembled in the United States. All of them run from 2025 through 2028, and then expire.

[beat]

Three. It raised the cap on deducting state and local taxes, known as the SALT cap, from 10,000 dollars to 40,000. That is temporary as well. In 2030, the cap snaps back to 10,000 dollars for everyone.

[beat]

Four. It paid for part of all this with about 1.1 trillion dollars in net spending reductions, largely in Medicaid, SNAP and student loans.

And the price. The Congressional Budget Office's final score is that the law adds about 3.4 trillion dollars to deficits, over the ten years from 2025 through 2034. Count the extra interest on the national debt, and the figure is about 4.1 trillion.

Whether that price bought growth, fairness, neither, or both is precisely what the three camps of the next lecture dispute.

Let's test that. It is April 2030, and a restaurant server is doing her taxes. Back in 2026 she enjoyed four benefits of this law. The tip deduction. The child tax credit of 2,200 dollars. The enlarged standard deduction. And the SALT cap of 40,000 dollars.

Which of those four are still there? Think it through.

[long pause]

Only two. The child tax credit and the enlarged standard deduction are permanent, and indexed to inflation.

The tip deduction expired after 2028, along with the deductions for overtime, for seniors and for car loans. And the SALT cap snapped back to 10,000 dollars in 2030.

The law deliberately mixed permanent structure with short-fuse benefits.

> The 2025 law made the rate structure permanent, and the new deductions temporary.

The budget-rule strategy behind that design is a subject for the third lecture.

## Who pays, and who bears

Now the second dial, the distribution. Who pays?

Start with the income tax alone, in the IRS data for tax year 2023. The top 1 percent of taxpayers earned 20.6 percent of reported income. They paid 38.4 percent of the income tax. The bottom half paid 3.3 percent.

Standing alone, the income tax is steeply progressive.

But the income tax is only half the system. The Congressional Budget Office keeps a fuller account, which adds payroll, corporate and excise taxes. Its figures for 2022 give the average total federal tax rate for each group of households.

For the lowest-earning fifth: 1.4 percent.

[beat]

For the highest-earning fifth: 23.2 percent.

[beat]

For the top 1 percent: 31.5 percent.

[pause]

Hear both halves of that. The system, all in, is still clearly progressive. And the bottom fifth's bill is not zero, once payroll taxes enter.

Taken alone, the bottom fifth's income-tax rate is negative: about minus 10 percent in 2022. Refundable credits, like the Earned Income Tax Credit, pay out cash. Payroll taxes then claw some of it back.

This is also the honest way to hear a famous talking point. "47 percent of Americans pay no federal income tax."

That figure was an estimate from the Tax Policy Center, for the deep recession year of 2009. It was true as far as it went, [beat] and misleading in every direction it was used.

By 2025, the share paying no federal income tax was about 40 percent. Most of them were elderly households, or low-wage workers with children. About two-thirds of them still paid payroll taxes. And almost all of them paid excise taxes, and bore some of the corporate tax.

> "Pays no income tax" was never "pays no tax."

One more tool, and it starts with something you have already heard.

Go back to the worker with the pay stub. Her employer wrote the check for half of her payroll tax. Who really paid that half?

[long pause]

She did, through lower wages. That was the economists' consensus.

Economists have a name for what we just asked. They call it incidence. The burden of a tax lands wherever behavior cannot dodge it, whatever name is on the check.

> Who bears a tax is not who writes the check.

Payroll is the first standard example. Here are two more.

The corporate tax is legally paid by the corporation. But a corporation is not a person who can bear anything. So the burden is divided. Shareholders bear some, in lower returns. Workers bear some, in lower wages. Customers bear some, in higher prices. The proportions remain genuinely contested.

And the tariff. Legally, it is paid by the importing company, at the port. That is why a tariff can be marketed as a tax on foreigners. But look at the evidence from the tariff rounds of 2018 and 2019. Foreign exporters barely cut their prices. So American firms and consumers bore nearly all of it, through higher prices.

Every camp in the next lecture wields incidence. The supply-side reformers wield it against the corporate tax. The progressives wield it against the payroll cap. The consumption-tax advocates wield it against the whole income base.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The income tax is a staircase. A higher bracket taxes only the dollars on that step, and brackets are never retroactive. So no raise can lower your take-home pay through the brackets.

[beat]

Two. Marginal is the rate on your next dollar. Effective is your average. On a salary of 100,000 dollars, the marginal rate was 22 percent, and the effective rate was 13.2.

[beat]

Three. Of every federal dollar, about half is income tax, and a third is payroll. Corporate is under nine cents. And the payroll tax is the income tax's mirror image: flat, and then capped.

[beat]

Four. The 2025 law made the rate structure permanent, and the new deductions temporary. Its official price is about 3.4 trillion dollars over ten years.

[beat]

And five. Who bears a tax is not who writes the check. By the economists' consensus, workers bear the payroll tax, including the employer's half. And "pays no income tax" was never "pays no tax."

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Everything so far is machinery, not argument. It does not tell you whether 31.5 percent at the top is too high or too low. It does not tell you whether the corporate tax should exist, or whether taxing income at all is the right idea.

Those are questions about the three dials. The level. The distribution. The base. [beat] Every reform position is a different setting of those three.

The next lecture takes the three serious answers, and gives each one at full strength.

The progressive-expansion case argues that the top of the distribution has pulled away, and that the tax system should pull back harder.

The supply-side reform case argues that rates distort work and investment more than the rhetoric admits, and that growth compounds.

The consumption-tax restructuring case argues that both of the others are fighting over the wrong base, and that the thing to tax is spending, not income.

Each will get the strongest version of its evidence, including the parts its own advocates would rather skip.

For now, go back to that office in December, and the colleague who turned down the raise. They declined free money, to dodge a rule that does not exist. [beat] You can show them the staircase now.
