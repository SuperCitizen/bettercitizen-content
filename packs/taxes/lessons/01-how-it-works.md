# How U.S. federal taxes actually work

Most political debate about taxes confuses three different questions: *how much* total revenue (the level), *who pays* (the distribution), and *what's taxed* (the base). Untangling them is the prerequisite to thinking about reform.

## What federal revenue actually consists of

Roughly, in FY 2023 (CBO data, ~$4.4T total federal revenue):

- **Individual income taxes**: ~50% of revenue. Tax on wages, salaries, interest, dividends, and other personal income. Progressive marginal-rate structure (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, 37%).
- **Payroll taxes (Social Security + Medicare)**: ~36% of revenue. Flat 12.4% on first $168,600 of wages (Social Security) + 2.9% on all wages (Medicare), split nominally between employer and employee.
- **Corporate income tax**: ~9% of revenue. 21% flat rate on corporate profits.
- **Excise taxes** (on gasoline, alcohol, tobacco, etc.): ~3%.
- **Other** (estate, customs, miscellaneous): ~2%.

Several things follow:

1. **Payroll taxes are larger than they appear**: most working Americans pay more in payroll taxes than in income taxes. The "tax burden" people feel is mostly payroll, not income.
2. **The corporate tax is small**: 9% of revenue, less than half of any of the major revenue sources. Reforms to "the corporate tax" affect a much smaller share of total revenue than rhetoric suggests.
3. **Excise + estate are small but symbolically large**: combined ~5% of revenue but politically charged.

## Marginal vs. effective rates

The most-misunderstood concept in tax debate. Two rates exist:

- **Marginal rate**: the rate on your *next dollar* of income. The 37% top bracket applies only to income above ~$609,000 (single, 2024). The 22% bracket applies to income between $47,150 and $100,525.
- **Effective rate**: total taxes paid divided by total income. For a single filer with $60,000 in wages: marginal rate 22%, effective rate ~11% (after accounting for the standard deduction and the lower brackets).

The U.S. system is *progressive* in this sense: average effective rates rise with income across the working population. CBO 2023 data on effective rates by quintile:
- Bottom quintile: ~0% federal income tax (often negative, due to refundable credits like EITC).
- Middle quintile: ~6% federal income tax, ~14% combined federal (incl. payroll).
- Top quintile: ~17% federal income tax, ~22% combined.
- Top 1%: ~25% federal income tax, ~30% combined.

The system is more progressive than it looks if you focus on income tax alone (because of refundable credits at the bottom), and less progressive than it looks if you ignore the regressivity of payroll taxes (which cap at $168,600).

## What the TCJA of 2017 actually did

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 was the largest tax change since the Reagan-era reforms. Its core elements:

1. **Reduced individual tax rates** across most brackets (top rate from 39.6% to 37%, etc.).
2. **Roughly doubled the standard deduction** (~$24,000 for joint filers, simplifying filing for most taxpayers).
3. **Capped the State and Local Tax (SALT) deduction at $10,000** — a major hit to high-tax states.
4. **Eliminated personal exemptions** (offset by higher standard deduction for most).
5. **Cut the corporate tax rate** from 35% to 21%.
6. **Created a 20% deduction for pass-through business income** (§199A) — sole proprietors, partnerships, S-corps.
7. **Doubled the estate-tax exemption** (~$11M individual, ~$22M couple).
8. **Limited business interest deductions** and changed expensing rules.
9. **Made the corporate provisions permanent** but **set most individual provisions to expire after 2025.**

This last point is critical. Without congressional action, in 2026:
- Individual rates revert to pre-2017 levels.
- Standard deduction returns to ~half its current level.
- Personal exemptions return.
- SALT cap goes away.
- Estate exemption halves.

The 2025 expiration is the largest single tax-policy event of the next several years. The size and direction of the renewal (or non-renewal) is a major political fight.

## The incidence question

Who actually bears a tax depends on economic responses, not on who writes the check.

Examples:
- **Payroll taxes**: nominally split 50/50 between employer and employee. In practice, most economists agree the employee bears nearly the full burden through lower wages — the "employer share" is, in effect, paid by reducing what would otherwise be wages.
- **Corporate taxes**: nominally on the corporation. Incidence falls on some combination of shareholders (lower returns), workers (lower wages), and consumers (higher prices). The split is empirically contested. CBO assumes 75% to capital, 25% to labor in its long-run analysis; some research suggests labor shares the burden more heavily.
- **Estate taxes**: nominally on the heir. Incidence is largely on the decedent's wealth, but behavioral effects (donation timing, asset sales) shift some burden in complex ways.

The lesson: the political debate often focuses on who *writes the check* rather than who *bears the cost*. Both perspectives in Lesson 2 will engage this.

## What states do

Federal tax policy is the focus of this pack, but states have their own systems:

- **State income taxes** — 9 states have no income tax (FL, TX, WA, NV, etc.); top rates among taxing states range from ~3% (NM) to ~13.3% (CA).
- **State and local sales taxes** — average ~7% combined; some states (NH, OR, MT, DE, AK) have no state sales tax.
- **Property taxes** — generally local, not state. Effective rates range from ~0.3% (HI, AL) to ~2%+ (NJ, IL).

The U.S. federal system is more progressive than most states' systems combined. State and local taxes are roughly proportional or mildly regressive in many states; the federal system carries most of the progressivity.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Progressive-expansion advocates** argue the U.S. system has become less progressive than peer countries (especially at the top), that wealth concentration has produced political and economic distortions, and that the right response is higher rates on top earners, wealth taxes, and enhanced transfer programs financed by them.
- **Supply-side reform advocates** argue marginal-rate elasticity matters more than political rhetoric admits, that economic growth is the most important variable for long-term living standards, and that the right response is lower rates and a broader base, with the deficit addressed through spending restraint.
- **Consumption-tax restructuring advocates** argue both income-tax-focused approaches miss the deeper problem — that taxing income (and especially capital income) discourages saving and investment, while a consumption-base tax (VAT, expenditure tax, X-tax) can raise the same revenue with fewer distortions and more progressivity than the current system.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
