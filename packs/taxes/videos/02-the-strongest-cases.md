## A curve on a napkin

As the story goes, one evening in 1974 the economist Arthur Laffer sat in a Washington restaurant and sketched a curve on a cocktail napkin. Tax rates on one axis, tax revenue on the other. A rate of zero raises nothing. A rate of 100 percent raises almost nothing, because nobody reports income to see it fully confiscated. Somewhere in between is a peak.

- Zero revenue at 0% and at 100%

> The curve is a constraint everyone shares. The peak is a fight.

## Where is the peak?

Above the peak, a tax cut can raise revenue. Below it, a cut loses revenue. Two benchmark estimates put the peak at about 63 percent and about 73 percent. The top American rate, with federal, Medicare and typical state taxes together, sits in the mid-40s.

- Benchmark peak: about 60 to 75%
- U.S. today: mid-40s

But economists who model long-run responses put the peak far lower, from the 20s to the 40s.

- Long-run models: 20s to 40s

## Progressive expansion

The progressive-expansion case argues that incomes at the very top rose and the tax system did not keep pace. It points to capital gains, which top out at 23.8 percent against 37 percent for wages, and to an estimated 696 billion dollars of tax owed but not paid in 2022. Its honest weak point: how much the top gained is disputed by serious scholars.

- Gains 23.8%, wages 37%
- Tax gap: $696 billion (2022)

> The progressive-expansion case: avoidance is a design flaw, and design can fix it.

## Supply-side reform

The supply-side reform case argues that marginal rates change behavior, and that the costs compound. It points to the best study of the 2017 corporate tax cut, which found firms raised domestic investment about 20 percent relative to baseline. Its honest weak point: the same study found growth recovered only about 2 points of a revenue cost equal to 41 percent of earlier corporate receipts.

- Investment: up about 20%
- Recovered: about 2 of 41 points

> The supply-side reform case: rates change behavior, and the costs compound.

## Consumption-tax restructuring

The consumption-tax restructuring case argues that the other two are fighting over the wrong base, because an income tax penalizes saving. It points to the rest of the rich world: every OECD country except the United States levies a value-added tax, or VAT. Its honest weak point is called fiscal illusion: a tax collected invisibly inside every price may make it easier for the state to grow.

- VAT: every OECD country except the U.S.
- Fiscal illusion: a tax nobody sees

> The consumption-tax case: tax what people spend, not what they save.

## Three bets

Each case is an empirical bet, and much of the evidence is contested. The lesson gives each one its full argument, and what its own advocates say would change their minds.
