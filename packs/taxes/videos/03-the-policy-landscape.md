## The cliff that never came

For seven years, everyone who worked on tax policy knew where the next war would be fought: December 31, 2025, the night the individual provisions of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act were scheduled to die. It never happened. The Senate passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act 51 to 50, on the vice president's tie-breaking vote, and President Trump signed it on July 4, 2025.

> The cliff is gone. Congress wrote a new set of clocks.

## Permanent, and ticking

The core is now permanent law: the seven-bracket rate structure with its top rate of 37 percent, the enlarged standard deduction, and the 2,200 dollar child tax credit.

- Permanent: brackets, standard deduction, child credit

But the most advertised provisions were given end dates. The deductions for tips, overtime, seniors and car-loan interest expire after 2028. The 40,000 dollar SALT cap reverts to 10,000 dollars in 2030.

- Tips and overtime: expire after 2028
- SALT cap: reverts in 2030

## Why sunset your own promise?

Two reasons. First, the budget window. A bill passed under reconciliation rules needs only 51 Senate votes, but it must fit an agreed deficit number over ten years. A deduction that lasts four years costs a quarter as much on paper as a permanent one.

- Four years: a quarter of the cost

Second, leverage. Every expiration is a future must-pass bill that both parties can campaign on.

- Every sunset: a future must-pass bill

> The sunset was the price of passage, not an oversight.

## The price

CBO scored the law at about 3.4 trillion dollars added to deficits over ten years. Gross federal debt has passed 39 trillion dollars. Debt held by the public is about 101 percent of GDP in 2026, and is projected to pass the record of 106 percent, set in 1946, around 2030.

- Added to deficits: about $3.4 trillion
- Debt held by the public: about 101%
- 1946 record: 106%

## One chart, three readings

Each camp reads that chart its own way. The progressive sees revenue of about 17.5 percent of GDP, and argues it must rise at the top. The supply-sider sees outlays at 23.3 percent, against a historical norm of 21.2, and argues the spending path is the variable. The consumption-taxer sees a gap too large for either, and argues a VAT is the only instrument of sufficient size.

- Progressive: revenue must rise at the top
- Supply-side: the spending path is the variable
- Consumption: only a VAT is large enough

> The national debt doesn't pick a side; it just compounds.
