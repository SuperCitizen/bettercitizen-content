# Judicial review and its critics

In the last days of his presidency, John Adams worked late signing commissions for dozens of new judges and justices of the peace — Federalist loyalists, packed into the judiciary before Thomas Jefferson's incoming Republicans could stop him. The commissions were signed and sealed. But in the rush, some were never *delivered*. When Jefferson took office in March 1801, he found a stack of them sitting on a desk in the State Department and told his Secretary of State, James Madison, to leave them there. One belonged to a Maryland businessman named William Marbury, who wanted his minor judgeship badly enough to sue — directly in the Supreme Court. Out of that clerical accident came the most consequential power any court in the world has ever claimed for itself.

## The case that armed the Court

Chief Justice John Marshall — who, in an irony no novelist would risk, had been Adams's Secretary of State and had failed to deliver the very commissions at issue — faced a trap. If the Court ordered Madison to hand over the commission, Jefferson would simply ignore the order, and the young Court would be exposed as powerless. If the Court refused, it would be confirming that the judiciary served at the executive's pleasure. Either path diminished the institution.

Marshall's 1803 opinion in *Marbury v. Madison* found a third path, and it is worth slowing down to see how the trick works. The Court held, first, that Marbury had a legal right to his commission and that the law owed him a remedy — a finding that scolded Jefferson on the merits. But it then held that the Court itself could not give him that remedy, because the statute Marbury had sued under — Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, read as letting such suits start in the Supreme Court — enlarged the Court's original jurisdiction beyond what Article III allows. A statute that conflicts with the Constitution, Marshall reasoned, is not law at all, and when the two collide a court must apply the Constitution: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

Notice what Marshall purchased and what he paid. Jefferson won the case — Marbury never got his judgeship — so there was nothing for the administration to defy. And in the act of *declining* power, the Court asserted a far larger one: [[term:judicial-review]], the authority to strike down acts of Congress. Nothing in Article III grants that authority in so many words. Marshall inferred it from the logic of a written constitution, from the judicial oath, and from the Supremacy Clause — and then did not use it against another federal statute for half a century.

<Checkpoint question="Why was it strategically brilliant for Marshall to rule against Marbury — the outcome Jefferson wanted — rather than for him?">
  Because an order in Marbury's favor would have been **ignored**, exposing the Court's weakness, while a simple surrender would have conceded the judiciary's subordination. By striking down Section 13, Marshall gave Jefferson the bottom-line result he wanted — nothing to defy — while establishing the precedent that courts may void acts of Congress. The Court claimed its greatest power in the same breath it refused to exercise any.
</Checkpoint>

## Hamilton's defense — and the record's complication

The idea did not appear from nowhere. In [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 78, Alexander Hamilton had argued that the judiciary, holding "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment," would be "the least dangerous" branch — and that judicial review is not judicial supremacy over the people but agency *for* them. The Constitution is the people's own enactment; an ordinary statute is merely the act of their agents in the legislature. When the two conflict, a court honoring the Constitution is enforcing the principal's instructions against a wayward agent, not elevating judges above voters.

It is an elegant theory, and the historical record promptly complicated it. The second time the Court struck down an act of Congress — *Dred Scott v. Sandford* in 1857 — it voided the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery in the territories and declared that Black Americans could not be citizens, a decision that accelerated the country toward civil war. Whatever judicial review is, the record shows it is not reliably heroic. The same power that produced [[term:brown-v-board-of-education]] produced *Dred Scott*; any honest theory of the power has to own both.

## The countermajoritarian difficulty

The modern debate's terms were set in 1962 by Yale law professor Alexander Bickel in *The Least Dangerous Branch* — the title is Hamilton's phrase, deployed with an eyebrow raised. Bickel named the problem that every justification of judicial review must answer: "The root difficulty is that judicial review is a counter-majoritarian force in our system." When the Court strikes down a statute, it thwarts the considered choice of elected representatives — actual people who can be voted out — on the authority of nine lawyers who cannot. Calling the Constitution itself the "will of the people" only relocates the problem, because the document's open-textured phrases (due process, equal protection, cruel and unusual) do not apply themselves; someone must choose among readings, and the someone is unelected.

Bickel's own answer was institutional modesty: courts should husband their legitimacy, deciding as little as possible as late as possible — the "passive virtues" of denying review, finding cases unripe, and avoiding constitutional questions. Later scholars offered different escapes; John Hart Ely's *Democracy and Distrust* (1980) argued review is democratic precisely when it polices the channels of democracy itself — protecting voting, speech, and shut-out minorities — and suspect everywhere else. But the difficulty has never been dissolved, only managed. Every interpretive school in American law is, at bottom, a competing strategy for managing it.

## Three answers to one question

If review is here to stay, the fight moves to *how* judges should read the document they enforce against majorities. Three schools dominate, and each deserves its strongest telling.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Originalist">
    The Constitution's words had a public meaning when they were ratified, and that meaning — not the judge's values — is the law. Justice Antonin Scalia made the canonical case in "Originalism: The Lesser Evil" (1989) and *A Matter of Interpretation* (1997): the whole point of a written constitution is to *fix* certain choices beyond the reach of transient majorities and transient judges alike. [[term:originalism]] answers Bickel directly — review is legitimate exactly because, and only insofar as, the judge is enforcing what the people actually enacted, through ratification and amendment, rather than what nine lawyers wish they had. A "living" Constitution, on this view, is just a Constitution that means whatever the current Court's majority says, which is no constraint at all: if the document evolves, it evolves at the ballot box, through Article V, not in chambers. Originalists concede the method sometimes yields outcomes the judge dislikes; that is the proof it is law and not preference.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Living constitutionalist">
    The Framers wrote in majestic generalities — "equal protection," "due process," "unreasonable searches" — and chose those words over specific codes deliberately. Justice Stephen Breyer's *Active Liberty* (2005) argues judges should read them to serve the document's animating purpose: a workable, participatory democracy in conditions the Framers could not have imagined — wiretaps, administrative agencies, a continental economy. David Strauss's *The Living Constitution* (2010) adds the descriptive point: American constitutional law has *always* developed the way the common law develops, precedent by precedent, with text as the starting point rather than the finish line — which is how a Constitution ratified by slaveholding states came, without relevant textual change, to forbid school segregation. The living constitutionalist's challenge to originalism is concrete: virtually everyone regards *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) as correct, yet the Congress that proposed the Fourteenth Amendment itself ran segregated schools in the District of Columbia. A method that struggles to generate the canonical case of the twentieth century, they argue, is not a constraint — it is an alibi.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Restraint (Thayerian)">
    Both camps above are arguing about how judges should wield a power this school thinks judges should barely use. James Bradley Thayer's 1893 Harvard Law Review article — the founding text of the restraint tradition — argued a court may strike down a statute only when the legislature has made not merely a mistake but "a very clear one, — so clear that it is not open to rational question." Reasonable constitutional disagreement belongs to the elected branches; a court that voids every law it thinks unconstitutional teaches legislatures to stop thinking constitutionally at all, outsourcing the public's constitutional conscience to nine people. Notice that this is not a theory of interpretation but a theory of *deference* — a Thayerian can be an originalist or a living constitutionalist about meaning and still vote to uphold, because the question is never "what does the Constitution mean?" but "is the legislature's reading rationally defensible?" Restraint is the only school that answers Bickel by shrinking the countermajoritarian act itself, and its partisans note that it travels across ideological eras: it counseled against striking New Deal economic statutes in the 1930s and against striking gun regulations or abortion laws today.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

The tabs hide a structural point worth making explicit: originalism and living constitutionalism are rival theories of *meaning*, while Thayerian restraint is a theory of *who decides* — which is why accusations of [[term:judicial-activism]] fly in every direction. Each school calls the others activist: originalists say living constitutionalism licenses judges to update the law; living constitutionalists point to conservative courts striking down campaign-finance and voting-rights statutes; Thayerians say both camps strike down too much. "Activism" describes whose ox is gored; the analytical question is always which interpretive commitments are doing the work.

<Checkpoint question="A judge writes: 'I believe this statute is terrible policy, and were I a legislator I would vote against it. But its constitutionality is fairly debatable, and that is enough to sustain it.' Which school is speaking?">
  **Thayerian restraint.** The judge is not resolving what the Constitution means and may even suspect the statute conflicts with it — she is deferring because the legislature's contrary reading is not a *clear* mistake. An originalist would ask what the text meant at ratification; a living constitutionalist would ask what reading best serves the document's purposes today. Only the Thayerian treats reasonable debatability itself as the end of the case.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson is not

This lesson did not settle which school is right — no lesson honestly could, because each school's strongest version survives its rivals' best attacks, which is what makes the ground genuinely contested. Nor did it settle a question all three schools share an interest in dodging: whether the *structure* housing the power — life tenure, nine seats, a docket chosen in secret — is itself due for renovation. That fight, running from Franklin Roosevelt's 1937 plan to a presidential commission in 2021 and a sitting president's reform proposal in 2024, is Lesson 3.
