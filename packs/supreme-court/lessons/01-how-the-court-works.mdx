# How the Supreme Court actually works

On conference days when the Court is in session, nine people walk into a room in the building across from the Capitol, shake hands, and close the door. No clerks. No secretaries. No recording. By tradition, the most junior justice takes notes and answers any knock. Inside that room, the [[org:supreme-court-of-the-united-states]] decides which of the thousands of disputes Americans have begged it to hear will get a hearing at all — and how the ones already argued will come out. Nearly everything the public sees, the marble plaza, the televised confirmation wars, the opinion-day scrums, happens outside that door. To understand the Court, start with the machinery the door conceals.

## The funnel: thousands ask, dozens are answered

Almost no one has a right to be heard by the Supreme Court. A party who loses in a federal court of appeals or a state's highest court must *petition* for review — formally, ask the Court to issue a writ of certiorari ordering the lower court to send up the record. By the Court's own description, roughly 5,000 to 7,000 new cases arrive every Term (the Term runs, by statute, from the first Monday in October to the first Monday in October). Plenary review — full briefing and oral argument — is granted in about 80 of them, and in recent Terms the argued docket has often run closer to sixty. Another hundred or so are disposed of summarily, without argument. Everyone else gets a one-line denial.

What gets through? The Court's Rule 10 says certiorari is granted "only for compelling reasons" — and the reasons are about the *legal system*, not the parties. The classic trigger is a circuit split: federal appeals courts in different regions reading the same federal law in conflicting ways, so that the law means one thing in Texas and another in Vermont. National importance helps; a sympathetic party with a badly decided case generally does not, because the Court does not sit to correct every error below.

The sorting itself is industrial. Most justices pool their law clerks into a shared "cert pool": each incoming petition is assigned to one clerk, who writes a memo summarizing the case and recommending a grant or denial for all the participating chambers. Petitions a justice flags go on the "discuss list" for conference; the rest are denied without ever being talked about. At conference, the vote to grant follows the **rule of four** — four of the nine justices suffice to take a case, a deliberate minority-protecting rule that keeps five justices from controlling the agenda completely.

## From briefs to the bench

Once certiorari is granted, the case enters its public phase. The petitioner files a merits brief (capped at 13,000 words under the Court's rules), the respondent answers, and replies follow. Around them accumulates a second literature: [[term:amicus-brief]] filings from governments, industries, scholars, and advocacy groups who are not parties but want the justices to see the stakes their way. When the federal government is not already a party, the Solicitor General often weighs in — and is sometimes invited to.

Oral argument is the only part of the process most people ever see, and it is shorter than its reputation: each side gets roughly thirty minutes, most of it consumed by the justices' questions. Arguments run October through April, typically two cases a morning on Mondays, Tuesdays, and Wednesdays of two-week sitting periods. Advocates do not get to deliver speeches; the justices use the hour to probe weak joints in the briefs, float hypotheticals, and — often visibly — negotiate with each other through their questions.

## The conference, the assignment, and the opinion

The decision happens back behind the closed door. When the Court is sitting, the justices confer on Wednesday afternoons and on Fridays, discussing that week's argued cases. The protocol is strict seniority: the Chief Justice states his view first, then each associate justice in descending order of service, each speaking without interruption. Then the tentative vote is taken.

Now comes the lever that gives the Chief Justiceship its quiet power: **opinion assignment**. If the Chief Justice is in the majority, he decides who writes the opinion of the Court — himself or any colleague in the majority. If he is in dissent, the most senior associate justice in the majority assigns. Assignment shapes doctrine: a 5–4 majority opinion assigned to its most cautious member will be written narrowly, because the author must hold five votes through every draft. A justice who agrees with the outcome but not the reasoning writes a *concurrence*; those who disagree write *dissents*. Drafts circulate for months, and votes occasionally flip — on rare occasions a circulating dissent has picked up a fifth vote and become the opinion of the Court. Nothing is law until the decision is announced; from that moment, the majority opinion binds every other court in the country as [[term:precedent]], under the doctrine of [[term:stare-decisis]].

  The **most senior associate justice in the majority** assigns. A justice's chambers runs on a staff of three to four law clerks — recent law graduates who screen petitions, prepare argument questions, and draft. The publication of a single Term's opinions, concurrences, dissents, and orders runs to thousands of pages, and some opinions are revised a dozen times before announcement.

Walk through the flow above and notice where the real choke points sit: almost all of the Court's power over its own agenda is exercised at stages one and two, in writing, in private, before any argument is heard — which is exactly why the next section's development worried people on both sides of the bench.

## The docket in the shadows

Everything described so far is the *merits docket* — the slow, public, fully briefed path. But the Court has always had a second track for emergencies: applications to stay a lower-court ruling or an execution, or to grant an [[term:injunction]], decided in days rather than months, often by unsigned order with little or no stated reasoning. In 2015, law professor William Baude gave this track a name that stuck: the **shadow docket**. (Granting a stay takes five votes, not the rule of four — a mismatch that means four justices can force a case to be heard but cannot pause the ruling below while it is.)

For most of the twentieth century the track stayed quiet. Then it grew — fast. Stephen Vladeck's count, begun in a 2019 Harvard Law Review essay and completed in a 2023 book, became the debate's central statistic: across the sixteen years of the George W. Bush and Obama administrations, the federal government asked the Court for emergency relief **eight times**; across the four years of the first Trump administration it asked **forty-one times**, and the Court granted relief in whole or part in most of them. Major policies — on immigration, the census, COVID-era restrictions, and abortion — were effectively set or unset by orders issued at speed, sometimes near midnight, with no argument and no majority opinion to scrutinize.

The sharpest criticism has come from inside the building. Dissenting when the Court declined to block Texas's S.B. 8 abortion law in September 2021, Justice [[person:elena-kagan]] wrote that "the majority's decision is emblematic of too much of this Court's shadow-docket decisionmaking — which every day becomes more unreasoned, inconsistent, and impossible to defend." Defenders answer that emergencies are real, that lower courts issuing nationwide injunctions forced the Court's hand, and that demanding full opinions on a days-long clock would produce worse reasoning, not better. The Court has since taken some emergency applications more formally — occasionally setting them for argument — but the volume has not returned to its old baseline.

<Checkpoint question="An order arrives at 11:40 p.m.: one paragraph, unsigned, staying a lower court's injunction against a federal policy. Which docket produced it, and what vote did it take?">
  The **emergency (shadow) docket**. A stay requires **five votes** — unlike the rule of four for certiorari — and the order binds the parties immediately even though there was no oral argument, no full briefing, and no signed majority opinion explaining the reasoning. That combination of speed, force, and silence is precisely what the shadow-docket argument is about.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson is not

This lesson explained the machine: the funnel, the conference, the assignment power, the two docket tracks. It deliberately did not ask the harder question the machine exists to serve — *why should nine unelected lawyers get to void what elected majorities enact at all?* Alexander Hamilton answered in Federalist No. 78 that a judiciary with "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment" would be the "least dangerous" branch. Whether he was right — and what originalists, living constitutionalists, and apostles of judicial restraint have made of the question since — is Lesson 2. The fight over restructuring the machine itself — term limits, expansion, jurisdiction, ethics — is Lesson 3.
