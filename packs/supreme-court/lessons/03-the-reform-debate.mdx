# The reform debate

The number nine appears nowhere in the Constitution. Article III creates "one supreme Court," says its judges "shall hold their Offices during good Behaviour," and stops. Everything else — the size of the bench, the shape of its jurisdiction, even whether the justices ride circuit on horseback — was left to Congress, and Congress has used the power: the Court had six seats in 1789, ten in 1863, and briefly seven before the Judiciary Act of 1869 settled on nine. So when, on July 29, 2024, [[person:joe-biden]] became the first sitting president since Franklin Roosevelt to put structural Court reform at the center of his agenda — endorsing eighteen-year term limits and a binding, enforceable ethics code — he was not proposing to amend a sacred number. He was reopening a question the Constitution never closed.

## How the debate caught fire

[[issue:supreme-court-reform]] had lived on law-review pages for decades. Four collisions moved it to the front page.

First, the appointments wars: the year-long blockade of [[person:merrick-garland]]'s 2016 nomination, and the eight-day sprint to confirm Amy Coney Barrett before the 2020 election. Whatever one thinks of either episode, together they taught both parties that the rules of the confirmation game are whatever the Senate majority says they are.

Second, the docket's stakes rose. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled *Roe v. Wade* after a half-century; *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) overruled the [[issue:chevron-deference-doctrine]], shifting power over the administrative state from agencies toward judges. Public confidence was sliding before the ruling even landed: polled in early June 2022 — after the *Dobbs* draft leaked, weeks before the decision issued — Gallup measured confidence in the Court at 25 percent, its lowest reading in roughly five decades.

Third, ethics. Investigative reporting by ProPublica in 2023 documented years of undisclosed luxury travel provided to Justice [[person:clarence-thomas]] by a billionaire friend with interests in conservative causes, and an undisclosed 2008 Alaska trip by Justice [[person:samuel-alito]] aboard a hedge-fund manager's jet. Both justices said they had understood the disclosure rules of the time to exempt such personal hospitality.

Fourth, a presidential commission made the arguments respectable. Created by executive order in April 2021, the 34-member Presidential Commission on the Supreme Court of the United States — co-chaired by Bob Bauer and Cristina Rodríguez, with members from across the ideological spectrum — produced a nearly 300-page final report in December 2021, approved unanimously. By design it made no recommendations; its lasting contribution was a map of the terrain, including two findings everyone now cites: "considerable, bipartisan support" among commissioners for eighteen-year term limits, and "profound disagreement" over expanding the bench.

## Term limits: the closest thing to consensus

The standard proposal — refined by scholars across the spectrum and embodied in bills introduced in recent Congresses, such as the Supreme Court TERM Act — is elegant: each justice serves a single, non-renewable **eighteen-year term**, staggered so that one seat turns over every two years. Every presidential term gets exactly two appointments. The actuarial lottery — under which one president may fill three seats and another none, and justices time retirements to friendly administrations — disappears.

The hard question is legal, and [[issue:judicial-term-limits]] turns on three words. Article III says judges hold their offices "during good Behaviour" — the textual anchor of life tenure. A constitutional amendment could clearly impose term limits, but amendments need two-thirds of each chamber and three-quarters of the states. So statutory designs get creative: after eighteen years a justice would not be *removed* but would take **senior status** — remaining a federal judge for life, hearing cases below or stepping in for recusals, while losing the regular seat on the Court's merits docket. Defenders note Congress already defines what "the office" of a judge entails through senior status for lower courts; skeptics answer that a justice involuntarily rotated off the Supreme Court has, in substance, been removed from a distinct office, whatever the statute calls it — and that a reform of this magnitude resting on a contested statutory theory would be litigated before the very justices it demotes.

<Checkpoint question="Why do most 18-year term-limit bills route justices into 'senior status' instead of simply ending their service — and what is the strongest objection anyway?">
  Because Article III's "good Behaviour" clause is read to guarantee **life tenure**, a statute that ended a justice's judicial service outright would be plainly unconstitutional. Senior status keeps the person a life-tenured federal *judge* — only the active Supreme Court seat rotates. The strongest objection: the Supreme Court seat is itself the constitutional office, so compulsory rotation off it is removal by another name — meaning the reform likely requires an amendment, with its far higher bar.
</Checkpoint>

## Expansion: the radioactive lever

Changing the Court's size, by contrast, is legally easy — it takes an ordinary statute, as the seven historical changes prove — and politically explosive, as 1937 proved. Franklin Roosevelt, fresh off a 46-state landslide and facing a Court that had struck down core New Deal statutes, proposed adding a justice for every sitting member over age 70½ who declined to retire: up to six new seats. His own party's Senate buried the plan, and the episode gave American politics the indelible phrase [[issue:court-packing]]. Yet the Court's same-spring shift — Justice Owen Roberts's vote in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish* (1937) upholding a minimum-wage law, remembered as "the switch in time that saved nine" — left a permanent ambiguity: the plan failed, and arguably worked.

The modern revival, the Judiciary Act of 2021, would add four seats (to thirteen, matching the number of circuits). The commission reported "profound disagreement," and the disagreement is about the equilibrium, not partisan symmetry-blindness. Proponents argue the bench's current composition is itself the product of hardball — Garland blocked, Barrett sprinted — and that refusing to respond ratifies the hardball. Opponents, including many sharp critics of the current Court, answer with the escalation problem: a Court expanded by one party at the first opportunity will be re-expanded by the other, and a bench that visibly resets with each unified government is no longer a court in any meaningful sense.

## The quieter levers: jurisdiction and ethics

Two further tools get less airtime but more lawyerly attention.

**Jurisdiction stripping.** Article III gives the Court its appellate jurisdiction "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." The Reconstruction Congress used that clause aggressively: in *Ex parte McCardle* (1869), the Court unanimously accepted that Congress could repeal its jurisdiction over a category of habeas appeals *while McCardle's case was pending*, and dismissed it. The precedent has never been overruled, but neither has it been tested at full strength — *United States v. Klein* (1872) warned that Congress may not use jurisdictional rules to dictate the outcome of cases, and modern scholars debate whether stripping all review of, say, a statute's constitutionality would survive. As a reform, it is a blunt instrument: it does not change who the justices are, only which questions they may answer — and every Congress that wields it hands the same axe to its successors.

**Ethics.** Until late 2023, Supreme Court justices were the only federal judges bound by no code of conduct; the Judicial Conference's code, and the complaint-and-discipline machinery of the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act, apply to the lower courts but not to the justices. On November 13, 2023, responding to the year's reporting, the Court adopted its first formal **Code of Conduct** — largely adapted from the lower-court code. The accompanying statement said the rules were mostly not new, and the document's signal feature, critics immediately noted, is what it lacks: any enforcement mechanism. No one investigates a complaint against a justice; recusal remains each justice's unreviewable personal call. Accountability proposals would change exactly that — a binding code with an inspector general or judicial-conference review, mandatory recusal standards, and gift-and-travel disclosure with teeth. Supporters call it the minimum any other branch lives under; resisters, including voices on the Court, warn that an enforcement body wielding complaints against sitting justices becomes a new pressure point on judicial independence — imagine strategic ethics complaints filed on the eve of every major case.

Read the table column by column and one asymmetry jumps out: the reforms easiest to enact (statutes) are the ones most feared as escalatory, while the reform with the broadest support (term limits) faces the highest legal bar. That asymmetry, more than any argument, explains why so much energy has settled on the ethics lever — the one place where the Court can act on itself.

<Checkpoint question="Congress, angry at a line of rulings, passes a statute stripping the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction over all challenges to a new immigration law — including cases already pending. What is the strongest precedent for the statute, and the strongest limit against it?">
  **For:** *Ex parte McCardle* (1869) — the Court unanimously dismissed a pending case after Congress repealed the jurisdiction it was proceeding under, reading the Exceptions Clause to mean what it says. **Against:** *United States v. Klein* (1872) — Congress may not use jurisdiction to prescribe the outcome of cases or bind courts to an unconstitutional rule of decision; and McCardle itself noted other jurisdictional routes remained open. A statute insulating a law from *all* constitutional review would press past anything the Court has actually upheld.
</Checkpoint>

## Three reform postures

The live debate is less a menu of policies than a collision of three postures toward the institution. Each, at its strongest:

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Institutionalist preservationist">
    The Court's only currency is legitimacy — Hamilton's point that it commands neither sword nor purse — and legitimacy is precisely what every structural intervention spends. The 1937 episode is the cautionary tale read correctly: even a president with the largest electoral mandate in modern history could not persuade his own party that packing the bench was anything but a seizure. Term limits by statute invite a legitimacy crisis worse than the disease — imagine two benches, each claiming to be the lawful Court, after the justices divide on the statute's validity. The composition grievances are real, but they are grievances about *Senate* norms, and should be fixed there. The Court has absorbed Dred Scott, Lochner, and the New Deal confrontation and recovered each time precisely because its structure stayed still while politics moved. Justice Breyer himself — no conservative — spent his final years on the bench warning that structural retaliation, once begun, has no obvious stopping point.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Structural reformer">
    The institution preservationists defend no longer exists. Appointments are already pure power politics — Garland and Barrett settled that — and the result is a bench whose composition reflects hardball and actuarial luck, not any principle anyone will defend out loud. A justice can now serve three decades or more; the stakes of each vacancy approach those of a presidential election, which is why confirmations have become scorched-earth. Eighteen-year staggered terms fix the *mechanism*: no seat is a lottery ticket, no justice times a retirement to a friendly White House — and the commission found the idea drew support across the ideological spectrum, because it has no partisan valence behind the veil of ignorance. Nearly every state supreme court and every major constitutional democracy uses terms or age limits; the United States is the outlier, and the burden of proof belongs on life tenure, an eighteenth-century answer to eighteenth-century life expectancy. As for the escalation objection to expansion: deterrence cuts both ways — a credible threat to respond to court-packing-by-blockade is what makes future blockades irrational.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Accountability reformer">
    Structure is the wrong fight — slow, constitutionally fraught, and beside the point. The fastest-eroding pillar of the Court's standing is not its size or tenure but the discovery that the nine most powerful judges in the country were the only federal judges bound by no enforceable ethics rules at all. Start there, because the public already agrees: disclosure with audits, gift and travel limits, recusal reviewed by someone other than the challenged justice — the apparatus every other branch and every lower court already survives. None of it requires an amendment; most of it has lower-court precedent; and unlike expansion it cannot be caricatured as a partisan power grab, since its rules bind whoever sits. The 2023 code conceded the principle — the justices themselves acknowledged written standards were owed — while withholding the one thing that makes standards real. Finish the job, and the Court's critics lose their best exhibit; skip it, and every structural argument returns with compound interest.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Notice what the three postures actually disagree about. The preservationist and the structural reformer share an empirical dispute — whether intervention or restraint better protects judicial independence over decades. The accountability reformer is making a different move entirely: sequencing, not principle. Many accountability reformers are structural reformers on a delay; many preservationists endorse the ethics agenda as a release valve. The coalitions are fluid because the positions answer different questions.

## What is unsettled

Nearly everything. Whether term limits can be done by statute is an open constitutional question no court has answered. Whether expansion would rebalance the Court or destroy it is a prediction, not a fact, and the 1937 precedent is claimed by both sides. Whether McCardle would hold if Congress stripped review at full strength has never been tested. Whether the 2023 code acquires enforcement — by the Court's own hand or by a statute whose constitutionality the justices would themselves judge — is pending as you read. The 2021 commission, with thirty-four of the country's best-credentialed disagreers, looked at all of it and declined, unanimously, to recommend; the 2024 presidential endorsement moved term limits and ethics from the seminar room to the platform, where they remain unenacted. What this pack can give you is the machinery (Lesson 1), the stakes of the power itself (Lesson 2), and the honest shape of the fight over its housing — which is now, unavoidably, part of the machinery too.
