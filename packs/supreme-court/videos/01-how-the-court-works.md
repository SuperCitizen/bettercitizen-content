## Behind the closed door

On conference days, nine people walk into a room, shake hands, and close the door. No clerks. No secretaries. No recording. By tradition, the most junior justice takes notes and answers any knock. Inside that room, the Supreme Court decides which disputes get a hearing at all.

- No clerks, no secretaries, no recording

> To understand the Court, start with the machinery the door conceals.

## Thousands ask, dozens are answered

Almost no one has a right to be heard by the Supreme Court. The party who lost below must petition for review. Roughly 5,000 to 7,000 new cases arrive every Term. About 80 get full briefing and oral argument. Roughly 98 percent of petitions are denied, without explanation.

- Petitions: 5,000 to 7,000 per Term
- Argued: about 80

The Court does not sit to correct every error. What gets through is a reason about the legal system: above all, appeals courts in different regions reading the same federal law in conflicting ways.

> A denial sets no precedent and implies no agreement with the decision below.

## Four votes, and five

Four of the nine justices suffice to take a case. This is the rule of four, and it keeps five justices from controlling the agenda completely.

- Rule of four: four votes grant review

The Court also has a second track, for emergencies: applications to stay a lower-court ruling, decided in days and not months, often by unsigned order with little or no stated reasoning. A stay takes five votes. So four justices can force a case to be heard, but cannot pause the ruling below while it is.

- A stay: five votes

## The docket in the shadows

In 2015 that second track got a name that stuck: the shadow docket. Across the sixteen years of the George W. Bush and Obama administrations, the federal government asked the Court for emergency relief 8 times. Across the four years of the first Trump administration it asked 41 times, and the Court granted relief in whole or in part in most of them.

- Sixteen years: 8 requests
- Four years: 41 requests

Justice Elena Kagan, in dissent, wrote that this decisionmaking "every day becomes more unreasoned, inconsistent, and impossible to defend." Defenders answer that emergencies are real, and that demanding full opinions on a days-long clock would produce worse reasoning, not better.

- Kagan: "unreasoned, inconsistent"
- Defenders: emergencies are real
