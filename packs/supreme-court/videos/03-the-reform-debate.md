## The number nine

The number nine appears nowhere in the Constitution. Article Three creates "one supreme Court," says its judges hold their offices "during good Behaviour," and stops. The rest was left to Congress. The Court had six seats in 1789, ten in 1863, and nine since 1869.

- 1789: six seats. 1863: ten. 1869: nine.

> The Court's structure is a question the Constitution never closed.

## Term limits

The standard proposal: each justice serves a single eighteen-year term, staggered so that one seat turns over every two years. Every presidential term gets exactly two appointments.

- One 18-year term, staggered
- Two appointments per presidential term

The hard question is legal. "During good Behaviour" is the textual anchor of life tenure. So the bills would not remove a justice after eighteen years. They would move the justice to senior status: still a federal judge for life, but off the regular seat. Defenders note that senior status already exists for lower-court judges. Skeptics answer that this is removal, whatever the statute calls it.

- "Good Behaviour": life tenure

## Expansion

Changing the Court's size is legally easy. It takes an ordinary statute, and Congress has changed the number seven times. Politically it is explosive. In 1937 Franklin Roosevelt proposed up to six new seats, and his own party's Senate buried the plan.

- Size: an ordinary statute
- 1937: the court-packing plan fails

> Easiest to enact, most feared. Broadest support, highest legal bar.

## Three postures

Three postures collide. The institutionalist preservationist argues that the Court's only currency is legitimacy, and that every structural intervention spends it. This posture points to 1937, when even a president with a landslide behind him could not persuade his own party.

- Preservationist: structure spends legitimacy

The structural reformer argues that appointments are already pure power politics, and that eighteen-year terms fix the mechanism, so that no seat is a lottery ticket. This posture points to the 2021 presidential commission, which found support for the idea across the ideological spectrum.

- Structural reformer: fix the mechanism

The accountability reformer argues that structure is the wrong fight, and that ethics comes first. This posture points to the Court's first Code of Conduct, adopted in November 2023 with no enforcement mechanism: no one investigates a complaint against a justice.

- Accountability reformer: enforceable ethics first

## What is unsettled

Nearly everything. Whether term limits can be done by statute is a question no court has answered. That commission, with its 34 members, studied all of it and, by design, made no recommendations. The lesson gives each posture at its strongest, and a fourth lever: stripping the Court's jurisdiction.

- 2021 commission: no recommendations
