## The commission left on a desk

In the last days of his presidency, John Adams signed commissions for dozens of new judges. In the rush, some were never delivered. Thomas Jefferson took office in March 1801, found them on a desk, and told his Secretary of State, James Madison, to leave them there. One belonged to William Marbury, who sued, directly in the Supreme Court.

- Signed and sealed, never delivered

> Out of a clerical accident, the Court claimed its greatest power.

## Marshall's third path

Chief Justice John Marshall faced a trap. If the Court ordered Madison to hand over the commission, Jefferson would simply ignore the order. If it refused, it would confirm that judges served at the executive's pleasure.

In 1803, in Marbury v. Madison, the Court held that Marbury had a legal right to his commission. But it held that the Court itself could not give him a remedy, because the statute he had sued under went beyond what Article Three allows. A statute that conflicts with the Constitution, Marshall reasoned, is not law at all.

- Marbury had a right to his commission
- The statute went beyond Article III

Jefferson won the case, so there was nothing to defy. And in declining power, the Court asserted a far larger one.

> Judicial review: the authority to strike down acts of Congress.

## Nine who cannot be voted out

In 1962 the Yale law professor Alexander Bickel named the problem: "judicial review is a counter-majoritarian force in our system." When the Court strikes down a statute, it thwarts the choice of elected representatives, who can be voted out, on the authority of nine lawyers who cannot.

- Bickel, 1962: the countermajoritarian difficulty

> The difficulty has never been dissolved, only managed.

## Three answers to one question

Three schools dominate. The originalist argues that the Constitution's words had a public meaning when they were ratified, and that meaning, not the judge's values, is the law.

- Originalist: the meaning when ratified

The living constitutionalist argues that the Framers chose majestic generalities deliberately, and that judges should read them to serve the document's purpose in conditions the Framers could not have imagined.

- Living constitutionalist: purpose, in new conditions

The Thayerian, the school of restraint, argues that a court may strike down a statute only when the legislature's mistake is very clear. If the statute's constitutionality is fairly debatable, that is enough to sustain it.

- Restraint: strike only a clear mistake

> Two are theories of meaning. Restraint is a theory of who decides.

## What the lesson holds

The lesson gives each school its strongest telling, and its sharpest challenge to the others. It does not say which is right. Each school's strongest version survives its rivals' best attacks.

- No school is declared right
