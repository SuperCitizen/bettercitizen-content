Picture a room in the building across from the Capitol. It is a conference day, and the Supreme Court is in session.

Nine people walk in. They shake hands. And they shut the door.

[beat]

No clerks. No secretaries. No recording. By tradition, the most junior justice takes the notes, and answers any knock.

[pause]

Inside that room the Court decides two things. It decides which of the thousands of disputes Americans have begged it to hear will get a hearing at all. And it decides how the cases already argued will come out.

Nearly everything the public sees happens outside that door: the marble plaza, the televised confirmation wars, the scrums on opinion day.

To understand the Court, start with the machinery behind that door.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to follow one case through that machinery, from a request for review to a published opinion. And you will be able to say who controls each stage.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the funnel: how thousands of requests become a few dozen cases.

Second, the public phase: the briefs and the oral argument.

Third, back behind the door: the vote, and the quiet power of choosing who writes.

And last, a second track, faster and quieter than the first, whose growth has started an argument.

## The funnel

Start with a fact that surprises people. [beat] Almost no one has a right to be heard by the Supreme Court.

Suppose you lose in a federal court of appeals, or in a state's highest court. You cannot just move up a level. You have to ask. You file a petition, and what you are asking for has a formal name: a writ of certiorari. It is an order telling the lower court to send the case up.

How many people ask? By the Court's own description, roughly 5,000 to 7,000 new cases arrive every Term. A Term is the Court's year, and it starts on the first Monday in October.

And how many get the full treatment, with full briefing and oral argument? [beat] About 80. In recent Terms, the argued docket has often run closer to sixty.

Another hundred or so are disposed of without argument. Everyone else gets a denial, one line long.

> Thousands ask. Dozens are answered.

A denial means less than you might think. It sets no precedent, and it implies no agreement with the lower court's decision. The Court has declined to look, and that is all.

So what gets through?

The Court's own Rule 10 says that review is granted "only for compelling reasons." And here is the part to hold on to. The reasons are about the legal system. [beat] They are not about the parties.

The classic trigger is called a circuit split. Federal appeals courts in different regions of the country are reading the same federal law in conflicting ways. So the law means one thing in Texas, and another thing in Vermont.

National importance helps. A sympathetic party with a badly decided case generally does not help, because the Court does not sit to correct every error made in the lower courts.

> The Court is not an error-correction service.

Put another way: the justices are not asking whether this person was wronged. They are asking whether the country's law needs a single answer.

Let's test that. Three petitions arrive at the Court.

The first comes from a sympathetic small business that clearly lost, on bad reasoning, in the lower court.

The second raises a technical tax question, on which the Fifth Circuit and the Ninth Circuit have issued squarely conflicting rules.

The third is a constitutional claim that the petitioner feels passionately about.

Which one is most likely to be granted? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The tax question.

Rule 10 points at reasons that belong to the system, and above all at conflicting interpretations of federal law among the circuits. The small business may have every sympathy on its side. But a wrongly decided case that stands alone usually stays wrongly decided. That is why roughly 98 percent of petitions are denied.

[pause]

Now, how do nine people sort thousands of petitions? The sorting is industrial.

Most of the justices pool their law clerks into a shared arrangement called the cert pool. Each incoming petition goes to one clerk. That clerk writes a memo that summarizes the case and recommends a grant or a denial, and the memo goes to every chamber that takes part.

A justice who wants a petition talked about flags it, and it goes onto what is called the discuss list. The rest are denied without ever being discussed at all.

Then, at conference, the justices vote. And here is a number to keep. It does not take a majority of the nine to hear a case. [beat] It takes four.

> The rule of four: four of the nine justices can take a case.

Why four? It is a deliberate protection for the minority. It keeps five justices from controlling the agenda completely.

## From briefs to the bench

Once review is granted, the door opens, and the case enters its public phase.

First come the briefs. The side that asked for review is the petitioner, and it files a brief on the merits. The other side, the respondent, answers. Replies follow.

Around those briefs a second literature piles up. Governments, industries, scholars and advocacy groups are not parties to the case, but they want the justices to see the stakes their way. What they file is called an amicus brief. And when the federal government is not already a party, the Solicitor General often weighs in for the United States, and is sometimes invited to.

Then comes oral argument. It is the only part of the process most people ever see, and it is shorter than its reputation. Each side gets roughly thirty minutes, and most of that time is consumed by the justices' questions.

Arguments run from October through April. Advocates do not get to deliver speeches. The justices use the hour to probe the weak joints in the briefs, to float hypotheticals, and, often visibly, to negotiate with each other through their questions.

Think about that last one. The lawyers are doing the answering. But some of those questions are really aimed at the other justices on the bench.

## Behind the door again

Then the door shuts again, because the decision is not made in the courtroom. It is made back in the conference room.

When the Court is sitting, the justices confer on Wednesday afternoons and on Fridays, and they discuss that week's argued cases. The protocol is strict seniority. The Chief Justice states his view first. Then each associate justice speaks, in descending order of service, and nobody interrupts. Only then is a tentative vote taken.

[pause]

Now comes the lever that gives the office of Chief Justice its quiet power. It is called opinion assignment.

If the Chief Justice is in the majority, he decides who writes the opinion of the Court: himself, or any colleague in the majority. If he is in dissent, the choice passes to the most senior associate justice in the majority.

Why should that matter so much? Because assignment shapes the law that comes out.

Take a case decided five to four. Assign the opinion to the most cautious member of that majority, and it will be written narrowly. It has to be, because the author cannot afford to lose a single vote.

> The author must hold five votes through every draft.

Not every justice signs on to the reasoning. A justice who agrees with the outcome, but not with the reasoning, writes a concurrence. Those who disagree with the outcome write dissents.

And then the drafts circulate, for months. Votes occasionally flip. On rare occasions a dissent in circulation has picked up a fifth vote, [beat] and become the opinion of the Court.

[pause]

Let's put the assignment rule to work. The Court votes six to three, and the Chief Justice is among the three dissenters.

Who assigns the majority opinion? And why might that assignment matter as much as the vote? Think it through.

[long pause]

The most senior associate justice in the majority assigns. The Chief is in dissent, so the lever passes down the line.

And it matters because the author controls how broad the opinion is. The same six votes can produce a ruling that is good for one case only. Or they can produce a doctrine that governs hundreds of future disputes.

> Assignment is agenda-setting by other means.

One rule remains, and it is the last step. Nothing is law until the decision is announced, and most announcements come in May and June. From that moment the majority opinion binds every other court in the country. It binds them as precedent, under a doctrine called stare decisis.

So here is the whole path, in order. A petition. Four votes to take it. The briefs. An hour of argument. A conference, and a tentative vote. An assignment, and months of drafts. And then an announcement.

Notice where the real choke points sit. Almost all of the Court's power over its own agenda is used at the first two steps: in writing, in private, before any argument is heard.

[pause]

## The second track

Everything so far has a name. It is the merits docket: the slow, public, fully briefed path.

Now picture a different kind of decision.

An order arrives at 11:40 at night. It is one paragraph long, and it is unsigned. A lower court had issued an injunction against a federal policy. This order stays that injunction, which is to say, it puts the injunction on hold.

There was no oral argument. There was no full briefing. There is no signed majority opinion to explain the reasoning. [beat] And the order binds the parties immediately.

[pause]

That order did not come from the merits docket. The Court has always had a second track, for emergencies. It takes applications to stay a lower-court ruling, or to stay an execution, or to grant an injunction. They are decided in days rather than months, often by an unsigned order, with little or no stated reasoning.

In 2015 a law professor, William Baude, gave this track a name that stuck. [beat] The shadow docket.

Before we go on, pull one number back. Near the start I gave you the vote it takes to hear a case. How many justices?

[long pause]

Four. The rule of four.

A stay is different. To grant a stay takes five.

> Four votes to hear a case. Five to grant a stay.

Consider what that mismatch allows. Four justices can force a case to be heard. But those same four cannot pause the lower court's ruling while the case is heard.

For most of the twentieth century, this second track stayed quiet. Then it grew, and it grew fast.

The count that became the central statistic of the debate is Stephen Vladeck's. He began it in a Harvard Law Review essay, and he completed it in a book in 2023.

Across the sixteen years of the George W. Bush and Obama administrations, the federal government asked the Court for emergency relief eight times.

Across the four years of the first Trump administration, it asked forty-one times. [beat] And the Court granted relief, in whole or in part, in most of them.

[pause]

Eight times in sixteen years. Then forty-one times in four.

Major policies were, in effect, set or unset this way: on immigration, on the census, on COVID-era restrictions, on abortion. It was done by orders issued at speed, sometimes near midnight, with no argument and no majority opinion to scrutinize.

Speed, force, and silence. That combination is what the argument is about.

## The argument over the second track

So is the growth of this track a problem? Here the ground is contested, and each side deserves its strongest telling.

Begin with what the two sides share. The Court has always had an emergency track, and the government's use of it grew. What they dispute is what to make of that.

First, the critics.

The sharpest criticism has come from inside the building. In September 2021 the Court declined to block a Texas abortion law, and Justice Elena Kagan dissented. Listen for the three charges at the end of her sentence.

"The majority's decision is emblematic of too much of this Court's shadow-docket decisionmaking, [beat] which every day becomes more unreasoned, inconsistent, and impossible to defend."

[pause]

Unreasoned. Inconsistent. Impossible to defend.

What are the critics trying to protect? Everything the merits docket supplies: argument, full briefing, and a signed opinion whose reasoning can be scrutinized. They argue that an order which sets national policy should come with its reasons. And they point to the count: forty-one requests in four years, and major policies decided at speed.

> The critics' case: orders that set major policy should come with reasons.

Now, the defenders.

What are the defenders trying to protect? The Court's ability to answer when an emergency is real. They argue that emergencies are real, and that an application of this kind has to be answered in days. They argue that demanding a full opinion on that clock would produce worse reasoning, not better. And they point to the lower courts, whose nationwide injunctions, on this account, forced the Court's hand.

> The defenders' case: emergencies are real, and a rushed opinion reasons worse.

What does the record settle? It settles that the track grew. Since then the Court has taken some emergency applications more formally, occasionally setting them for argument. But the volume has not returned to its old baseline.

What the record cannot settle is which cost is worse: an order without reasons, or reasons written against the clock. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

[pause]

So take the side you find less convincing, the critics or the defenders. State its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The funnel. Thousands ask, and dozens are answered. Roughly 5,000 to 7,000 petitions arrive each Term, and about 80 cases are argued. The Court chooses for the sake of the legal system, not the parties. It is not an error-correction service.

[beat]

Two. The rule of four. Four of the nine justices are enough to take a case. A stay takes five.

[beat]

Three. The decision is made in conference, in order of seniority, and then the opinion is assigned. The Chief Justice assigns when he is in the majority. When he is not, the most senior associate justice in the majority does. The author must hold five votes through every draft, and nothing is law until the decision is announced.

[beat]

And four. There are two dockets. The merits docket is slow, public and fully briefed. The emergency docket, the shadow docket, decides in days, often with no signed opinion. Its critics argue that orders which set major policy should come with reasons. Its defenders argue that emergencies are real, and that a rushed opinion reasons worse.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not asked. We have the machine: the funnel, the conference, the assignment power, the two tracks. We have not asked the harder question that the machine exists to serve. Why should nine unelected lawyers get to void what elected majorities enact at all?

[pause]

Alexander Hamilton had an answer, in Federalist 78. A judiciary with "neither force nor will, but merely judgment," he wrote, would be the "least dangerous" branch.

Was he right? And what have originalists, living constitutionalists, and the advocates of judicial restraint made of the question since? That is the next lecture. The fight over rebuilding the machine itself, through term limits, expansion, jurisdiction and ethics, is the one after that.

For now, go back to that room. Nine people, a handshake, and a shut door. [beat] You know what happens behind it now. The next question is why that room should hold so much power at all.
