Here is a number you will not find in the Constitution. [beat] Nine.

Article Three creates "one supreme Court." It says that its judges "shall hold their Offices during good Behaviour." [beat] And then it stops.

Everything else was left to Congress: the size of the bench, the shape of its jurisdiction, even whether the justices would ride circuit on horseback.

And Congress has used that power. The Court had six seats in 1789. It had ten in 1863. It briefly had seven. [beat] Then the Judiciary Act of 1869 settled on nine.

> The number nine appears nowhere in the Constitution.

Now come forward to July 29, 2024. On that day Joe Biden became the first sitting president since Franklin Roosevelt to put structural reform of the Court at the center of his agenda. He endorsed eighteen-year term limits for the justices, and a binding, enforceable ethics code.

He was not proposing to amend a sacred number. He was reopening a question the Constitution never closed.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture you will be able to name the four main levers of reform, and say what each one would legally require. And you will be able to state the three postures people take toward pulling them.

We will take it in four steps.

First, how the debate caught fire.

Second, the two loud levers: term limits, and expansion.

Third, the two quieter ones: jurisdiction, and ethics.

And last, the three postures.

## How the debate caught fire

Reform of the Court had lived on law-review pages for decades. Four collisions moved it to the front page.

First, the appointments wars. In 2016 the Senate majority declined, for nearly a year, to act on the nomination of Merrick Garland. In 2020 Amy Coney Barrett was confirmed eight days before the presidential election. Whatever one thinks of either episode, together they taught both parties the same thing: the rules of the confirmation game are whatever the Senate majority says they are.

[pause]

Second, the stakes of the docket rose. In 2022, in a case called Dobbs, the Court overruled Roe v. Wade after a half-century.

Third, ethics. In 2023, investigative reporting by ProPublica documented years of undisclosed luxury travel, provided to Justice Clarence Thomas by a billionaire friend. It also documented an undisclosed trip to Alaska by Justice Samuel Alito, aboard a hedge-fund manager's jet. Both justices said they had understood the disclosure rules of the time to exempt such personal hospitality.

And fourth, a presidential commission made the arguments respectable.

It was created by executive order in April 2021. It had 34 members, from across the ideological spectrum. That December it produced a final report, approved unanimously.

And by design, it made no recommendations.

> The commission mapped the ground and, by design, recommended nothing.

What it left behind was a map of the terrain, with two findings that everyone now cites. Among the commissioners there was "considerable, bipartisan support" for eighteen-year term limits. [beat] And there was "profound disagreement" over expanding the bench.

Hold on to those two phrases. Considerable, bipartisan support. Profound disagreement. They mark the difference between the two loudest levers.

## The loud levers

Start with term limits, the closest thing to consensus.

The standard proposal is elegant. Each justice serves a single term of eighteen years, which cannot be renewed. The terms are staggered, so that one seat turns over every two years. Every presidential term gets exactly two appointments.

[beat]

What disappears is the actuarial lottery, in which one president may fill three seats and another none, and justices time their retirements to friendly administrations.

The hard question is legal, and it turns on three words: [beat] "during good Behaviour." That phrase in Article Three is the textual anchor of life tenure.

A constitutional amendment could clearly impose term limits. But an amendment needs two-thirds of each chamber of Congress, and three-quarters of the states.

So the designs that work by statute get creative. After eighteen years, a justice would not be removed. [beat] The justice would take what is called senior status. That means remaining a federal judge for life, hearing cases in the lower courts, or stepping in when a justice is recused. What goes is the regular seat on the Court's merits docket.

Let's stop and work something out. Why would a term-limit bill route a justice into senior status, instead of simply ending the justice's service? And what is the strongest thing a skeptic could say back? Think it through.

[long pause]

The first half comes from those three words. "Good behaviour" is understood to guarantee life tenure, so a statute that ended a justice's judicial service outright would be plainly unconstitutional. Under senior status, only the active seat on the Supreme Court rotates.

Defenders of the design argue that Congress already defines what the office of a judge entails, through senior status for the lower courts.

Skeptics argue that the seat on the Supreme Court is itself the constitutional office. A justice rotated off it involuntarily has, in substance, been removed, whatever the statute calls it. That is the strongest objection: removal by another name. [beat] And if it holds, the reform needs an amendment after all, with its far higher bar.

[pause]

Now expansion, and here everything reverses.

At the start of this lecture I told you how the Court came to have nine seats. So what would it take, legally, to give it thirteen? Take a moment.

[long pause]

An ordinary act of Congress, signed by the president. [beat] No amendment. Congress has changed the Court's size seven times by statute, most recently in 1869. The barrier to expansion is political, not constitutional.

> Expansion is legally easy and politically explosive.

How explosive? The proof is 1937.

Franklin Roosevelt was fresh off a landslide in which he had carried 46 states. He faced a Court that had struck down core New Deal statutes. So he proposed to add up to six new justices.

The Senate, controlled by his own party, buried the plan. And the episode gave American politics an indelible phrase: court-packing.

Yet that same spring the Court shifted, in a move remembered as "the switch in time that saved nine." And that left a permanent ambiguity. The plan failed. [beat] And, arguably, it worked.

[pause]

The modern revival is a proposal called the Judiciary Act of 2021. It would add four seats, for a Court of thirteen. This is where the commission reported its profound disagreement.

Proponents argue that the bench's current composition is itself the product of hardball: Garland blocked, Barrett sprinted. Refusing to respond, they argue, ratifies the hardball.

Opponents, and they include many sharp critics of the current Court, argue from escalation. A Court expanded by one party at the first opportunity will be expanded again by the other. And a bench that visibly resets with each unified government is no longer a court in any meaningful sense.

## The quieter levers

Two further tools get less airtime, and more attention from lawyers.

The first is jurisdiction stripping. Article Three gives the Court its appellate jurisdiction "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." In plainer words, Congress can take categories of appeals away from the Court.

The Reconstruction Congress used that clause aggressively. In 1869, in a case called Ex parte McCardle, Congress repealed the Court's jurisdiction over a category of appeals while the case itself was pending. The Court unanimously accepted that Congress could do it, and dismissed the case.

That precedent has never been overruled. But neither has it been tested at full strength. In 1872, in a case called Klein, the Court warned that Congress may not use jurisdictional rules to dictate the outcome of cases.

So the strongest precedent for this lever is McCardle. [beat] The strongest limit against it is Klein.

As a reform, it is a blunt instrument. It does not change who the justices are, only which questions they may answer. And every Congress that wields it hands the same axe to its successors.

[pause]

The second quiet lever is ethics.

Until late 2023, the justices of the Supreme Court were the only federal judges bound by no code of conduct. The lower courts have a code, and machinery for complaints and discipline. Neither applied to the justices.

On November 13, 2023, responding to that year's reporting, the Court adopted its first formal Code of Conduct. The statement that came with it said the rules were mostly not new.

Critics immediately noted the document's signal feature, which is what it lacks: any means of enforcement. No one investigates a complaint against a justice. And recusal, a justice's stepping aside from a case, remains each justice's personal call, which no one reviews.

> The Court's first ethics code has no enforcement mechanism.

Accountability proposals would change exactly that. They call for a binding code, and for someone to enforce it: an inspector general, or review by the judicial conference.

Resisters, and they include voices on the Court, argue that an enforcement body wielding complaints against sitting justices becomes a new pressure point on judicial independence. Imagine strategic ethics complaints, filed on the eve of every major case.

Supporters argue that enforcement is the minimum any other branch has to live under.

Now set the four levers side by side: term limits, expansion, jurisdiction stripping, ethics. Which one drew the broadest support? And is it one of the easy ones to enact? Think back.

[long pause]

Term limits drew the broadest support. [beat] And term limits face the highest legal bar. Meanwhile the reforms that are easiest to enact, the ones that need only a statute, are the ones most feared as escalation.

> The reform with the broadest support faces the highest legal bar.

That asymmetry, more than any argument, explains why so much energy has settled on ethics. It is the one place where the Court can act on itself.

## Three postures

That is the menu of policies. But the debate as it is fought is less a menu than a collision of three postures toward the institution.

Here is each at its strongest. For each one, listen for the same things: what it is trying to protect, its strongest argument, and what it points to.

First, the institutionalist preservationist.

The preservationist is trying to protect the Court's legitimacy. The argument begins with Hamilton's point, that the Court commands neither sword nor purse. Legitimacy is its only currency, and legitimacy is precisely what every structural intervention spends.

The preservationist argues that term limits by statute invite a legitimacy crisis worse than the disease. Imagine the justices dividing over whether the statute is valid. Imagine two benches, [beat] each claiming to be the lawful Court.

The grievances about composition are real, on this view. But they are grievances about Senate norms, and they should be fixed there.

And the preservationist points to the record. In 1937, even a president with the largest electoral mandate in modern history could not persuade his own party that packing the bench was anything but a seizure. The Court has absorbed Dred Scott, Lochner, and the New Deal confrontation, and recovered each time, precisely because its structure stayed still while politics moved. And Justice Breyer, no conservative, spent his final years on the bench warning that structural retaliation, once begun, has no obvious stopping point.

> The preservationist case: legitimacy is the Court's only currency, and structural change spends it.

Second, the structural reformer.

The structural reformer is trying to protect a bench whose composition rests on principle. The argument begins by rejecting the premise: the institution that preservationists defend no longer exists. Appointments are already pure power politics. Garland and Barrett settled that. And the result is a bench that reflects hardball and actuarial luck, not any principle anyone will defend out loud.

The structural reformer argues that eighteen-year staggered terms fix the mechanism. A justice can now serve three decades or more, so the stakes of each vacancy approach those of a presidential election. That is why confirmations have become scorched-earth. Under fixed terms, no seat is a lottery ticket, and no justice times a retirement to a friendly White House.

To the escalation objection, the structural reformer argues that deterrence cuts both ways. A credible threat to respond to court-packing by blockade is what makes future blockades irrational.

And the structural reformer points to the record. The commission found that term limits drew support across the ideological spectrum. Nearly every state supreme court, and every major constitutional democracy, uses terms or age limits. The United States is the outlier. So the burden of proof, on this view, belongs on life tenure: an eighteenth-century answer to eighteenth-century life expectancy.

> The structural reformer's case: fix the mechanism, so no seat is a lottery ticket.

Third, the accountability reformer.

The accountability reformer is trying to protect the Court's standing. The argument begins by setting structure aside as the wrong fight: slow, constitutionally fraught, and beside the point. The fastest-eroding pillar of the Court's standing is not its size or its tenure. It is the discovery that the nine most powerful judges in the country were the only federal judges bound by no enforceable ethics rules at all.

The accountability reformer argues for starting there, because the public already agrees. Disclosure, with audits. Limits on gifts and travel. Recusal reviewed by someone other than the challenged justice. It is the apparatus that every other branch and every lower court already survives.

None of it requires an amendment, on this view, and most of it has precedent in the lower courts. And unlike expansion, it cannot be caricatured as a partisan power grab, because its rules bind whoever sits.

And the accountability reformer points to the record. The 2023 code conceded the principle: the justices themselves acknowledged that written standards were owed. What it withheld is the one thing that makes standards real. Finish the job, and the Court's critics lose their best exhibit. [beat] Skip it, and every structural argument returns with compound interest.

> The accountability reformer's case: start with ethics rules that bind whoever sits.

[pause]

Notice what the three actually disagree about.

The preservationist and the structural reformer share an empirical dispute: whether intervention or restraint better protects judicial independence over decades.

The accountability reformer is making a different move entirely. It is about sequencing, not principle. Many accountability reformers are structural reformers on a delay. Many preservationists endorse the ethics agenda as a release valve. The coalitions are fluid, because the positions answer different questions.

So take the posture you find least convincing. State its best case, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The number nine appears nowhere in the Constitution. Congress set it by statute, in 1869.

[beat]

Two. Four levers, and what each would take. Term limits: an amendment, or a contested senior-status statute, because of "good behaviour." Expansion: an ordinary statute. Jurisdiction stripping: also a statute, with McCardle for it and Klein against it. Ethics: the 2023 code exists, and it has no enforcement mechanism.

[beat]

Three. The asymmetry. The reform with the broadest support faces the highest legal bar. The reforms easiest to enact are the ones most feared.

[beat]

Four. The 2021 commission mapped the ground and, by design, recommended nothing. It found considerable, bipartisan support for term limits, and profound disagreement over expansion.

[beat]

And five. Three postures. The preservationist: legitimacy is the Court's only currency, and structural change spends it. The structural reformer: fix the mechanism, so no seat is a lottery ticket. The accountability reformer: start with ethics rules that bind whoever sits.

[pause]

What is unsettled? [beat] Nearly everything.

Whether term limits can be done by statute is an open constitutional question, and no court has answered it. Whether expansion would rebalance the Court or destroy it is a prediction, not a fact, and both sides claim 1937. And whether the 2023 code acquires enforcement is still pending: by the Court's own hand, or by a statute whose constitutionality the justices would themselves judge.

The president's endorsement in 2024 moved term limits and ethics from the seminar room to the platform. There they remain, unenacted.

[pause]

This is the last lecture of the three, so here is where the argument goes. The first lecture gave you the machinery. The second gave you the stakes of the power itself. This one gave you the fight over the housing of that power. And that fight is now, unavoidably, part of the machinery too.

So go back to where we started. Article Three creates one supreme Court, gives its judges their offices during good behaviour, and stops. [beat] Everything after that stop was left open. It is open still.
