It is the last days of John Adams's presidency, and Adams is working late.

He is signing commissions, dozens of them, for new judges and justices of the peace. They are Federalist loyalists, and he is packing them into the judiciary before Thomas Jefferson's incoming Republicans can stop him.

The commissions are signed. They are sealed. But in the rush, some of them are never delivered.

[beat]

In March 1801, Jefferson takes office. He finds a stack of those commissions sitting on a desk in the State Department. And he tells his Secretary of State, James Madison, to leave them there.

One of them belongs to a Maryland businessman named William Marbury. Marbury wants his minor judgeship badly enough to sue, and he sues directly in the Supreme Court.

[pause]

Out of that clerical accident came the most consequential power any court in the world has ever claimed for itself.

[pause]

The power is called judicial review: the authority of a court to strike down an act of Congress. Last time we walked through the Court's machinery, and we left the hardest question standing. Why should unelected judges be able to void what elected majorities enact?

By the end of this lecture you will be able to say three things. Where that power came from. What the deepest objection to it is. And how three schools of thought answer the objection.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the case: how John Marshall claimed the power.

Second, the classic defense of it, and what the record did to that defense.

Third, the difficulty that every defense has to answer.

And last, three answers, each in its strongest form.

## The case that armed the Court

Begin with the man who had to decide Marbury's case: Chief Justice John Marshall.

There is an irony here that no novelist would risk. Marshall had been Adams's Secretary of State. He was the one who had failed to deliver the very commissions at issue.

And now he faced a trap.

[beat]

Suppose the Court orders Madison to hand over the commission. Jefferson simply ignores the order, and the young Court is exposed as powerless.

Suppose instead the Court refuses. Then it confirms that the judiciary serves at the executive's pleasure.

Either path diminishes the institution.

[pause]

Marshall's opinion came in 1803, in Marbury v. Madison, and it found a third path. It is worth slowing down to see how the trick works, because it has two steps.

Step one. The Court held that Marbury had a legal right to his commission, and that the law owed him a remedy. That finding scolded Jefferson on the merits.

Step two. The Court held that it could not give him that remedy itself. Marbury had sued under a statute: Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789. That section was understood to let suits like his start in the Supreme Court. And that, the Court held, enlarged its original jurisdiction beyond what Article Three allows.

So a statute and the Constitution collide. What then?

A statute that conflicts with the Constitution, Marshall reasoned, is not law at all. When the two collide, a court must apply the Constitution. And then he wrote the sentence to remember.

"It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

[pause]

To say what the law is.

Now look at who won. Marbury never got his judgeship. [beat] Jefferson got the outcome he wanted.

So why was it strategically brilliant for Marshall to rule against Marbury, rather than for him? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because an order in Marbury's favor would have been ignored, and that would have exposed the Court's weakness. And a simple surrender would have conceded that the judiciary was subordinate.

By striking down Section 13 instead, Marshall gave Jefferson the bottom-line result he wanted. There was nothing for the administration to defy. And in the same opinion, he established the precedent that courts may void acts of Congress.

> In the act of declining power, the Court claimed a far larger one.

Nothing in Article Three grants that authority in so many words. Marshall inferred it: from the logic of a written constitution, from the judicial oath, and from the Supremacy Clause. And then the Court did not use it against another federal statute for half a century.

## Hamilton's defense, and the record

That is how the power arrived. Now, how is it defended?

The idea did not appear from nowhere. Years before Marbury, in Federalist 78, Alexander Hamilton had argued that the judiciary would be "the least dangerous" branch. It holds, he wrote, "neither force nor will, but merely judgment."

[beat]

Neither force nor will. Merely judgment.

And Hamilton went further. Judicial review, he argued, is not the supremacy of judges over the people. It is agency for the people.

Here is the logic. The Constitution is the people's own enactment. An ordinary statute is merely the act of their agents in the legislature. So when the two conflict, a court that honors the Constitution is not raising judges above voters. [beat] It is enforcing the people's own instructions against a wayward agent.

> Hamilton's defense: the court enforces the people's Constitution against their own agents.

It is an elegant theory. [beat] And the historical record promptly complicated it.

Remember that the power rested for half a century. The second time the Court struck down an act of Congress was in 1857, in Dred Scott v. Sandford.

The Court voided the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery in the territories. It declared that Black Americans could not be citizens. And the decision accelerated the country toward civil war.

[pause]

Whatever judicial review is, the record shows it is not reliably heroic. This is the power that would later produce Brown v. Board of Education, the decision against school segregation.

> The same power that produced Brown produced Dred Scott.

Any honest theory of the power has to own both.

## The counter-majoritarian difficulty

So the power has a brilliant origin, an elegant defense, and a mixed record. Now for the objection that every defense must answer.

The terms of the modern debate were set in 1962, by a Yale law professor named Alexander Bickel. His book was called The Least Dangerous Branch. That title is Hamilton's phrase, deployed with an eyebrow raised.

Bickel named the problem in one sentence. "The root difficulty is that judicial review is a counter-majoritarian force in our system."

[pause]

A counter-majoritarian force. A force that works against the majority.

Here is what he meant. When the Court strikes down a statute, it thwarts the considered choice of elected representatives. Those are actual people, who can be voted out. And it does so on the authority of nine lawyers [beat] who cannot.

> Bickel's difficulty: unelected judges void the choices of elected majorities.

You might reach for Hamilton's reply. The Constitution is the will of the people, and the judges are only enforcing it. But Bickel's point is that this only relocates the problem.

Why? Because the document's open phrases do not apply themselves. Due process. Equal protection. Cruel and unusual. Someone must choose among the possible readings, and that someone is unelected.

One caution, because this is often misheard. Bickel's difficulty is not the complaint that judges are secretly partisan and vote their politics. That is a separate complaint.

Imagine judges who are perfectly honest, with no politics at all. Does the difficulty go away? [beat] It does not. They would still be overriding elected majorities from an unelected seat.

The difficulty is structural. It is not about how judges behave.

[pause]

Bickel offered an answer of his own, and later scholars offered other escapes. But the difficulty has never been dissolved, only managed. And every interpretive school in American law is, at bottom, a competing strategy for managing it.

## Three answers to one question

If review is here to stay, the fight moves to how. How should judges interpret the document they enforce against majorities?

Three schools dominate, and each deserves its strongest telling. For each one, listen for the same things: who speaks for it, what it is trying to protect, its strongest argument, and its challenge to the others.

But first, pull the question back. A moment ago Bickel named a difficulty. Can you still state it, in a sentence of your own?

[long pause]

Unelected judges with life tenure void the choices of elected majorities. And they must explain why that is legitimate in a democracy.

Each school is an answer to that. Here is the first.

The originalist. The canonical case was made by Justice Antonin Scalia, in a book in 1997 called A Matter of Interpretation.

The originalist argues that the Constitution's words had a public meaning when they were ratified. And that meaning, not the judge's values, is the law. In plainer words, the judge's task is to find the meaning, not to supply it.

What is the originalist trying to protect? The whole point of a written constitution, which is to fix certain choices beyond the reach of transient majorities and transient judges alike.

So originalism answers Bickel directly. Review is legitimate exactly because, and only insofar as, the judge is enforcing what the people actually enacted, through ratification and amendment. Not what nine lawyers wish they had enacted. On this view the unelected judge is not the one choosing. The choice was made by the people who ratified the words.

The originalist's challenge to the rival view is this. A living Constitution is just a Constitution that means whatever the current Court's majority says, and that is no constraint at all. If the document evolves, it evolves at the ballot box, through Article Five. [beat] It does not evolve in chambers.

And originalists point to a feature of their own method: it sometimes yields outcomes the judge dislikes. That, they argue, is the proof that it is law and not preference.

> The originalist case: the text means what its words publicly meant when ratified.

Now the second school: the living constitutionalist. The case was made by Justice Stephen Breyer, in a book in 2005 called Active Liberty. And by David Strauss, in a book in 2010 called The Living Constitution.

The living constitutionalist argues that the Framers wrote in majestic generalities, such as "equal protection," "due process," and "unreasonable searches." And they chose those words over specific codes deliberately.

What is the living constitutionalist trying to protect? The document's animating purpose. Breyer argues that judges should interpret those general words to serve a workable, participatory democracy. And to serve it in conditions the Framers could not have imagined: wiretaps, administrative agencies, a continental economy.

Strauss adds a point about how the law has in fact behaved. American constitutional law has always developed the way the common law develops: precedent by precedent, with the text as the starting point rather than the finish line. That is how a Constitution ratified by slaveholding states came, without any relevant change in its text, to forbid school segregation.

The living constitutionalist's challenge to the rival view is concrete. Virtually everyone regards Brown v. Board of Education as correct. Yet the Congress that proposed the Fourteenth Amendment itself ran segregated schools in the District of Columbia.

And living constitutionalists point to what follows from that. A method that struggles to generate the canonical case of the twentieth century, they argue, is not a constraint. [beat] It is an alibi.

> The living constitutionalist case: general words, interpreted for their purpose, precedent by precedent.

And the third school: restraint, called Thayerian restraint after James Bradley Thayer. His article in the Harvard Law Review, in 1893, is the founding text of the tradition.

The first two schools are arguing about how judges should wield a power. The Thayerian argues that judges should barely use it. [beat] A court may strike down a statute, Thayer argued, only when the legislature has made not merely a mistake, but a very clear one. In his words: "so clear that it is not open to rational question."

What is the Thayerian trying to protect? The public's own constitutional conscience. Reasonable constitutional disagreement, on this view, belongs to the elected branches. A court that voids every law it thinks unconstitutional teaches legislatures to stop thinking constitutionally at all, and outsources that conscience to nine people.

Notice that this is not a theory of interpretation. It is a theory of deference. A Thayerian can be an originalist or a living constitutionalist about meaning, and still vote to uphold the law. The question is never what the Constitution means. The question is whether the legislature's reading is rationally defensible.

So the Thayerian's challenge goes to both rivals at once: both camps strike down too much. Restraint is the only school that answers Bickel by shrinking the counter-majoritarian act itself.

And Thayerians point to how the idea travels across ideological eras. It counseled against striking down New Deal economic statutes in the 1930s. It counsels against striking down gun regulations or abortion laws today.

> The restraint case: strike down a statute only for a mistake beyond rational question.

[pause]

Step back from the three, and a structural point comes into view. Originalism and living constitutionalism are rival theories of meaning. Thayerian restraint is a theory of who decides.

> Two schools argue about meaning. The third argues about who decides.

That is why the charge of judicial activism flies in every direction. Each school calls the others activist. To originalists, living constitutionalism licenses judges to update the law. To living constitutionalists, the activists are conservative courts striking down campaign-finance and voting-rights statutes. To Thayerians, both camps strike down too much.

So the word activism describes whose ox is gored. The analytical question is always which interpretive commitments are doing the work.

Let's test your ear for the three. A judge writes this in an opinion.

"I believe this statute is terrible policy, and were I a legislator I would vote against it. But its constitutionality is fairly debatable, and that is enough to sustain it."

Which school is speaking? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Thayerian restraint.

The judge is not resolving what the Constitution means. She may even suspect that the statute conflicts with it. She is deferring, because the legislature's contrary reading is not a clear mistake.

An originalist would ask what the text meant at ratification. A living constitutionalist would ask which reading best serves the document's purposes today. Only the Thayerian treats reasonable debatability itself as the end of the case.

One more exercise, and this one has no answer from me. Of the three schools, take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Marbury v. Madison, 1803. John Marshall ruled against Marbury by striking down the statute Marbury had sued under. In the act of declining power, the Court claimed a far larger one: the power, in Marshall's words, to say what the law is.

[beat]

Two. Hamilton's defense. The judiciary has neither force nor will, but merely judgment, and a court that enforces the Constitution is enforcing the people's instructions against their own agents. Then the complication: the same power that produced Brown produced Dred Scott.

[beat]

Three. Bickel's difficulty, from 1962. Unelected judges void the choices of elected majorities. The difficulty is structural, and it has never been dissolved, only managed.

[beat]

Four. Three ways of managing it. For the originalist, with Scalia, the text means what its words publicly meant when ratified. For the living constitutionalist, with Breyer and Strauss, constitutional law develops the way common law does, precedent by precedent. For the Thayerian, a court strikes down a statute only when the legislature's mistake is clear beyond rational question.

[beat]

And five. Two of those schools argue about meaning. The third argues about who decides. And activism is the name each one gives the others.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not told you which school is right, and no lecture honestly could. Each school's strongest version survives its rivals' best attacks. That is what makes this ground contested.

And there is a question that all three schools share an interest in dodging. Is the structure that houses the power itself due for renovation? Life tenure. Nine seats. A docket chosen in secret.

[pause]

That fight runs from Franklin Roosevelt's plan in 1937 to a presidential commission in 2021. And then to a sitting president's reform proposal, in 2024. It is the subject of the next lecture.

For now, go back to that desk in the State Department, and the stack of commissions that nobody delivered. [beat] A clerical accident. And we are still arguing over what the Court made of it.
