## Two books, one complaint

Two books published a year apart lodged the same complaint against modern liberalism. Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue, in 1981, grew into a foundational text for conservative religious thought. Michael Sandel's Liberalism and the Limits of Justice, in 1982, became the founding critique for the communitarian left.

- MacIntyre: After Virtue, 1981
- Sandel: Liberalism and the Limits of Justice, 1982

[beat]

Four decades on, both camps are working from the same philosophical core.

## Community comes first

Liberal political philosophy treats individuals as primary, and asks how they should associate. Communitarianism treats community as primary, and asks how individuals are formed within it.

- Liberalism: the individual is primary
- Communitarianism: community is primary

Sandel's critique is that the unencumbered self is a fiction. Actual selves come into being through families, neighborhoods, religious communities, languages and shared traditions.

> The self is constituted by community membership, not prior to it.

That is not a case against the individual. Communitarians argue that genuine individuality is developed within communities, not in opposition to them.

- Not anti-individual

## Why it crosses left and right

Those commitments can be deployed in support of either progressive or conservative political projects. On the left, the argument is that pure left-liberalism misses the civic dimension of progressive politics. On the right, the argument is that pure free-market thinking does not protect the family, religious community and shared cultural inheritance. In the middle, the argument is that both major parties have lost touch with shared civic life.

- Left: Sandel, Walzer, Reich
- Right: MacIntyre, Reno
- Middle: Etzioni

> Communitarian describes a philosophical commitment more than a partisan alignment.

## Three streams

Three streams share the core and sharpen it differently. The civic-republican stream holds that political participation and democratic citizenship are the central shared goods. The religious-traditionalist stream holds that religious community, traditional family and inherited moral frameworks are the deepest carriers of shared moral life. The progressive stream holds that shared social provision, such as healthcare, education and public spaces, is the institutional expression of communitarian values.

- Civic-republican: democratic citizenship
- Religious-traditionalist: religious community and family
- Progressive: shared social provision

The lesson does not settle which stream draws the right political conclusions. It has the full list of shared commitments, the five foundational thinkers, and four things communitarianism is not. The next lesson examines each stream in detail.
