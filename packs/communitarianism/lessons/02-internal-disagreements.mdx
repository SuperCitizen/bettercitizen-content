# Internal disagreements within the tradition

Seat Michael Sandel, R. R. Reno, and Robert Reich on one panel and ask whether American individualism has hollowed out shared life, and you will hear a single diagnosis three times over — atomization, civic decay, the unencumbered self. Then ask about same-sex marriage, school vouchers, or immigration, and the consensus shatters along lines that look suspiciously like the ordinary left-right divide the tradition claims to transcend. That is the puzzle of this lesson: the three streams agree on the philosophical commitments outlined in Lesson 1 but diverge — sometimes bitterly — on what those commitments imply for politics, religion, and public policy. Watching exactly where they break from one another is the fastest way to see what each one is really claiming.

## 1. The civic-republican stream

Core commitment: democratic citizenship is itself a constitutive shared good. Citizens engage in public life through deliberation, civic participation, public-spirited action. The state should support institutions that enable this — public spaces, public education, civic education, mass-participation organizations, public-service careers.

Contemporary intellectual figures: [[person:michael-sandel]] (most prominent), Robert Reich, Danielle Allen, Mark Lilla (in some moods).

Key claims:
- Democratic citizenship is not just a means to choose leaders or protect rights; it is an activity through which people develop and exercise civic virtues. A society that allows democratic life to wither — even if its formal procedures are intact — has lost something essential.
- The conventional left-right framing misses what's at stake. Both progressive rights-based liberalism and conservative free-market liberalism produce atomized individuals disengaged from public life. The recovery of robust civic participation is a project that cuts across this axis.
- Public institutions matter. Public schools, public libraries, public parks, public-service careers (teaching, military, civil service) are sites where shared civic identity is formed. The 1990s-era project of converting public institutions to private ones ([[issue:charter-schools]], privatized prisons, reduced public-service hiring) damaged civic life regardless of efficiency arguments.
- Sandel's *The Tyranny of Merit* (2020) makes a recent influential statement: the meritocratic individualism that has become dominant in elite-liberal politics undermines the dignity of ordinary work and the civic solidarity that democratic politics requires (see [[term:meritocracy]]).
- Allen's reading of the Declaration of Independence develops a civic-republican framework in which equality is foundational to democratic citizenship and Black political thought has been at the center of American civic-republican tradition.

Disagreements with religious-traditionalists: religious-traditionalist communitarianism is too parochial. Genuine civic life requires inclusion across religious and cultural differences; building it on particular religious traditions excludes citizens who don't share them.

Disagreements with progressive communitarians: progressive communitarianism remains too policy-instrumental. Civic participation is not just a means to better policy outcomes; it has intrinsic value as the activity of democratic citizenship.

## 2. The religious-traditionalist stream

Core commitment: religious communities are the deepest carriers of tradition, virtue, and shared moral life. Modern liberal politics has been progressively secularized in ways that damage these communities and the moral framework they provide. Recovering them is essential for human flourishing.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Alasdair MacIntyre, Stanley Hauerwas, R. R. Reno, Patrick Deneen, the *First Things* circle, certain streams of Catholic and Reformed political theology.

Key claims:
- MacIntyre's *After Virtue* argues that modern moral discourse has become incoherent because we've lost the shared frameworks (Aristotelian-Thomist virtue ethics, social roles tied to functioning communities) that gave moral terms their meaning. Recovery is a long-term project that requires deep engagement with tradition.
- Religious communities — Catholic parishes, Protestant congregations, Jewish congregations, Muslim mosques, Buddhist sanghas — remain among the most consistent institutional frameworks for moral formation, mutual aid, and civic engagement. Public policy should not displace them or weaken their distinctive identities.
- Modern individualism has produced widespread loneliness, family breakdown, and meaninglessness that the broader political conversation underweights. The "deaths of despair" (Anne Case and [[person:angus-deaton]]) are partly evidence of community collapse.
- The liberal commitment to "neutrality" between competing comprehensive doctrines is itself a substantive (and controversial) philosophical position. Public life that excludes religious vocabulary is not neutral; it privileges secular individualism over religious community.
- Religious traditions disagree among themselves; pluralism within and across religious communities is real and important. But the practical alternative to particular religious tradition is not "neutral public reason" but "secular liberalism," which is itself a particular tradition.

Disagreements with civic-republicans: pure civic-republican framings underestimate how thin civic identity becomes when stripped of religious-traditional substance. The U.S. civic-republican tradition was historically intertwined with Protestant moral culture; trying to maintain it in pure secular form drains it of motivating power.

Disagreements with progressive communitarians: progressive communitarianism uses communitarian rhetoric but accepts most of the substantive policy positions of pure progressive liberalism. This is communitarianism in name only; genuine communitarian commitments require more substantive disagreement with progressive priorities (especially on family, gender, sexuality, and religious liberty).

## 3. The progressive-communitarian stream

Core commitment: shared social provision (universal healthcare, education, childcare, housing) is the institutional expression of communitarian values in modern democratic societies. The progressive policy program — when framed in civic-republican rather than rights-based terms — is itself communitarian.

Contemporary intellectual figures: Robert Reich, Michael Walzer, the *Dissent* circle (in part), [[person:cornel-west]] (overlap with civic-republican stream), some streams of religious-progressive thought (Black Liberation Theology, certain Catholic-progressive voices).

Key claims:
- Universal social programs are communitarian by their nature: they create shared institutions that all citizens access, generating the civic commons that progressive societies are built around. Targeted programs may be more efficient at reaching the poorest but don't generate the same civic-shared identity.
- Public infrastructure (transit, parks, libraries, public schools, public universities) is communitarian infrastructure. It's where civic identity is built and shared experiences happen. Privatization of these institutions weakens civic life regardless of efficiency arguments.
- The labor movement is itself communitarian. Trade unions are institutions of solidarity that build shared identity and political voice (see [[issue:union-rights]]). Their decline since the 1970s has weakened both progressive politics and civic life broadly.
- Progressive politics needs to recover the moral-religious vocabulary it once had. The civil rights movement was rooted in Black church traditions; the labor movement drew on religious-moral frameworks. Pure secular-procedural liberalism has produced a progressive politics that struggles to motivate beyond instrumental policy claims.
- Walzer's pluralist communitarianism — different goods belong to different spheres with different distributive principles — provides a non-dogmatic framework for thinking about how to combine markets, public provision, and democratic citizenship.

Disagreements with civic-republicans: civic-republicanism without substantive policy commitments becomes mere rhetoric. The progressive-communitarian project requires substantive social-democratic policy — universal healthcare, free public college, robust labor rights — to deliver what civic-republican rhetoric promises.

Disagreements with religious-traditionalists: religious-traditionalist communitarianism in U.S. politics has been allied with conservative cultural politics that excludes women, LGBTQ individuals, and religious minorities from full civic equality. The communitarian commitment to shared civic life cannot be reconciled with this exclusion.

## The same question, three answers

Put one question to all three streams — *what does American civic life most need recovered?* — and the disagreement becomes concrete:

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Civic-republican communitarian">
    **Democratic participation itself.** Rebuild the sites where citizens
    deliberate and act together — public schools, libraries, civic
    associations, public-service careers. Policy outcomes matter, but the
    deeper loss is the *activity* of citizenship: a country can pass good
    laws and still hollow out, because participation is the good, not just a
    means to goods.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Religious-traditionalist communitarian">
    **The moral frameworks that make civic life possible.** Participation is
    downstream of formation, and formation happens in families and religious
    communities. A civic identity with no substantive moral tradition behind
    it is too thin to ask sacrifice of anyone — so the first task is to stop
    public policy from displacing the institutions that actually form
    citizens.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Progressive communitarian">
    **Shared institutions everyone uses.** Universal healthcare, public
    schools, transit, parks, unions — these are where shared identity is
    built in a diverse modern society. Rhetoric about civic virtue without
    the institutional substance is empty; the commons must be funded and
    universal, or "community" remains a sermon rather than a practice.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## Where each stream lands on specific issues

| Issue | Civic-republican | Religious-traditionalist | Progressive-communitarian |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal healthcare | Strong support | Mixed | Strong support |
| Public schools (vs. vouchers) | Strong support for public | Mixed (some support choice) | Strong support for public |
| Same-sex marriage | Generally support | Generally oppose | Strong support |
| Abortion | Mixed | Generally restrict | Generally protect |
| Free public college | Generally support | Mixed | Strong support |
| Religious liberty (broad) | Mixed | Strong support | Mixed |
| Affirmative action | Mixed | Generally oppose | Generally support |
| Universal child allowance | Strong support | Strong support | Strong support |
| Public-service requirement (ROTC, civil-service) | Strong support | Generally support | Generally support |
| Strong labor rights | Strong support | Mixed | Strong support |
| Mass surveillance | Generally oppose | Generally oppose | Generally oppose |
| Immigration | Generally welcoming | Generally restrictionist | Generally welcoming |

The streams diverge sharply on cultural questions (sexuality, religion, family) and on immigration. They converge on civic infrastructure, labor rights, and most social-provision policies.

## What this means for political coalitions

Communitarianism doesn't have a political party; its adherents are distributed across both major parties and adjacent political projects. The intellectual influence has been substantial — Sandel's work has shaped public discourse on meritocracy and democracy; MacIntyre's has shaped conservative religious thought; Walzer's has shaped Democratic policy circles.

Recent political moments have produced communitarian-resonant coalitions:
- The Sanders-AOC progressive movement combines social-democratic policy with civic-republican framing.
- The post-2016 national-conservative movement combines religious-traditionalist communitarian commitments with some economic-policy departures from libertarian conservatism (see [[ideology:post-liberalism]] for the adjacent tradition).
- The "Abundance" / "Build Things" synthesis ([[person:ezra-klein]] and Derek Thompson) has communitarian resonances in its insistence that what gets built — transit, housing, public works — shapes shared life, not just individual welfare.

None of these coalitions *is* the communitarian movement; each borrows from the shared core while answering the streams' open questions differently. Which is the point: knowing the three streams lets you read coalitions that party labels obscure.

What this lesson did not settle: whether the three streams are rivals or allies in practice — that depends on how each relates to the *other* American traditions, which is where the next lesson goes.
