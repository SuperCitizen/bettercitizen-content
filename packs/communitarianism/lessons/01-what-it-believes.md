# What communitarianism actually believes

Communitarianism is the most cross-cutting of the major contemporary political traditions. It has serious adherents on the political left (Sandel, Walzer, Reich), the political right (MacIntyre, Reno, the *First Things* circle), and in the religious-philosophical middle (Etzioni). The core commitments are similar across these positions; the political alignments diverge.

## A core that holds across the family

Most communitarians, regardless of more specific orientation, share commitments roughly as follows:

**The self is constituted by community membership, not prior to it.** Liberal political philosophy from Locke through Rawls treats individuals as primary and asks how they should associate. Communitarianism treats community as primary and asks how individuals are formed within it. Sandel's *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) made this critique systematically: the "unencumbered self" of Rawlsian theory is a fiction; actual selves come into being through families, neighborhoods, religious communities, languages, and shared traditions.

**Some goods are intrinsically shared.** Public parks, civic institutions, languages, traditions, religious practices, cultural inheritances — these are not goods that exist independently and then get distributed. They exist only as shared practices in which people participate. The idea that all goods can be reduced to private holdings to be distributed misses the existence of these constitutively shared goods.

**Civic participation has intrinsic value.** Democratic citizenship is not merely a mechanism for choosing leaders; it is an activity in which people develop and exercise certain virtues — deliberation, public-spiritedness, prudence, justice. A society that allows all political activity to be replaced by private consumption and procedural compliance loses something essential. Sandel's *Democracy's Discontent* (1996) makes this case at book length.

**Pure rights-based liberalism produces atomization.** Defenses of liberalism — both the left-liberal rights-based variant and the libertarian property-based variant — have produced societies where individuals are increasingly disconnected from family, neighborhood, religious community, and shared civic life. The communitarian diagnosis: this is not an unfortunate side effect of otherwise valuable institutions; it is what those institutions, when taken to their logical conclusion, produce.

**Public policy should support community institutions.** The state should not be neutral about whether civic institutions, families, and religious communities flourish. Programs that strengthen these institutions are appropriate; programs that displace them or weaken them by accident should be redesigned.

**Tradition has authority but is not above critique.** Inherited traditions encode practical wisdom that abstract reasoning cannot replicate. But traditions also contain blind spots, exclusions, and historical injustices that internal critique can address. Communitarian thought respects tradition while accepting that traditions can and must be reformed from within.

## The foundational thinkers

Alasdair MacIntyre (1929-) — *After Virtue* (1981) is the foundational text. MacIntyre argued that modern moral discourse has become incoherent because we've lost the shared frameworks (Aristotelian and Thomist virtue ethics, social roles tied to functioning communities) that gave moral terms their meaning. His later work develops a Christian-Aristotelian alternative. Influential across both left and right communitarianism.

Michael Sandel (1953-) — *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) is the foundational left-communitarian critique of Rawlsian liberalism. Sandel's later work — *Democracy's Discontent*, *The Tyranny of Merit* — extends the critique to American politics, arguing that progressive liberalism has lost the civic-republican vocabulary that historically made progressive politics possible. The most prominent contemporary communitarian.

Charles Taylor (1931-) — *Sources of the Self* (1989), *A Secular Age* (2007). Canadian philosopher whose work on the philosophical anthropology of selfhood and on the relationship between religion and modernity has shaped communitarian thought across left and right. Taylor's work on multiculturalism and recognition has influenced both progressive identity-politics and conservative religious-traditionalist thought.

Michael Walzer (1935-) — *Spheres of Justice* (1983) provides a pluralist-communitarian theory of justice. Different goods (medical care, education, citizenship, money, recognition) belong to different spheres with different distributive principles. Walzer's pluralism distinguishes him from both libertarian-monism (markets for everything) and Rawlsian-monism (single principle of justice for all goods). Influential on progressive communitarianism.

Amitai Etzioni (1929-2023) — sociologist who founded the Communitarian Network and wrote *The Spirit of Community* (1993). More popular than philosophical; his policy proposals tried to operationalize communitarian theory in mainstream American politics. Influential on the New Democrat movement of the 1990s.

## Why communitarianism crosses left and right

The conventional left-right axis is organized around questions like: How much redistribution? How active should government be? How should tradition be related to progressive change?

The communitarian tradition cuts across these questions because its central commitments — community, civic participation, shared goods, critique of atomized individualism — can be deployed in support of either progressive or conservative political projects.

**Communitarianism on the political left:** Sandel, Walzer, Reich. The argument: pure left-liberalism (focused on individual rights, market regulation, redistributive transfers) misses the civic dimension of progressive politics. The most successful progressive moments — the New Deal coalition, the civil rights movement — drew on shared civic identity, religious moral language, and community organizing. Recovering this is essential for progressive politics today.

**Communitarianism on the political right:** MacIntyre, Reno, the religious-traditionalist tradition. The argument: pure right-liberalism (focused on free markets, individual property rights, procedural neutrality) has produced a society that conservatives should be unhappy with. The traditional family, religious community, shared cultural inheritance — these are conservative values that pure free-market thinking does not protect. Recovering them requires a more robustly communitarian conservatism.

**Communitarianism in the middle:** Etzioni, Tony Blair / Bill Clinton's "Third Way" influences. The argument: both major parties have lost touch with the shared civic life that political legitimacy requires. A communitarian middle, neither progressive nor conservative in the conventional senses, can recover this.

The result is that "communitarian" describes a philosophical commitment more than a partisan alignment. It cuts across the left-right axis in ways that the other ideologies in this pack do not.

## What communitarianism is NOT

A few common misconceptions:

**Not "anti-individual."** Communitarians are not opposed to individual freedom. They argue that genuine individuality is developed within communities, not in opposition to them. The disagreement with libertarianism is about the relationship between individual and community, not about whether individuals matter.

**Not "anti-progress."** Communitarian thinkers from MacIntyre on the right to Sandel on the left have all been engaged in serious critique and reform of inherited institutions. The Burkean presumption-against-rapid-change is one strand of communitarian thought (the religious-traditionalist), but progressive communitarianism explicitly seeks reform.

**Not "anti-state."** Communitarians vary widely on the role of the state, but most accept active state support for community institutions. The disagreement with both libertarianism and pure laissez-faire is about whether the state should help or stay out.

**Not just "left vs. right" with extra steps.** Communitarianism makes a substantive philosophical claim that cuts across conventional partisanship. Treating it as merely a sub-variety of progressive or conservative politics misses what's distinctive.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson sharpens:

- **Civic-republican communitarians** emphasize political participation, public deliberation, and democratic citizenship as central. Their politics is left-leaning but distinct from pure progressivism.
- **Religious-traditionalist communitarians** emphasize religious community, traditional family, and inherited cultural-moral frameworks. Their politics is right-leaning but distinct from libertarian-conservative coalitions.
- **Progressive communitarians** emphasize shared social provision (healthcare, education, public spaces), civic-republican framing of progressive policy, and inclusive democratic identity. Their politics is solidly left but with rhetorical and substantive differences from pure rights-based progressivism.
