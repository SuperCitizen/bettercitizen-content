# How communitarianism relates to other traditions

Communitarianism's distinctive feature among contemporary political traditions is its cross-cutting position. Substantial overlaps exist with conservatism, progressivism, and (to a lesser extent) socialism. The deep disagreement is with libertarianism — and even there, the disagreement is more philosophical than total.

## What communitarianism shares with conservatism

Substantial overlap with both traditionalist and civic-republican streams of conservatism. Many of the substantive concerns are identical:

- **Civic and religious institutions matter.** Communitarians and traditionalist conservatives agree that family, religious community, neighborhood organizations, and shared cultural inheritance are essential to human flourishing.
- **Pure individualism is corrosive.** Both traditions are critical of the atomizing effects of pure rights-based liberalism (and of pure free-market individualism).
- **Tradition has authority.** The Burkean conservative presumption-against-rapid-change overlaps with communitarian respect for tradition.
- **Skepticism of meritocratic individualism.** Sandel's left-communitarian critique and traditionalist conservative critique converge on the post-1990s liberal-meritocratic synthesis.

What divides them: traditional conservatives are committed to inherited cultural-religious institutions in their particular forms; progressive communitarians are committed to inclusive multicultural civic frameworks. The disagreement is about which inheritances matter and how change should occur.

The religious-traditionalist stream of communitarianism aligns substantially with traditionalist conservatism; the civic-republican and progressive streams align less but still share the critique of pure individualism.

## What communitarianism shares with progressivism

Substantial overlap with the civic-republican stream of progressivism, less with identity-justice progressivism:

- **Civic-republican overlap is direct.** Sandel, Reich, and Walzer are progressive communitarians; the substantive overlap with mainstream liberal-progressive politics is large.
- **Universal social provision.** Both traditions support universal healthcare, free public education, public infrastructure, and shared social goods. The communitarian framing emphasizes the civic-shared nature of these institutions; progressive framing emphasizes equity and rights.
- **Concern about meritocratic individualism.** Sandel's communitarian critique aligns with progressive concerns about the dignity of work and the limits of credential-based stratification.
- **Public-service careers and civic infrastructure.** Both traditions support public schools, libraries, parks, and civil service.

What divides them: progressive politics has historically been organized around individual-rights frameworks (civil rights, women's rights, LGBTQ rights). Communitarianism is more comfortable arguing for shared civic identity that crosses these particular identity claims. The tension is not absolute — progressive communitarians try to combine both — but it is real.

Walzer, Sandel, and Reich are working at this intersection.

## What communitarianism shares with socialism

Real overlap with both democratic-socialist and social-democratic streams:

- **Shared social provision.** Universal healthcare, education, housing, childcare — all communitarian and socialist commitments.
- **Critique of pure market individualism.** Communitarian critique of liberal individualism and socialist critique of capitalist atomization converge substantially.
- **Worker organization.** Trade unions are communitarian institutions in their own right — shared institutions of solidarity. Socialist support for labor rights and communitarian support for civic infrastructure overlap.
- **Decommodification of basic goods.** Both traditions argue that healthcare, education, and basic housing should not be allocated by ability to pay.

What divides them: socialism has a more substantial economic-restructuring program (worker ownership, public production, market-skeptical institutions); communitarianism is more politically moderate on economic structure but more focused on civic-cultural questions. The progressive-communitarian and democratic-socialist streams overlap most.

## What communitarianism shares with libertarianism

Less than with the others, but real:

- **Skepticism of bureaucratic centralization.** Both traditions worry about distant, unaccountable institutions — though libertarians focus on government and communitarians on both government and corporate.
- **Civil society as alternative to state.** Some communitarian streams (especially religious-traditionalist) emphasize voluntary civic and religious association over state action; libertarians do similarly.
- **Federalism and subsidiarity.** Local, community-level decision-making is preferred by both.

What divides them: libertarians treat individual choice as foundational; communitarians treat community membership as constitutive of selfhood. Libertarians emphasize property rights as foundational; communitarians treat property as one good among several with limits set by communal commitments. The bleeding-heart libertarian stream (Tomasi) is the closest libertarian engagement with communitarian concerns.

## "American" communitarianism vs. European equivalents

European political traditions don't have a direct equivalent to American communitarianism. Closest analogues:

- **Catholic social teaching.** Subsidiarity (decisions at the most-local appropriate level), solidarity (commitment to one another's welfare), the dignity of labor. Foundational for both progressive and conservative European Catholic political thought.
- **Hegelian/civic-republican traditions in continental Europe.** Charles Taylor's work draws heavily on this background.
- **Communitarian / Christian-democratic parties.** Germany's CDU has historically been communitarian-influenced (subsidiarity, Catholic social teaching). The U.K. Labour Party's "Blue Labour" tendency (Maurice Glasman) is a communitarian-progressive engagement.

European political space generally has stronger institutional support for communitarian commitments (welfare states, subsidiarity, religious accommodation) than American political space does. American communitarianism often functions more as critique of American political culture than as established political program.

## Current points of cross-ideological convergence

Several specific issues where the partisan alignment is misleading:

**Universal child allowance.** Mitt Romney's Family Security Act, Senator Brown's Child Tax Credit expansion, the ARPA temporary expansion — broad bipartisan substantive support. Communitarian framing (supporting families as institutions) bridges left and right.

**Anti-Big-Tech coalitions.** Senator Hawley (R) and Senator Klobuchar (D) share substantive policy positions on platform monopolies. Communitarian framing (defending community institutions against impersonal market forces) underlies both.

**National service / civil service careers.** Bipartisan support for expanded national service programs (AmeriCorps, military expansion, civil-service hiring). Communitarian framing (civic participation is itself valuable) crosses left and right.

**Loneliness and "deaths of despair."** Putnam's work on community decline, Anne Case and Angus Deaton's work on midlife mortality — these draw bipartisan substantive concern. Communitarian framing (community matters for human flourishing) underlies both.

**Local journalism + community newspapers.** Bipartisan recognition that the collapse of local journalism has weakened civic life. Various subsidy proposals draw cross-tradition support.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position about communitarianism — whether you identify with the tradition or not — requires:

1. A view on **which stream best fits one's values** (civic-republican, religious-traditionalist, or progressive-communitarian).
2. A view on **how communitarian commitments translate into specific policy** in current American political conditions.
3. A view on **how the tradition relates to other traditions** — where it overlaps, where it diverges, where the strongest critiques bite.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
