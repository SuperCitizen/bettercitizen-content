Picture a panel of three. Michael Sandel. R. R. Reno. Robert Reich.

[beat]

Ask them one question. Has American individualism hollowed out shared life?

You will hear a single diagnosis, three times over. Atomization. Civic decay. The unencumbered self.

[pause]

Now ask about same-sex marriage. Or school vouchers. Or immigration.

[beat]

The consensus shatters. And it shatters along lines that look suspiciously like the ordinary left-right divide, the very divide this tradition claims to transcend.

[pause]

That is our puzzle. Last time, we laid out the philosophical commitments that communitarians share. The three streams of the tradition agree on those commitments. They diverge, sometimes bitterly, on what the commitments imply for politics, for religion, and for public policy.

And watching exactly where they break from one another is the fastest way to see what each one is really claiming.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to tell the three streams apart, to name the thinkers behind each, and to state each stream's strongest critique of the other two. And you should be able to say where the three land on actual policy.

We will take it in four steps. First, the civic-republican stream. Second, the religious-traditionalist stream. Third, the progressive-communitarian stream. And last, the issues: where the three split, where they meet, and what that does to political coalitions.

Each stream gets the same treatment, in the same order. Who holds it. What it is trying to protect. Its argument, in its own terms. The evidence it points to. And its complaint against the other two.

Then each stream gets the same question. What does American civic life most need recovered? [beat] Listen for three different answers.

## The civic-republican stream

Begin with the civic-republican stream, and with who holds it. Its most prominent voice is Michael Sandel. With him are Danielle Allen, Robert Reich, and, in some moods, Mark Lilla.

What is this stream trying to protect? Democratic citizenship itself. It holds that citizenship is a constitutive shared good: in last time's terms, a good that exists only in the sharing. Citizens engage in public life through deliberation, civic participation, and public-spirited action. And the state should support the institutions that make that possible.

Now the argument, in its own terms. Democratic citizenship is not just a means to choose leaders or to protect rights. It is an activity, through which people develop and exercise civic virtues. So a society that lets democratic life wither has lost something essential, even if its formal procedures are intact.

And the conventional left-right framing, on this view, misses what is at stake. Progressive rights-based liberalism and conservative free-market liberalism both produce atomized individuals, disengaged from public life. Recovering civic participation cuts across that axis.

[beat]

The evidence this stream points to comes in three parts.

First, public institutions. Public schools, libraries, parks and public-service careers are sites where shared civic identity is formed. So this stream argues that the project of the 1990s, converting public institutions to private ones, damaged civic life, whatever the efficiency arguments. Its examples are charter schools and privatized prisons.

Second, Sandel's book of 2020, The Tyranny of Merit. It argues that meritocratic individualism has become dominant in elite-liberal politics, and that it undermines two things: the dignity of ordinary work, and the civic solidarity that democratic politics requires.

Third, Danielle Allen's reading of the Declaration of Independence. It develops a civic-republican framework in which equality is foundational to democratic citizenship. And in that framework, Black political thought has been at the center of the American civic-republican tradition.

[beat]

Now its complaints against the other two streams.

Against the religious-traditionalists: they are too parochial. Genuine civic life requires inclusion across religious and cultural differences. Build it on particular religious traditions, and you exclude the citizens who do not share them.

Against the progressive communitarians: they remain too policy-instrumental. Civic participation is not just a means to better policy outcomes. It has intrinsic value, as the activity of democratic citizenship.

[beat]

And so to the question. What does American civic life most need recovered?

> The civic-republican answer: democratic participation itself.

Rebuild the sites where citizens deliberate and act together. Policy outcomes matter, on this view, but the deeper loss is the activity of citizenship. A country can pass good laws and still hollow out, because participation is the good, and not just a means to goods.

## The religious-traditionalist stream

Now the religious-traditionalist stream, built the same way. Who holds it? Alasdair MacIntyre, Stanley Hauerwas, R. R. Reno and Patrick Deneen. Around them stand the First Things circle, and certain streams of Catholic and Reformed political theology.

What is this stream trying to protect? Religious communities. It holds that they are the deepest carriers of tradition, of virtue, and of shared moral life.

Now the argument, in its own terms. Modern liberal politics has been progressively secularized, in ways that damage those communities and the moral framework they provide. Recovering them is essential for human flourishing.

The argument has a sharp point, and it concerns neutrality. Liberalism commits itself to neutrality between competing comprehensive doctrines. This stream argues that the commitment is itself a substantive philosophical position, and a controversial one. A public life that excludes religious vocabulary is not neutral. [beat] It privileges secular individualism over religious community.

Religious traditions disagree among themselves, and this stream grants it. But the practical alternative to a particular religious tradition, it argues, is not neutral public reason. It is secular liberalism, which is itself a particular tradition.

[beat]

The evidence this stream points to also comes in three parts.

First, MacIntyre's After Virtue. It argues that modern moral discourse has become incoherent, because we have lost the shared frameworks that gave moral terms their meaning.

Second, the religious communities themselves: Catholic parishes, Protestant and Jewish congregations, Muslim mosques, Buddhist sanghas. They remain among the most consistent institutional frameworks for moral formation, mutual aid, and civic engagement.

Third, what modern individualism has produced: widespread loneliness, family breakdown, and meaninglessness, which this stream argues the broader political conversation underweights. It points to the deaths of despair described by Anne Case and Angus Deaton, as partly evidence of community collapse.

[beat]

Now its complaints against the other two streams.

Against the civic-republicans: they underestimate how thin civic identity becomes when it is stripped of religious and traditional substance. The American civic-republican tradition was historically intertwined with Protestant moral culture. Try to maintain it in a purely secular form, and you drain it of motivating power.

Against the progressive communitarians: they use communitarian rhetoric, but accept most of the substantive policy positions of pure progressive liberalism. That is communitarianism in name only. Genuine communitarian commitments would require more substantive disagreement with progressive priorities, especially on family, gender, sexuality, and religious liberty.

[beat]

And the same question. What does American civic life most need recovered?

> The religious-traditionalist answer: the moral frameworks that make civic life possible.

Participation, on this view, is downstream of formation, and formation happens in families and religious communities. A civic identity with no substantive moral tradition behind it is too thin to ask sacrifice of anyone. So the first task is to stop public policy from displacing the institutions that actually form citizens.

[pause]

Let's test the two streams we have so far. Here is a scene.

A senator defends a school-prayer bill. The senator's argument is that civic identity without religious substance is "too thin to motivate sacrifice."

Which stream is speaking? And which stream objects first, and on what grounds? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The senator is the religious-traditionalist voice. The claim is that civic life, stripped of religious and traditional substance, loses its motivating power.

The civic-republican objects first. Genuine civic life must include citizens across religious difference. So building it on a particular tradition excludes some citizens from the shared identity it is supposed to create.

And notice where that fight takes place. Both sides are arguing within the communitarian core.

> The fight is over which inheritance carries civic life, not whether shared life matters.

## The progressive-communitarian stream

The third stream, in the same order. Who holds it? Robert Reich, whose name you also heard in the first stream, and Michael Walzer. With them are Cornel West, who overlaps with the civic-republican stream, part of the Dissent circle, and some streams of religious-progressive thought.

What is this stream trying to protect? Shared social provision: universal healthcare, education, childcare, housing. It holds that such provision is the institutional expression of communitarian values in modern democratic societies. And it holds that the progressive policy program is itself communitarian, when it is framed in civic-republican terms rather than rights-based ones.

Now the argument, in its own terms. Universal social programs are communitarian by their nature. They create shared institutions that all citizens use, and those institutions generate a civic commons. Targeted programs may be more efficient at reaching the poorest. But they do not generate the same shared civic identity.

[beat]

The evidence this stream points to comes, once more, in three parts.

First, the labor movement. Trade unions are institutions of solidarity, which build shared identity and political voice. And their decline since the 1970s, this stream argues, has weakened both progressive politics and civic life broadly.

Second, the movements of the past. The civil rights movement was rooted in Black church traditions, and the labor movement drew on religious and moral frameworks. So progressive politics, on this view, needs to recover the moral and religious vocabulary it once had. A purely secular, procedural liberalism has produced a progressive politics that struggles to motivate beyond instrumental policy claims.

Third, Walzer's pluralist communitarianism: different goods belong to different spheres, with different principles of distribution. This stream points to it as a non-dogmatic framework for combining markets, public provision, and democratic citizenship.

[beat]

Now its complaints against the other two streams.

Against the civic-republicans: civic-republicanism without substantive policy commitments becomes mere rhetoric. It takes social-democratic policy to deliver what that rhetoric promises: universal healthcare, free public college, robust labor rights.

Against the religious-traditionalists: in American politics, they have been allied with a conservative cultural politics that excludes women, LGBTQ individuals, and religious minorities from full civic equality. The communitarian commitment to shared civic life cannot be reconciled with that exclusion.

[beat]

And the same question, one last time. What does American civic life most need recovered?

> The progressive-communitarian answer: shared institutions everyone uses.

Universal healthcare, public schools, transit, parks, unions. These are where shared identity is built in a diverse modern society. Rhetoric about civic virtue, without the institutional substance, is empty. The commons must be funded and universal, or community remains a sermon rather than a practice.

## Where they split, and where they meet

Three streams, and three answers to one question. Before we go on, can you still give all three answers?

[long pause]

Democratic participation itself. [beat] The moral frameworks that make civic life possible. [beat] Shared institutions everyone uses.

Now watch those answers turn into positions. Line the three streams up against a dozen policy issues, and one pattern stands out.

> The streams diverge sharply on cultural questions and on immigration.

The cultural questions are sexuality, religion, and family. Take same-sex marriage. The civic-republicans generally support it, the religious-traditionalists generally oppose it, and the progressive communitarians strongly support it. On immigration, two streams are generally welcoming, and the religious-traditionalists are generally restrictionist.

And the streams converge elsewhere: on civic infrastructure, on labor rights, and on most social-provision policies. Mass surveillance is one more meeting point. All three generally oppose it.

[pause]

Let's test that pattern. Of all those issues, exactly one draws strong support from all three streams. Here are four candidates. Universal healthcare. A universal child allowance. Strong labor rights. Free public college.

Which one is it? Think about what each stream is trying to protect.

[long pause]

The universal child allowance. [beat] And the reason has three parts. It strengthens families as institutions, which is the religious-traditionalist priority. It is universal social provision, which is the progressive priority. And it is delivered through a shared civic commitment, which is the civic-republican priority.

The other three candidates all get a mixed response from the religious-traditionalists. That stream's communitarianism runs through family and congregation, more than through state programs.

[pause]

So what does this mean for coalitions? Communitarianism does not have a political party. Its adherents are distributed across both major parties, and across adjacent political projects.

Its intellectual influence, though, has been substantial. Sandel's work has shaped public discourse on meritocracy and democracy. MacIntyre's has shaped conservative religious thought. Walzer's has shaped Democratic policy circles.

And recent political moments have produced coalitions that resonate with communitarian ideas. Three of them.

The progressive movement of Sanders and AOC combines social-democratic policy with civic-republican framing.

[beat]

The national-conservative movement of the years since 2016 combines religious-traditionalist communitarian commitments with some departures, in economic policy, from libertarian conservatism.

[beat]

And the synthesis called Abundance, from Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson, insists that what gets built shapes shared life, and not just individual welfare. Transit. Housing. Public works.

None of these coalitions is the communitarian movement. Each borrows from the shared core, while answering the streams' open questions differently. And that is the point.

> Knowing the three streams lets you read coalitions that party labels obscure.

One more thing before we gather up. You have heard three streams, and each one's complaints against the others. One of the three probably convinced you least.

Take that stream. State its best case, in its own terms, the way its most serious adherent would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The three streams share one diagnosis. They break apart over what it implies for politics, for religion, and for public policy.

[beat]

Two. The civic-republican stream: Sandel and Allen. It protects democratic citizenship, and its answer is democratic participation itself. It finds the religious-traditionalists too parochial, and the progressive communitarians too policy-instrumental.

[beat]

Three. The religious-traditionalist stream: MacIntyre, Hauerwas, Reno. It protects religious communities, and its answer is the moral frameworks that make civic life possible. It finds a purely secular civic identity too thin, and progressive communitarianism communitarian in name only.

[beat]

Four. The progressive-communitarian stream: Reich and Walzer. It protects shared social provision, and its answer is shared institutions everyone uses. It finds civic-republicanism without policy mere rhetoric, and the religious-traditionalists allied with a cultural politics that excludes.

[beat]

And five. The streams diverge sharply on cultural questions and on immigration. They converge on civic infrastructure, labor rights, and most social provision. One policy draws strong support from all three: the universal child allowance. And knowing the three streams lets you read coalitions that party labels obscure.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Are the three streams rivals, or allies, in practice?

That depends on how each one relates to the other American traditions. And that is where the next lecture goes.

For now, go back to the panel. Sandel, Reno, Reich. One diagnosis, three times over. [beat] They agree on what was lost. They disagree, sometimes bitterly, on what to recover.
