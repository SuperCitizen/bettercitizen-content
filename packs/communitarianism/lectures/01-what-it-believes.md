Two books, published a year apart.

[beat]

In 1981, the philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre publishes After Virtue. In 1982, Michael Sandel publishes Liberalism and the Limits of Justice.

The two books lodge the same complaint against modern liberalism. Then they travel to what became opposite ends of the political spectrum.

MacIntyre's book grew into a foundational text for conservative religious thought. Sandel's became the founding critique for the communitarian left.

Four decades on, MacIntyre's heirs write for a publication called First Things, and Sandel makes the progressive case against meritocracy. [beat] And both camps are working from the same philosophical core.

[pause]

That is the puzzle. How does one philosophy end up at both ends?

The philosophy is communitarianism. Of the major political traditions of our time, it is the most cross-cutting. It has serious adherents on the political left, on the political right, and in the middle.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to state what communitarians believe, the way they would state it themselves. And you should be able to tell their philosophy apart from any speech that praises community.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the core: the commitments that most communitarians share.

Second, what follows from that core, and what does not.

Third, the five thinkers who built the tradition, and a surprise about its name.

And last, the politics: why the same core lands on the left, on the right, and in three separate streams.

One note before we begin. You will hear this tradition the way its adherents would put it, in its strongest form. When I say that communitarians argue something, that is their case. It is not a ruling.

## The core

Start with a question that sounds abstract, and is not. Which comes first: the individual, or the community?

[beat]

Liberal political philosophy, from Locke through Rawls, starts with the individual. Individuals are primary, and the question is how they should associate.

Communitarianism turns that around. Community is primary, and the question is how individuals are formed within it.

> For communitarians, the self is constituted by community membership, not prior to it.

Constituted. [beat] Hold on to that word. It means made up of. On this view, you are not a finished person who then joins a family, a neighborhood and a language. They came first, and you were formed inside them.

Sandel made this critique systematically, in that book of 1982. His target was the "unencumbered self" of Rawls's theory: a self prior to its communities. That unencumbered self, Sandel argued, is a fiction. Actual selves come into being through families, neighborhoods, religious communities, languages, and shared traditions.

[pause]

That is the first commitment. Two more complete the core.

The second: some goods are intrinsically shared. Think of public parks, civic institutions, languages, traditions, religious practices. Communitarians argue that these are not goods that exist independently and then get distributed. They exist only as shared practices, in which people take part.

Put another way, the idea that every good can be reduced to a private holding, and then handed out, misses something. It misses the goods that exist only in the sharing.

[beat]

The third: civic participation has value in itself. Democratic citizenship, communitarians argue, is not merely a mechanism for choosing leaders. It is an activity in which people develop and exercise certain virtues: deliberation, public-spiritedness, prudence, justice.

So a society that lets all political activity be replaced by private consumption and procedural compliance loses something essential. Sandel makes that case at book length, in Democracy's Discontent.

[pause]

Three commitments, then. The self is constituted by community. Some goods exist only as shared practices. And taking part in civic life has value in itself.

Let's test that core. Here is a scene.

A mayor pitches a subsidy for a downtown stadium. The mayor's argument: "It will bring the community together."

Is that a communitarian argument, in the philosophical sense? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not by itself. [beat] Communitarianism is not any appeal to community. The philosophical claim is that selves are constituted by membership, and that some goods exist only as shared practices.

The mayor's pitch treats togetherness as a pleasant byproduct of a private development. And that is exactly the instrumental framing that communitarians criticize.

So here is a test you can carry with you, for any argument that uses the word community.

> The test: is the shared practice the good itself, or only a means?

## What follows, and what does not

That is the core. Now, what do communitarians build on it? A diagnosis, and two conclusions.

The diagnosis has a name. [beat] Atomization.

Defenses of liberalism, communitarians argue, have produced societies where individuals are increasingly disconnected: from family, from neighborhood, from religious community, from shared civic life. And the claim covers both variants of liberalism: the left-liberal variant built on rights, and the libertarian variant built on property.

Listen to how strong the diagnosis is. Atomization, communitarians argue, is not an unfortunate side effect of otherwise valuable institutions. It is what those institutions produce, when they are taken to their logical conclusion.

[pause]

The evidence they point to is Robert Putnam's book Bowling Alone, published in 2000. It documented declines since the 1960s in three things: membership in civic organizations, informal social ties, and social trust. Communitarians of every stream cite that evidence when they claim that something has in fact been lost.

Now the two conclusions.

One is about policy. The state, on this view, should not be neutral about whether civic institutions, families, and religious communities flourish. Programs that strengthen them are appropriate. Programs that displace them, or weaken them by accident, should be redesigned.

[beat]

The other is about tradition. Inherited traditions, communitarians argue, encode a practical wisdom that abstract reasoning cannot replicate. But traditions also contain blind spots, exclusions, and historical injustices. So tradition has authority, [beat] and it is not above critique. Traditions can be reformed from within, and they must be.

[pause]

That is what follows from the core. Now for what does not follow. There are three common misreadings, and a fourth that we will come back to.

The first misreading is that communitarianism is against the individual. Let me put that one to you as a question.

Here are two statements. One: individual freedom must yield whenever community norms conflict with it. Two: genuine individuality is developed within communities, not in opposition to them.

Which of the two is the communitarian view? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The second. Genuine individuality is developed within communities, not in opposition to them.

The first statement is the authoritarian misreading, and communitarians explicitly reject it. Their claim is about how individuality is formed. It is not a claim that individuality should be overridden.

> The communitarian move changes the order of explanation. It does not demote individuals.

Community first, then individuals formed within it. So the disagreement with libertarianism is about the relationship between individual and community. It is not about whether individuals matter.

[beat]

The second misreading is that communitarianism is against progress. But its thinkers, from MacIntyre on the right to Sandel on the left, have all been engaged in serious critique and reform of inherited institutions.

[beat]

The third misreading is that communitarianism is against the state. Communitarians vary widely on the role of the state, but most accept active state support for community institutions.

[beat]

And the fourth misreading is that all of this is only left against right, with extra steps. Hold that one. We need the thinkers first.

## The thinkers, and the name

Who built this tradition? Five names, and then a surprise.

First, Alasdair MacIntyre, and the book we began with, After Virtue. MacIntyre argued that modern moral discourse has become incoherent, because we have lost the shared frameworks that gave moral terms their meaning. His influence runs across both left and right communitarianism.

[beat]

Second, Michael Sandel, the most prominent communitarian of the present day. His book of 1982 is the foundational left-communitarian critique of Rawls's liberalism. His later books argue that progressive liberalism has lost the civic-republican vocabulary that historically made progressive politics possible.

[beat]

Third, Charles Taylor, a Canadian philosopher, and the author of Sources of the Self. Taylor's subjects are selfhood, and the relationship between religion and modernity. His work has shaped communitarian thought across left and right.

[beat]

Fourth, Michael Walzer, and a book called Spheres of Justice. Walzer argues that different goods belong to different spheres, each with its own principle of distribution. So he stands apart from two rivals: markets for everything, and a single principle of justice for all goods. He is influential on progressive communitarianism.

[beat]

And fifth, Amitai Etzioni, a sociologist, who founded the Communitarian Network and wrote The Spirit of Community. Etzioni was more popular than philosophical. His policy proposals tried to put communitarian theory to work in mainstream American politics.

[pause]

MacIntyre. Sandel. Taylor. Walzer. Etzioni.

Now the surprise. Of the four philosophers on that list, [beat] not one embraced the label.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the communitarian tag was pinned on those four by others, usually critics. MacIntyre denied the affiliation outright. Only Etzioni's political movement, in the 1990s, claimed the name for itself.

So the word is best understood like this.

> Communitarian is the name of a critique, not of a card-carrying school.

It names a family of arguments against the unencumbered self. It does not name a club with a membership list.

## Left, right, and middle

Now we can go back to the two books, and to the fourth misreading. Why does one philosophy land at both ends of the spectrum?

Think about what the conventional left-right axis is organized around. How much redistribution? How active should government be? How should tradition relate to progressive change?

The communitarian tradition cuts across those questions. Its central commitments can be deployed in support of a progressive political project, or of a conservative one.

So listen to the same core, argued from three places. For each: who argues it, what they argue, and what they point to.

On the political left, the names are Sandel, Walzer and Reich. They argue that pure left-liberalism, with its focus on individual rights, market regulation and redistributive transfers, misses the civic dimension of progressive politics. They point to the most successful progressive moments: the New Deal coalition, and the civil rights movement. Both drew on shared civic identity, religious moral language, and community organizing. Recovering that, they argue, is essential for progressive politics today.

[beat]

On the political right, the names are MacIntyre, Reno, and the religious-traditionalist tradition. They argue that pure right-liberalism, with its focus on free markets, individual property rights and procedural neutrality, has produced a society that conservatives should be unhappy with. They point to the traditional family, religious community, and shared cultural inheritance: conservative values that pure free-market thinking does not protect. Recovering them, they argue, requires a more robustly communitarian conservatism.

[beat]

And in the middle, the names are Etzioni, and the Third Way politics of Tony Blair and Bill Clinton. They argue that both major parties have lost touch with the shared civic life that political legitimacy requires. They point to a communitarian middle, neither progressive nor conservative in the conventional senses, as the way to recover it.

[pause]

Three arguments, from three places on the spectrum. And under each of them, the same core.

That answers the fourth misreading. Communitarianism is not just left against right with extra steps. Treat it as a sub-variety of progressive or conservative politics, and you miss what is distinctive.

> Communitarian describes a philosophical commitment more than a partisan alignment.

In plainer words: it names a philosophy, not a party.

## Three streams

A shared core, though, is not a shared politics. Inside the tradition there are three streams. They share the core, and they sharpen it differently.

Before I name them, pull the core back. It had three parts. Can you still give them?

[long pause]

The self, constituted by community. Goods that exist only as shared. And civic participation, valuable in itself.

Every stream holds all three. And every stream cites the same evidence of loss: Putnam's Bowling Alone. What separates them is what each one places at the center.

So each stream gets the same four things: its center, its argument, a name to attach to it, and its politics.

First, the civic-republican communitarian. For this stream, the central shared goods are political participation, public deliberation, and democratic citizenship. It argues that a society whose formal procedures are intact, but whose civic life has withered, has lost something essential. Its statement at book length is Sandel's Democracy's Discontent. And its politics lean left, but they are distinct from pure progressivism.

> The civic-republican stream: participation has intrinsic value, not just instrumental value.

Second, the religious-traditionalist communitarian. For this stream, the deepest carriers of shared moral life are religious community, traditional family, and inherited cultural and moral frameworks. It argues that secularized liberal politics has damaged them. One of its books is R. R. Reno's Return of the Strong Gods. And its politics lean right, but they are distinct from libertarian-conservative coalitions, which it holds protect markets better than they protect communities.

> The religious-traditionalist stream: public life that excludes religious vocabulary is not neutral.

Third, the progressive communitarian. For this stream, the center is shared social provision: healthcare, education, public spaces. It argues that progressive policy should be framed in civic-republican terms, rather than purely in terms of rights. A thinker influential on it is Walzer, with his separate spheres. And its politics are solidly left, but they differ in rhetoric and in substance from pure rights-based progressivism.

> The progressive stream: shared social provision is communitarian values in institutional form.

Three streams, one core. And one of the three probably sits furthest from your own instincts.

Take that one. What is the strongest case for it, in its own terms?

[pause 5s]

Hold on to your answer.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. For communitarians, the self is constituted by community membership, not prior to it. That move changes the order of explanation. It does not demote individuals.

[beat]

Two. The core has three parts: constitutive community, shared goods, and civic participation valued in itself. On it communitarians build a diagnosis, atomization, and two conclusions. Policy should support community institutions. And tradition has authority, but is not above critique.

[beat]

Three. The test for a communitarian argument: is the shared practice the good itself, or only a means?

[beat]

Four. Five thinkers: MacIntyre, Sandel, Taylor, Walzer, Etzioni. And a name that most of them never chose. Communitarian is the name of a critique, not of a card-carrying school.

[beat]

And five. Communitarian describes a philosophical commitment more than a partisan alignment. It turns up on the left, on the right and in the middle. And it runs in three streams: civic-republican, religious-traditionalist, and progressive.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Which stream draws the right political conclusions from the shared core? And what do those commitments imply for actual policy?

That is where the argument continues, and it is still open. It is also where the next lecture picks up, with each of the three streams examined in detail.

For now, go back to the two books. One year apart. One complaint against modern liberalism. [beat] They parted ways in politics. They never parted ways on the core.
