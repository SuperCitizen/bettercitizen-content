February 2021. Mitt Romney, the Republican Party's presidential nominee of 2012, makes a proposal. Send American parents up to 350 dollars a month for each young child.

[beat]

A month later, congressional Democrats pass a temporary child allowance of their own: 300 dollars a month for children under six. It is part of the American Rescue Plan. And it passes over unified Republican opposition.

[pause]

Two nearly identical policies. Two opposed coalitions. [beat] And one justification that was available to both: families are institutions worth supporting as such, not just households to means-test.

That justification is communitarian. And that episode is this lecture in miniature.

[pause]

Last time, we looked inside the tradition, at its three streams. This time we look outward, at its neighbors: conservatism, progressivism, socialism, and libertarianism.

Here is the claim we are going to test. What is distinctive about communitarianism, among contemporary political traditions, is its cross-cutting position. It overlaps substantially with conservatism and with progressivism, and to a lesser extent with socialism. Its deep disagreement is with libertarianism. And even there, the disagreement is more philosophical than total.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to say what each of those four traditions shares with communitarianism, and what divides them.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two large neighbors, conservatism and progressivism, and the answer that liberal theory gave. Second, socialism and libertarianism. Third, where each of the three streams finds its allies. And last, the issues where the partisan alignment is misleading.

For each neighbor, the same two questions. What is shared? [beat] And what divides?

## Conservatism and progressivism

Start with conservatism. The overlap is substantial, and many of the concerns are identical. There are four.

One: civic and religious institutions matter. Communitarians and traditionalist conservatives agree that family, religious community, neighborhood organizations, and shared cultural inheritance are essential to human flourishing.

Two: pure individualism is corrosive. Both traditions are critical of the atomizing effects of pure rights-based liberalism, and of pure free-market individualism.

Three: tradition has authority. The Burkean conservative's presumption against rapid change overlaps with the communitarian respect for tradition.

And four: skepticism of meritocratic individualism. Sandel's critique, from the communitarian left, and the traditionalist conservative critique converge on one target. That target is the liberal, meritocratic synthesis of the years since the 1990s.

[beat]

So what divides them? Traditional conservatives are committed to inherited cultural and religious institutions in their particular forms. Progressive communitarians are committed to inclusive, multicultural civic frameworks. The disagreement is about which inheritances matter, and how change should occur.

Put another way, both sides hold that inheritance matters. They part over which inheritance, and over how it may change.

[pause]

Now progressivism. Here the overlap is substantial with the civic-republican stream of progressivism, and smaller with identity-justice progressivism.

Again there are four points of contact. The first is direct: Sandel, Reich and Walzer are progressive communitarians, so the overlap with mainstream liberal-progressive politics is large. The second is universal social provision: both traditions support universal healthcare, free public education, public infrastructure, and shared social goods.

The third is a concern about meritocratic individualism, about the dignity of work and the limits of sorting people by credentials. And the fourth is support for public schools, libraries, parks, and the civil service.

But listen to how the two traditions frame the same programs. The communitarian framing emphasizes that these institutions are civic and shared. The progressive framing emphasizes equity and rights.

That difference in framing points to the divide. Progressive politics has historically been organized around frameworks of individual rights: civil rights, women's rights, LGBTQ rights. Communitarianism is more comfortable arguing for a shared civic identity, one that crosses those particular identity claims.

The tension is not absolute. Progressive communitarians try to combine both. [beat] But it is real.

[pause]

One more thing belongs here, because liberal theory did not ignore the communitarian critique. It answered it.

In 1993, John Rawls published Political Liberalism. In it he recast his framework as "political, not metaphysical". By that he meant an arrangement for citizens who hold different comprehensive doctrines, and not a theory of the self. The book is widely taken as the liberal accommodation of the communitarian challenge of the 1980s.

Bruce Ackerman made a different liberal move, in a book called We the People. He interpreted American constitutional history itself in civic-republican terms.

So hold on to this.

> The boundary between the traditions is a negotiation, not a wall.

## Socialism and libertarianism

Two more neighbors. Socialism first.

Here too the overlap is real, with both democratic socialism and social democracy. And it has four parts.

Shared social provision: universal healthcare, education, housing, childcare. A converging critique: the communitarian critique of liberal individualism meets the socialist critique of capitalist atomization. And worker organization: trade unions are communitarian institutions in their own right, shared institutions of solidarity.

The fourth part needs a word of its own. Both traditions argue that healthcare, education and basic housing should not be allocated by ability to pay. The name for that argument is decommodification. [beat] Decommodification: basic goods, not allocated by ability to pay.

So what divides them? Socialism has the more substantial program of economic restructuring: worker ownership, public production, market-skeptical institutions. Communitarianism is more politically moderate on economic structure, and more focused on civic and cultural questions. The two streams that overlap most are the progressive-communitarian and the democratic-socialist.

[pause]

Now libertarianism. Communitarianism shares less with it than with the other three. But what it shares is real, and it comes to three things.

First, a skepticism of bureaucratic centralization. Both traditions worry about distant, unaccountable institutions, though libertarians focus on government, and communitarians on government and corporations alike.

Second, civil society as an alternative to the state. Some communitarian streams, especially the religious-traditionalist, emphasize voluntary civic and religious association over state action. Libertarians do similarly.

Third, both prefer that decisions be made locally, at the level of the community. The names for that preference are federalism and subsidiarity.

[beat]

Then comes the divide, and it is the deep one. Libertarians treat individual choice as foundational. Communitarians treat community membership as constitutive of selfhood. Libertarians emphasize property rights as foundational. Communitarians treat property as one good among several, with limits set by communal commitments.

> Libertarians start from choice and property. Communitarians start from community as constitutive.

Let's test the four neighbors. Here are four positions.

One: treating individual choice as the foundation of political life. Two: preferring local, community-level decision-making to distant, centralized institutions. Three: treating property rights as prior to communal commitments. Four: decommodifying healthcare and education.

Only one of the four is a genuine overlap between communitarians and libertarians. Which one? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The second: the preference for local decision-making. Subsidiarity and federalism are the real common ground, because both traditions distrust distant, unaccountable power.

The first and the third are precisely where the two divide. Communitarians treat membership as constitutive, not choice, and not property. And the fourth is an overlap with socialism, not with libertarianism.

Keeping the overlaps sorted by tradition is the core skill here.

> Communitarianism shares different things with different neighbors.

With conservatism: civic and religious institutions, tradition's authority, the critique of atomization. With progressivism: universal provision, civic infrastructure, the dignity of work. With socialism: decommodification, and unions as solidarity. With libertarianism: subsidiarity, civil society, and distrust of centralized power.

It borrows a different shared ground from each neighbor. And that is why its adherents can land in either party.

## Where each stream finds its allies

So, is communitarianism left or right? [beat] That question has no single answer. The reason is the three streams from last time, which relate to the neighboring traditions differently.

Each stream gets the same three points: which neighbor stands nearest, where the friction is, and how it treats the rest.

First, the civic-republican communitarian. Its closest working relationship is with mainstream progressivism, through shared support for public institutions and universal provision. But it has a standing complaint against that ally: rights-language and policy wonkery are not a civic life. It treats libertarianism as its deepest opponent, and traditionalist conservatism as a sometime ally against atomization.

> The civic-republican stream: closest to mainstream progressivism, with a standing complaint.

Second, the religious-traditionalist communitarian. It stands closest to traditionalist conservatism and the post-liberal right. But it rejects the free-market individualism of the older conservative coalition. It can make common cause with the left on family policy, and against Big Tech, though that alliance frays on sexuality, religion, and schooling.

> The religious-traditionalist stream: closest to traditionalist conservatism, while rejecting free-market individualism.

Third, the progressive communitarian. It stands closest to social democracy, and it holds that universal programs and unions are the communitarian institutions of a modern, diverse society. But it treats the religious-traditionalist alliance with cultural conservatism as the tradition's liability. It works to fuse rights-based inclusion with a shared civic identity.

> The progressive stream: closest to social democracy, through universal programs and unions.

Three streams, three nearest neighbors.

[pause]

One comparison sharpens the American picture. European political traditions have no direct equivalent to American communitarianism. Among the nearest analogues is Catholic social teaching, with its ideas of subsidiarity, solidarity, and the dignity of labor. And Germany's CDU, a Christian-democratic party, has historically been communitarian-influenced.

The difference is institutional. European political space generally gives communitarian commitments stronger institutional support: welfare states, subsidiarity, religious accommodation. American communitarianism often functions more as a critique of American political culture than as an established political program.

[pause]

A check on your memory, before the last step. For each neighbor, I gave you what divides it from communitarianism. Take the first neighbor, conservatism. Can you still say what the divide was?

[long pause]

## Where the party map misleads

Now the last step: the specific issues where the partisan alignment is misleading. There are five. For each, listen for the communitarian framing underneath.

First, the universal child allowance, where we began. Romney's Family Security Act. Sherrod Brown's long-running push to expand the child tax credit. The temporary monthly credit in the American Rescue Plan. The framing is support for families as institutions. It bridges left and right, even when the coalitions refuse to merge.

[beat]

Second, the coalitions against Big Tech. Josh Hawley, a Republican, and Senator Klobuchar, a Democrat, share substantive policy positions on platform monopolies. The framing under both is the defense of community institutions against impersonal market forces.

[beat]

Third, national service and civil-service careers. Expanded national service programs have bipartisan support: AmeriCorps, military expansion, civil-service hiring. The framing is that civic participation is itself valuable.

[beat]

Fourth, loneliness and the deaths of despair. Putnam's work on community decline, and the work of Anne Case and Angus Deaton on midlife mortality, draw substantive concern from both parties. The framing is that community matters for human flourishing.

[beat]

And fifth, local journalism. There is bipartisan recognition that the collapse of local journalism has weakened civic life, and various subsidy proposals draw support across traditions.

Let's use that last one as a test. Here is a scene.

A bill would subsidize local newsrooms. It attracts two co-sponsors: a religious conservative, and a progressive.

What communitarian framing makes that pairing coherent? And what objection is each sponsor most likely to meet from inside their own party? Think it through.

[long pause]

The shared framing is that local journalism is civic infrastructure. It is a constitutively shared good that markets are failing to sustain, like parks or libraries.

Now the objections. The progressive sponsor's party will object on grounds of press independence. Can a subsidized press hold government accountable? The conservative sponsor's party will object on market-individualist grounds. If readers won't pay, why should taxpayers?

Notice where both objections come from. [beat] They come from the liberal wings of each coalition. And that is the pattern of this whole lecture.

> Communitarian convergence forms across parties and meets resistance within them.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Communitarianism shares different things with different neighbors. That is why its adherents can land in either party, and why the question of left or right has no single answer.

[beat]

Two. Three of the divides. With conservatism: which inheritances matter, and how change should occur. With progressivism: rights-based identity frameworks, against shared civic identity. With socialism: economic restructuring, against a civic and cultural focus.

[beat]

Three. The deep divide is with libertarianism: choice and property as foundational, against community as constitutive. And even there, the two share subsidiarity, civil society, and a distrust of centralized power.

[beat]

Four. Each stream has a different nearest neighbor. For the civic-republican stream, mainstream progressivism. For the religious-traditionalist stream, traditionalist conservatism. For the progressive stream, social democracy. And liberal theory answered the critique: the boundary between the traditions is a negotiation, not a wall.

[beat]

And five. Communitarian convergence forms across parties and meets resistance within them. The child allowance. Big Tech. National service. The deaths of despair. Local journalism.

[pause]

Here is what these three lectures have not settled, and cannot settle for you. A defensible position on communitarianism, whether you identify with the tradition or not, requires four things.

A view on which stream best fits your values. A view on how communitarian commitments translate into specific policy, in current American political conditions. A view on how the tradition relates to the others: where it overlaps, where it diverges, and where the strongest critiques bite. And an honest accounting of what evidence would change your position.

So try one piece of that now. Go back to the deep divide: choice as foundational, or community as constitutive. One of those two starting points convinces you less. Take that one, and state the best case for it.

[pause 5s]

That is where the argument goes from here: to you. When you are back at a screen, the app has a coach that will engage you on each of those four things, a place to save your declared position, and a steel-man drill.

The discipline of having to construct the strongest version of a view you reject is where defended positions become refined ones.
