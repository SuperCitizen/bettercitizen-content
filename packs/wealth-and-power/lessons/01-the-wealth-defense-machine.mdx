# The wealth-defense machine

Between 2014 and 2018, the twenty-five richest Americans grew about $401 billion richer. Over those same five years they paid $13.6 billion in federal income tax. ProPublica, which obtained the underlying IRS files and published them on June 8, 2021, divided one number by the other and called the result a 3.4 percent "true tax rate" — a phrase the reporters had to invent, because it measures tax against *wealth growth*, and the United States taxes neither wealth nor unsold gains. Warren Buffett's fortune rose an estimated $24.3 billion in that window; he paid $23.7 million, a true tax rate of one-tenth of one percent. Sit with the number, then ask the question this lesson exists to answer: is 3.4 percent an outrage, or just arithmetic?

The honest answer is *both*, and untangling which part is which is the whole job. Almost nothing in the ProPublica files was illegal. The machinery that produced a 3.4 percent rate is a body of statute, regulation, and professional practice that any sufficiently wealthy person can hire. This lesson maps that machinery — what it is, what it shields, and how concentrated the wealth it defends has become. It deliberately withholds the verdict. Whether the machine actually *bends policy* is Lesson 2; what to *do* about it is Lesson 3.

## What a "wealth-defense industry" is — and is not

Start with definition discipline, because the loose version of this idea is a conspiracy theory and the rigorous version is not. The rigorous version comes from political scientist Jeffrey Winters, whose 2011 book *Oligarchy* makes an unusual move: it defines [[term:oligarchy]] not as a *form of government* — not "rule by the few" in the textbook sense — but as **the politics of wealth defense by actors who command concentrated material resources**. On this account an oligarch is simply someone rich enough that defending the fortune becomes a central political project, and oligarchy is the set of activities that defense generates. It is a behavior, not a regime type, which is why Winters can argue that oligarchy and [[term:democracy]] are "compatible and often fused."

Winters and Benjamin Page, in their 2009 article "Oligarchy in the United States?", split wealth defense into two tasks. **Property defense** is protecting the fortune from outright seizure — by mobs, by revolution, by confiscatory expropriation. **Income defense** is the subtler, modern task: protecting the fortune from *lawful* redistribution, above all from progressive taxation. Their key historical claim is that the United States is a "civil oligarchy": the *state* now guarantees property rights so reliably that the rich no longer need private armies, and so they redirect almost all of their wealth-defense energy into income defense — into the law firms, accountants, banks, and tax planners who keep the legal redistribution from biting.

It is in the 2011 book that Winters gives that apparatus a name: the **Income Defense Industry**, the network of specialized professionals whose product is a lower effective tax rate for the very rich. (The earlier 2009 article describes "armies of professional, skilled actors" and "salaried advocates" but does not yet use the capitalized label — a small distinction worth getting right, because the coinage matters to the argument.) A decade later, the inequality writer Chuck Collins popularized a broader variant. In *The Wealth Hoarders* (2021) he describes a "secret army of tax attorneys, accountants and wealth managers" — he calls them "enablers" and "the getaway car drivers" — paid millions to hide trillions for the richest 0.01 percent, and he names the apparatus the [[term:wealth-defense-industry]], crediting the underlying concept to Winters.

> **Common misconception:** "Wealth defense" means a smoke-filled room. It does not. The claim is the opposite — that no coordination is required, because the incentives align without it. A demand (keep my effective rate low) meets a supply (highly paid professionals) inside a body of law that rewards the transaction. That is a *market*, not a plot, and it is exactly why the machinery is hard to dislodge: there is no central node to arrest.

## The ladder: oligarchy, plutocracy, kleptocracy

Three words get used as if they were synonyms, and the differences are load-bearing for everything that follows. Place them on a ladder:

- **Oligarchy** is rule, or outsized influence, by a small group — *however that group acquired its standing.* The few might be priests, generals, or party officials. Wealth is one possible basis, not a defining one.
- **[[term:plutocracy]]** is the *subtype* in which the few hold power specifically *because* they are rich — power that derives from concentrated wealth. Every plutocracy is an oligarchy; not every oligarchy is a plutocracy.
- **[[term:kleptocracy]]** — literally "rule by thieves" — is defined by *illegality*: enrichment through the subversion of public institutions, looting the state itself. The distinguishing mark is the crime.

The ladder matters because it locates the American argument precisely. The serious claim about the United States is *plutocracy* — that concentrated wealth, professionally defended, has outsized influence — and emphatically **not** kleptocracy. The machinery in this lesson is, with rare exceptions, lawful. Confusing the two is the single most common error in this debate: it lets critics overstate (treating tax avoidance as theft) and lets defenders dismiss (pointing out, correctly, that no law was broken, as if that ended the conversation). Hold the distinction, and the real question sharpens: not "is it a crime?" but "is a body of law that produces a 3.4 percent rate the law we meant to write?"

## The mechanisms, by statute

Income defense is not magic; it is a handful of specific provisions in the Internal Revenue Code, used in combination. Four are worth knowing by section number, because the section number is where the argument actually lives.

The keystone is **buy, borrow, die**, and its engine is **26 U.S.C. sec. 1014**. Section 1014 says that when you inherit an asset, its income-tax *basis* — the figure from which a future taxable gain is measured — resets to the asset's fair-market value at the owner's death. This is the **stepped-up basis**. Pair it with two further facts: unrealized gains are not income until you sell, and loan proceeds are not income at all. Now the three steps fall out. *Buy* and hold appreciating assets, paying no tax as they rise. *Borrow* against them to fund your life — the bank lends against the stock, and a loan is not taxable. *Die* holding the assets, at which point sec. 1014 erases every unrealized gain accrued over a lifetime, so the heirs inherit at the stepped-up value and the appreciation is never income-taxed by anyone. The Budget Lab at Yale (2025) analyzes exactly this sequence; it is the cleanest single explanation of how a multibillion-dollar fortune can generate a 3.4 percent true tax rate without anyone cheating.

Read the flow as a relay, the way Lesson 1 of the dark-money pack reads its pipeline: at no step is anything concealed or illegal, and yet the income tax — the federal government's main instrument for taxing the rich — never touches the gain. The tax does not so much get evaded as get *designed around*, by provisions each defensible on its own terms.

Three more provisions round out the field guide. **Carried interest** is the share of a fund's profits (often 20 percent) that a private-equity or hedge-fund manager keeps as compensation. Because it is structured as a share of investment gains rather than salary, it is taxed at the lower long-term capital-gains rate instead of the ordinary-income rate that applies to wages. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 added **26 U.S.C. sec. 1061**, which claws some of this back by recharacterizing the profit as ordinary income *unless* the underlying asset was held more than three years — a narrowing, not a closing, of the gap. A **dynasty trust** pairs the generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax exemption with state law: a handful of states (South Dakota and Nevada among them) have abolished the old Rule Against Perpetuities, so a trust formed there can hold sheltered wealth across many generations rather than the limited term old common law allowed. And a **donor-advised fund** (DAF), governed by **26 U.S.C. sec. 4966** since the Pension Protection Act of 2006, gives the donor an immediate, full charitable deduction the year the money goes in — yet, unlike a private foundation with its 5 percent annual payout requirement, a DAF has *no federal minimum payout at all*. The deduction is instant; the charity may wait indefinitely.

Wealth that wants to leave the country has its own infrastructure. Economist Gabriel Zucman estimated, in *The Hidden Wealth of Nations* (2015), that roughly 8 percent of the world's household financial wealth — about $7.6 trillion as of 2014 — sat in offshore tax havens, around three-quarters of it undeclared. (That is a 2014 estimate, not a current figure.) The United States has since pushed back: FATCA, enacted in 2010, forces foreign banks to report accounts held by U.S. persons or face a 30 percent withholding penalty. And the leak-driven journalism — the Panama Papers (2016, 11.5 million files from one firm) and the Pandora Papers (2021, 11.9 million-plus documents from fourteen providers, reported by more than 600 ICIJ journalists) — exposed the scale of these structures. The crucial, frequently-missed point: most of what those leaks documented was *lawful*. The shell companies and offshore trusts were, in the main, legal instruments. That is precisely what makes the offshore world a wealth-*defense* story rather than a wealth-*crime* story.

## How concentrated is the wealth being defended?

The mechanisms matter because of what they protect, so anchor the scale in verified numbers — carefully, because this terrain is full of contested decimals. The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts put the top 1 percent's share of U.S. household net worth in the low-30s percent in recent data — call it about 31 to 32 percent. Applying its own (somewhat different) methodology, the work of [[person:emmanuel-saez]] and Gabriel Zucman puts the top 1 percent's share around 38 percent in 2018, up roughly ten points since 1989, with the top 10 percent holding something like 77 to 78 percent of all wealth (Saez and Zucman, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2020). The series disagree on the exact figure; they agree emphatically on the direction.

The deeper pattern is a long-run U-shape, which the figure below traces for the very top sliver — the wealthiest one-thousandth of households.

Read the curve as a sentence. Wealth concentration at the very top was near its modern maximum on the eve of the Great Depression, collapsed across the New Deal and the mid-century decades of high top tax rates and broad growth, bottomed out in the 1970s, and has climbed steeply since — from roughly 7 percent in 1978 to about 22 percent by 2012 (Saez and Zucman, *QJE*, 2016). That return toward [[term:gilded-age]] levels is the empirical fact that makes the wealth-defense question urgent: this is not a permanent feature of American capitalism but a historically specific reversal, which means policy and institutions plausibly drove it — and could plausibly bend it back. *Whether* they did, and *whose* policy, is the fight Lesson 2 takes up.

Now the contested centerpiece, handled with care. ProPublica's "true tax rate" — the 3.4 percent we opened with — is genuinely the most arresting number in the file, and it is also methodologically unconventional, and both things are true at once. The denominator is *wealth growth*, mostly unrealized capital gains, which the U.S. has never treated as income. Critics are right that this is not the rate anyone's tax return computes; defenders of the framing reply that *that is the point* — the conventional measures miss the gains entirely, which is how a fortune can grow by tens of billions while the tax bill stays trivial. You do not have to pick the better denominator to take the lesson, which is that the choice of denominator is itself where this argument is fought.

## Legal, not lawless — and the line where that stops

The thread running through every mechanism above is a single distinction: **tax avoidance** (arranging affairs to owe less, lawfully) versus **tax evasion** (illegally failing to pay what is owed). Buy-borrow-die, carried interest, dynasty trusts, donor-advised funds, most offshore structures — these are avoidance. They exploit the law as written. That is the wealth-defense industry's entire business model, and it is why the honest framing of this pack is *plutocracy, not kleptocracy*.

There is a separate, illegal magnitude, and conflating it with avoidance corrupts the whole debate. The IRS estimates the **tax gap** — legally owed tax that is not paid on time — at a gross $696 billion for tax year 2022 ($606 billion net after late payments and enforcement). That is evasion, plus error and nonfiling; it is the part the law already prohibits. The wealth-defense industry is, definitionally, the *other* thing: it operates in daylight, files accurate returns, and owes nothing more because the code asks for nothing more.

> **Why this matters:** Reformers and defenders often talk past each other because they are answering different questions. "Did they break the law?" — almost always no. "Did the law produce a result we would have chosen on purpose?" — that is contestable, and it is a question about the *statute*, not the taxpayer. Keeping the two apart is the discipline this whole pack rests on.

One live front shows the stakes. In *Moore v. United States* (2024), the Supreme Court considered whether income must be "realized" before Congress may tax it — a question that bears directly on whether a federal wealth tax or a tax on unrealized gains would even be constitutional under the Sixteenth Amendment. The Court upheld the specific tax at issue on narrow grounds, in an opinion by Justice Kavanaugh, while several justices wrote separately and the broader realization question was left unresolved. That unresolved question is part of why structural reform is hard, and it waits for Lesson 3.

## What this lesson is not

This lesson is the machinery, not the verdict — the plumbing, deliberately stopping short of the judgment. You now own the mechanism: a *wealth-defense* (or *income-defense*) apparatus is the lawful market in professional services that lowers the effective tax rate on concentrated wealth; the United States is, in Winters's terms, a *civil oligarchy* where property defense has given way to income defense; the tools are specific statutes — sec. 1014, sec. 1061, the GST exemption, sec. 4966 — used in combination; the wealth they protect has climbed back toward Gilded-Age concentration; and the conduct is, with rare exceptions, *avoidance, not evasion*, which makes the live question "is it right?" rather than "is it a crime?"

What this lesson did **not** establish is whether any of it actually *captures policy* — whether the machine merely keeps rich people's taxes low or whether it bends the laws and budgets that govern everyone. That is genuinely contested, and Lesson 2 steel-mans all three camps on it: the **oligarchy realist**, who reads the concentration and the apparatus as evidence that wealth has bent policy toward the rich; the **pluralist skeptic**, who insists the "domination" evidence is overread and demands rigorous causal proof before structural remedies; and the **public-choice limited-government** view, which locates the disease not in private wealth but in the state's discretionary power to sell favors. As Lesson 1 here showed, the machinery is real and mostly legal. Whether it rules is the next question.
