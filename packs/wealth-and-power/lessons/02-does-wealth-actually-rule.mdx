# Does wealth actually rule? The debate, steel-manned

In 2014, two political scientists ran the most ambitious test the question had ever received. Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page assembled 1,779 distinct policy questions from 1981 to 2002, matched each to what surveys showed average citizens wanted and what affluent citizens wanted, and asked whose preferences predicted what government actually did. Their conclusion landed like a verdict: average citizens have "little or no independent influence." The headlines wrote themselves — *America is an oligarchy*. Three years later, J. Alexander Branham, Stuart Soroka, and Christopher Wlezien ran much the same data and reported that the middle class and the rich agree about 90 percent of the time. Same evidence, opposite headline. The gap between those two readings is the lesson.

Lesson 1 established the machinery — the legal, professionalized apparatus that defends large fortunes against taxation, what Chuck Collins popularized as the **Wealth Defense Industry**. It withheld a verdict on purpose. This lesson takes up the harder question: does any of that machinery actually *capture policy*? It is genuinely contested terrain, so what follows steel-mans all three serious camps before naming what the evidence can and cannot settle.

## The oligarchy case: capacity, drift, and the affluent's edge

Start with the strongest version of the claim that concentrated wealth rules. It rests on four pillars, each from a different scholar, that reinforce one another.

The first is **capacity**. In their 2009 article "Oligarchy in the United States?", Jeffrey Winters and Benjamin Page built what they called Material Power Indices from IRS and estate data and argued that the top sliver — roughly the top 0.1 percent — holds the sheer resources to dominate on the issues that matter most to them, above all taxation. Crucially, this is a claim about *potential*. Winters and Page were explicit that oligarchs "need not occupy formal office, conspire together, or even engage extensively in politics in order to prevail." An [[term:oligarchy]], on this reading, is not a smoke-filled room; it is a distribution of resources so skewed that the wealthy get their way without having to try very hard.

The second pillar is **the affluent's edge when preferences diverge**. Gilens's *Affluence and Influence* (2012) found that when the policy preferences of high-income and low- or middle-income Americans pull apart, policy tracks the affluent. The third is **representational weighting**: Larry Bartels, studying U.S. senators' roll-call votes in the late 1980s and early 1990s, found that constituents at the 75th income percentile carried nearly three times the influence of those at the 25th, and the bottom third got essentially no weight at all. The fourth is **drift**. In *Winner-Take-All Politics* (2010), Jacob Hacker and Paul Pierson argued that the explosion of top-end inequality after the 1970s was not the impersonal weather of markets but the product of sustained, organized political activity — including "policy drift," the deliberate decision *not* to update rules (the minimum wage, financial regulation, tax brackets) as the economy changed. Inaction, they insisted, is a policy choice, and a quiet one is easy to win. The carried-interest preference from Lesson 1 is a textbook specimen: decades of bipartisan promises to close it yielded only the 2017 law's modest three-year holding rule, never repeal — a fortune-favoring provision kept alive less by any single victory than by the absence of one. Drift, on this account, is how concentrated interests prevail without leaving fingerprints.

Stacked together, these make a formidable case for a functioning [[term:plutocracy]] — a polity formally equal but operated for the wealthy. But notice the seam between pillar one and the rest, because the skeptics will pry at it.

<Checkpoint question="Winters and Page measure that the top 0.1% hold enough resources to dominate tax policy if they chose to. Gilens finds that when rich and poor disagree, policy follows the rich. Which one demonstrates that wealth actually changed an outcome — and which only shows it could?">
  Winters-Page measures **capacity** — the *potential* to prevail, inferred from the distribution of resources, not from any specific win. Gilens measures something closer to **capture** — an observed correlation between affluent preferences and outcomes when groups diverge. The distinction is the whole ballgame: showing someone *could* dominate is not the same as showing they *did*, and even a correlation between rich preferences and policy is not yet proof the preferences *caused* the policy. Hold that gap; the next section drives a truck through it.
</Checkpoint>

## The pluralist reply: 90 percent agreement and coincidental representation

The skeptics do not deny that the rich are rich. They deny that the "domination" finding survives a hard look at the data — and they come at it two ways.

The first is the overlap problem. When Branham, Soroka, and Wlezien re-examined the Gilens-Page issues in "When Do the Rich Win?" (2017), they found that the middle class and the rich actually *agree* about 90 percent of the time, with meaningful divergence on only about 185 policies. On the rare issues where the two groups split, the rich prevailed modestly more than half the time — a real edge, but a narrow one. And much of the inequality they did find was what they called **negative power**: the middle and the rich, when they happen to agree, can jointly *block* something the poor want, which is a very different and more diffuse phenomenon than a wealthy elite *commanding* outcomes against everyone else.

What the picture shows is that the dramatic finding lives entirely inside that thin right-hand slice. On roughly nine issues in ten, asking "did the rich beat the middle class?" has no answer, because they wanted the same thing. That is the empirical wedge the skeptics drive into the oligarchy story.

The second line of attack is sharper and more theoretical. Peter Enns, in "Relative Policy Support and Coincidental Representation" (2015), pointed out that income groups' preferences are extremely highly correlated — about 0.9 — across issues. When two groups want nearly the same things nearly all the time, a government that simply followed the *median voter* would automatically appear to "represent the affluent," because the affluent's preferences sit right on top of everyone else's. Enns called this **coincidental representation**: the preference-gap method cannot tell genuine elite domination apart from a democracy that happens to deliver outcomes the rich also like. Correlation, in other words, is doing work the authors attributed to causation.

> **Think it through:** You and a billionaire order from the same menu and pick the same dish nine times out of ten. The tenth time you disagree, the kitchen makes the billionaire's choice slightly more often. When the meal matches your taste — were you *catered to*, or were you a coincident beneficiary who would have gotten that dish anyway?

Behind both replies stands an older tradition. Robert Dahl's *Who Governs?* (1961), studying who actually decided things in New Haven, argued that American politics is **pluralist**: power is dispersed across competing groups that win on different issues at different times, checked by elections, parties, and courts — a direct rebuttal to the "power elite" theorists (C. Wright Mills, Floyd Hunter) who saw a unified ruling class. The pluralist skeptic's bottom line is not "wealth is harmless." It is "before we rebuild the tax code, show me causation, not coincidence."

This is exactly the kind of exchange that makes the field honest. Gilens published a direct response in the same 2015 symposium. Coincidental alignment, Gilens argued, is *not* democratic influence: when ordinary citizens get outcomes they like only because the affluent happened to want the same thing, they are "coincident beneficiaries rather than causes" of policy — and a democracy in which the non-affluent matter only when they agree with the affluent is an impoverished one, however pleasant the outcomes feel. Enns, in his rejoinder, held his ground: a normative claim about what *ought* to count as influence, he countered, cannot rescue a method that on the numbers still cannot separate capture from coincidence. The argument did not resolve; it clarified. The realist and the skeptic are not disputing the same number from different motives — they are disputing what a correlation between preferences and policy is *allowed to prove*.

## The public-choice reframe: the disease is discretion, not wealth

Both camps so far take "concentrated private wealth" as the thing to explain. A third tradition says they have misdiagnosed the patient. On this view the danger is not large fortunes as such but the **discretionary power of the state to grant favors** — and wherever that power exists, wealth will compete for it. The cure is therefore not a wealth tax but a smaller menu of favors to buy.

The mechanism has a name: [[term:rent-seeking]], the expenditure of real resources to capture government-granted privileges rather than to create value. Gordon Tullock sketched the idea in 1967, and Anne Krueger coined the term in her 1974 paper "The Political Economy of the Rent-Seeking Society." A tariff, a license, an exclusive contract, a tax carve-out — each is a prize, and firms will burn lawyers, lobbyists, and campaign dollars competing for it, a waste that grows directly with how much the state has to hand out. Inequality, on this account, is partly a *byproduct* of discretionary power, not the source of it.

George Stigler's "The Theory of Economic Regulation" (1971) supplies the next move. [[term:regulatory-capture]], Stigler argued, is the normal tendency, not the exception: regulation "is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit," because the regulated industry is the most motivated and best-organized claimant on the agency. And Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) explains *why* the well-resourced few beat the diffuse many without any conspiracy at all: a small group whose members each capture a large slice of the benefit organizes easily, while millions who would each gain a little face a free-rider problem and stay home. The tilt toward concentrated interests is structural — it needs no oligarchy, only ordinary incentives.

What, then, is the cure? Here the tradition's constitutional wing speaks. In *The Calculus of Consent* (1962), James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock argued that the way to limit the conversion of wealth into favors is to constrain government's discretionary power at the *constitutional* level — behind a "veil of uncertainty," before anyone knows which interests they will later hold, so the rules are written for fairness rather than for whoever is winning today. Buchanan won the 1986 Nobel Memorial Prize in part for this work. Shrink what government can be lobbied to grant, and you shrink the prize that wealth chases. (Lesson 3 picks up where these camps split on remedies, and the [[org:cato-institute]] is one institutional home of the public-choice view.)

Finally, the tradition disputes the premise that fortunes are even durable enough to constitute a ruling class. Joseph Schumpeter, in *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), described [[term:creative-destruction]] — the process that "incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within," routinely displacing dominant firms and the fortunes built on them. There is a live empirical fight here. Steven Kaplan and Joshua Rauh (2013) found that the share of the Forbes 400 who "grew up wealthy" fell from about 60 percent in 1982 to roughly 32 percent by 2011, reading top fortunes as increasingly the market's reward to scalable talent rather than to inheritance. But Philipp Korom, Mark Lutter, and Jens Beckert (2017), analyzing the same list, pushed back: even as the *entry* became more entrepreneurial, heirs were significantly more likely to *remain* on the list — inherited advantage persists. Churn at the door does not prove churn at the top.

<Checkpoint question="A senator inserts a narrow tax provision that benefits one industry, which had lobbied hard for it. The oligarchy realist, the pluralist skeptic, and the public-choice scholar each see something different. What does each emphasize?">
  The **oligarchy realist** sees capacity converted to capture — concentrated wealth bending a specific outcome, exactly the tax-favoring edge Winters-Page predicted. The **pluralist skeptic** asks whether this is one win among many (did labor, consumers, or a rival industry win the next ten fights?) and whether the provision actually diverged from what most voters wanted. The **public-choice scholar** sees rent-seeking and capture as the *predictable product of a tax code with favors to grant* — and concludes the fix is removing the discretionary carve-out power, not taxing the lobbyist's client. Same fact, three diagnoses, three cures.
</Checkpoint>

## The three camps, at full strength

Here is each position as its smartest advocate would put it — including the line each one concedes would change its mind. Read all three, especially the one you arrived predisposed to reject.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Oligarchy realist">
    Formal political equality coexists with plutocratic operation. The top 0.1% hold the *capacity* to prevail on the issues they care about most — taxation above all — and they need not conspire to do it (Winters & Page, 2009). When preferences diverge, policy tracks the affluent (Gilens, 2012); senators weight the top of the income distribution roughly three-to-one over the bottom and ignore the poorest third (Bartels). And the inequality itself was *built* by sustained organized politics and deliberate "drift," not impersonal markets (Hacker & Pierson). As one prominent and contested estimate, Saez and Zucman's *Triumph of Injustice* (2019) calculated that in 2018 the top 400 families paid a lower all-in tax rate (about 23%) than the bottom half (about 24.2%) — a finding Auten and Splinter dispute, since it turns on how transfers, refundable credits, and corporate-tax incidence are counted. Even setting that contested number aside, the structural case stands. The cure: tax and disclose concentrated wealth before its capacity hardens into permanent advantage.

    **What would change its mind:** rigorous causal evidence that the affluent's apparent edge is coincidental alignment rather than influence — that on the divergent issues, the rich win no more than a median-voter model predicts, and that "drift" reflects genuine gridlock rather than organized blocking.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Pluralist skeptic">
    The "domination" evidence is overread. Rich and middle-class Americans agree about 90% of the time; meaningful divergence appears on only ~185 of 1,779 issues, and even there the rich prevail only modestly more than half the time (Branham, Soroka & Wlezien, 2017). Because income groups' preferences correlate at about 0.9, a pure median-voter democracy would *automatically* look like it favors the affluent — so the preference-gap method cannot distinguish capture from coincidental representation (Enns, 2015). Power is genuinely dispersed across competing groups, parties, elections, and courts, winning and losing on different issues (Dahl, 1961). None of this says wealth is harmless; it says structural remedies demand causal proof, not a correlation the data can't disentangle — so the prudent course is evidence-gated: remedy demonstrated harms, and defer sweeping structural change until causation is shown.

    **What would change its mind:** evidence that survives the identification problem — natural experiments or design-based studies showing that, holding median preferences fixed, *changes in elite preference or elite spending* causally move policy, replicated beyond the divergent-issue slice.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Public-choice limited-government">
    The disease is the state's discretionary power to sell favors, not private wealth as such. Wherever government can grant a tariff, license, contract, or carve-out, firms burn real resources competing for it — rent-seeking (Tullock 1967; Krueger 1974) — and agencies are captured by the industries they regulate (Stigler 1971). Concentrated interests organize easily and diffuse majorities don't (Olson 1965), so the tilt is structural and needs no oligarchy. A wealth tax leaves the favor-granting power intact and simply moves the contest; the real cure is constitutional constraint on what government can be lobbied to grant (Buchanan & Tullock 1962). And dominant fortunes are less durable than the realist assumes — creative destruction keeps displacing them (Schumpeter 1942; Kaplan & Rauh 2013).

    **What would change its mind:** evidence that concentrated wealth captures policy *even where the state's discretionary favors are minimal* — that fortunes entrench across generations regardless of rent availability (the Korom-Lutter-Beckert persistence finding, extended), so that shrinking government would not shrink the capture.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Notice that the three are not symmetric. The realist and the skeptic dispute the *same* evidence — whether the preference-policy correlation proves influence. The public-choice scholar disputes the *frame*: even granting that wealth shapes policy, the lever is the state's discretion, so the realist and skeptic are both fighting over the wrong variable. A reader who collapses all three into "pro-wealth-tax versus anti-wealth-tax" has missed that the deepest disagreement is about what the question even is.

## What's settled, what's contested

**Settled.** Wealth in the United States is highly concentrated: the top 1% hold roughly the low-30s percent of net worth (Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts), and the top 0.1% share rose from about 7% in 1978 to roughly 22% by 2012 (Saez & Zucman, *QJE* 2016). The affluent do enjoy *some* edge when preferences diverge — both Gilens-Page and the Branham reanalysis agree the rich win more than half the divergent contests. Concentrated interests organize more easily than diffuse ones (Olson is not seriously disputed). And the machinery of Lesson 1 is real and largely legal.

**Genuinely contested.** Whether that edge amounts to domination or merely a modest tilt inside a small slice of issues. Whether the preference-policy correlation reflects causal influence or Enns's coincidental representation — an identification problem the field has clarified but not closed. Whether top fortunes are durable dynasties (Korom-Lutter-Beckert) or churning rewards to talent (Kaplan-Rauh). And whether the right lever is the *wealth* or the *discretion* — the realist-versus-public-choice split that no amount of preference data can adjudicate, because it is a disagreement about mechanism, not measurement.

**What this lesson is not.** It did not decide whether America is an oligarchy. That is precisely the live question, and the honest answer is that the strongest evidence on each side is real and the methods cannot yet settle it. What a defensible position *can* do is pick a frame, defend it against the strongest opposing case rather than the weakest, and name the evidence that would move it. Lesson 3 carries these same three camps into the live 2025–26 policy fight — the Warren Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act, *Moore v. United States*, the global minimum tax, and the antitrust revival — where the abstract disagreement about whether wealth rules becomes a concrete disagreement about what, if anything, to do.
