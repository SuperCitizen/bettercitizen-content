# The policy landscape: taxing and limiting concentrated wealth

"Two cents." It is Elizabeth Warren's signature line, and the 2026 bill behind it is more ambitious than the slogan. The Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act — S. 4246 in the 119th Congress, reintroduced with Representatives Pramila Jayapal and Brendan Boyle on March 26, 2026 — would levy a flat 2 percent a year on a household's net worth above $50 million and add a 1 percent surtax above $1 billion, for 3 percent at the top. By its sponsors' own arithmetic it would touch about 260,000 households — the top roughly 0.15 percent — and raise $6.2 trillion over a decade. Then the twist that makes the whole fight legible: in June 2024 the Supreme Court decided a case widely billed as the wealth tax's constitutional reckoning, *Moore v. United States* — and sidestepped the wealth-tax question, with a footnote that pointedly reserved judgment on "taxes on holdings, wealth, or net worth." The most consequential wealth-tax ruling of the decade decided nothing about wealth taxes. This lesson maps the live policy fight as of mid-2026, and carries Lesson 2's three camps into it.

## The two-cent bill, read closely

A [[term:wealth-tax]] — a recurring levy on the *stock* of net worth rather than the *flow* of income — is the reformers' headline instrument because, as Lesson 1 showed, the existing machine is built to keep the stock untouched. The buy-borrow-die strategy (borrow against appreciated assets, never sell, let heirs inherit at a stepped-up basis) means a fortune can compound for a lifetime and across generations while producing almost no taxable income. A levy on net worth bypasses the realization question entirely: it taxes what you *have*, not what you *sell*.

The Warren bill is engineered against the avoidance Lesson 1 catalogued. It includes a 40 percent **exit tax** on the net worth of any ultra-millionaire who renounces U.S. citizenship to escape the levy — a direct answer to capital flight. It pairs the rate with $100 million in new IRS anti-evasion and enforcement funding, on the theory that a wealth tax lives or dies on valuation and audit capacity. And it reaches worldwide assets, closing the offshore route that Gabriel Zucman's *The Hidden Wealth of Nations* (2015) estimated held about 8 percent of global household financial wealth — roughly $7.6 trillion as of 2014, around three-quarters of it undeclared.

One number deserves a flashing caveat. The $6.2 trillion is the *sponsors'* estimate, drawn from analysis the senator's office commissioned — not a score from the Congressional Budget Office or the Joint Committee on Taxation, the nonpartisan scorekeepers whose numbers Congress actually books. Wealth-tax revenue projections are unusually fragile because they depend on assumptions about valuation, avoidance, and behavioral response that reasonable analysts dispute by wide margins. Treat $6.2 trillion as an advocacy figure, not a settled forecast.

<Checkpoint question="Why does a wealth tax reach fortunes that the income tax structurally cannot — and which Lesson 1 mechanism does it most directly neutralize?">
  The income tax only bites when gains are *realized* (sold). Buy-borrow-die exploits exactly that: borrow against appreciated assets instead of selling, and at death the heirs' basis steps up to current value, erasing the gain forever. A wealth tax ignores realization — it taxes the net-worth *stock* annually — so the unsold, never-realized appreciation that buy-borrow-die shelters becomes taxable simply by existing.
</Checkpoint>

## The constitutional tripwire — and what Moore actually held

Here the argument leaves policy and enters the Constitution, which contains a genuinely awkward clause. Article I requires that "direct taxes" be **apportioned** among the states by population — meaning a state with one-tenth the population must bear one-tenth the tax, regardless of where the wealth sits. Apportioning a net-worth tax is functionally impossible: it would force wildly different rates in rich and poor states. So the threshold question is whether a federal wealth tax counts as a "direct tax" requiring apportionment. The 16th Amendment (1913) carved income out of that requirement — "taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment" — but a wealth tax is, by design, not an income tax. The fear traces to *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust* (1895), where the Court struck down the federal income tax as an unapportioned direct tax, the very ruling the 16th Amendment was written to reverse.

This is why *Moore v. United States* drew a crowd. The case concerned the 2017 Mandatory Repatriation Tax, which taxed U.S. shareholders on a controlled foreign corporation's accumulated earnings even though those earnings had never been distributed. The Moores argued they had no *realized* income, and that taxing unrealized gains exceeded the 16th Amendment — a theory that, if adopted, would have built a constitutional wall around any wealth tax before one was ever drafted.

Decided on June 20, 2024, the Court upheld the tax **7-2**. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for a five-justice majority; Justices Barrett and Alito concurred only in the judgment; Justices Thomas and Gorsuch dissented. (The 7-2 framing matters: this was not the 5-4 cliffhanger it is often misremembered as.) But the holding was deliberately narrow — it addressed only whether Congress may attribute an entity's realized, undistributed income to its shareholders and tax them on it. And a footnote *expressly reserved* the questions that actually matter for the wealth-tax fight: double-taxing both an entity and its shareholders, "taxes on holdings, wealth, or net worth," and "taxes on appreciation." *Moore* neither blessed a wealth tax nor killed one. It carefully stepped around the live question — which means a future Court inherits it cold.

## The estate tax, the quieter battleground

While the wealth tax dominates the headlines, the policy that already taxes accumulated fortunes — the federal [[term:estate-tax]] — has been moving in the opposite direction, and it teaches the dynamic in miniature. For decedents dying in 2025 the estate-and-gift exclusion was $13.99 million per individual at a 40 percent top rate, meaning a married couple could pass roughly $28 million untaxed. The 2017 tax law had scheduled that elevated exemption to revert downward after 2025; instead, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 eliminated the reversion and made the exclusion permanent at $15 million per individual ($30 million per couple), indexed for inflation, from January 1, 2026 (26 U.S.C. 2010(c) as amended).

That number is the hinge for the **dynasty trust** — a trust designed to hold wealth across generations while minimizing transfer tax at each handoff. The generation-skipping transfer-tax exemption tracks the same figure, and a trust funded up to it can in principle compound untaxed for a very long time. How long depends on *state* law: several states (South Dakota and Nevada among them) abolished the centuries-old Rule Against Perpetuities, which traditionally forced trusts to terminate, so a trust domiciled there can endure for generations or longer. The word reformers reach for — "forever" — is a state-law artifact, not a federal guarantee. And the **stepped-up basis** at death (26 U.S.C. 1014) sits underneath all of it, resetting the heirs' cost basis to market value so the decedent's lifetime appreciation escapes income tax altogether — the back end of buy-borrow-die.

<Callout type="misconception">
  "We already have a wealth tax — it's called the estate tax." Not quite. The estate tax is a *one-time* transfer levy at death on the slice of an estate above a $15 million-per-person exclusion, and the stepped-up basis plus dynasty trusts are engineered to blunt it. A net-worth tax is an *annual* levy on the living. The estate tax taxes a fortune once, on the way out, and lets the unrealized gain underneath it disappear; that is a different instrument doing a different job.
</Callout>

## The structural tools: minimum taxes, transparency, antitrust

Taxing the rich is one lever; *limiting concentration* is another, and three structural tools advanced and then partly retreated in 2024–25 — a pattern worth seeing whole.

The first is the **OECD/G20 Pillar Two** global minimum tax: a 15 percent minimum effective rate on multinational groups with revenue above 750 million euros, with its core "income inclusion rule" applying from the start of 2024. The design is clever — if any country undertaxes a multinational's profits, another country can top the rate up to 15 percent, draining the incentive to shift profit to tax havens. But the United States never enacted Pillar Two, and on June 28, 2025 the G7 announced a "side-by-side" understanding that would exclude U.S.-parented groups from Pillar Two's income-inclusion and under-taxed-profits rules, tied to dropping a proposed retaliatory "revenge tax" (the mooted Internal Revenue Code section 899). The global floor, in other words, now excludes U.S.-parented groups.

The second is ownership transparency. The **Corporate Transparency Act**, enacted January 1, 2021, created a Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) registry of the *beneficial owners* — the real human beings — behind anonymous shell companies, the structures the Pandora Papers (2021) and Panama Papers (2016) showed are routinely used to hold wealth (many of them, it bears stressing, entirely lawful). It was the U.S. answer to anonymous ownership. Then on March 26, 2025 FinCEN issued an interim final rule exempting *all* U.S.-formed entities and U.S. persons, narrowing the reporting requirement to foreign-formed companies — gutting the registry's reach over domestic shells.

The third is [[term:antitrust]] — the body of law restraining monopoly and the concentration of market power, and the place where the limit-concentration project gained the most ground. Under Chair Lina Khan (June 15, 2021 to January 20, 2025), the Federal Trade Commission, with the Justice Department, issued new **2023 Merger Guidelines** on December 18, 2023, replacing the 2010/2020 versions after more than 30,000 public comments. The guidelines revived a structural-presumption approach skeptical of mergers that entrench dominant firms — a sharp turn from the consumer-welfare framework that had governed since the 1980s, and the agenda that anti-monopoly groups like the [[org:open-markets-institute]] had pressed for a decade.

## The European cautionary tale — and its rebuttal

Whenever a wealth tax is proposed in the United States, Europe's experience is summoned as Exhibit A for the prosecution. The OECD counted twelve member countries levying an individual **net wealth tax** in 1990; by 2020 only about three remained — Norway, Spain, and Switzerland (France replaced its general wealth tax with a narrower real-estate levy in 2018). The caveat matters: that count is of a specific instrument under a specific definition, in a specific year. The OECD's own 2018 study (*The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes in the OECD*) attributed the retreat to four recurring problems: the difficulty of valuing illiquid assets year after year, narrow bases riddled with exemptions, avoidance and capital flight, and revenue that was modest relative to the administrative burden — on the order of 0.2 percent of GDP in Spain up to about 1.0 percent in Switzerland.

That is the cautionary tale at its strongest. But the same OECD report supplies the rebuttal, which is why the European record is genuinely contested rather than settled. The OECD found that the *claimed* large negative economic effects of wealth taxes had "limited empirical support." And the one durable success — Switzerland, where cantonal wealth taxes have run for over a century — survives precisely because it does the opposite of the failed designs: it taxes a *broad* base (including financial assets, with few carve-outs) at *low* rates. The design-lesson reading, associated with [[person:emmanuel-saez]] and Gabriel Zucman, is that the European failures were failures of *design*, not of *concept*: high rates on exemption-riddled narrow bases invited the avoidance that doomed them, whereas a low-rate broad-base tax with third-party valuation and strong enforcement (the Warren bill's $100 million for the IRS gestures at exactly this) is a different animal. Whether the United States could actually build that animal — given asset complexity an order of magnitude beyond Switzerland's — is the open question the European record cannot resolve in either direction.

## The three camps read the same map

Every fact in this lesson feeds all three of the camps Lesson 2 steel-manned. Here is each reading the same landscape at full strength.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Oligarchy realist">
    The map confirms the thesis. A net-worth tax touching 0.15 percent of households cannot clear the Senate; *Moore* ducked the constitutional question rather than settle a rule the wealthy fear; the estate exclusion was made *permanent* at $15 million per person in 2025; Pillar Two was carved open for U.S. firms; the beneficial-ownership registry was gutted for domestic shells within four years of passage. This is what an [[term:oligarchy]] looks like in a formally equal democracy — a [[term:plutocracy]] in operation if not in form — not stolen elections but a policy apparatus that bends every concentration-limiting instrument toward the rich, exactly as Winters, Hacker and Pierson, and Gilens and Page describe. The remedy is to tax and disclose concentrated wealth directly: a broad-base, low-rate wealth tax with real IRS funding, a restored transparency registry, and revived antitrust. **What would change this view:** sustained enactment and enforcement of any of these against the wealth-defense industry's resistance.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Pluralist skeptic">
    Read the same map without the conspiracy. The estate exclusion rose because a bipartisan coalition — including many merely affluent farmers and small-business owners, not just billionaires — has long disliked the "death tax"; that is contested democratic politics, not domination. *Moore* dividing 7-2 with cross-cutting concurrences is a Court wrestling honestly with a hard text, not a Court taking orders. And the wealth tax stalls not because the rich command the Senate but because the public is ambivalent and the policy is genuinely hard to administer — the European record is real. As Branham, Soroka and Wlezien show, income groups agree about 90 percent of the time, and where they diverge the rich prevail only modestly more than half the time; before adopting structural remedies as sweeping as an annual net-worth tax, demand rigorous causal proof that wealth, not ordinary coalition politics, is driving these outcomes. **What would change this view:** evidence that policy tracks the preferences of the top tenth of a percent specifically, against majorities, across many issues.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Public-choice limited-government">
    The whole map is misread by both camps, because the disease is not private wealth, and not [[term:capitalism]] as such — it is the state's discretionary power to grant favors, which wealth then competes to capture. George Stigler's "The Theory of Economic Regulation" (1971) and the **rent-seeking** literature (Tullock, Krueger) explain every item here: the tax code is a thicket of carve-outs *because* Congress sells them, and the wealth-defense industry is simply the predictable market in **regulatory capture** that any discretionary state creates. A wealth tax adds a vast new valuation power to game; James Buchanan's Nobel-winning **public-choice** analysis predicts it becomes another exemption-riddled instrument, exactly as Europe's did. Even antitrust, on this view, can become a tool incumbents wield against rivals. The cure is to shrink what government can be lobbied to grant — flatten and broaden the base, end the special provisions — not to hand the state a new lever. **What would change this view:** evidence that concentrated wealth distorts policy even where the state holds little discretionary favor to sell.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## What this lesson is not

It is not a prediction, and it is not a verdict on whether the stalemate is capture or healthy contestation — the three camps each have a coherent reading, and every fact here feeds all three. What is reasonably settled: there is no federal wealth tax, and *Moore* left the constitutional door neither open nor shut; the estate exclusion was made permanent at $15 million per person; Pillar Two, the Corporate Transparency Act, and the Khan-era antitrust turn each advanced and then partly retreated through executive discretion. What remains genuinely contested: whether a U.S. wealth tax would survive apportionment review, whether it could be administered at all, whether the European record is a warning or a design lesson — and, beneath all of it, the Lesson 2 question of whether concentrated wealth actually rules or merely wins the ordinary fights it would win anyway. Pick the camp you find most persuasive, then build the strongest case against it: that is the discipline this pack has been training the whole way through.
