"Two cents."

It is Elizabeth Warren's signature line, and the bill behind it is more ambitious than the slogan.

The Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act was reintroduced in March 2026. It would levy a flat 2 percent a year on a household's net worth above 50 million dollars. Above 1 billion dollars, it adds a 1 percent surtax. [beat] So 3 percent at the top.

By its sponsors' own arithmetic, it would touch about 260,000 households, and raise 6.2 trillion dollars over a decade.

[pause]

Then the twist. In June 2024, the Supreme Court decided a case widely billed as the wealth tax's constitutional reckoning. It was called Moore v. United States.

And the Court sidestepped the question. A footnote pointedly reserved judgment on "taxes on holdings, wealth, or net worth."

> The most consequential wealth-tax ruling of the decade decided nothing about wealth taxes.

Last time, three camps argued over whether wealth rules. This time, the same three camps meet the policy fight itself, as it stands in mid-2026. By the end, you should be able to describe the tools on the table, say what happened to each, and state how each camp explains the result.

Four stops. First, the wealth tax: the bill, and the constitutional tripwire under it. Second, the tools that already exist: the estate tax, and three structural tools. Third, Europe's record, which has two readings. And last, the three camps, looking at one map.

## The two-cent bill

Start with the instrument. The income tax is a tax on a flow: what comes in. A wealth tax is a recurring levy on a stock: the net worth itself.

Reformers reach for it because of a strategy you already know: buy, borrow, die. Borrow against appreciated assets. Never sell. Let the heirs inherit at a stepped-up basis. A fortune can compound for a lifetime, and across generations, while producing almost no taxable income.

A levy on net worth bypasses all of that.

> A wealth tax taxes what you have, not what you sell.

The Warren bill is engineered against avoidance. Three features.

First, an exit tax of 40 percent, on the net worth of any ultra-millionaire who renounces citizenship to escape the levy. That is an answer to capital flight.

Second, 100 million dollars in new funding for IRS enforcement. The theory is that a wealth tax lives or dies on valuation and audit capacity.

Third, it reaches worldwide assets, which shuts the offshore route.

One caution, about the biggest number. The 6.2 trillion dollars is the sponsors' estimate. It comes from analysis the senator's office commissioned. It is not a score from the Congressional Budget Office or the Joint Committee on Taxation, the nonpartisan scorekeepers whose numbers Congress actually books.

Revenue projections for a wealth tax are unusually fragile. They depend on assumptions about valuation, avoidance and behavior, and reasonable analysts dispute those by wide margins. So treat 6.2 trillion as an advocacy figure, not a settled forecast.

Let's test the instrument. Why does a wealth tax reach fortunes that the income tax structurally cannot? And which mechanism does it most directly neutralize? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The income tax bites only when a gain is realized: when the asset is sold. Buy, borrow, die exploits exactly that. You borrow instead of selling, and at death the heirs' basis steps up to current value, erasing the gain.

A wealth tax ignores realization. It taxes the stock of net worth every year. So the unsold appreciation that buy, borrow, die shelters becomes taxable by existing.

## The constitutional tripwire

That is the policy. Now the Constitution, which contains an awkward clause.

Article One requires that "direct taxes" be apportioned among the states by population. In practice: a state with one-tenth of the population must bear one-tenth of the tax, wherever the wealth sits.

For a tax on net worth, that is functionally impossible. It would force wildly different rates in rich states and poor ones. So the threshold question is whether a federal wealth tax counts as a direct tax.

The Sixteenth Amendment, in 1913, carved income out of that requirement. It allows "taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment." But a wealth tax is, by design, not an income tax.

[pause]

That is why Moore drew a crowd. The case concerned a 2017 tax on U.S. shareholders of foreign corporations. It taxed them on the corporation's accumulated earnings, though those earnings had never been distributed to them.

The Moores argued that they had no realized income, and that taxing unrealized gains exceeded the Sixteenth Amendment. Had the Court adopted that theory, it would have built a constitutional wall around any wealth tax, before one was ever drafted.

The Court upheld the tax, seven to two. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for a majority of five. Justices Barrett and Alito concurred only in the judgment. Justices Thomas and Gorsuch dissented. It was not the five-to-four cliffhanger it is often misremembered as.

But the holding was deliberately narrow. It addressed one thing: whether Congress may attribute an entity's realized, undistributed income to its shareholders, and tax them on it.

And a footnote expressly reserved the questions that matter most here. Listen for two phrases. It reserved "taxes on holdings, wealth, or net worth." [beat] And it reserved "taxes on appreciation."

> Moore neither blessed a wealth tax nor killed one.

Put another way: the Court stepped around the question, and a future Court inherits it cold.

Let's test that. A friend tells you: "The Court upheld the federal wealth tax, ending the apportionment debate." What is wrong with that sentence? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Two things. There is no federal wealth tax to uphold. What the Court upheld was the 2017 tax, on a narrow ground. And it did not resolve the apportionment question. The footnote set wealth, holdings and appreciation aside, on purpose.

## The tools already on the books

The wealth tax takes the headlines. Meanwhile the tax that already reaches accumulated fortunes has been moving the other way. That is the federal estate tax.

For people dying in 2025, the first 13.99 million dollars per person passed untaxed, and the top rate above that was 40 percent. That exclusion was scheduled to fall after 2025. Instead, a 2025 law made it permanent, at 15 million dollars per person, indexed for inflation.

That figure is also the hinge for the dynasty trust. And underneath both sits the stepped-up basis, the back end of buy, borrow, die.

One misconception to clear away. Some say we already have a wealth tax, and it is called the estate tax. Not quite.

An estate tax is levied once, at death. A net-worth tax, every year.

The estate tax falls on the slice of an estate above the exclusion, on the way out. A net-worth tax falls on the living, annually. Different instruments, different jobs.

[pause]

Taxing the rich is one lever. Limiting concentration is another. Three structural tools advanced in these years, and then partly retreated.

First, a global minimum tax, known as Pillar Two. It sets a minimum effective rate of 15 percent on large multinational groups. If one country undertaxes a group's profits, another country can top the rate up, which drains the incentive to shift profit to tax havens.

But the United States never enacted it. And in June 2025, the G7 announced a "side-by-side" understanding that would exclude U.S.-parented groups from two of its rules.

Second, ownership transparency. The Corporate Transparency Act, enacted in 2021, created a federal registry of beneficial owners: the real human beings behind anonymous shell companies. Many of those companies, it bears stressing, are entirely lawful.

Then, in March 2025, the agency that keeps the registry issued a rule exempting all U.S.-formed entities and U.S. persons. Reporting narrowed to foreign-formed companies.

Third, antitrust: the body of law restraining monopoly and concentrated market power. Under Chair Lina Khan, the Federal Trade Commission and the Justice Department issued new Merger Guidelines in December 2023. They revived an approach skeptical of mergers that entrench dominant firms. That was a sharp turn from the consumer-welfare framework that had governed since the 1980s. How long it lasts depends on who leads the agencies.

Now a question about all three. Pillar Two, the transparency registry, the Merger Guidelines. Each advanced, and then partly retreated. What do those retreats have in common about where the rollback happened? Think it through.

[long pause]

None was reversed by Congress. None was struck down on the merits by a court.

The carve-out from Pillar Two came from a negotiation by the executive branch. The registry's exemption came from an agency rule. And the future of the Merger Guidelines turns on who chairs the agencies.

Concentration-limiting policy is unusually reversible, because so much lives in agency discretion.

And agency discretion changes hands with administrations. The instruments exist. What happened to each is a pattern, and the three camps explain it very differently.

## Europe, twice

Whenever a wealth tax is proposed in the United States, Europe is summoned. The record has two readings, and both lean on the OECD.

Take the cautionary reading first. The OECD counted twelve member countries levying an individual net wealth tax in 1990. By 2020, only about three remained: Norway, Spain and Switzerland. One caveat: that is a count of a specific instrument, under a specific definition.

The OECD's 2018 study points to four recurring problems. The difficulty of valuing illiquid assets, year after year. Narrow bases, riddled with exemptions. Avoidance and capital flight. And revenue that was modest beside the administrative burden: from about 0.2 percent of GDP in Spain, up to about 1 percent in Switzerland.

> The cautionary reading: of twelve wealth-tax countries in 1990, about three remained by 2020.

Now the design-lesson reading, associated with Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman. It points to the same report.

The OECD found that the claimed large negative economic effects of wealth taxes had "limited empirical support." And there is one durable success: Switzerland, where cantonal wealth taxes have run for over a century.

On this reading, Switzerland survives because it does the opposite of the failed designs. It taxes a broad base, with few carve-outs, at low rates. High rates on narrow bases invited the avoidance that doomed the others. A low-rate, broad-base tax with strong enforcement is a different animal.

> The design-lesson reading: Europe's failures were failures of design, not of concept.

Whether the United States could build that animal is open. American assets are an order of magnitude more complex than Switzerland's. The European record cannot resolve the question, in either direction.

One thing to pull back before we go on. I opened with the bill's largest number: 6.2 trillion dollars. Whose estimate was it?

[long pause]

The sponsors'. Not the nonpartisan scorekeepers'. An advocacy figure, not a settled forecast.

## Three camps, one map

Now the three camps from last time. Every fact so far feeds all three. Each gets the same treatment: how it explains the map, what it points to, what it would do, and what would change its view.

The oligarchy realist first. On this reading, the map confirms the thesis.

Here is what this camp points to. A net-worth tax touching 0.15 percent of households cannot clear the Senate. Moore ducked the constitutional question. The estate exclusion was made permanent. Pillar Two was carved open for U.S. firms. And the ownership registry was gutted for domestic shells, within four years of passage.

The realist argues that this is what an oligarchy looks like in a formally equal democracy. Not stolen elections. A policy apparatus that bends every concentration-limiting instrument toward the rich. It is the pattern, on this view, that Winters, Hacker and Pierson, and Gilens and Page describe.

> The oligarchy realist's reading: every concentration-limiting instrument bends toward the rich.

The remedy, on this view, is to tax and disclose concentrated wealth directly: a broad-base, low-rate wealth tax with real IRS funding, a restored registry, and revived antitrust. What would change this view? Sustained enactment and enforcement of any of these, against the resistance of the wealth-defense industry.

[pause]

Now the pluralist skeptic, on the same map.

Here is what this camp points to. The estate exclusion rose because a bipartisan coalition has long disliked what it calls the "death tax." That coalition includes many merely affluent farmers and small-business owners, and not just billionaires. Moore divided seven to two, with cross-cutting concurrences. And the wealth tax stalls, on this view, because the public is ambivalent and the policy is hard to administer. The European record is real.

The skeptic argues that this is a Court wrestling honestly with a hard text, and a democracy contesting a hard policy. Income groups agree about 90 percent of the time, and where they diverge, the rich prevail only modestly more than half the time.

> The pluralist skeptic's reading: contested democratic politics, not domination.

The remedy, on this view, waits on proof. Before adopting anything as sweeping as an annual net-worth tax, demand rigorous causal evidence that wealth is driving these outcomes, and not ordinary coalition politics. What would change this view? Evidence that policy tracks the preferences of the top tenth of a percent specifically, against majorities, across many issues.

[pause]

Now the public-choice, limited-government view, on the same map.

Here is what this camp points to. The tax code is a thicket of carve-outs. Europe's wealth taxes became riddled with exemptions. And the wealth-defense industry itself is the predictable market that any discretionary state creates. It is what Stigler's theory of regulation, and the rent-seeking literature, explain.

The public-choice scholar argues that both other camps misread the map. The disease is not private wealth, and not capitalism as such. It is the state's discretionary power to grant favors, which wealth then competes to capture. The carve-outs are there because Congress sells them. A wealth tax adds a vast new valuation power to game. Even antitrust can become a tool that incumbents wield against rivals.

> The public-choice reading: the disease is the state's power to grant favors.

The remedy, on this view, is to shrink what government can be lobbied to grant: flatten and broaden the base, and end the special provisions. Do not hand the state a new lever. What would change this view? Evidence that concentrated wealth distorts policy even where the state holds little discretionary favor to sell.

[pause]

Now do what this series has trained from the start. Pick the camp you find most persuasive. Then build the strongest case against it.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. A wealth tax taxes what you have, not what you sell. The Warren bill: 2 percent a year above 50 million dollars, 3 percent above 1 billion. Its 6.2 trillion dollars is the sponsors' figure, not an official score.

[beat]

Two. Moore neither blessed a wealth tax nor killed one. The Court upheld a different tax, seven to two, and reserved the question of taxes on wealth.

[beat]

Three. An estate tax is levied once, at death, and its exclusion is now permanent at 15 million dollars per person. Three structural tools, the global minimum tax, the ownership registry and the merger guidelines, advanced and then partly retreated. Concentration-limiting policy is unusually reversible, because so much lives in agency discretion.

[beat]

Four. Europe has two readings. The cautionary reading: of twelve wealth-tax countries in 1990, about three remained by 2020. The design-lesson reading: Europe's failures were failures of design, not of concept.

[beat]

And five. Three camps, one map. The realist: every concentration-limiting instrument bends toward the rich. The skeptic: contested democratic politics, not domination. The public-choice scholar: the disease is the state's power to grant favors.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not a prediction. And it is not a verdict on whether the stalemate is capture, or healthy contestation.

Three questions stay open. Whether a U.S. wealth tax would survive apportionment review. Whether it could be administered at all. And whether the European record is a warning, or a design lesson.

Beneath all three sits the question from last time: whether concentrated wealth actually rules, or merely wins the ordinary fights it would win anyway.

From here, the argument goes where those questions go. To a future Court, which inherits the constitutional question cold. To the agencies, where so much of this policy changes hands. And to the evidence each camp has named as the kind that would move it.

So go back to the slogan. "Two cents." [beat] You can now say what the bill would tax, what the Court left undecided, and what each camp sees in the fact that it has stalled.
