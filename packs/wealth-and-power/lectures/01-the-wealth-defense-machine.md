Two numbers. Hold them side by side.

Between 2014 and 2018, the 25 richest Americans grew about 401 billion dollars richer.

Over those same five years, they paid 13.6 billion dollars in federal income tax.

[beat]

ProPublica obtained the IRS files behind those figures, and published them in 2021. Its reporters divided one number by the other, and got 3.4 percent. They called it a "true tax rate."

They had to invent the phrase. It measures tax against the growth of wealth, and the United States taxes neither wealth nor unsold gains.

[pause]

Sit with that number. Then ask the question this lecture exists to answer. Is 3.4 percent an outrage? Or is it just arithmetic?

[pause]

The honest answer is both. Untangling which part is which is the whole job.

Almost nothing in those files was illegal. The machinery behind a 3.4 percent rate is a body of statute, regulation and professional practice, and any sufficiently wealthy person can hire it.

By the end, you should be able to say what that machinery is, name its working parts, and keep two questions apart: is it legal, and is it right.

Four steps. First, the words: what a wealth-defense industry is, and three terms that are easy to confuse. Second, the mechanisms. Third, the scale of the wealth being defended. And last, the line where legal stops. Then we come back to 3.4 percent.

The verdict we hold back, on purpose. Whether this machine bends policy is the next lecture. What to do about it is the one after.

## What wealth defense is, and is not

Start with a definition, and hold it tightly. The loose version of this idea is a conspiracy theory. The rigorous version is not.

The rigorous version comes from the political scientist Jeffrey Winters, in a 2011 book called Oligarchy. He does not define oligarchy as a form of government, the textbook rule by the few. He defines it as the politics of wealth defense, by actors who command concentrated material resources.

In plainer words: an oligarch is someone rich enough that defending the fortune becomes a central political project. Oligarchy is a behavior, not a type of regime. That is why Winters can argue that oligarchy and democracy are "compatible and often fused."

Winters and Benjamin Page, in a 2009 article, split that defense into two tasks.

The first is property defense: protecting the fortune from outright seizure. From mobs. From revolution. From expropriation.

The second is income defense, the subtler, modern task: protecting the fortune from lawful redistribution, and above all from progressive taxation.

Their historical claim is that the United States is a civil oligarchy. The state now guarantees property rights so reliably that the rich no longer need private armies. So almost all of their wealth-defense energy goes into income defense: into the law firms, accountants, banks and tax planners who keep lawful redistribution from biting.

Put another way: the fortune is safe from seizure, so the effort goes into the tax bill.

[pause]

Winters gave that apparatus a name in the 2011 book: the Income Defense Industry. Its product is a lower effective tax rate for the very rich. A decade later, the inequality writer Chuck Collins popularized a broader label, the Wealth Defense Industry, and credited the concept to Winters.

Now the caution. Wealth defense does not mean a smoke-filled room. The claim is the opposite: no coordination is required, because the incentives align without it.

There is a demand: keep my effective rate low. There is a supply: highly paid professionals. And there is a body of law that rewards the transaction.

> Wealth defense is a market, not a plot.

That is exactly why the machinery is hard to dislodge. There is no central node to arrest.

Now three words that get used as if they were synonyms. The differences matter for everything that follows. Picture a ladder with three rungs.

The first rung is oligarchy, used broadly: rule, or outsized influence, by a small group, however that group acquired its standing. The few might be priests, or generals, or party officials. Wealth is one possible basis, not the defining one.

The second rung is plutocracy: the subtype in which the few hold power specifically because they are rich. Every plutocracy is an oligarchy. Not every oligarchy is a plutocracy.

The third rung is kleptocracy. Literally, rule by thieves. It is defined by illegality: enrichment by subverting public institutions, by looting the state itself. The distinguishing mark is the crime.

[pause]

The ladder locates the American argument precisely.

> The serious claim about the United States is plutocracy, not kleptocracy.

The claim is that concentrated wealth, professionally defended, has outsized influence. It is not a claim about theft.

Confusing those two rungs is the most common error in this debate, and it misleads in both directions. It lets critics overstate, by treating tax avoidance as theft. And it lets defenders dismiss, by pointing out, correctly, that no law was broken, as if that ended the conversation.

Let's test the ladder. Picture a country. Its richest families pay little tax, using entirely legal structures. And they fund the campaigns of the officials who keep those structures in place.

Which of the three words fits most precisely? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Plutocracy. Kleptocracy requires illegality, and nothing in that description is a crime. The power plainly derives from wealth, which makes it plutocracy, the subtype of oligarchy.

So hold the distinction, and the real question sharpens. It is not "is it a crime?" [beat] It is this: "is a body of law that produces a 3.4 percent rate the law we meant to write?"

## The mechanisms

That is the vocabulary. Now the machinery itself.

Income defense is not magic. It is a handful of specific provisions in the tax code, used in combination. We will take one slowly, and three more briefly.

The slow one is called buy, borrow, die. Its engine is a single provision: section 1014 of the tax code.

To follow it, you need one term. When you sell an asset, your taxable gain is measured from a starting figure. That figure is the basis.

Section 1014 says that when you inherit an asset, its basis resets, to the asset's fair-market value at the owner's death. That reset is called the stepped-up basis.

Add two further facts. A gain you have not sold is unrealized, and unrealized gains are not income. And the proceeds of a loan are not income at all.

Now the three steps fall out.

Buy. You buy appreciating assets and hold them: stock, real estate, a private company. As they rise, you pay no tax, because you have not sold.

Borrow. You borrow against those assets to fund your life. Borrowed money is not income, because you owe it back. And the asset never has to be sold.

Die. You die holding the assets. At that point, section 1014 erases every unrealized gain built up over a lifetime. The heirs inherit at the stepped-up value, and the appreciation is never income-taxed by anyone.

[pause]

> Buy. Borrow. Die. The income tax never touches the gain.

Notice what did not happen. At no step was anything concealed, and at no step was anything illegal.

> The tax is not so much evaded as designed around.

Each provision is defensible on its own terms. And that sequence is the cleanest single explanation of how a multibillion-dollar fortune can generate a 3.4 percent true tax rate without anyone cheating.

Let's run it once, with numbers. A founder holds 2 billion dollars of stock, and she has never sold. She borrows 40 million dollars a year against it, to live on. At her death, she leaves the stock to her children.

Across her lifetime, how much of the gain does the federal income tax reach? And which single provision is most responsible? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

Almost none of it. She never sells, so the gain stays unrealized and untaxed. The loans are not income. And at her death, section 1014 steps the basis up to fair-market value, erasing the gain for her heirs.

Section 1014 is the keystone. Without it, the deferred gain would eventually be taxed, when the children sold. A separate estate tax may apply, above a large exemption. But that is a different tax, and it waits for a later lecture.

Three more provisions, briefly. For each, listen for what it shields.

First, carried interest. A private-equity or hedge-fund manager keeps a share of the fund's profits as compensation, often 20 percent. Because that share is structured as investment gain rather than salary, it is taxed at the lower capital-gains rate, not the rate on wages. A 2017 law narrowed the gap, for assets held three years or less. It did not close it.

Second, the dynasty trust. A handful of states, South Dakota and Nevada among them, have abolished the old rule that limited how long a trust could last. A trust formed there, paired with a federal exemption from the generation-skipping transfer tax, can hold sheltered wealth across many generations.

Third, the donor-advised fund. The donor takes an immediate, full charitable deduction in the year the money goes in. Yet the fund has no federal minimum payout at all. The deduction is instant. The charity may wait indefinitely.

[pause]

Wealth that leaves the country has its own infrastructure. The economist Gabriel Zucman estimated that, as of 2014, roughly 8 percent of the world's household financial wealth sat in offshore tax havens. And leak-driven journalism, the Panama Papers and the Pandora Papers, exposed the scale of those structures.

Here is the point that is often missed. Most of what those leaks documented was lawful. That is what makes the offshore world a story of wealth defense, and not a story of wealth crime.

## How concentrated the wealth is

So that is the machine. What is it protecting?

Go carefully, because this ground is full of contested decimals. Take one question: what share of household wealth does the top 1 percent hold?

The Federal Reserve's accounts put it at about 31 to 32 percent, in recent data. The economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, using a somewhat different method, put it around 38 percent in 2018.

> The series disagree on the exact figure. They agree on the direction.

And over the long run, the direction traces a U.

Follow the very top sliver: the wealthiest one-thousandth of households. In 1929, on the eve of the Great Depression, that sliver held close to 25 percent of the country's wealth.

Then the share collapsed, across the New Deal and the mid-century decades of high top tax rates and broad growth. By 1978, it stood at roughly 7 percent.

Since then it has climbed steeply. By 2012, it was back to about 22 percent.

[pause]

A quarter. Then 7 percent. Then 22. Down one side of the U, and most of the way up the other.

The shape is the finding, not any single point. Concentration like this is not a permanent feature of American capitalism. It is a historically specific reversal. So policy and institutions plausibly drove it, and could plausibly bend it back. Whether they did, and whose policy it was, is the fight the next lecture takes up.

## Legal, not lawless

One thread runs through every mechanism so far. It is the difference between two words that sound alike.

Tax avoidance is arranging your affairs to owe less, lawfully. Tax evasion is illegally failing to pay what is owed.

Buy, borrow, die. Carried interest. Dynasty trusts. Donor-advised funds. Most offshore structures. [beat] These are avoidance. They exploit the law as written. That is the wealth-defense industry's entire business model.

Now pull something back from earlier. I gave you a ladder with three rungs, and one of them is defined by a crime. Which one?

[long pause]

Kleptocracy. The conduct in this lecture is avoidance, not evasion, and that is why the honest framing is plutocracy, not kleptocracy.

There is a separate, illegal magnitude, and conflating it with avoidance corrupts the whole debate. It is the tax gap: tax that is legally owed, and not paid on time. The IRS estimates it at a gross 696 billion dollars, for tax year 2022.

That is evasion, plus error and nonfiling. It is the part the law already prohibits. The wealth-defense industry is, by definition, the other thing. It operates in daylight. It files accurate returns. And it owes nothing more, because the code asks for nothing more.

So reformers and defenders often talk past each other, because they are answering different questions.

"Did they break the law?" Almost always, no.

"Did the law produce a result we would have chosen on purpose?" That is contestable. And it is a question about the statute, not about the taxpayer.

> Legal is one question. Right is another.

## Outrage, or arithmetic

Now back to the number we opened with. 3.4 percent.

It is the most arresting number in the ProPublica files. Its method is also unconventional. Both things are true at once.

The issue is the denominator: the bottom of the fraction. ProPublica divided tax by wealth growth. And wealth growth is mostly unrealized gain, which the United States has never treated as income.

So there are two readings of the figure, and each has a real argument.

Critics of the figure argue that this is not the rate anyone's tax return computes.

Defenders of the figure argue that this is the point. The conventional measures miss the gains entirely, and that is how a fortune can grow by tens of billions of dollars while the tax bill stays trivial.

> The choice of denominator is where the argument is fought.

Let's test all of it at once. Two commentators are discussing the files. The first says ProPublica "proved billionaires are tax cheats." The second says the figure is "meaningless, because no one is taxed on unrealized gains."

What is wrong with each? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first confuses avoidance with evasion. The files documented lawful structures, not cheating. That is the kleptocracy error.

The second hides behind a true premise. Unrealized gains are not income under current law. But that is the design choice ProPublica set out to highlight, not a reason the number is meaningless.

The defensible middle runs like this. The rate is real, and it uses an unconventional denominator on purpose, to surface gains the income tax never reaches. Whether that denominator should count is a policy question. It is separate from whether anyone broke the law.

You do not have to pick the better denominator. But try something harder. You probably lean one way already: toward outrage, or toward arithmetic. Take the reading you find less convincing, and state its best case, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Wealth defense is a market, not a plot. It is the lawful market in professional services that lowers the effective tax rate on concentrated wealth. In Winters's terms, the United States is a civil oligarchy, where property defense has given way to income defense.

[beat]

Two. The ladder. Oligarchy, plutocracy, kleptocracy. The serious claim about the United States is plutocracy, not kleptocracy.

[beat]

Three. The tools are specific, and they work in combination. The stepped-up basis of section 1014 erases a lifetime of unrealized gains at the owner's death. Carried interest taxes a manager's profit share at capital-gains rates rather than wage rates. A dynasty trust holds sheltered wealth across generations. A donor-advised fund gives an immediate deduction, with no required payout.

[beat]

Four. The wealth being defended has climbed back toward the levels of the Gilded Age. The series disagree on the exact figure. They agree on the direction.

[beat]

And five. The conduct is, with rare exceptions, avoidance, not evasion. Legal is one question. Right is another.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We have the plumbing. We do not have the judgment. Does the machine merely keep rich people's taxes low? Or does it bend the laws and budgets that govern everyone?

That is contested, and the next lecture gives three camps their strongest case.

The oligarchy realist reads the concentration, and the apparatus, as evidence that wealth has bent policy toward the rich.

The pluralist skeptic reads the evidence of domination as overread, and wants rigorous causal proof before structural remedies.

And the public-choice view, the limited-government view, reads the disease as lying elsewhere: not in private wealth, but in the state's discretionary power to sell favors.

[pause]

For now, go back to the two numbers we started with. 401 billion dollars of new wealth. 13.6 billion dollars of income tax. [beat] You know now how both can be true, with almost no law broken. The machinery is real, and mostly legal. Whether it rules is the next question.
