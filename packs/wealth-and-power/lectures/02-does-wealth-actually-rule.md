In 2014, two political scientists ran the most ambitious test this question had ever received.

Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page assembled 1,779 policy questions, from 1981 to 2002. For each one, they matched what surveys showed average citizens wanted, and what affluent citizens wanted. Then they asked whose preferences predicted what government actually did.

Their conclusion landed like a verdict. Average citizens have "little or no independent influence."

[pause]

The headlines wrote themselves. America is an oligarchy.

Three years later, three other scholars ran much the same data: Alexander Branham, Stuart Soroka and Christopher Wlezien. They reported that the middle class and the rich agree about 90 percent of the time.

[beat]

Same evidence. Opposite headline.

> The gap between those two readings is the whole debate.

Last time, the machinery: the legal, professional apparatus that defends large fortunes against taxation. We held back the verdict on purpose. This time, the harder question: whether any of that machinery actually captures policy.

Three serious camps contest it. By the end, you should be able to state each camp's case the way its smartest advocate would, and say what the evidence settles and what it cannot.

Four stops. First, the oligarchy realist. Second, the pluralist skeptic. Third, the public-choice limited-government view. And last, what is settled and what is not.

Each camp gets the same treatment: what it argues, the evidence it points to, the cure it proposes, the hardest reply it faces, and what would change its mind.

## The oligarchy realist

Begin with the oligarchy realist. What worries this camp is a gap between form and operation.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Formal political equality coexists with plutocratic operation.

> The oligarchy realist's case: a polity formally equal, but operated for the wealthy.

Now the evidence this camp points to. It rests on four pillars, each from a different scholar.

First, capacity. Jeffrey Winters and Benjamin Page, in a 2009 article, built indices of material power from IRS and estate data. They argued that the top sliver, roughly the top 0.1 percent, holds the sheer resources to dominate on the issues that matter most to them. Above all, taxation.

That is a claim about potential. Oligarchs, they wrote, "need not occupy formal office, conspire together, or even engage extensively in politics in order to prevail."

Need not conspire. On this reading, an oligarchy is not a smoke-filled room. It is a distribution of resources so skewed that the wealthy get their way without having to try very hard.

Second, the affluent's edge. Martin Gilens, in a 2012 book called Affluence and Influence, studied what happens when the preferences of high-income Americans and of everyone below them pull apart. He found that policy tracks the affluent.

Third, weighting. The political scientist Larry Bartels studied the roll-call votes of U.S. senators in the late 1980s and early 1990s. He found that constituents at the 75th income percentile carried nearly three times the influence of those at the 25th. [beat] And the bottom third got almost no weight at all.

Fourth, drift. Jacob Hacker and Paul Pierson, in a 2010 book called Winner-Take-All Politics, argued that the explosion of top-end inequality after the 1970s was not the impersonal weather of markets. It was the product of sustained, organized political activity.

Part of that activity they called "policy drift": the deliberate decision not to update the rules as the economy changes. The minimum wage. Financial regulation. Tax brackets. Inaction, they insisted, is a policy choice, and a quiet one is easy to win.

You met a specimen last time: carried interest. Decades of bipartisan promises to end it yielded only the 2017 law's modest three-year holding rule, and never repeal. Drift, on this account, is how concentrated interests prevail without leaving fingerprints.

[pause]

Capacity. The affluent's edge. Weighting. Drift.

And the cure this camp proposes: tax and disclose concentrated wealth, before its capacity hardens into permanent advantage.

Let's test that evidence. Winters and Page measure that the top 0.1 percent hold enough resources to dominate tax policy, if they chose to. Gilens finds that when rich and poor disagree, policy follows the rich.

Which of the two demonstrates that wealth actually changed an outcome? And which only shows that it could? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Winters and Page measure capacity: the potential to prevail. It is inferred from the distribution of resources, and not from any specific win.

Gilens measures something closer to capture: an observed correlation between affluent preferences and outcomes, when groups diverge.

That is the hardest reply the realist faces. Showing that someone could dominate is not the same as showing that they did. And even a correlation between rich preferences and policy is not yet proof that the preferences caused the policy.

Hold on to that seam, between capacity and capture. The next camp pries at it.

And what would change the realist's mind? Rigorous causal evidence that the affluent's apparent edge is coincidental alignment rather than influence. Evidence that on the issues where groups diverge, the rich win no more than a median-voter model predicts. And evidence that drift reflects genuine gridlock, rather than organized blocking.

## The pluralist skeptic

Now the pluralist skeptic, built the same way. This camp does not deny that the rich are rich, and it does not argue that wealth is harmless. What worries it is a remedy that outruns the proof.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The evidence of domination is overread. Structural remedies demand causal proof, and what the realist offers is a correlation the data cannot disentangle.

> The pluralist skeptic's case: show me causation, not coincidence.

Now the evidence this camp points to. Three things.

First, the overlap. Go back to the second headline. Branham, Soroka and Wlezien re-examined the Gilens and Page issues in a 2017 article called "When Do the Rich Win?" They found that the middle class and the rich agree about 90 percent of the time. Meaningful divergence appeared on only about 185 policies.

And on those rare issues, the rich prevailed modestly more than half the time. A real edge, but a narrow one.

Put that another way. On roughly nine issues in ten, asking whether the rich beat the middle class has no answer, because they wanted the same thing. The dramatic finding lives entirely inside the thin slice that is left.

Second, a sharper and more theoretical point. Peter Enns, in a 2015 article, pointed out that the preferences of income groups are extremely highly correlated across issues: about 0.9.

Follow what that does. Suppose a government did nothing but follow the median voter. Because the affluent's preferences sit right on top of everyone else's, that government would automatically appear to represent the affluent.

Enns called this coincidental representation. The method cannot tell genuine elite domination apart from a democracy that happens to deliver outcomes the rich also like.

Here is a picture of it. You and a billionaire order from the same menu, and pick the same dish nine times out of ten. When the meal matches your taste, the method cannot say whether you were catered to.

Third, an older tradition. Robert Dahl, in a 1961 book called Who Governs?, studied who actually decided things in New Haven. He argued that American politics is pluralist. Power is dispersed across competing groups that win on different issues at different times, checked by elections, parties and courts.

And the cure this camp proposes is gated by evidence: remedy demonstrated harms, and defer sweeping structural change until causation is shown.

Let's test that evidence too. Take Enns's argument at full strength. Does it prove that the rich do not influence policy? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It does not. Enns's point is identification, not exoneration.

When preferences overlap that tightly, the correlation between affluent wishes and outcomes is consistent with both stories: elite domination, and an honest median-voter democracy. So the test is underpowered to demonstrate capture. It does not prove that the rich lack influence.

Now the hardest reply the skeptic faces. It came from Gilens, in a 2015 symposium, answering Enns directly.

Coincidental alignment, Gilens argued, is not democratic influence. Suppose ordinary citizens get outcomes they like only because the affluent happened to want the same thing. Then they are "coincident beneficiaries rather than causes" of policy.

[pause]

Beneficiaries, rather than causes. A democracy in which the non-affluent matter only when they agree with the affluent, Gilens argued, is an impoverished one, however pleasant the outcomes feel.

Enns held his ground. That is a claim about what ought to count as influence, he countered. It cannot rescue a method that, on the numbers, still cannot separate capture from coincidence.

The argument did not resolve. It clarified.

> The realist and the skeptic dispute what a correlation is allowed to prove.

And what would change the skeptic's mind? Evidence that survives the identification problem. Natural experiments, or design-based studies. They would have to show that, holding median preferences fixed, changes in elite preference or elite spending causally move policy. And they would have to replicate beyond the thin slice of divergent issues.

## The public-choice view

Both camps so far take concentrated private wealth as the thing to explain. The third, the public-choice limited-government view, argues that they have misdiagnosed the patient. Same treatment.

What worries this camp is not large fortunes as such. It is the discretionary power of the state to grant favors. Wherever that power exists, wealth will compete for it.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Inequality is partly a byproduct of discretionary power, and not the source of it. So the cure is not a wealth tax. It is a smaller menu of favors to buy.

> The public-choice case: the disease is discretion, not wealth.

Now the evidence this camp points to. Four things.

First, a mechanism with a name: rent-seeking. Rent-seeking is spending real resources to capture privileges that government grants, rather than to create value. Gordon Tullock sketched the idea in 1967. Anne Krueger coined the term in 1974.

A tariff, a license, an exclusive contract, a tax carve-out: each is a prize. Firms will burn lawyers, lobbyists and campaign dollars competing for it. And that waste grows directly with how much the state has to hand out.

Second, capture. George Stigler argued in 1971 that regulatory capture is the normal tendency, not the exception. Regulation, he wrote, "is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit."

Third, why the few beat the many with no conspiracy at all. Mancur Olson explained it in 1965, in The Logic of Collective Action. A small group whose members each capture a large slice of the benefit organizes easily. Millions who would each gain a little face a free-rider problem, and stay home.

So the tilt toward concentrated interests is structural. It needs no oligarchy, only ordinary incentives.

Fourth, churn. This camp disputes that fortunes are durable enough to make a ruling class. Joseph Schumpeter, in 1942, described creative destruction: the process that "incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within." It routinely displaces dominant firms, and the fortunes built on them.

Steven Kaplan and Joshua Rauh, in 2013, examined the Forbes 400. The share who grew up wealthy fell from about 60 percent in 1982 to roughly 32 percent by 2011.

[pause]

Now the cure this camp proposes. It comes from James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, in a 1962 book called The Calculus of Consent. Constrain government's discretionary power at the constitutional level. Do it behind a "veil of uncertainty," before anyone knows which interests they will later hold. Then the rules are written for fairness, and not for whoever is winning today.

In plainer words: shrink what government can be lobbied to grant, and you shrink the prize that wealth chases. A wealth tax, on this view, leaves the favor-granting power intact, and only moves the contest.

Now the hardest reply this camp faces. It concerns the churn. In 2017, Philipp Korom, Mark Lutter and Jens Beckert analyzed the same Forbes list, and pushed back. Entry had become more entrepreneurial. But heirs were significantly more likely to remain on the list. Inherited advantage persists.

Churn at the door does not prove churn at the top.

And what would change this camp's mind? Evidence that concentrated wealth captures policy even where the state's discretionary favors are minimal. Evidence that fortunes entrench across generations, whatever rents are available. Then shrinking government would not shrink the capture.

Let's put all three camps to work on one scene. A senator inserts a narrow tax provision. It benefits one industry, and that industry had lobbied hard for it.

What does each camp emphasize? Think it through.

[pause 5s]

The oligarchy realist sees capacity converted to capture: concentrated wealth bending a specific outcome, on taxes, where Winters and Page predicted it.

The pluralist skeptic asks whether this is one win among many. Did labor, or consumers, or a rival industry win the next ten fights? And did the provision actually diverge from what most voters wanted?

The public-choice scholar sees the predictable product of a tax code with favors to grant. The fix, on that view, is removing the power to carve out, not taxing the lobbyist's client.

> Same fact. Three diagnoses. Three cures.

One more thing about these three: they are not symmetric. The realist and the skeptic dispute the same evidence. The public-choice scholar disputes the frame. Even granting that wealth shapes policy, this camp argues, the lever is the state's discretion.

So the deepest disagreement is about what the question even is.

## Settled, and contested

Now sort what is settled from what is not. And begin by pulling something back. Early on, I gave you a number: 185. What was it counting?

[long pause]

The policies where the rich and the middle class meaningfully diverge. About 185, out of 1,779.

Settled, four things. Wealth in the United States is highly concentrated. The affluent do enjoy some edge when preferences diverge: both the original study and the reanalysis agree that the rich win more than half of those contests. Concentrated interests organize more easily than diffuse ones. And the machinery from last time is real, and largely legal.

[pause]

Contested, four things. Whether that edge amounts to domination, or to a modest tilt inside a small slice of issues. Whether the correlation between preferences and policy reflects causal influence, or coincidental representation. Whether top fortunes are durable dynasties, or churning rewards to talent.

And whether the right lever is the wealth, or the discretion. No amount of preference data can decide that one. It is a disagreement about mechanism, not about measurement.

[pause]

Now choose the camp you arrived predisposed to reject. State its case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The oligarchy realist's case: a polity formally equal, but operated for the wealthy. Its evidence: capacity, the affluent's edge, weighting, and drift. Its cure: tax and disclose concentrated wealth.

[beat]

Two. The pluralist skeptic's case: show me causation, not coincidence. Its evidence: agreement about 90 percent of the time, coincidental representation, and dispersed power. Its cure: remedy demonstrated harms, and wait for causal proof before structural change.

[beat]

Three. The public-choice case: the disease is discretion, not wealth. Its evidence: rent-seeking, regulatory capture, the free-rider problem, and creative destruction. Its cure: constrain what government can be lobbied to grant.

[beat]

Four. Measuring capacity is not demonstrating capture, and correlation is not causation. The realist and the skeptic dispute what a correlation is allowed to prove.

[beat]

And five. Each camp names the evidence that would move it. For the realist, causal proof of coincidence. For the skeptic, causal proof of elite influence. For the public-choice scholar, capture where the state has few favors to sell.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It did not decide whether America is an oligarchy. The strongest evidence on each side is real, and the methods cannot yet settle it. What a defensible position can do is pick a frame, defend it against the strongest opposing case rather than the weakest, and name the evidence that would move it.

The next lecture carries these same three camps into the policy fight of 2025 and 2026: a proposed wealth tax, a case called Moore v. United States, the global minimum tax, and the antitrust revival. There, the disagreement about whether wealth rules becomes a disagreement about what, if anything, to do.

For now, go back to the two headlines. One set of data, 1,779 issues. [beat] One reading finds an oligarchy. The other finds agreement, nine times in ten. You can now say what each reading counts, and what neither one can prove.
