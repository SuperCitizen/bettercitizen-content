## Same evidence, opposite headline

In 2014, Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page assembled 1,779 policy questions and asked whose preferences predicted what government actually did. Their conclusion: average citizens have little or no independent influence. Three years later, three other political scientists ran much the same data, and reported that the middle class and the rich agree about 90 percent of the time.

- 1,779 policy questions
- Rich and middle class agree: about 90%

> Same evidence, opposite headline. The gap between the readings is the lesson.

## The oligarchy realist

Oligarchy realists argue that formal political equality coexists with a system operated for the wealthy. They point to capacity: Winters and Page measured that the top 0.1 percent hold the resources to prevail on taxation, a claim about what they could do. And they point to Gilens, who found that when preferences diverge, policy tracks the affluent. Their cure is to tax and disclose concentrated wealth.

- Capacity: what the wealthy could do
- Observed: policy tracks the affluent

> The oligarchy realist: when preferences diverge, policy tracks the affluent.

## The pluralist skeptic

Pluralist skeptics argue that the domination evidence is overread. They point to the reanalysis: meaningful divergence on only about 185 issues, where the rich prevail modestly more than half the time. And they point to Peter Enns, who found that income groups' preferences correlate at about 0.9, so a government following the median voter would appear to favor the affluent. Their course is to require causal proof before structural remedies.

- Divergence: about 185 issues
- A correlation is not yet causation

> The pluralist skeptic: show causation, not coincidence.

## The public-choice view

Public-choice scholars argue that the disease is the state's discretionary power to grant favors, not private wealth. They point to rent-seeking: wherever government can grant a tariff, a license or a tax carve-out, firms burn real resources competing for it. Their cure is to shrink what government can be lobbied to grant.

- Rent-seeking: competing for granted privileges

> The public-choice view: the disease is discretion, not wealth.

## What the methods cannot settle

Settled: wealth is highly concentrated, and the affluent enjoy some edge when preferences diverge. Contested: whether that edge is domination or a modest tilt, and whether the right lever is the wealth or the discretion.

- Settled: some edge for the affluent
- Contested: domination, or a modest tilt

The lesson gives each camp at full strength, with the scholars behind it and what would change its mind. It does not decide whether America is an oligarchy.
