## Two cents

Two cents. It is Elizabeth Warren's signature line for a tax on great fortunes. In June 2024 the Supreme Court decided a case widely billed as the wealth tax's constitutional reckoning, Moore against United States. It sidestepped the wealth-tax question.

- Moore v. United States, June 2024

> The most consequential wealth-tax ruling of the decade decided nothing about wealth taxes.

## The bill, read closely

A wealth tax is a yearly levy on net worth. It taxes what you have, not what you sell. The Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act, reintroduced in March 2026, would take 2 percent a year above 50 million dollars, and 3 percent above 1 billion.

- 2% a year above $50 million
- 3% above $1 billion

Its sponsors say it would raise 6.2 trillion dollars over a decade. That is the sponsors' own estimate, not a score from the nonpartisan scorekeepers whose numbers Congress books.

- $6.2 trillion: an advocacy figure, not a forecast

## What Moore held

The Constitution requires direct taxes to be apportioned among the states by population. For a tax on net worth, that is functionally impossible. So the threshold question is whether a wealth tax counts as a direct tax.

In Moore, the Court upheld a 2017 tax, seven to two, on deliberately narrow grounds. A footnote expressly reserved the question of taxes on holdings, wealth or net worth.

- Upheld, seven to two, on narrow grounds
- Wealth taxes: expressly reserved

> Moore neither blessed a wealth tax nor killed one.

## Three readings of one map

Other tools for limiting concentration each advanced, then partly retreated: a global minimum tax, an ownership registry, new merger guidelines.

Oligarchy realists read the map as confirmation: a policy apparatus that bends every such instrument toward the rich.

- Oligarchy realist: the map confirms the thesis

Pluralist skeptics read it as contested democratic politics: the public is ambivalent, and the policy is hard to administer.

- Pluralist skeptic: ordinary coalition politics

Public-choice scholars read it as the predictable result of a state with favors to grant, and a wealth tax as one more lever to game.

- Public-choice: the state's discretion is the disease
