# Who decides, Washington or your state?

Suppose thirteen soccer clubs, each with its own fields, coaches and house rules, decide to form a league. They write a short charter that gives the new league office a few named jobs: it will schedule the matches, hire the referees and run the championship. Anything the charter does not mention stays with the clubs.

After a few seasons the league office announces a limit on how many hours a week each club may practice, on the reasoning that tired players make for poor matches. The clubs object that practice was never on the list. The office answers that a power over the matches means little without a power over the things that affect them.

The charter cannot settle this quarrel, because both sides are reading the same words, and someone has to decide how far a named power reaches.

[[term:federalism]] is the same arrangement on the scale of a country: a national government and state governments, each with authority of its own, under a written charter that divides the work between them. This lesson looks at how the Constitution draws that division, and at the long argument over how far the national government's named powers reach.

## A list on one side and a rule on the other

The Constitution treats the two levels of government differently. For Congress it provides a list. Article I, Section 8 names the powers of the national legislature one by one, among them the powers to tax, to borrow, to coin money, to declare war, to establish post offices and to regulate commerce "among the several States." Powers on this list are called *enumerated powers*, meaning powers that are counted out by name.

For the states there is no list. The Tenth Amendment, ratified in 1791 with the rest of the Bill of Rights, supplies a rule in its place: "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." These *reserved powers* cover most of what government does at close range, including schools, policing, the licensing of professions and the creation of cities and counties.

A third group belongs to both levels. Each can tax, borrow, build roads, set up courts and punish crimes, and these shared powers are called *concurrent powers*.

One more sentence holds the structure together. Article VI declares that the Constitution, and "the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof," are "the supreme Law of the Land," and that judges in every state are bound by them. When a valid federal law and a state law conflict, the federal law prevails.

## What the designers promised

When the Constitution was sent to the states for approval in 1787, its critics warned that the new national government would swallow the states. James Madison answered them in a newspaper essay, one of the series now known as *The Federalist*.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Federalist No. 45 (1788)">

"The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite. The former will be exercised principally on external objects, as war, peace, negotiation, and foreign commerce ... The powers reserved to the several States will extend to all the objects which, in the ordinary course of affairs, concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people, and the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the State."

</Callout>

<Checkpoint question="Madison wrote this essay to win votes for ratification. According to the passage, which level of government would matter more in an ordinary person's life? Is the passage better read as a description of the Constitution or as a prediction about how it would work?">

According to Madison, the states would matter more. He assigns the national government "external objects" such as war, peace and foreign trade, and he leaves to the states everything that concerns "the lives, liberties, and properties of the people." The passage is partly a description, since the enumerated powers really are a short list. It is also a prediction, made by an advocate, about how that list would be used, and the rest of this lesson asks how well it held.

</Checkpoint>

## The first test: a bank that was not on the list

The prediction was tested early. In 1816 Congress chartered a national bank, although the word "bank" appears nowhere among the enumerated powers. Two years later Maryland passed a tax aimed at every bank in the state that its own legislature had not chartered. James McCulloch, the cashier of the national bank's branch in Baltimore, went on doing business without paying, and the state sued.

The dispute reached the Supreme Court in 1819 as *McCulloch v. Maryland*, and it raised two questions. The first was whether Congress could create a bank at all. Chief Justice John Marshall held that it could, because the list in Article I ends with a power to make all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying the other powers into effect. A government that may tax, borrow and support armies may choose a bank as a useful means of doing those things. "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution," Marshall wrote, "and all means which are appropriate ... are constitutional." Powers of this kind are called *implied powers*.

The second question was whether Maryland could tax the bank, and Marshall held that it could not. The national government, "though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action," and a state that could tax a federal institution could tax it out of existence. In his words, "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."

The first great test therefore went to the national government on both points. In the terms of the soccer league, the office had won the argument that a named job brings with it the means of doing that job.

## How far does "commerce" reach?

Few enumerated powers have been fought over as long as the power to regulate commerce among the states. [[term:commerce-clause]] is the name given to that single phrase in Article I, and its history can be told through four cases.

**Gibbons v. Ogden (1824).** New York had granted a monopoly over steamboat traffic on its waters, and a rival operator held a federal license. Marshall ruled for the federal license. "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic," he wrote, "but it is something more: it is intercourse," and it therefore included navigation between one state and another.

**Wickard v. Filburn (1942).** A federal law tried to hold up the price of wheat by limiting how much each farmer could grow. Filburn, who ran a small dairy and poultry farm in Ohio, was allotted 11.1 acres of wheat for 1941 and sowed 23. He was penalized $117.11 for the 239 bushels he harvested from the extra land. Part of his wheat never left the farm, since he fed some of it to his animals and ground some into flour for his own household, and he argued that such wheat was not commerce among the states. The Court disagreed. His own effect on the demand for wheat "may be trivial by itself," it said, but his contribution, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial." After *Wickard*, almost any activity that affected a national market when added up across the country appeared to lie within the reach of Congress.

**United States v. Lopez (1995).** In 1992 a twelfth-grade student in San Antonio, Texas, carried a concealed handgun into his high school, and he was charged under a federal law, the Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990. By five votes to four the Court held that Congress had gone beyond the commerce power. Possessing a gun in a school zone, the Court said, "is in no sense an economic activity." If that counted as commerce, it was hard to see what would not, and Congress would hold the general authority over health, safety and order that belongs to the states.

**Gonzales v. Raich (2005).** California allowed the use of marijuana as medicine, and federal agents seized and destroyed six cannabis plants that belonged to a patient. She and a second patient argued that marijuana grown and used inside one state for medical purposes, and never sold, was beyond the reach of Congress. The Court ruled against them by six votes to three, and it relied on *Wickard*. Congress may regulate local activity that is not itself commercial when that activity is part of a national market it is entitled to control.

Read together, the last two cases resist a partisan summary. *Lopez* struck down a federal gun law, a result welcomed by advocates of gun rights. *Raich* upheld federal drug law against a state's own policy, a result that disappointed supporters of medical marijuana. People who favor state authority in one dispute often find that they prefer national authority in the next.

## Two near misses

A common summary says that the Tenth Amendment gives the states their powers. That is close to the truth and wrong in a way that matters. The amendment grants nothing. It says only that whatever was not handed to the national government remains where it was, so it cannot tell you by itself whether a federal law is valid. You have to ask how far an enumerated power reaches, which is why the cases in this lesson turn on words such as "commerce" and "necessary and proper."

A second common summary says that federal law always defeats state law. The Supremacy Clause protects only federal laws "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution, which means laws that Congress had the power to pass. In *Lopez* a federal law lost, and it lost to the limits of the list in Article I.

## What this lesson does not settle

Madison promised powers that were "few and defined," and whether that promise was kept depends on whom you ask. One view holds that a national economy needs national rules, that the commerce power was written to supply them, and that voters can remove a Congress that overreaches. The other view holds that if wheat grown to feed a farmer's own animals is commerce among the states, then the list in Article I has no edges, and a government of enumerated powers has quietly become a government of general power.

*Lopez* says that an edge exists, and *Raich* shows how hard it is to find. Each new federal law that reaches into daily life reopens the argument the thirteen clubs were having.
