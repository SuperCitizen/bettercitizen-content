# Can Washington make a state go along?

Return to the league of thirteen soccer clubs. The league office wants every club to adopt its new youth training program, which is not among the jobs the charter gave it. It cannot order the clubs to adopt the program, but the charter does allow it to spend the league's dues, so it offers a share of that money to any club that takes up the program. Most of the clubs accept.

A few seasons later the office raises the stakes. A club that refuses the next program will lose every dollar it receives from the league, including the money that already pays for its fields. The clubs are still free to say no, at least on paper. Whether a choice of that kind is still a choice is the question this lesson asks about the national government and the states.

This lesson takes up three things that happen when Washington and a state want different results: Washington may pay the state, it may try to order the state, and the state may try to resist.

## Paying: the power of the purse

Article I gives Congress the power to tax and to spend for the "general Welfare of the United States." Nothing in that clause confines the spending to the subjects on the enumerated list, and Congress has long used money to reach policies that it could not command.

The sums involved are large. In fiscal year 2022 the federal government sent $1.112 trillion directly to state governments, which came to 36.1 percent of all the revenue those governments took in. More than half of all federal grant money now goes to a single program, Medicaid, which pays for health care for people with low incomes. Money on that scale rarely arrives without conditions attached.

The leading case on such conditions is *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987). South Dakota allowed nineteen-year-olds to buy beer containing up to 3.2 percent alcohol. In 1984 Congress directed that any state permitting people under twenty-one to buy alcohol would lose a percentage of its federal highway money. Whether Congress could have set a national drinking age outright was doubtful, because the Twenty-first Amendment, which ended Prohibition, leaves much of the regulation of liquor to the states. South Dakota argued that the condition was a way of doing indirectly what Congress could not do directly.

The Court upheld the condition by seven votes to two, without deciding whether a direct national drinking age would have been allowed. Chief Justice William Rehnquist's opinion accepted that Congress may attach conditions to federal money, provided that the spending serves the general welfare, that the condition is stated clearly, and that it relates to the purpose of the money, which in this case was safe travel on the highways. He added one further limit: a financial inducement must not be "so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion.'" South Dakota stood to lose 5 percent of certain highway funds, and the Court called that "relatively mild encouragement."

A quarter of a century passed before that limit was used against Congress. The Affordable Care Act of 2010 required the states to extend Medicaid to many more adults, and a state that declined risked losing all of its existing Medicaid money as well as the new funds. In *National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius* (2012), Chief Justice John Roberts concluded that this was "much more than 'relatively mild encouragement'" and called it "a gun to the head." The threatened loss of "over 10 percent of a State's overall budget," he wrote, was "economic dragooning." The Court's remedy was to let the expansion stand as an offer that each state could accept or refuse, while barring the federal government from taking away the money a state already received.

The first version resembles *Dole*. The condition is clear, it relates to the purpose of the money, and a state that says no simply goes without a new benefit. The second version resembles *NFIB*, because it threatens money that states already depend on in order to force them into a new program. Whether it crosses the line would depend on how large that money is, and the two cases give only rough guidance.

</Checkpoint>

## Ordering: what Congress cannot do

If Congress cannot buy a state's cooperation, may it simply command it? The Court has answered no, in a line of cases known as the *anti-commandeering* doctrine. To commandeer something is to seize it for your own use.

The first of these cases, *New York v. United States* (1992), concerned a federal law on the disposal of low-level radioactive waste. The Court held that Congress "may not commandeer the States' legislative processes by directly compelling them to enact and enforce a federal regulatory program." Five years later the question returned. The Brady Act told the chief law enforcement officer of every locality to run background checks on people buying handguns until a national system was ready. Two sheriffs, Jay Printz of Ravalli County, Montana, and Richard Mack of Graham County, Arizona, went to court, and in *Printz v. United States* (1997) they won by five votes to four.

<Callout type="sources" title="Read the document: Printz v. United States (1997)">

"The Federal Government may neither issue directives requiring the States to address particular problems, nor command the States' officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program. It matters not whether policymaking is involved, and no case-by-case weighing of the burdens or benefits is necessary; such commands are fundamentally incompatible with our constitutional system of dual sovereignty."

</Callout>

<Checkpoint question="Justice Antonin Scalia, who wrote this passage, says that 'no case-by-case weighing of the burdens or benefits is necessary.' What kind of argument does that phrase rule out? Would the outcome change if the federal program were one that you strongly supported?">

The phrase rules out any defense that depends on the merits of the program. The rule protects a state's officers from federal command whatever the policy is, so a supporter of the Brady Act and an opponent receive the same answer. That is what makes it a rule about the structure of the government, and it is why people with very different aims can later claim it.

</Checkpoint>

Beginning in 2017, the federal government moved to withhold certain law enforcement grants from cities and states that limited their cooperation with federal immigration authorities. Those cities and states relied on *Printz*, and several federal trial judges held that a federal statute restricting such local policies violated the anti-commandeering doctrine. A rule established by sheriffs who objected to a gun control law was now protecting cities that objected to immigration enforcement.

The Court extended the rule again in *Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association* (2018). A federal law made it unlawful for a state to authorize betting on sports, and New Jersey wanted to permit it. The Court struck the federal law down, on the ground that the Constitution gives Congress no power "to issue direct orders to the governments of the States." Forbidding a legislature to change its law is as much a command as requiring it to pass one.

## Resisting: what a state cannot do

Here is the near miss. It is tempting to conclude from these cases that a state may refuse to obey a federal law it considers wrong, but the cases stand for something narrower. A state may decline to lend its own officers and its own legislature to a federal program. It may not block federal officers from enforcing a valid federal law themselves, and it may not declare that law void inside its borders. That second idea is called *nullification*, and the Court has rejected it when it came from opposite directions.

In 1854 Sherman Booth of Milwaukee was arrested by federal authorities for helping a man escape from slavery, in violation of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850. The Supreme Court of Wisconsin ordered Booth released and later set aside his federal conviction. In *Ableman v. Booth* (1859) the Supreme Court of the United States reversed, holding that a state court has no power over a prisoner held under federal authority.

A century later the roles were reversed. In 1957 the Governor of Arkansas used the state's National Guard to keep nine Black students out of Central High School in Little Rock, and state officials claimed that they were not bound by the Court's ruling against segregated schools. In *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958) a unanimous Court rejected that claim and pointed to Article VI, which makes the Constitution "the supreme Law of the Land."

Wisconsin was resisting slavery and Arkansas was defending segregation, and the constitutional answer was the same in both cases.

## Why let the states differ at all?

Justice Louis Brandeis gave a famous defense of this friction in 1932. Oklahoma had required a license of anyone who wanted to sell ice, the Court struck the requirement down, and Brandeis dissented: "It is one of the happy incidents of the federal system that a single courageous state may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country."

Madison had offered a different defense in *Federalist No. 51*. Power in "the compound republic of America," he wrote, is divided first between two governments and then among the branches of each, and "a double security arises to the rights of the people."

## What this lesson does not settle

The cases leave at least three questions open. The first is where pressure turns into compulsion. Five percent of certain highway funds was mild encouragement and all of a state's Medicaid money was a gun to the head, and the Court has not said where the line falls between them.

The second is whether the laboratory is worth its cost. Experiments in fifty states also mean that a person's protections can change at a state line, and this lesson includes states that used their independence to deny rights as well as states that used it to extend them.

The third is whether anyone holds a consistent position. The sheriffs in *Printz* and the cities that resisted immigration enforcement made the same constitutional argument for opposite political ends. A reader should ask whether that weakens the argument or shows why the rule has to be the same for everyone.
