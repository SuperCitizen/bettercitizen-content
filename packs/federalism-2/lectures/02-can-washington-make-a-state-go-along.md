Return to the league of thirteen soccer clubs. The league office wants every club to adopt its new youth training program, and that program is not among the jobs the charter gave it.

So the office cannot order the clubs to adopt it. But the charter does allow the office to spend the league's dues. It offers a share of that money to any club that takes up the program. [beat] Most of the clubs accept.

A few seasons later, the office raises the stakes. A club that refuses the next program will lose every dollar it receives from the league, [beat] including the money that already pays for its fields.

[pause]

The clubs are still free to say no. [beat] At least on paper.

Whether a choice of that kind is still a choice is the question this lecture puts to the national government and the states.

Last time, we asked how far Washington's listed powers reach. Now suppose Washington and a state want different results. Three things can happen, and by the end you will know the limit on each. First, Washington may pay the state. Second, it may try to order the state. And third, the state may try to resist. Then we will ask why the states are allowed to differ at all.

## Paying: the power of the purse

Article One gives Congress the power to tax, and to spend for the "general Welfare of the United States." Nothing in that clause confines the spending to the subjects on the enumerated list.

> Congress has long used money to reach policies that it could not command.

The sums are large. In fiscal year 2022, the federal government sent more than a trillion dollars directly to state governments. [beat] That came to more than a third of all the revenue those governments took in. And more than half of all federal grant money now goes to a single program, Medicaid, which pays for health care for people with low incomes.

Money on that scale rarely arrives without conditions attached. So which conditions are allowed?

The leading case is South Dakota v. Dole, from 1987. South Dakota allowed nineteen-year-olds to buy beer containing up to 3.2 percent alcohol. In 1984, Congress directed that any state permitting people under twenty-one to buy alcohol would lose a percentage of its federal highway money.

Why the highway money? Because it was doubtful that Congress could have set a national drinking age outright. The Twenty-first Amendment, which ended Prohibition, leaves much of the regulation of liquor to the states. South Dakota argued that the condition was a way of doing indirectly what Congress could not do directly.

The Court upheld the condition, by seven votes to two. Chief Justice William Rehnquist wrote that Congress may attach conditions to federal money, provided three things are true. [beat] The spending serves the general welfare. The condition is stated clearly. And the condition relates to the purpose of the money, which in this case was safe travel on the highways.

Then he added one further limit. Listen for where it draws the line. An offer of money must not be "so coercive as to pass the point at which pressure turns into compulsion."

[pause]

South Dakota stood to lose 5 percent of certain highway funds. [beat] The Court called that "relatively mild encouragement."

> Pressure is allowed. Compulsion is not.

A quarter of a century passed before that limit was used against Congress.

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 required the states to extend Medicaid to many more adults. A state that declined risked losing all of its existing Medicaid money, as well as the new funds.

In 2012, in a case known as NFIB v. Sebelius, Chief Justice John Roberts concluded that this was much more than mild encouragement. He called it [beat] "a gun to the head."

[pause]

The Court let the expansion stand, as an offer that each state could accept or refuse. But it barred the federal government from taking away the money a state already received.

Now use both cases. Congress creates a new grant for building schools, and offers it to any state that adopts a federal reading curriculum. A state that declines loses only the new grant.

Two years later, Congress changes the law. [beat] Now a state that declines loses all of its federal education money.

How does each version fare, under Dole and under NFIB? Work it through.

[pause 5s]

The first version resembles Dole. The condition is clear, it relates to the purpose of the money, and a state that says no goes without a new benefit and nothing more.

The second version resembles NFIB, because it threatens money that states already depend on, in order to force them into a new program. Whether it crosses the line would depend on how large that money is. The two cases give only rough guidance.

## Ordering: what Congress cannot do

So Washington may pay, within limits. If it cannot buy a state's cooperation, may it command it?

[beat]

The Court has answered no, in a line of cases known as the anti-commandeering doctrine. To commandeer something is to seize it for your own use.

The first of these cases, New York v. United States, came in 1992. Congress, the Court held, may not commandeer a state's lawmaking by compelling it to enact and enforce a federal program.

Five years later, the question returned. A federal law called the Brady Act gave a task to the chief law enforcement officer of every locality. Until a national system was ready, that officer was to run background checks on people buying handguns.

Two sheriffs went to court, Jay Printz of Montana and Richard Mack of Arizona. In Printz v. United States, in 1997, they won, by five votes to four.

Justice Antonin Scalia wrote the opinion. Its central sentence is long, so listen for the two things the federal government may not do.

"The Federal Government may neither issue directives requiring the States to address particular problems, [beat] nor command the States' officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program."

[pause]

No directives to the states. [beat] No commands to their officers.

> Congress may not order a state to carry out a federal program.

Then Scalia added a phrase worth slowing down for. In his words, "no case-by-case weighing of the burdens or benefits is necessary."

[pause]

Here is a question that only that phrase can answer. What kind of argument does it rule out? And would the outcome change if the federal program were one that you strongly supported?

[long pause]

The phrase rules out any defense that depends on the merits of the program. The rule protects a state's officers from federal command whatever the policy is. So a supporter of the Brady Act and an opponent receive the same answer.

> The rule is about structure. It does not weigh the merits of the program.

And that is why people with very different aims can later claim it.

[beat]

They did. Beginning in 2017, the federal government moved to withhold certain law enforcement grants from cities and states that limited their cooperation with federal immigration authorities. Those cities and states relied on Printz. And several federal trial judges held that a federal statute restricting such local policies violated the anti-commandeering doctrine.

A rule established by sheriffs who objected to a gun control law was now protecting cities that objected to immigration enforcement.

[pause]

The Court extended the rule again in 2018, in Murphy v. NCAA. A federal law made it unlawful for a state to authorize betting on sports, and New Jersey wanted to permit it. The Court struck the federal law down. The Constitution, it said, gives Congress no power "to issue direct orders to the governments of the States."

Forbidding a legislature to change its law is as much a command as requiring it to pass one.

## Resisting: what a state cannot do

Here is the near miss. It is tempting to conclude from these cases that a state may refuse to obey a federal law it considers wrong. But the cases stand for something narrower.

A state may decline to lend its own officers and its own legislature to a federal program.

[beat]

It may not block federal officers from enforcing a valid federal law themselves. And it may not declare that law void inside its borders. That second idea is called nullification.

> A state may withhold its own help. It may not obstruct or nullify.

The Court has rejected nullification when it came from opposite directions, in two cases a century apart.

In 1854, federal authorities arrested Sherman Booth, of Milwaukee, for helping a man escape from slavery, in violation of the Fugitive Slave Act. The Supreme Court of Wisconsin ordered Booth released, and later set aside his federal conviction.

In 1859, in Ableman v. Booth, the Supreme Court of the United States reversed. A state court, it held, has no power over a prisoner held under federal authority.

[pause]

A century later, the roles were reversed. In 1957, the Governor of Arkansas used the state's National Guard to keep nine Black students out of Central High School, in Little Rock. State officials claimed that they were not bound by the Court's ruling against segregated schools.

In 1958, in Cooper v. Aaron, a unanimous Court rejected that claim. It pointed to Article Six, which makes the Constitution "the supreme Law of the Land."

> Wisconsin resisted slavery. Arkansas defended segregation. The constitutional answer was the same.

Let's put the three moves together. Congress passes a valid law, and federal agents begin to enforce it. A state opposes the law. Which of these four things may the state lawfully do?

One: arrest the federal agents who are enforcing the law.

Two: declare the law void within the borders of the state.

Three: decline to assign its own police to help enforce the law.

Four: tax the federal agency until it stops enforcing the law.

[long pause]

Only the third. Under Printz, a state may refuse to lend its officers to a federal program. It may not obstruct federal officers or nullify the law, as Ableman and Cooper show. And McCulloch bars a state from taxing a federal operation out of existence.

## Why let the states differ at all?

Paying, ordering, resisting. [beat] All of it is friction. So why let the states differ at all?

Justice Louis Brandeis gave a famous defense of this friction in 1932, in a dissent. Listen for the word laboratory.

"It is one of the happy incidents of the federal system that a single courageous state may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; [beat] and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country."

[pause]

One state runs the experiment. [beat] The rest of the country does not bear the risk.

Madison had offered a different defense, in Federalist 51. In what he called "the compound republic of America," power is divided first between two governments, and then among the branches of each. And so, he wrote, [beat] "a double security arises to the rights of the people."

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. I said at the start that three things can happen. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Washington pays. Washington orders. A state resists. Each has its limit, so here are four things to keep.

One. Paying is allowed, within limits. A condition on a grant must be clear, and related to the money, and it must leave the state a real choice. In Dole, losing 5 percent of certain highway funds was mild encouragement. In NFIB, losing all of a state's Medicaid money was a gun to the head.

[beat]

Two. Ordering is not allowed. Congress may not order a state to carry out a federal program. That is the anti-commandeering rule of New York, Printz and Murphy, and it is a rule about structure.

[beat]

Three. Resisting is allowed only as refusal. A state may withhold its own help. It may not obstruct federal officers or nullify a federal law, as Ableman and Cooper show.

[beat]

And four. Two defenses of the friction. Brandeis: a state may serve as a laboratory. Madison: a double security for the rights of the people.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture does not settle. The cases leave at least three questions open.

The first is where pressure turns into compulsion. The Court has marked the two ends, and it has not said where the line falls between them.

The second is whether the laboratory is worth its cost. Experiments in fifty states also mean that a person's protections can change at a state line. And we have met states that used their independence to deny rights, as well as states that used it to extend them.

The third is whether anyone holds a consistent position. The sheriffs in Printz, and the cities that resisted immigration enforcement, made the same constitutional argument for opposite political ends. It is worth asking whether that weakens the argument, or shows why the rule has to be the same for everyone.

[pause]

That is where the argument goes from here. And it brings us back to the soccer league, and to the clubs that were free to say no, at least on paper. You know now the test for an offer like that, when Washington makes it to a state. Pressure is allowed. [beat] Compulsion is not.
