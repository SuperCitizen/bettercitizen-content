Suppose thirteen soccer clubs decide to form a league. Each club has its own fields, its own coaches and its own house rules.

So they write a short charter. It gives the new league office a few named jobs. The office will schedule the matches, hire the referees and run the championship. [beat] Anything the charter does not mention stays with the clubs.

[pause]

After a few seasons, the league office announces a limit on how many hours a week each club may practice. Its reasoning is that tired players make for poor matches.

The clubs object that practice was never on the list.

The office answers that a power over the matches means little without a power over the things that affect them.

So who is right? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The charter cannot settle this quarrel, because both sides are reading the same words.

> Someone has to decide how far a named power reaches.

Now put that quarrel on the scale of a country. Federalism is the same arrangement: a national government and state governments, each with authority of its own, under a written charter that divides the work between them.

By the end, you will be able to say how the Constitution draws that division, and why it is still argued over. We will take four steps. First, the division itself: a list on one side, and a rule on the other. Second, a promise that James Madison made about that list. Third, two tests of the promise, one over a bank and one over a single word. And last, two mistakes that sit near the truth.

## A list on one side, a rule on the other

For Congress, the Constitution provides a list. Article One, section eight, names the powers of the national legislature one by one. Among them are the powers to tax, to borrow, to coin money, to declare war, to establish post offices, [beat] and to regulate commerce "among the several States."

Powers on that list are called enumerated powers, which means powers that are counted out by name.

For the states, there is no list. [beat] The Tenth Amendment, ratified in 1791 with the rest of the Bill of Rights, supplies a rule in its place. Listen for the word reserved.

"The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, [beat] are reserved to the States," it says, and it ends with the words "or to the people."

[pause]

Reserved to the States. Those reserved powers cover most of what government does at close range, including schools, policing, the licensing of professions, and the creation of cities and counties.

A third group belongs to both levels. Each can tax, borrow, build roads, set up courts and punish crimes, and these shared powers are called concurrent powers.

> Congress has a list. The states have a rule. Some powers are shared.

One more sentence holds the structure together. Article Six declares that the Constitution, and the federal laws made under it, are "the supreme Law of the Land," and that judges in every state are bound by them.

So when a valid federal law and a state law conflict, the federal law prevails. [beat] Keep the word valid in mind. We will need it near the end.

## What the designers promised

When the Constitution was sent to the states for approval, in 1787, its critics warned that the new national government would swallow the states.

James Madison answered them in 1788, in a newspaper essay now known as Federalist 45. Here is his answer.

"The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. [beat] Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite."

[pause]

Few and defined. Numerous and indefinite.

The federal powers, he went on, would be used mainly on external objects, such as war, peace and foreign commerce. And the states? [beat] Listen to how much he leaves them.

"The powers reserved to the several States will extend to all the objects which, in the ordinary course of affairs, [beat] concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people."

[pause]

Now two questions that only that passage can answer. Remember, Madison wrote this essay to win votes for ratification. According to his words, which level of government would matter more in an ordinary person's life? And is he describing the Constitution, or predicting how it would work?

[pause 5s]

According to Madison, the states would matter more. He assigns the national government external objects, such as war and foreign trade. He leaves to the states everything that concerns the lives, liberties and properties of the people.

As for the second question, the passage is partly a description, since the enumerated powers really are a short list. But it is also a prediction, made by an advocate, about how that list would be used.

> Madison's promise: federal powers would be few and defined.

## The first test: a bank that was not on the list

That prediction was tested early. In 1816, Congress chartered a national bank. [beat] Yet the word bank appears nowhere among the enumerated powers.

Two years later, Maryland passed a tax aimed at every bank in the state that its own legislature had not chartered. James McCulloch, the cashier of the national bank's branch in Baltimore, went on doing business without paying, and the state sued.

The dispute reached the Supreme Court in 1819, as McCulloch v. Maryland, and it raised two questions.

The first was whether Congress could create a bank at all. Chief Justice John Marshall held that it could. His reason was the way the list in Article One ends: with a power to make all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying the other powers into effect.

A government that may tax, borrow and support armies may choose a bank as a useful means of doing those things. Powers of this kind are called implied powers.

The second question was whether Maryland could tax the bank, and Marshall held that it could not. The national government, he wrote, "though limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action." And a state that could tax a federal institution could tax it out of existence.

In his words, [beat] "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."

[pause]

So the first great test went to the national government on both points. In the terms of our soccer league, the office had won its argument.

> A named power brings with it the means of carrying it out.

## The second test: how far does commerce reach?

Now the single word. [beat] Commerce. Few enumerated powers have been fought over as long as the power to regulate commerce among the states. That phrase in Article One is known as the Commerce Clause, and its history can be told through four cases.

The first is Gibbons v. Ogden, in 1824. New York had granted a monopoly over steamboat traffic, and a rival operator held a federal license. Marshall ruled for the federal license, because commerce included navigation between one state and another.

The second is Wickard v. Filburn, in 1942, and it is the one to remember.

A federal law tried to hold up the price of wheat by limiting how much each farmer could grow. Filburn ran a small dairy and poultry farm in Ohio. For 1941, he was allotted about eleven acres of wheat. [beat] He sowed 23, and he was penalized for what he harvested from the extra land.

Part of his wheat never left the farm. He fed some of it to his animals, and he ground some into flour for his own household. Wheat like that, he argued, was not commerce among the states.

The Court disagreed. His own effect on the demand for wheat "may be trivial by itself," it said. But his contribution, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial."

> Wickard: small local acts count when their combined effect is large.

After Wickard, almost any activity that affected a national market, when added up across the country, appeared to lie within the reach of Congress.

[pause]

Was anything left outside it? [beat] The third case, United States v. Lopez, in 1995, says yes.

A twelfth-grade student in San Antonio, Texas, had carried a concealed handgun into his high school. He was charged under a federal law, the Gun-Free School Zones Act.

By five votes to four, the Court held that Congress had gone beyond the commerce power. Possessing a gun in a school zone, the Court said, "is in no sense an economic activity." If that counted as commerce, it was hard to see what would not. Congress would then hold the general authority over health, safety and order that belongs to the states.

> Lopez: the commerce power has an outer limit.

The fourth case is Gonzales v. Raich, in 2005. California allowed the use of marijuana as medicine, and federal agents seized and destroyed six cannabis plants that belonged to a patient. She and a second patient argued that their marijuana lay beyond the reach of Congress. It was grown and used inside one state, for medical purposes, and it was never sold.

Before I tell you how that came out, think back. Something grown for its owner's own use, and never sold. Which earlier case does that sound like?

[long pause]

It sounds like Wickard, and the wheat that never left the farm. The Court ruled against the patients, by six votes to three, and it relied on Wickard. Congress may regulate local activity that is not itself commercial, when that activity is part of a national market it is entitled to control.

[pause]

Read together, the last two cases resist a partisan summary. Lopez struck down a federal gun law. Raich upheld federal drug law against a state's own policy. People who favor state authority in one dispute often find that they prefer national authority in the next.

## Two near misses

Two common summaries are close to the truth, and wrong in a way that matters.

The first says that the Tenth Amendment gives the states their powers. [beat] It does not.

> The Tenth Amendment grants nothing. It keeps what was never handed over.

It says only that whatever was not handed to the national government remains where it was. So the amendment cannot tell you, by itself, whether a federal law is valid.

[pause]

The second summary says that federal law always defeats state law. Here is where we need the word valid. The Supremacy Clause protects only federal laws "made in Pursuance" of the Constitution, which means laws that Congress had the power to pass. In Lopez, a federal law lost, [beat] and it lost to the limits of the list in Article One.

> Federal law is supreme only when Congress had the power to pass it.

Let's put both corrections to work. Congress passes a law regulating an activity, and a state objects that the subject belongs to the states. Which question decides whether the federal law is valid? Is it whether the Tenth Amendment names the activity as a state power? Or is it whether an enumerated power, with its implied powers, reaches the activity?

[long pause]

It is the second. The Tenth Amendment names no state powers. It reserves whatever was not delegated, and so the real question is how far the delegated powers extend. That is why our cases turned on the word commerce, and on the phrase necessary and proper.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Congress has a list, the enumerated powers. The states have a rule, the Tenth Amendment, and it reserves what was not delegated. And some powers, the concurrent ones, are shared.

[beat]

Two. Madison's promise, in Federalist 45. Federal powers would be few and defined, and the powers of the states numerous and indefinite.

[beat]

Three. McCulloch. Congress has implied powers, because a named power brings with it the means of carrying it out. And a state may not tax a federal bank.

[beat]

Four. The commerce cases. Gibbons: commerce includes navigation. Wickard: small local acts count when their combined effect is large. Lopez: the commerce power has an outer limit. Raich: federal drug law reaches marijuana that is never sold.

[beat]

And five. The two near misses. The Tenth Amendment grants nothing. And federal law is supreme only when Congress had the power to pass it.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture does not settle. Madison promised powers that were few and defined, and whether that promise was kept depends on whom you ask.

One view holds that a national economy needs national rules, that the commerce power was written to supply them, and that voters can remove a Congress that overreaches.

The other view holds that if wheat grown to feed a farmer's own animals is commerce among the states, then the list in Article One has no edges. On that view, a government of enumerated powers has quietly become a government of general power.

Lopez says that an edge exists. [beat] Raich shows how hard it is to find.

[pause]

The next lecture asks what happens when Washington and a state want different results: when Washington pays, when it tries to order, and when a state tries to resist.

For now, go back to the thirteen clubs, and their quarrel over practice. Both sides were reading the same charter. [beat] Each new federal law that reaches into daily life reopens the argument those clubs were having.
