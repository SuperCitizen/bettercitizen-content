## Is it still a choice?

A league of soccer clubs has an office that cannot order the clubs to adopt its training program. So it offers a share of the league's dues to any club that does. Most accept.

A few seasons later the office raises the stakes. A club that refuses the next program will lose every dollar it receives, including the money that already pays for its fields.

> Washington may pay a state, try to order it, or meet resistance.

## Paying

In 1984 Congress directed that any state permitting people under twenty-one to buy alcohol would lose a percentage of its federal highway money. In South Dakota v. Dole, in 1987, the Court upheld the condition. South Dakota stood to lose 5 percent of certain highway funds, and the Court called that "relatively mild encouragement."

- Dole, 1987: 5% of certain highway funds

In 2012 the Court met the other case. A state that declined to extend Medicaid risked losing all of its existing Medicaid money. Chief Justice John Roberts called that "a gun to the head."

- NFIB v. Sebelius, 2012: all existing Medicaid money

> A condition on money must leave the state a real choice.

## Ordering

May Congress simply command a state? The Court has answered no. The Brady Act told local law enforcement officers to run background checks on people buying handguns. Two sheriffs went to court, and in Printz v. United States, in 1997, they won by five votes to four.

- Printz, 1997: no commanding state officers

The Court said that no weighing of burdens or benefits is necessary, so the rule holds whatever the policy is. Beginning in 2017, cities and states that limited their cooperation with federal immigration authorities relied on Printz.

- The same rule, for opposite ends

## Resisting

A state may decline to lend its own officers to a federal program. It may not block federal officers, and it may not declare a federal law void inside its borders. That idea is called nullification.

> A state may withhold its help. It may not nullify a federal law.

The Court rejected it in 1859, when a Wisconsin court freed a man convicted under the Fugitive Slave Act. It rejected it again in 1958, when Arkansas officials claimed they were not bound by the ruling against segregated schools.

- Ableman v. Booth, 1859
- Cooper v. Aaron, 1958

## What the cases leave open

Five percent of certain highway funds was mild encouragement, and all of a state's Medicaid money was a gun to the head. The Court has not said where the line falls between them.

- Where does pressure become compulsion?

The lesson has the sums Washington sends the states, the cases in the Court's own words, and two more open questions: whether fifty laboratories are worth their cost, and whether anyone holds a consistent position.
