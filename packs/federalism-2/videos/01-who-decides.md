## Thirteen clubs and a charter

Suppose thirteen soccer clubs form a league. Their charter gives the league office a few named jobs, and anything it does not mention stays with the clubs.

Then the office limits how many hours each club may practice. The clubs object that practice was never on the list. The office answers that a power over the matches means little without a power over the things that affect them.

> Someone has to decide how far a named power reaches.

## A list and a rule

For Congress, the Constitution provides a list. Article One, section eight, names the powers one by one, among them the power to regulate commerce "among the several States."

- Enumerated powers: listed for Congress

For the states there is no list. The Tenth Amendment supplies a rule: powers not delegated to the United States, nor prohibited to the states, "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

- Reserved powers: left to the states

In 1788 James Madison promised that the federal powers would be "few and defined."

> Madison's promise: federal powers "few and defined."

## A farmer's wheat

A federal law limited how much wheat each farmer could grow. Filburn, a farmer in Ohio, was allotted 11.1 acres and sowed 23. Part of his wheat never left the farm, and he argued that such wheat was not commerce among the states.

- Allotted 11.1 acres, sowed 23

In 1942 the Supreme Court disagreed. His own effect on the demand for wheat "may be trivial by itself," but his contribution, "taken together with that of many others similarly situated, is far from trivial."

> Wickard v. Filburn: small local acts count when they add up.

## Is there an edge?

In 1995 the Court found one. A student who carried a handgun into his high school was charged under a federal law. In United States v. Lopez the Court held that Congress had gone beyond the commerce power.

- Lopez, 1995: a federal law loses

So federal law does not always defeat state law. The Supremacy Clause protects only laws that Congress had the power to pass.

In Gonzales v. Raich the Court relied on Wickard. Federal drug law reached marijuana grown and used inside one state, and never sold.

- Raich, 2005: federal law wins

> Lopez says an edge exists. Raich shows how hard it is to find.

## What the cases do not settle

Whether Madison's promise was kept depends on whom you ask. One view holds that a national economy needs national rules, and that the commerce power was written to supply them. The other view holds that if wheat grown to feed a farmer's own animals is commerce, the list has no edges.

- One view: a national economy needs national rules
- The other: the list has no edges
