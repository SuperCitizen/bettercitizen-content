## A statue for the enemy

In 1936 the Polish economist Oskar Lange, a socialist, proposed a monument to socialism's most relentless enemy. A statue of Ludwig von Mises, he wrote, ought to occupy an honorable place in the great hall of the socialist state's planning board.

[beat]

Mises had forced socialists to confront a problem their movement had spent fifty years not thinking about.

- Mises, 1920: the calculation problem

## Guesses in the dark

The argument is not about greed or laziness. Grant the planners every virtue, and the problem remains.

Should this steel go to rails, bridges or tractors? In a market economy, the price of steel reflects everyone else's competing bids for it. But prices for capital goods emerge from actual exchange, and exchange requires owners.

- Prices come from exchange between owners

Abolish private ownership of the means of production, Mises argued, and you abolish those prices, and with them any way to compare one production plan against another.

- No prices, no way to compare plans

> The planning board does not lack good intentions. It lacks a unit of account.

[check q1]

## A verdict that cuts both ways

Lange answered that a planning board could simulate the market: post prices, watch for shortages and gluts, and adjust. The second half of the century reversed that verdict, in the world. By the 1980s the planned economies were falling visibly behind.

- Lange: a board that simulates the market

The debate discredited comprehensive central planning, and nearly every contemporary socialist concedes that. It barely addressed what most democratic socialists and social democrats advocate: market economies with public enterprises, and worker-owned firms operating in real markets.

- Discredited: comprehensive central planning
- Barely addressed: worker-owned firms in markets

> Mises won the argument he started.

[check q2]

## Three streams

Socialists also disagree with one another, about end-states and strategy.

The social democrat argues that public ownership of major industry was tried by democrats and retreated from, and that the durable winnings are the National Health Service, pensions and bargaining rights.

- Social democrat: defend and extend the winnings

The democratic socialist argues that welfare states erode, because concentrated private capital eventually buys the politics that unwinds them. Only spreading ownership locks the gains in.

- Democratic socialist: spread ownership

The Marxist-revolutionary argues that capital can veto elected governments without breaking a single law, so transformation requires mass movements built outside the state.

- Marxist-revolutionary: power outside the state
