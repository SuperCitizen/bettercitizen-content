# What socialism actually believes

On November 2, 1920, more than nine hundred thousand Americans voted for a presidential candidate in a federal prison cell. Eugene V. Debs — Convict No. 9653 — was three years into a ten-year sentence at the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary for a speech against the World War I draft, and he ran his fifth and final campaign from inside: no rallies, no train tours, just written statements issued through the prison gate. He polled 914,191 votes, about 3.4 percent. Here is the puzzle those voters left us. Three years earlier, the Bolsheviks had seized power in Russia and called what they were building socialism. The Americans who voted for a prisoner in 1920 — garment workers, Milwaukee Germans, prairie wheat farmers — overwhelmingly wanted something else: the eight-hour day, public ownership of railroads and utilities, old-age insurance, and the ballot as the way to get all of it. The same word covered both projects then, and it covers both Bernie Sanders and the Soviet Union in American arguments now. Until you can take the word apart, you cannot think clearly about anything labeled with it. Taking it apart is this lesson's job.

## One word, four families

Strip away a century of polemics and [[term:socialism]] makes one core claim: the major productive assets of an economy — factories, land, banks, infrastructure, what Marx called the *means of production* — should be socially owned or controlled rather than held as private capital. Everything that divides socialists from one another lives inside two follow-up questions. *How much* of the economy should be socially owned — everything, the "commanding heights," scattered worker cooperatives, or merely a decommodified slice (health care, schooling) carved out of an otherwise private economy? And *by what means* — ballots, courts, and parliaments, or a revolutionary party that does not intend to share power? Plot those two questions as axes and the confusing word resolves into a legible map.

Read the map's four named families carefully, because American discourse routinely collapses them. **Marxist–Leninist state socialism** (the USSR from 1928, Maoist China, Cuba, North Korea) combined comprehensive state ownership and central planning with single-party rule. **Democratic socialism** wants substantial social ownership — cooperatives, public enterprises, socialized finance — achieved and maintained *only* through democratic politics; its adherents regard the Soviet model as a catastrophe, not an embarrassment to be explained away. **[[ideology:social-democracy]]** accepts a predominantly private market economy and uses taxes, transfers, universal services, and strong unions to civilize it; the difference from democratic socialism is that social democracy treats this as the destination, not a way station. And the **Nordic model** is social democracy in practice — which is why Danish Prime Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen, speaking at Harvard's Kennedy School in 2015 while Sanders was invoking Denmark on debate stages, drew the line himself: "I know that some people in the US associate the Nordic model with some sort of socialism. Therefore I would like to make one thing clear. Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."

<Checkpoint question="A country holds free multiparty elections, has an economy that is roughly 90 percent privately owned, and funds universal health care, college, and childcare with taxes worth 44 percent of GDP. Which family is it — and what single feature rules out the others?">
  That is **social democracy** (in practice, the Nordic model — the 44 percent figure is Denmark's). The ruling feature is *ownership*: production stays overwhelmingly private and market-allocated, so it is not democratic socialism (which seeks substantial social ownership) and certainly not state socialism (which abolishes private capital and competitive elections). High taxes socialize *consumption*, not the means of production.
</Checkpoint>

## What Marx actually claimed

You cannot understand any branch of this family without the trunk. Karl Marx (1818–1883) made three claims worth stating as he made them, not as his enemies or his worshippers restate them.

First, **historical materialism**: the engine of history is not ideas but the organization of production. Feudalism gave way to capitalism when new productive technologies outgrew old property relations, and *The Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Friedrich Engels) predicted capitalism would in turn produce the class — industrial workers — that would outgrow it. Politics, law, and official morality, Marx argued, largely track the interests of whoever owns the productive apparatus: "The executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie."

Second, **surplus value**, the analytical core of *Capital* (1867). A worker is paid a wage; the worker's labor produces output worth more than that wage; the difference — surplus value — is kept by the owner of capital. Profit, on this account, is not a reward for thrift or risk but unpaid labor time, which makes exploitation a structural feature of the wage relation rather than a misfortune of bad employers. Mainstream economics abandoned the labor theory of value on which this rests in the 1870s — a genuine problem for the doctrine, and Lesson 2 gives the critics their full say — but the moral intuition underneath it (those who do the work should govern and benefit from it) survives in every socialist stream.

Third, **crisis and concentration**: competition forces capitalists to accumulate, accumulation concentrates ownership, and the system lurches through recurrent crises of overproduction. Note what Marx did *not* supply: any blueprint for running a planned economy, and almost nothing about how a workers' state should be governed. The famous distributive slogan — "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs," from his *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875) — describes a far-future abundance, not a policy. The one-party vanguard state was Lenin's addition, not Marx's design; contemporary democratic socialists generally keep Marx's diagnosis of capitalism's tendencies and discard both the prophecy of inevitable revolution and everything Lenin built.

## Reform or revolution: the split that defines everything after

By the 1890s, German Social Democrats faced an awkward fact: capitalism was not collapsing, and workers were winning real gains through unions and the ballot. Eduard Bernstein drew the conclusion in *Evolutionary Socialism* (1899): the predicted breakdown was not coming, and socialism would arrive — if it arrived — through democratic [[term:suffrage]], labor organization, and patient reform. For Bernstein, the movement itself mattered more than anyone's image of the final goal. Rosa Luxemburg's reply, *Social Reform or Revolution* (1899), held that reforms were worth fighting for but could never transform the system that conceded them; a movement that settled into reformism would end up administering capitalism, not replacing it.

History then ran the experiment. In August 1914, most European socialist parties voted to fund the World War their congresses had sworn to oppose, shattering the Second International. In October 1917, Lenin's Bolsheviks took power in Russia and built a one-party state. By the early 1920s, every country's left was permanently split into a Communist branch loyal to Moscow's model and a social-democratic branch committed to parliaments — rivals, frequently enemies, ever since. When the word "socialism" lands in your feed today, it is carrying freight from this split.

<Checkpoint question="A 2026 op-ed warns that a “socialist” city council member's agenda leads where socialism always leads — to Soviet-style tyranny. What question, drawn from the 1899–1917 story, does the op-ed have to answer before its inference works?">
  *Which branch?* After Bernstein, Luxemburg, and 1917, "socialism" names two lineages with opposed positions on the decisive question — democratic means versus one-party revolution. The op-ed's inference only works if the council member descends from the Leninist branch; if she descends from the Bernstein–social-democratic branch (as nearly all elected U.S. socialists do), the historical record being invoked belongs to a different family. Whether the *economic* critique of socialism still applies is a separate, serious question — Lesson 2 takes it up.
</Checkpoint>

## The American tradition: ballots, sewers, and prison cells

American socialism's high-water marks are mostly forgotten, and they are stranger than the caricature. The Socialist Party of America, founded in 1901, was a genuine electoral party. In 1910, Milwaukee elected Emil Seidel the first Socialist mayor of a major American city and sent Victor Berger to Washington as the first Socialist in Congress. Milwaukee's Socialists governed on honest budgets, public parks, municipal water, and sanitation — rivals sneered that they were mere "sewer socialists," and they wore it as a badge: Daniel Hoan held the mayor's office from 1916 to 1940, and Frank Zeidler won three terms from 1948 to 1960, through the deepest [[term:cold-war]] years. In 1912 Debs, with Seidel as his running mate, polled 901,551 votes — 6 percent, still the party's best share — against Taft, Roosevelt, and Wilson.

Then came the repression and the absorption. The Espionage Act prosecutions of World War I put Debs in the Atlanta penitentiary, where his 1920 campaign drew the 914,191 votes this lesson opened with; President Harding commuted his sentence in late 1921. At his 1918 sentencing Debs gave American radicalism its most quoted credo: "while there is a lower class, I am in it, and while there is a criminal element I am of it, and while there is a soul in prison, I am not free." Norman Thomas, the party's six-time nominee, won 884,885 votes in 1932 — and then watched Franklin Roosevelt's [[term:new-deal]] enact much of the Socialist platform's social-insurance agenda while the party itself withered. McCarthy-era anticommunism finished the job of making the label radioactive.

The thread survived through books rather than ballots. Michael Harrington's *The Other America* (1962), an exposé of poverty amid affluence, helped set the agenda for the War on Poverty; in 1982 Harrington founded the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) — not a party but an organization working inside Democratic primaries. It stayed tiny for three decades. The arc since is the story of the contemporary revival, compressed in the table below.

| Year | Event | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1901 | Socialist Party of America founded | A real electoral party, strongest in the Midwest and among immigrant unions |
| 1910 | Seidel wins Milwaukee; Berger wins a House seat | "Sewer socialism": competent municipal government as the showcase |
| 1912 | Debs: 901,551 votes (6%) | The party's best-ever share of the presidential vote |
| 1920 | Debs, from prison: 914,191 votes (3.4%) | Most raw votes ever for a Socialist Party nominee |
| 1932 | Norman Thomas: 884,885 votes (2.2%) | The New Deal then absorbs much of the platform |
| 1948–1960 | Zeidler serves three terms as Milwaukee mayor | The tradition's last big-city office for 65 years |
| 1962 | Harrington publishes *The Other America* | Socialist analysis shapes the War on Poverty |
| 1982 | Harrington founds DSA | Strategy shifts to working inside Democratic primaries |

| 2018 | Ocasio-Cortez unseats a 10-term incumbent | DSA members enter Congress |

Bernie Sanders's 2016 primary run — roughly 13.2 million votes, 43 percent, more than 20 state contests won against Hillary Clinton — did not win, but it moved the word back into circulation, and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez's 57–43 upset of Joe Crowley in a 2018 Queens–Bronx primary put DSA members in Congress. DSA's own membership counts trace the wave: about 6,500 members in 2014, a self-reported peak near 95,000 in 2021, a sag to around 50,000 by early 2025, then a claimed surge to 100,000 by February 2026 around Zohran Mamdani's New York mayoral victory. Treat all those figures as the organization's own numbers; no outside audit exists. Mamdani's win — and what his first months in office reveal — is the live case study Lesson 3 examines in detail.

<Checkpoint question="Debs won 6 percent in 1912; Sanders won 43 percent in 2016. Does that mean socialism is seven times stronger? What makes the two numbers non-comparable?">
  Different denominators, different games. Debs's 6 percent was a share of the *entire November electorate*, running as a third party against the full force of a two-party system. Sanders's 43 percent was a share of *Democratic primary voters only*, competing inside one party — a self-selected, smaller, more sympathetic pool. Harrington's 1982 pivot from third-party politics to Democratic primaries is precisely what makes the modern numbers bigger; whether it also diluted what "socialist" means is a live argument inside the tradition.
</Checkpoint>

## Why the Nordic confusion persists — and why precision matters

If the Nordics are not socialist, why does every American argument about socialism end up in Copenhagen? Because both sides find the confusion useful. Sympathizers point to Denmark to show "socialism" works; critics point to Venezuela to show it starves; both are usually talking about social democracy and state socialism respectively, and talking past each other. The honest accounting runs both ways. Nordic economies are aggressively capitalist in production — open trade, easy business formation, private ownership, no statutory minimum wage (unions handle it) — while socializing more consumption than Americans typically imagine, with tax revenue in the low-to-mid 40s as a share of GDP against about 25 percent in the United States. A democratic socialist, note, agrees with the critic here: DSA-style socialists say explicitly that Denmark is *not* their end state, because it leaves capital ownership untouched. Lesson 3 puts verified numbers on the comparison.

> **Common misconception:** "Socialism is when the government does things, and the more things it does, the more socialist it is." On that definition the Pentagon, the interstate highways, and Social Security are socialism — and the word stops doing any work. The tradition's own definition is about *who owns and controls production*. Keep ownership, means, and welfare spending as three separate dials and most shouting matches about socialism become legible disagreements.

## What this lesson did not settle

This lesson built the map and the lineage — it deliberately did not adjudicate. It did not test whether a socialized economy can actually allocate resources rationally (the calculation debate, the strongest critique the tradition faces), did not weigh the famine record of the Marxist–Leninist branch or what Venezuela proves, and did not sort out what democratic socialists, social democrats, and revolutionaries believe about each other — that is Lesson 2. And it left the most interesting current question — what happens when a democratic socialist actually governs the largest city in the United States — for Lesson 3. A defensible view of socialism starts where this lesson ends: with the right family named before the argument begins.
