# What socialism actually believes

The hardest question about socialism is the first one: what does the word actually mean? In contemporary American usage, "socialism" can refer to anything from Bernie Sanders' policy program (universal healthcare, free public college) to the Soviet Union's command economy. The two positions have almost nothing in common substantively. Untangling this is the prerequisite to thinking about any specific socialist position.

## The crucial distinction

Two distinct families of position have both been called "socialism":

**Democratic socialism / social democracy** — democratically governed economies that combine market mechanisms with extensive social provision (universal healthcare, education, childcare, housing), strong labor rights, redistributive taxation, and significant public ownership in some sectors. Think Sweden, Denmark, the German social-democratic tradition, the British Labour Party, contemporary Scandinavian welfare states, and the Bernie Sanders / Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) program in the U.S.

**State socialism** — political-economic systems where the means of production are owned and operated by the state, and political power is concentrated in a single party that claims to represent the working class. Think the Soviet Union, Maoist China, North Korea, Cuba, East Germany. These are typically authoritarian and often command-economy structures.

The distinction is not a rhetorical move; it's substantive. Democratic socialists generally view state-socialist regimes as failed (or actively harmful) while sharing some intellectual heritage with their early aspirations. They typically reject command economies, single-party rule, and abolition of all market exchange. Most American "democratic socialists" would, in European context, be called social democrats — supporters of capitalism with strong welfare state and labor protections, not abolition of market exchange.

This pack focuses primarily on democratic socialism / social democracy, which is the relevant tradition for contemporary American politics. State socialism is examined briefly in the historical context.

## A core that holds across democratic-socialist positions

Most democratic socialists, regardless of more specific orientation, share commitments roughly as follows:

**Working class as the central political subject.** The working class — broadly defined as those who depend on wages or salaries rather than significant capital ownership — is the political subject around which socialist politics organizes. The interests of capital owners and the interests of workers are, in the long run, opposed; democratic politics is the means by which workers' interests can prevail.

**Economic democracy.** Democracy should not be confined to political institutions while the economic sphere remains hierarchical and unaccountable. Workers should have voice in decisions about their workplaces, communities, and the economy as a whole. This is the substantive claim that distinguishes democratic socialism from welfare-state liberalism: not just transfers and regulation, but economic democratization.

**Decommodification of basic needs.** Healthcare, education, housing, and childcare should not be commodities allocated by ability to pay. They are foundational to human flourishing and political participation. Universal provision (Medicare for All, free public college, social housing, universal childcare) is the socialist policy framework.

**Collective bargaining and labor power.** Labor rights — to organize, strike, bargain collectively — are not just legal protections but central political commitments. Worker organization is the primary mechanism by which working-class interests get represented in economic life.

**Redistributive taxation and wealth limitation.** Concentrated wealth produces concentrated political power, which undermines democracy. Progressive taxation, wealth taxes, and inheritance limitations are not just fiscal instruments but democratic ones — they prevent the formation of an oligarchy that captures political institutions.

**Skepticism of pure market exchange.** Markets work well for many purposes but produce real harms (concentration, exploitation, environmental destruction, distributional unfairness) that democratic governance should address. The market is one tool among several, not the natural ordering of social life.

## The foundational thinkers

Karl Marx (1818-1883) — *Capital* (1867) is the foundational text of socialist economic analysis. Marx's critique of capitalism — that the wage relation extracts surplus value from workers, that capital accumulation produces concentration and crisis, that the state in capitalist society serves capital — remains influential. Most contemporary democratic socialists do not endorse Marx's revolutionary politics but accept significant portions of his economic analysis.

Eduard Bernstein (1850-1932) — *Evolutionary Socialism* (1899) is the canonical statement of revisionist Marxism: that socialism could be achieved through democratic political action and gradual reform rather than revolution. Bernstein's framework underpins most contemporary democratic-socialist thought. His was the great theoretical battle of pre-WWI European Marxism: revolution or reform.

Eugene V. Debs (1855-1926) — founded the Socialist Party of America in 1901, ran for President five times (peaking at 6% of the vote in 1912), spent time in prison for opposing WWI conscription. The American socialist tradition's intellectual and political founder. Debs's vision was democratic socialism — universal suffrage, labor rights, social insurance, public ownership of major industries — not Soviet-style state socialism.

Aneurin Bevan (1897-1960) — Architect of the British National Health Service. *In Place of Fear* (1952) is the canonical mid-20th-century democratic-socialist text. Bevan's Labour government created the welfare state — NHS, social housing, expanded education, nationalization of major industries — that became the template for European social democracy.

Olof Palme (1927-1986) — Prime Minister of Sweden 1969-1976, 1982-1986. Architect of mature Swedish social democracy. His emphasis on economic democracy, full employment, and gender equality shaped the Scandinavian model.

John Roemer (1945-) — *A Future for Socialism* (1994) is foundational for the market-socialist tradition: an economy with broadly distributed ownership of capital (citizen-shareholder funds, worker cooperatives) but with market mechanisms for resource allocation. Important for thinking about post-Cold-War socialist economic possibilities.

Bhaskar Sunkara, Vivek Chibber, and contemporary DSA voices — represent the post-2008 American democratic-socialist surge. *The Socialist Manifesto* (Sunkara) is a popular contemporary statement.

## The historical phases

**Early labor movement (~1860-1914).** Industrialization produced labor organization, socialist parties (in Europe) and the IWW + Socialist Party of America in the U.S. The Second International gathered socialist parties internationally. Significant intellectual diversity but a generally common political agenda: universal suffrage, labor rights, social insurance, opposition to imperialism.

**The 1914 split + the Russian Revolution.** WWI and the Russian Revolution split the international socialist movement. The Communist branch (which became Soviet-aligned) embraced revolution and one-party rule; the social-democratic branch embraced parliamentary politics and gradual reform. The split has shaped socialist politics ever since.

**Mid-century social democracy (~1945-1980).** European social-democratic parties built welfare states (NHS in U.K., extensive welfare systems in Scandinavia, Germany's social market economy). The U.S. New Deal coalition was a left-of-center coalition but not formally socialist; American socialism remained a small movement.

**The 1990s to 2008.** Social-democratic parties in Europe (Tony Blair's New Labour, Gerhard Schröder's SPD) moved toward market-friendly policy, often called "third way" politics. The Soviet collapse discredited state socialism. Socialism as a label fell out of mainstream Western political vocabulary.

**Post-2008 surge.** The financial crisis, growing inequality, and the climate crisis revived interest in socialist analysis and policy. Sanders 2016 in the U.S. and Corbyn 2015-2019 in the U.K. brought democratic socialism back to mainstream political discourse. DSA membership grew from ~6,000 in 2014 to ~90,000 by 2024. The Sanders policy program (Medicare for All, Green New Deal, free public college) is essentially European social democracy — neither state socialism nor revolutionary Marxism.

## Why this lesson is contested

This is an ideology pack, not an issue pack. The contestation is internal — within the tradition, what direction should socialism take?

Three perspectives the next lesson sharpens:

- **Democratic socialists** emphasize substantial economic democratization, public ownership in some sectors, and the long-run goal of transcending market capitalism through worker ownership and democratic planning.
- **Social democrats** emphasize regulated capitalism with strong welfare states. Socialism is framed less as transcending capitalism than as humanizing it through democratic institutions.
- **Marxist-revolutionary socialists** maintain the more radical critique of capitalism and skepticism of reformist politics. They are smaller in number but intellectually distinct.

The next lesson examines these in detail.
