November 2, 1920. Election day. One of the candidates for president is in a federal prison cell.

His name is Eugene V. Debs. Inside the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary, he is Convict Number 9653. He is eighteen months into a ten-year sentence, for a speech against the World War One draft.

This is his fifth campaign, and his last, and he runs it from inside. No rallies. No train tours. Just written statements, issued through the prison gate.

[beat]

More than nine hundred thousand Americans vote for him.

[pause]

Here is the puzzle those voters left us.

Three years earlier, the Bolsheviks had seized power in Russia. They called what they were building socialism.

The Americans who voted for a prisoner in 1920 were garment workers, Milwaukee Germans, prairie wheat farmers. Overwhelmingly, they wanted something else. The eight-hour day. Public ownership of railroads and utilities. Old-age insurance. And the ballot, as the way to get all of it.

The same word covered both projects then. It covers both Bernie Sanders and the Soviet Union in American arguments now. And until you can take the word apart, you cannot think clearly about anything labeled with it.

Taking it apart is the job of this lecture. By the end, you should hear the word socialism and know which question to ask back. We will take it in four steps.

First, the word itself: one claim, two questions, and four families.

Second, what Karl Marx actually claimed.

Third, the split between reform and revolution, which defines everything after it.

And last, the American tradition, from that prison cell to the mayor's office in New York, with a stop in Copenhagen on the way.

## One word, four families

Strip away a century of polemics, and socialism makes one core claim.

Think of the major productive assets of an economy. Factories. Land. Banks. Infrastructure. Marx called them the means of production. The claim is that they should be socially owned or controlled, not held as private capital.

> Socialism's core claim: the means of production should be socially owned or controlled.

Notice what that claim is about: who owns the things that produce.

Everything that divides socialists from one another sits inside two follow-up questions.

The first question is how much of the economy should be socially owned. Everything. Or only the commanding heights. Or scattered worker cooperatives. Or merely a slice, like health care and schooling, carved out of an otherwise private economy.

The second question is by what means. Ballots, courts and parliaments. Or a revolutionary party that does not intend to share power.

[beat]

How much. And by what means.

Now picture those two questions as the two axes of a map. Four families sit on it, and American arguments routinely collapse them. Take them one at a time, and remember: a place on that map is a description, not an endorsement.

The first is Marxist-Leninist state socialism. Think of the Soviet Union from 1928, of Maoist China, Cuba, North Korea. It combined comprehensive state ownership and central planning with single-party rule.

The second is democratic socialism. It wants substantial social ownership: cooperatives, public enterprises, socialized finance. And it wants that ownership achieved and maintained only through democratic politics. Its adherents regard the Soviet model as a catastrophe, not as an embarrassment to be explained away.

> The democratic socialist position: substantial social ownership, won and kept only through democratic politics.

The third is social democracy. It accepts a predominantly private market economy. Then it uses taxes, transfers, universal services and strong unions to civilize that economy. And here is what separates it from democratic socialism. Social democracy treats that arrangement as the destination, not as a way station.

> The social democratic position: a predominantly private market economy, civilized. That is the destination.

The fourth is the Nordic model, and it is social democracy in practice. The Nordic economies are overwhelmingly private and market-driven. What they socialize is consumption: health care, education, childcare. Not production.

A Danish prime minister drew that line himself. In 2015, while Bernie Sanders was invoking Denmark on debate stages, Lars Løkke Rasmussen spoke at Harvard's Kennedy School.

"I know that some people in the US associate the Nordic model with some sort of socialism. Therefore I would like to make one thing clear. [beat] Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy. Denmark is a market economy."

[pause]

Denmark is a market economy.

Let's test the map. Here is a country.

It holds free elections, with many parties. Its economy is roughly 90 percent privately owned. And it funds universal health care, college and childcare with taxes worth 45 percent of GDP.

Which family is it? And what single feature rules out the others? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is social democracy. In practice, the Nordic model: that tax figure is Denmark's, for 2024.

The deciding feature is ownership. Production stays overwhelmingly private. So this is not democratic socialism, which seeks substantial social ownership. And it is certainly not state socialism, which abolishes private capital and competitive elections.

High taxes socialize consumption. They do not socialize the means of production.

## What Marx actually claimed

That is the map. Now the trunk the whole family grows from, because you cannot understand any branch without it.

Karl Marx made three claims worth stating as he made them. Not as his enemies restate them, and not as his worshippers do.

The first claim is called historical materialism. The engine of history, Marx argued, is not ideas. It is the organization of production. Feudalism gave way to capitalism when new productive technologies outgrew the old property relations. And in 1848, in The Communist Manifesto, he predicted that capitalism would meet the same fate. It would produce the class that would outgrow it: industrial workers.

On this view, politics, law and official morality largely track the interests of whoever owns the productive apparatus.

[pause]

The second claim is surplus value. It is the analytical core of his book Capital, from 1867. Follow the steps. A worker is paid a wage. The worker's labor produces output worth more than that wage. The owner of capital keeps the difference. And that difference is surplus value.

So profit, on this account, is not a reward for thrift or for risk. It is unpaid labor time. And that makes exploitation a structural feature of the wage relation, not a misfortune of bad employers.

One caution, and it matters. The argument rests on the labor theory of value, and mainstream economics abandoned that theory in the 1870s. That is a genuine problem for the doctrine, and the next lecture gives the critics their full say. But the moral intuition underneath survives in every socialist stream: those who do the work should govern it, and benefit from it.

The third claim is crisis and concentration. Competition forces capitalists to accumulate. Accumulation concentrates ownership. And the system lurches through recurrent crises of overproduction.

[pause]

Historical materialism. Surplus value. Crisis and concentration. [beat] Now notice what Marx did not supply.

He supplied no blueprint for running a planned economy. And he wrote almost nothing about how a workers' state should be governed.

And the one-party vanguard state? [beat] That came later, from someone else.

> The one-party state was Lenin's addition, not Marx's design.

So what do democratic socialists do with Marx today? Generally, they keep his diagnosis of capitalism's tendencies. They discard the prophecy of inevitable revolution. And they discard everything Lenin built.

## Reform or revolution

Here is the turn. By the 1890s, German Social Democrats faced an awkward fact. Capitalism was not collapsing. And workers were winning real gains, through unions and through the ballot.

Two socialists answered that fact in the same year, 1899. Their argument is the hinge of this story, so each gets the same hearing.

Eduard Bernstein argued, in a book called Evolutionary Socialism, that the predicted breakdown was not coming. Socialism would arrive, if it arrived, through democratic suffrage, labor organization, and patient reform. For Bernstein, the movement itself mattered more than anyone's image of the final goal.

> Bernstein's case: socialism arrives, if it arrives, by suffrage, organization and patient reform.

Rosa Luxemburg argued back, in a work called Social Reform or Revolution. Reforms were worth fighting for, she held. But they could never transform the system that conceded them. A movement that settled into reformism would end up administering capitalism, not replacing it.

> Luxemburg's case: reforms can never transform the system that conceded them.

Before we go on, do something harder than agreeing. Choose the case you find less convincing, Bernstein's or Luxemburg's. Then state it at its best, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

Then history ran the experiment.

August 1914. Most European socialist parties vote to fund the World War that their own congresses had sworn to oppose. The vote shatters the Second International.

October 1917. Lenin's Bolsheviks take power in Russia, and they build a one-party state.

By the early 1920s, the left in every country is permanently split. One branch is Communist, and loyal to Moscow's model. The other is social-democratic, and committed to parliaments. They have been rivals, and frequently enemies, ever since.

So when the word socialism lands in your feed today, it is carrying freight from this split.

Let's put that to work. It is 2026, and you are reading an op-ed. It warns that a socialist city council member's agenda leads where socialism always leads: to Soviet-style tyranny.

Before that inference works, the op-ed has to answer one question. What is it?

[long pause]

Which branch?

After Bernstein, after Luxemburg, after 1917, the word names two lineages. And they hold opposed positions on the decisive question: democratic means, or one-party revolution.

The op-ed's inference works only if the council member descends from the Leninist branch. Suppose she descends from the other one, the branch of Bernstein and the social democrats. Nearly all elected socialists in the United States do. Then the historical record being invoked belongs to a different family.

> Before an argument about socialism starts, ask: which branch?

One caution. Whether the economic critique of socialism still applies is a separate question, and a serious one. The next lecture takes it up.

## The American tradition

Now come home. The high-water marks of American socialism are mostly forgotten, and they are stranger than the caricature.

The Socialist Party of America, founded in 1901, was a genuine electoral party.

1910. Milwaukee elects Emil Seidel, the first Socialist mayor of a major American city. And it sends Victor Berger to Washington, as the first Socialist in Congress.

Milwaukee's Socialists governed on honest budgets, public parks, municipal water, and sanitation. Their rivals sneered that they were mere "sewer socialists." [beat] They wore it as a badge.

1912. Debs runs for president against Taft, Roosevelt and Wilson, and wins 6 percent of the vote. That is still the party's best share.

Then came the repression. The Espionage Act prosecutions of World War One put Debs in the Atlanta penitentiary. At his sentencing, in 1918, he gave American radicalism its most quoted credo. It ends like this. "While there is a soul in prison, I am not free."

[pause]

From that prison he ran the campaign we began with. No Socialist Party nominee ever won more raw votes.

Then came the absorption. Norman Thomas was the party's nominee six times. After his run in 1932, he watched Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal enact much of the Socialist platform's social-insurance agenda, while the party itself withered. McCarthy-era anticommunism finished the job of making the label radioactive.

The thread survived through books, not ballots. In 1962, Michael Harrington published The Other America, an exposé of poverty amid affluence. It helped set the agenda for the War on Poverty. And in 1982, Harrington founded the Democratic Socialists of America, or DSA. It is not a party. It is an organization that works inside Democratic primaries. For three decades, it stayed tiny.

Then the revival. In 2016, Bernie Sanders ran in the primaries against Hillary Clinton, and took 43 percent of the vote. He did not win, but he moved the word back into circulation.

And in 2025, Zohran Mamdani was elected mayor of New York. It is the most prominent democratic-socialist office ever won in the United States.

Now test two figures from that story. Debs won 6 percent in 1912. Sanders won 43 percent in 2016. Is socialism seven times stronger? Or are those two numbers impossible to compare? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Different denominators. Different games.

Debs's 6 percent was a share of the entire November electorate, won as a third party against the full force of a two-party system. Sanders's 43 percent was a share of Democratic primary voters only. That is a smaller pool, self-selected and more sympathetic.

And the difference traces back to Harrington. His pivot in 1982, from third-party politics to Democratic primaries, is precisely what makes the modern numbers bigger. Whether it also diluted what socialist means is an open argument inside the tradition.

## Why the argument ends up in Copenhagen

One stop left. If the Nordic countries are not socialist, why does every American argument about socialism end up in Copenhagen?

Because both sides find the confusion useful.

Sympathizers point to Denmark, to show that socialism works. Critics point to Venezuela, to show that it starves. And each is usually describing something else. The sympathizers are describing social democracy. The critics are describing state socialism. They are talking past each other.

The honest accounting runs both ways. In production, the Nordic economies are aggressively capitalist: open trade, easy business formation, private ownership, and no statutory minimum wage, because unions handle it. In consumption, they socialize more than Americans typically imagine. Their tax revenue runs in the low-to-mid forties as a share of GDP. In the United States, the figure is about 26 percent.

And notice who agrees with the critic here. A democratic socialist does. Socialists in the DSA mold say explicitly that Denmark is not their end state, because it leaves capital ownership untouched.

That brings us to a common misconception. "Socialism is when the government does things, and the more things it does, the more socialist it is."

On that definition, the Pentagon is socialism. So are the interstate highways, and so is Social Security. [beat] And the word stops doing any work.

The tradition's own definition is about who owns and controls production.

Near the start, I gave you two questions that sort this whole family. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

How much social ownership. And by what political means. [beat] Now add a third thing, separate from both: welfare spending.

> Ownership, means, and welfare spending are three separate dials.

Keep those three dials apart, and most shouting matches about socialism become legible disagreements.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Socialism's core claim: the means of production should be socially owned or controlled. Socialists divide over two questions. How much, and by what means.

[beat]

Two. Four families. Marxist-Leninist state socialism: comprehensive state ownership and central planning, under single-party rule. Democratic socialism: substantial social ownership, won and kept only through democratic politics. Social democracy: a predominantly private market economy, civilized by taxes, transfers, universal services and strong unions. And the Nordic model: social democracy in practice, with market-driven production and heavily socialized consumption.

[beat]

Three. Marx made three claims: historical materialism, surplus value, and crisis and concentration. He supplied no blueprint for a planned economy. The one-party state was Lenin's addition, not Marx's design.

[beat]

Four. The split. Bernstein's case: socialism arrives, if it arrives, by suffrage, organization and patient reform. Luxemburg's case: reforms can never transform the system that conceded them. Since 1917, the word has named two lineages. So before an argument about socialism starts, ask: which branch?

[beat]

And five. The American tradition runs through ballots: Debs, Milwaukee's sewer socialists, Norman Thomas, Harrington and DSA, Sanders, Mamdani. The Nordic countries are market economies with large welfare states. And ownership, means, and welfare spending are three separate dials.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It built the map and the lineage. It did not judge.

It did not test whether a socialized economy can actually allocate resources rationally. That is the calculation debate, the strongest critique the tradition faces. And it did not weigh the famine record of the Marxist-Leninist branch, or what Venezuela proves.

All of that is the next lecture. The one after it asks what happens when a democratic socialist actually governs the largest city in the United States.

For now, go back to that cell in Atlanta, in 1920. More than nine hundred thousand people voted for the man inside it. [beat] You can now say what they wanted, and by what means. A defensible view of socialism starts there: with the right family named, before the argument begins.
