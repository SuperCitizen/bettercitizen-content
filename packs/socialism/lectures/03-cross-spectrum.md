The night of November 4, 2025. New York City is electing a mayor.

The winner is thirty-four years old. He is a state assemblyman, and a member of the Democratic Socialists of America. He takes 50.8 percent of the vote. Former governor Andrew Cuomo, running as an independent, takes 41.3.

Turnout passes 2.2 million, the city's highest for a mayor's race in more than three decades. And the winner had already done the harder thing in June. He had beaten Cuomo in the Democratic primary, an upset over the most famous name in New York politics.

[beat]

His name is Zohran Mamdani. On January 1, 2026, he takes office: the city's youngest mayor since 1892, and its first Muslim one.

[pause]

A word that ended American careers for seventy years was now attached to the chief executive of the country's largest city. And both parties spent the next year arguing about what, exactly, had won.

That argument is our subject: where socialism's borders with its neighbors actually run, and what the one experiment now running tells us so far. We will take it in four steps.

First, the experiment itself: what Mamdani ran on, and what has become of it.

Second, three borders: with progressivism, with conservatism, and with libertarianism.

Third, the Nordic numbers, which discipline both sides.

And last, what a defensible position on this tradition requires.

## The Mamdani experiment

Mamdani ran on affordability, not abolition. Five planks.

Freeze the rent on the city's rent-stabilized apartments, roughly one million of them. Make city buses fare-free. Provide universal childcare, from six weeks old to five years. Open five city-owned grocery stores, one in each borough. And phase the city's minimum wage up to 30 dollars by 2030.

The money was to come from a 2 percent surtax on incomes above one million dollars, and from a higher corporate rate. Both require approval from the state, in Albany.

Here the first lecture's test earns its keep: ask who owns the things that produce. So sort those planks into three categories. Social ownership: production in public hands. Decommodified provision: a service delivered free at the point of use. And regulation of private markets: ownership stays private, and the state sets a price floor or a ceiling.

Five planks, three categories. The rent freeze. The buses. The childcare. The grocery stores. The minimum wage. Which goes where? Take your time.

[pause 5s]

Social ownership: the grocery stores, and only the grocery stores. Five pilots, in a city with thousands of stores. That is the lone plank that puts production in public hands.

Decommodified provision: the buses and the childcare.

Regulation of private markets: the rent freeze and the minimum wage. Private ownership stays put.

> Mamdani's program is overwhelmingly social-democratic in mechanism.

In substance, it is the sewer-socialist playbook of Milwaukee in 1910, at New York scale. Prove that the label governs competently, by delivering visible material goods.

So what has been delivered? As of mid-2026, the evidence is instructively mixed. One delivery. One stall. Two pending.

The delivery is childcare. In January 2026, Mamdani and Governor Kathy Hochul announced a state-funded partnership. It phases in free childcare for the city's two-year-olds, starting in high-need neighborhoods. Hochul is a centrist Democrat, who needed his voters as much as he needed her budget. And that partnership is the first installment of the universal-childcare pledge.

The stall is the buses. Fare-free buses require state action, and they did not survive their first budget season in Albany. In April 2026, Mamdani conceded that the pledge would not be met in year one. He pointed to pilot language in legislative budget proposals: fifteen fare-free routes, not the whole system. And he reframed full delivery as a goal for the end of his term.

Two planks are pending. The first grocery sites have been announced, but no store has opened. And the rent freeze hangs on the Rent Guidelines Board, where Mamdani named six of the nine members. They face a binding vote, scheduled for June 25, 2026. A preliminary vote in May kept rent increases on the table.

Characterize all of that cautiously. Nothing has failed. One major plank has partially shipped.

> Every remaining plank depends on institutions the mayor does not control.

That structure of constraints is the real finding. Last time we met Przeworski's electoral dilemma, as theory. Here it is with a city attached. The program that wins votes is the affordability program. And the parts that need the consent of richer governments, Albany's taxes and the MTA's buses, move at the pace of coalition politics, not of mandates.

So there are two ways to read the record so far.

> One reading is vindication: material wins, delivered democratically.

> The other reading is dilution: social democracy with a bolder label.

Last time we met three streams: the social democrat, the democratic socialist, and the Marxist-revolutionary. Which reading you choose is nearly a perfect test of which stream you occupy.

So do the harder thing. Take the reading you find less convincing, and state its best case, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The border with progressivism

Now the borders. In American politics, the nearest neighbor is progressivism, and that border is genuinely blurry. Sanders, the Squad and Elizabeth Warren campaign on overlapping programs: universal health coverage, labor rights, child benefits, taxes on concentrated wealth.

But the analytical line is clean. Progressives want to regulate and tax private capitalism into fairness. Socialists locate the problem in ownership itself, and treat regulation as scaffolding for an eventual change in who owns.

> Progressives regulate and tax private capitalism. Socialists locate the problem in ownership itself.

Warren describes herself as "a capitalist to my bones." Set that beside DSA's constitution. They are not describing the same destination, even when this year's bills match.

The scholar who supplies both camps with their shared evidence is Thomas Piketty. In 2014, in Capital in the Twenty-First Century, he assembled two centuries of tax records. And he argued that when the return on capital exceeds economic growth, wealth concentrates mechanically. On his account, the mid-twentieth-century compression that Americans remember as normal was an anomaly of war and policy, and it is now reversing.

Progressives take Piketty as a case for wealth taxes within capitalism, which is Piketty's own remedy. Socialists take the same data as proof that ownership, not just income, is the variable that matters.

One dataset, two end-states. [beat] Agreement on the diagnosis. A sharp split on the prescription.

## The border with conservatism

The second border holds the strangest convergence of the 2020s. National conservatives and democratic socialists have voted together: on industrial policy, on antitrust, on trade with China, on child benefits.

That was predicted, in structure, eighty years ago. Karl Polanyi, in a book of 1944 called The Great Transformation, argued that land, labor and money are "fictitious commodities." Treat them as pure market goods, and society itself starts to tear apart. So a market society always generates what he called a double movement: a protective counter-thrust rises against it, demanding shelter from commodification.

Here is the part that is easy to miss.

> Polanyi's point: the protective counter-movement is not the property of the left.

The factory acts of the nineteenth century came partly from Tory paternalists. And Bismarck built the world's first social-insurance state explicitly to take the socialists' issue away. On this reading, today's right-populism and left-populism are two branches of one reaction, to forty years of market expansion.

What divides the branches is who counts as the society being protected.

The conservative counter-movement defends an inherited community: nation, faith, family forms. And it accepts hierarchy inside that community. The socialist counter-movement defends a universal class of the economically vulnerable. And it attacks hierarchy as such.

That is why the same bundle of policies can belong to either tradition. And it is why the coalition reliably fractures on immigration, gender and religion, even as it holds on Amazon and Beijing.

Let's test that. A senator supports sectoral bargaining, a generous child allowance, and aggressive antitrust. The same senator supports strict immigration limits, and tax preferences for traditional families.

Why can't the first three items tell you whether this is a socialist? And what does tell you? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first three cannot tell you, because both left and right generate protective economic programs. That list is the counter-movement's shared toolbox, not a membership card.

Two other things decide it. One is the boundary of solidarity: universal and egalitarian, or national, traditional and hierarchical. The other is the attitude toward ownership, and this senator shows no interest in worker or public ownership.

So the bundle is national-conservative. It is Polanyian protection, in service of an inherited community.

## The border with libertarianism

With libertarianism, the border is a cliff.

Libertarians treat private property as a pre-political right, and the market as freedom's natural form. Socialists treat property regimes as political artifacts, which can be redesigned.

> For libertarians, property is a pre-political right. For socialists, it is a political artifact.

In 1944, in The Road to Serfdom, Hayek put the strongest anti-socialist claim on the table: comprehensive economic planning must eventually consume political liberty. The twentieth century graded that claim, and the grade came in two parts.

Everywhere comprehensive planning was tried, the claim was vindicated.

As a prediction about welfare states, it was falsified. They have remained among the freest societies on earth. And Hayek himself, in that same book, endorsed a guaranteed social minimum. That is often forgotten.

Even across a cliff, the two traditions meet in odd places. Civil liberties and surveillance. War powers. Hostility to corporate subsidies and bailouts: "socialism for the rich" is a complaint both of them file.

And universal basic income, which has two family trees. On one side it descends from Milton Friedman's negative income tax. On the other, from left proposals to decommodify survival itself. Many socialists, though, prefer services and jobs guarantees to cash that leaves markets in charge.

## The Nordic mirror

Every American argument about socialism eventually invokes Scandinavia. So we end with the verified numbers. They discipline both sides.

Start with tax revenue as a share of GDP, in 2024. In Denmark, 45.2 percent. In the United States, 25.6. The OECD average sits between them, at 34.1. So the gap between Denmark and the United States is nearly 20 points of GDP. With that money, the Nordic countries buy universal health care, education, childcare, and income security.

That is the part Americans expect. Here is the part they don't.

Not one Nordic country has a statutory minimum wage. [beat] Not one. Wage floors are set sector by sector, through collective bargaining. And that works because of union membership. In Denmark, it is 67 percent. In the United States, 10 percent. Across the Nordic countries, union density runs five to six times the American rate.

Two more facts. Denmark pairs its welfare state with what is called flexicurity: hiring and firing are easier there than in much of the United States. And Norway's state oil stake feeds a sovereign wealth fund that has passed 2 trillion dollars. It is the world's largest, and it owns about 1.5 percent of the world's listed shares.

So on some dials, the Nordic package is more market-liberal than America. On others, it is more collectivized. Remember Rasmussen's line from the first lecture: "Denmark is a market economy." And remember that Sanders admires Denmark. Both are telling the truth, about different parts of the same package.

The honest conclusion cuts at everyone.

> Nordic-style policy means union density and broad middle-class taxes, not just the benefits.

And calling it socialism misreads what the Nordics built: the most successful social democracy on record, atop emphatically private ownership.

One last test. A candidate proposes Nordic-style socialism for America: Medicare for All, plus a federal minimum wage of 22 dollars.

Which half of that proposal is actually Nordic? And which half points the opposite way?

[long pause]

Universal, tax-funded health coverage is genuinely Nordic.

A high statutory minimum wage points the opposite way. No Nordic country has any statutory minimum. Their wage floors come from sectoral bargaining, backed by union density of 50 to 67 percent. That is an institution the United States, at 10 percent, lacks. So the candidate is proposing an American statutory substitute for a Nordic institution that bargaining built.

And the label is off regardless. The Nordic countries are market economies with big welfare states. That is social democracy, not socialism in the ownership sense.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Mamdani ran on affordability, not abolition. His program is overwhelmingly social-democratic in mechanism. As of mid-2026: one delivery, one stall, two pending. And every remaining plank depends on institutions the mayor does not control.

[beat]

Two, three and four are the borders. Before I hand them back, try it yourself. Progressivism. Conservatism. Libertarianism. What divides socialism from each?

[long pause]

With progressivism: a shared bill docket, divided by the ownership question.

With conservatism: a shared Polanyian reflex, divided by who "we" are.

With libertarianism: shared enemies in the corporate state, divided by the deepest line in political philosophy, the status of property.

And there is a fourth neighbor, communitarianism. There, a shared suspicion of atomizing markets, divided over whether community or class is the unit that matters.

[beat]

And five. The Nordic countries built social democracy, atop emphatically private ownership. Nordic-style policy means union density and broad middle-class taxes, not just the benefits.

[pause]

A defensible position on this tradition, whether yours is inside it or against it, now requires four commitments.

Which stream, if any, fits current conditions. Where you place the evidence of the Mamdani experiment, as it accumulates. Where the strongest critiques bite, and where they miss: Mises and Hayek on calculation, the historical record, Przeworski's dilemma. And what evidence would change your mind. [beat] A rent-board vote. A grocery store's books. A union-density trendline.

Here is what this lecture has not settled: the experiment it opened with. As of mid-2026, the rent-freeze vote is scheduled, the pilot stores are unopened, and the fate of the bus plan rests with Albany. Durable conclusions about Mamdani's mayoralty are not yet available, and this series will not pretend otherwise.

So the argument goes where the evidence goes. When you are back at a screen, the app has a place to declare a position of your own. It also has a steel-man drill, to run on the view you reject. Constructing the strongest version of the other side is where a defended position becomes a refined one.

For now, go back to election night. A word that ended American careers for seventy years, attached to the mayor of the country's largest city. [beat] What exactly won that night is still being argued. You now know where the lines of that argument run.
