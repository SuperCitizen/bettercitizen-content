1936. A Polish economist named Oskar Lange proposes a monument. Lange is a socialist. And the statue he has in mind is of socialism's most relentless enemy.

Here are his words. "A statue of Professor Mises ought to occupy an honorable place in the great hall of the Ministry of Socialization or of the Central Planning Board of the socialist state."

[pause]

A statue of Professor Mises, in the great hall of the planning board.

Why? Because Ludwig von Mises had forced socialists to confront the one problem their movement had spent fifty years not thinking about.

The joke had teeth on both ends. Lange was conceding that Mises had identified a real flaw, and possibly a fatal one. And he was boasting that he, Lange, had just solved it.

[beat]

Whether he actually solved it is the most consequential argument in the history of economics. And socialists of every kind have been answering it, in different ways, ever since.

Last time we drew the map of the socialist family. This time the family meets its strongest critics, and then argues with itself. We will take it in four steps.

First, the critique: the calculation problem, Lange's answer, and the verdict.

Second, the historical record, stated plainly.

Third, three streams of the family, each at its strongest.

And last, worker ownership: what it proves, and what it doesn't.

## The calculation problem

Mises published his essay in 1920. It is not about greed, or corruption, or laziness.

Grant the planners every virtue. Make them incorruptible, tireless, devoted to the common good. [beat] The problem remains, because it is a problem of arithmetic, not of motivation.

Here is the arithmetic. An economy must constantly choose among plans that are all technically feasible. Should this steel go to rails, to bridges, or to tractors? Each option consumes resources that have thousands of alternative uses.

In a market economy, prices compress all of that into a common denominator. The price of steel reflects, however imperfectly, everyone else's competing bids for it. So the planner of one project can calculate whether using steel here creates value, or destroys it.

Now follow Mises's chain, one link at a time.

Prices for capital goods emerge from actual exchange. Exchange requires owners. So abolish private ownership of the means of production, Mises argued, and you abolish the markets for capital goods. With the markets go the prices. And with the prices goes any way to compare one production plan against another.

[pause]

The planning board does not lack good intentions. It lacks a unit of account.

Socialism, in his phrase, is the abolition of rational economy. Choices among plans become educated guesses in the dark.

In 1945, Friedrich Hayek deepened the critique. Even with the prices, he argued, a planning board would lack the knowledge an economy runs on. The foreman's sense that this machine is wearing out. The buyer's hunch about next season. That knowledge is dispersed across millions of minds, and much of it cannot be articulated at all. So no central body can collect it in time.

What carries it, Hayek argued, is the price system. He called it "a system of telecommunications." When the price of tin moves, every user of tin is told, instantly, to economize on it. Nobody writes a memo.

So the two critiques stack. For Mises, the planners have no prices to calculate with. For Hayek, even with prices, they could never gather what prices carry.

## Lange's answer, and the verdict

Lange accepted the challenge on its own terms. Keep public ownership of industry, he proposed, but let a Central Planning Board simulate the market.

The board posts prices, and managers treat them as given. Then the board watches the inventories. A shortage means the price was too low, so raise it. A glut means the price was too high, so lower it.

Trial and error, Lange argued, would grope toward the same equilibrium a market finds. [beat] Minus the capitalists.

Through mid-century, much of the economics profession scored the round for Lange.

[pause]

The second half of the century reversed that verdict. Not on the blackboard. In the world. Three things did the damage.

First, the dynamic problem. Lange's scheme mimics a market at rest. But capitalism's real work is discovery, and a board adjusting yesterday's prices cannot conjure tomorrow's innovations.

Second, incentives. A state firm that cannot go bankrupt gets bailed out, so its managers learn to bargain with the ministry, not to economize. That is called the soft budget constraint.

Third, the empirical record. By the 1980s, the planned economies were falling visibly behind market economies with similar starting points: East Germany against West, North Korea against South. And between 1989 and 1991, the Soviet bloc dissolved.

But state the verdict honestly, because it cuts in both directions.

The debate discredited comprehensive central planning, and nearly every contemporary socialist concedes that.

But most democratic socialists and social democrats do not advocate a planning board. They advocate market economies with strong unions, public enterprises competing alongside private ones, and worker-owned firms operating in real markets.

So reach back to Mises's chain, from owners down to prices. Does it touch a worker-owned firm that sells in a real market?

[long pause]

It does not. Recall the links. No owners, no exchange. No exchange, no prices. No prices, no way to compare plans. A worker-owned firm in a real market keeps the owners, the exchange and the prices. What changes is who the owners are.

So the calculation critique does not touch proposals like that, whatever their other difficulties. In fact, the debate barely addressed them.

> Mises won the argument he started. Whether he won every argument is disputed.

## The historical record

The stakes of that debate were never academic. Here is the human ledger.

The Marxist-Leninist branch of the family produced the two deadliest famines of the twentieth century.

The first was Stalin's. His forced collectivization of agriculture stripped peasants of land and grain, for a state pursuing crash industrialization. It culminated in the famine of 1930 to 1933. Modern archival scholarship puts the excess deaths between roughly 5.5 and 8.7 million. About 3.5 to 3.9 million of those were in Ukraine, where the famine is called the Holodomor.

A generation later came Mao's. The Great Leap Forward collectivized China's countryside into communes, with production targets detached from reality. The famine of 1958 to 1962 killed, by credible scholarly estimates, somewhere between 15 and 45 million people, or more. Even a famine historian who urges caution across that whole range accepts that this was, by death toll, history's worst famine.

[pause]

Add the gulag, the purges, the sealed borders, and the one-party police states that every comprehensive-planning regime built. That pattern is the strongest single argument the tradition's critics possess.

> The critics' strongest argument: every attempt to abolish private ownership wholesale came with dictatorship.

Is that coincidence? Or does total economic power require total political power, as Hayek claimed? The critic's question deserves a straight answer, not a change of subject.

The democratic socialist's straight answer has two parts.

Part one: the record has a real boundary. Democratic-socialist and social-democratic parties have governed for decades. Postwar Britain, building the National Health Service. Sweden, across most of a century. No famines, no camps, no stolen elections. And the socialists who insisted on democratic means were among the Leninist states' first victims. Democratic socialists repudiated that branch in real time, not in retrospect.

> The democratic socialist's answer: the famine record belongs to the one-party, command-economy branch.

Part two: the history was learned as a constraint. The contemporary democratic-socialist program is built around one claim: socialization must never outrun democratic consent. That is why its proposals run through elections, co-ops and municipal pilots, and not through decrees.

The critic may reply that this only proves the program will stall at social democracy. That reply comes back.

[pause]

One more case, because both sides now reach for it: Venezuela. It deserves precision, not slogans.

Hugo Chávez's program meant expropriations, price and currency controls, and oil revenues spent as a political instrument. Under Nicolás Maduro, an economic collapse of historic scale followed.

What caused it is genuinely contested. The collapse compounded gross mismanagement, and the destruction of price signals, with an oil-price crash, and, after 2017, with United States sanctions on finance and oil.

So each slogan skips something. "Socialism did it" skips the oil and the sanctions. "Sanctions did it" skips the fact that the contraction and the hyperinflation began years before them.

What the case cleanly shows is narrower, and damning enough. A petrostate that dismantles market signals, and concentrates discretionary economic power without institutional checks, has no brakes when the money runs out.

Now use the map from last time. A debater says: "Venezuela proves Medicare for All would wreck the United States."

There are two distinct errors in that sentence. Can you name them?

[long pause]

The first is a family error. Venezuela's program sits at the state-socialist end of the map. Medicare for All would take one service off the market, inside a market economy. The word for that is decommodified, and it sits at the social-democratic end.

The second is an attribution error. Even for Venezuela itself, serious analysis does not read the collapse off the label.

And notice the symmetry. "Denmark proves socialism works" commits the same family error, in the other direction.

## Three streams

Now inside the family, where the disagreement is about end-states and strategy.

Three streams. Each gets the same hearing: its end-state, its exemplars, its strongest argument, and the evidence it points to.

The social democrat.

The end-state is regulated capitalism, with a universal welfare state. The exemplars are postwar Scandinavia, and Bevan's National Health Service.

The social democrat argues that the democratic socialist's end-state is a mirage that wastes political capital. The durable winnings of a century of labor politics are the health service, pensions and bargaining rights, all fully compatible with private ownership. So defend that estate and extend it. Do not gamble it on an ownership transformation that no electorate has ever endorsed.

The evidence the social democrat points to is Britain. Democrats there nationalized coal, rail and steel, and it produced enough inefficiency that Labour itself retreated. As for the revolutionary's road, it has been walked, and it ends in the record we just went through.

> The social democrat's case: defend the winnings. No electorate has endorsed an ownership transformation.

The democratic socialist.

The end-state is an economy with substantial worker and public ownership, won democratically. The exemplars run from Debs and Harrington to Sanders, DSA and Mamdani.

The democratic socialist argues that the social democrat's equilibrium does not hold. Taxes and transfers leave the source of political power untouched. Only spreading ownership locks the gains in: co-ops, public options, socialized finance. And against the revolutionary, democratic means are a principle, not a tactic. Socialization that outruns consent has already failed, catastrophically.

The evidence the democratic socialist points to is erosion. The welfare states built at mid-century have been eroding since the 1980s. On this account, concentrated private capital eventually buys the politics that unwinds them.

> The democratic socialist's case: only spreading ownership locks the gains in.

The Marxist-revolutionary.

The end-state is the abolition of the wage relation, and of private capital. The exemplars are Luxemburg's heirs.

The Marxist-revolutionary argues that both reformist streams mistake the state for a neutral tool. Capital can veto an elected government without breaking a single law: investment strikes, capital flight, bond-market pressure. So a socialism that confines itself to elections will be permitted exactly as much change as capital can tolerate. That is social democracy, at best. Transformation requires mass movements, and dual power built outside the state, so that when capital strikes, workers hold levers of their own.

The evidence the Marxist-revolutionary points to is the same retreat of the welfare states. On this account, it is evidence for the structural analysis, not against it.

> The Marxist-revolutionary case: elections permit only as much change as capital can tolerate.

And the two democratic streams give the revolutionary one reply. The strategy has no democratic exit. Every seizure of power "on behalf of" workers has ended with power belonging to the seizers.

[pause]

Three streams, three end-states. One of them probably sits furthest from your own instincts. Take that one, and state its best case, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

Why does that argument never resolve? Remember the critic's reply: a program bound by democratic consent will stall at social democracy. The political scientist Adam Przeworski formalized it, in a book of 1986 called Paper Stones.

He traced a century of European socialist parties, and found a structural trap. Industrial workers never became an outright electoral majority, anywhere. So socialist parties faced a forced choice. Stay a pure class party, and lose. Or broaden the appeal to clerks, farmers and shopkeepers, and win, at the cost of diluting the socialist program.

No socialist party has ever won a majority on a platform of actually transforming property relations.

> On Przeworski's reading, social democracy is the equilibrium that electoral competition imposes on socialism.

Not a betrayal of socialism. An equilibrium.

Apply it. A DSA chapter is debating how to run its candidates. One side wants "decommodify housing, socialize utilities." The other wants "lower rents, cheaper power."

What trade-off does Przeworski's model predict?

[long pause]

The explicit program mobilizes the committed, but it caps the coalition below a majority. The broad affordability frame can win. But the voters it wins are voting for cheaper power, not for socialism. So the mandate that results is social-democratic, whatever the candidate's card says.

And there is no clean escape, because the electorate needed to win contains a majority of people who are not socialists. Every elected socialist, from the sewer socialists to Mamdani, has lived inside that trade-off.

## What worker ownership proves

One place remains where democratic socialists believe they hold empirical ground, and not only theory: the worker cooperative.

The reference case is Mondragon, founded in 1956 in the Basque Country of Spain. It is now the world's largest federation of worker cooperatives, with about 70,000 people.

Weigh that evidence both ways.

Here is what it proves. Worker-owned firms can run capital-intensive industry, banking and retail for seven decades. They can survive recessions, and compete in export markets. The claim that economic democracy is impossible at the scale of a firm is dead.

And here is the scale, stated just as honestly. Mondragon's workforce is about three-hundredths of one percent of the European Union's workforce.

> Cooperatives are socialism's strongest existence proof, and so far its weakest scaling story.

That is why social democrats conclude that the co-op sector will remain a valuable niche, inside a conventional economy.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The calculation problem. Abolish private ownership of the means of production, and you abolish the prices that let one plan be compared with another. Hayek added the knowledge problem: what an economy knows is dispersed, and prices carry it.

[beat]

Two. Lange answered with a planning board that simulates the market. The debate discredited comprehensive central planning. It barely addressed market economies with worker and public ownership. Mises won the argument he started. Whether he won every argument is disputed.

[beat]

Three. The record. The Marxist-Leninist branch produced the two deadliest famines of the twentieth century. The critics' strongest argument: every attempt to abolish private ownership wholesale came with dictatorship. The democratic socialist's answer: the famine record belongs to the one-party, command-economy branch.

[beat]

Four. Three streams. The social democrat's case: defend the winnings. The democratic socialist's case: only spreading ownership locks the gains in. The Marxist-revolutionary case: elections permit only as much change as capital can tolerate. And on Przeworski's reading, social democracy is the equilibrium that electoral competition imposes on socialism.

[beat]

And five. Cooperatives are socialism's strongest existence proof, and so far its weakest scaling story.

[pause]

Two things are settled, as much as anything in political economy is. Comprehensive central planning fails. And the democratic and Leninist branches of socialism are different lineages, with different ledgers.

Three things stay open. Whether economic democracy can scale beyond the firm. Whether social democracy is a stable resting place, or an unstable one. And whether climate policy reopens calculation questions that everyone thought closed.

The next lecture leaves the seminar room, for the one place where these abstractions currently have a payroll, a police department, and a rent board: New York City.

For now, go back to Lange's joke. A socialist, offering his movement's most relentless enemy a statue in the great hall. [beat] The question Mises asked is the one every socialist has been answering ever since.
