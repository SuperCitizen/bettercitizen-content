## The first libertarian president

In December 2023, for the first time anywhere, a self-described libertarian took office as president of a G20 country. Javier Milei calls himself an anarcho-capitalist in theory and a minarchist in practice, and he campaigned waving a chainsaw.

- Argentina, December 2023

> Argentina became the tradition's first national-scale experiment.

## Two readings of the same numbers

Monthly inflation peaked at 25.5 percent in December 2023, and fell to between 2 and 2.5 percent by late 2025. The government posted Argentina's first primary fiscal surplus since 2010. The poverty rate spiked to 52.9 percent in the first half of 2024, before falling to 31.6 percent by mid-2025, its lowest level since 2018.

- Monthly inflation: 25.5%, then 2 to 2.5%
- Poverty: 52.9%, then 31.6%

Supporters say stabilization is vindicated. Critics say the recovery was purchased with a year of severe and avoidable pain.

> The experiment is young, and both readings are defensible.

## Strange borders

Libertarianism overlaps with conservatism on economics, with progressivism on civil liberties, and with socialism on war and corporate power.

- Conservatism: economics
- Progressivism: civil liberties
- Socialism: war and corporate power

> Which neighbor looks closest depends on which question you ask.

## Winning without winning

The Libertarian Party is electorally negligible. Gary Johnson's 3.3 percent in 2016 is its all-time high. Yet its agenda keeps becoming law, carried by other parties. As of mid-2026, 24 states have legalized adult-use cannabis, and the military has been an all-volunteer force since 1973.

- Best presidential result: 3.3%
- Adult-use cannabis: 24 states

> Never the presidency, repeatedly the argument.

## Four readings of the scoreboard

Minarchists see vindication of the long game through institutions.

- Minarchist: the long game

Anarcho-capitalists see crumbs, because the administrative state grew the whole time.

- Anarcho-capitalist: crumbs

Bleeding-heart libertarians see proof that wins come fastest when packaged with concerns other traditions share.

- Bleeding-heart: shared concerns win

Critics of the tradition see wins that are real but selective, in decades when surveillance, debt and executive power expanded.

- Critics: real but selective

The lesson follows each border in turn, from the cracking alliance with conservatism to the divide with socialism over property, and returns to Argentina stream by stream.
