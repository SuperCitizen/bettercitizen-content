## A quarter for Wilt Chamberlain

In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Robert Nozick asks you to imagine your favorite theory of fair distribution, fully realized. Now suppose Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract: every home-game ticket includes an extra twenty-five cents for him. A million fans pay cheerfully, and he ends the year 250,000 dollars richer than everyone else.

- 25 cents a ticket, a million fans

Nozick's question: if the start was just, and every step was a free choice, what exactly is unjust about the result?

> Nozick's moral: liberty upsets patterns.

## The intuition underneath

You do not have to accept the argument. But the tradition is built on the intuition it isolates: voluntary exchange between rights-respecting adults is presumptively just, and holding any preferred pattern against it requires ongoing coercion.

- Voluntary exchange: presumptively just

Most political traditions ask what the state should do about a problem. Libertarians ask first what entitles the state to act at all.

> The burden of proof sits on whoever proposes to restrict liberty.

## The non-aggression principle

Initiating force or fraud against a person or their property is wrong, for individuals and equally for the state. The principle restricts initiating force, not resisting it.

- Applies to individuals and to the state
- Initiating force, not resisting it

On this accounting, drug prohibition, occupational licensing and conscription are standing acts of aggression that require strong justification or repeal.

> Initiating force or fraud is wrong, for a person or for a state.

## One family, several streams

The family divides over a single question: how much state, if any?

Minarchists hold that the state is legitimate at minimal scope: rights protection, courts, national defense. This is Nozick's position.

- Minarchist: a minimal state

Anarcho-capitalists hold that even that much is illegitimate, and that competing private providers can supply every function.

- Anarcho-capitalist: no state at all

Bleeding-heart libertarians hold that libertarian institutions need moral preconditions, such as a welfare floor, and that markets are justified partly by what they do for the worst-off.

- Bleeding-heart: a welfare floor

## What it is not, and what is open

Libertarianism is often shelved as Republican Lite. Yet on surveillance, criminal-justice reform and drug policy, libertarians routinely line up with progressives, and they reject tariffs and industrial policy.

- Often shelved as "Republican Lite"

The lesson walks the canon in six moves, from Locke to Buchanan, and it leaves two arguments open: the family's internal fight, and the replies of the outside critics.

- Left open: the internal fight, the critics
