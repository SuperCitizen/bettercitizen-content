Imagine a society arranged exactly the way you think is fair. Perfect equality, if that is your view. Or distribution by need. Whatever pattern you endorse, suppose it has been fully realized.

[beat]

Now a basketball player signs a contract. His name is Wilt Chamberlain, and he is the most electrifying draw of his era. The contract has one unusual term. Every ticket to a home game includes an extra twenty-five cents, and that quarter drops into a box with his name on it.

The fans know the deal, and they pay cheerfully. Over the season, a million of them turn out. Chamberlain ends the year 250,000 dollars richer than everyone else.

[pause]

That scene comes from the philosopher Robert Nozick, in a book of 1974 called Anarchy, State, and Utopia. And here is the question he built it to ask.

The starting distribution was just. You chose it yourself. Every step away from it was a free choice, by people spending shares that you had certified as fairly theirs. So what exactly is unjust about the result?

[long pause]

The argument's own answer is: nothing. And Nozick presses further. Suppose you insist on restoring your pattern. Notice what that requires: continuous interference with what free people do with their own holdings. You would be taxing the quarter back out of Chamberlain's box. You would be forbidding what Nozick called "capitalist acts between consenting adults."

He stated the moral in three words.

> Nozick's moral: liberty upsets patterns.

You do not have to accept that argument. The third lecture in this series gives the strongest replies to it. But you cannot understand libertarianism without feeling its pull.

The tradition is built on the intuition that the Chamberlain case isolates. Voluntary exchange between adults who respect each other's rights is presumptively just. And maintaining any preferred social pattern against it requires ongoing coercion, which itself demands justification.

It is a distinct tradition, and it follows that single intuition wherever it leads, including places where neither major party will go.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to state what libertarians believe, the way they would state it themselves. We will take it in four steps.

First, the core: the commitments the whole family shares.

Second, the canon: six moves, each a different kind of argument.

Third, the streams: how the family divides over a single question.

And last, what libertarianism is not.

## The core

Libertarians disagree among themselves about plenty, and the next lecture is entirely about that. But a recognizable core holds across the family. It has six parts. We will take the first three slowly, and the last three more quickly.

One. Liberty is the political value, not one value among several.

Equality, community, security and virtue all matter to libertarians as human goods. But on this view they are to be pursued through free choice, not imposed through coercion. The commitment works as a presumption. Individual self-determination is the default, and the burden of proof always sits on whoever proposes to restrict it.

> The libertarian presumption: liberty is the default, and restriction carries the burden of proof.

Here is what that means in practice. Most political traditions ask what the state should do about a problem. Libertarians ask first what entitles the state to act on that problem at all.

Two. The non-aggression principle.

It says that initiating force or fraud against a person, or against their property, is wrong. Wrong for individuals, and equally wrong for the state. Many libertarians say especially for the state, because state coercion is systematic, legally privileged and inescapable.

> The non-aggression principle: no one may initiate force or fraud. Not even the state.

Listen to the verb. The principle restricts initiating force, not resisting it. Defensive force is another matter entirely.

Now follow where the principle leads. On this accounting, drug prohibition, occupational licensing and conscription are not policy choices to be weighed casually. For many libertarians, neither is redistributive taxation. They are standing acts of aggression, and they require strong justification or repeal.

Let's test that principle before we go on. Three things happen in your town.

A shop owner refuses to stock a book he finds offensive.

The state fines an unlicensed hair braider 2,000 dollars. The offense is braiding without 1,500 hours of cosmetology training.

And activists organize a boycott that bankrupts a bakery.

Which of the three does the non-aggression principle condemn? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only the fine.

The shop owner and the boycotters are making non-violent choices about their own property and their own patronage. Those choices may be objectionable. They are not aggression. The licensing fine is different. It is force, initiated by the state, against a peaceful exchange between willing adults. And that is exactly what the principle targets.

Notice what the principle does not ask about. The bakery in the third case is just as bankrupt. But no one initiated force against it.

The principle cuts at coercion, not at outcomes.

Now, back to the count.

Three. Self-ownership, with property as its extension.

The claim is that you own yourself: your body, your labor, your time. And property in external things, on John Locke's account, arises when you mix your labor with the unowned world.

That single move underwrites both halves of the libertarian agenda. The economic half: your earnings are yours. And the personal half: your body, your medical choices and your consensual relationships are yours too.

[pause]

Four. Suspicion of concentrated power, with the state as the paradigm case. The state is uniquely dangerous, on this view, because it claims a monopoly on legitimate violence. Concentrated private power can be dangerous too, but libertarians argue that it usually needs the state's help to do durable harm. So the libertarian remedy is to shrink the prize: a government that cannot dispense favors cannot be captured at scale.

Five. Voluntary cooperation as the alternative. The caricature has libertarians wanting everyone alone on a homestead. The tradition's actual answer is a dense web of voluntary institutions: markets, contracts, mutual-aid societies, charities. The claim is partly moral: cooperation you chose differs in kind from cooperation extracted at legal gunpoint. And it is partly empirical: voluntary orders, libertarians argue, have produced most of what we call prosperity.

Six. Civil liberties are core, not garnish. Free speech, due process, privacy, sexual autonomy, religious liberty. The Libertarian Party's very first platform, in 1972, called for repealing all laws creating "crimes without victims". That included laws against voluntary sexual relations, and laws against drug use. Neither major party would touch those positions for decades. And on surveillance, criminal-justice reform and drug policy, libertarians routinely line up with progressives against conservatives.

## Six moves, six kinds of argument

That is the core. Now, where does it come from?

The tradition's intellectual spine can be walked in six steps. Each thinker adds a different kind of argument, so listen for the kind.

First, John Locke, who supplies the rights foundation. Natural rights that precede government. Property as an extension of labor. And political authority that is legitimate only by consent. His Two Treatises, from 1689, is the common ancestor of classical liberalism and libertarianism alike. The Declaration of Independence speaks of "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness." That phrase tracks Locke's own: "life, liberty, and estate."

Second, John Stuart Mill, who supplies the liberty principle. In 1859, in On Liberty, Mill argued that "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, [beat] is to prevent harm to others."

To prevent harm to others. That is the harm principle, and it remains the standard libertarian test for legitimate state action. Notice what it excludes: protecting adults from themselves, and enforcing the majority's moral tastes.

Third, Friedrich Hayek, who supplies an argument about knowledge.

He made it in a twelve-page article of 1945, called "The Use of Knowledge in Society."

The knowledge a modern economy runs on, Hayek argued, exists only as millions of fragments: dispersed, local, often tacit. No planning board could ever assemble them. Prices, on Hayek's account, solve the problem that no committee can. The price system tells each actor what they need to know, and nothing more, about events they will never directly observe.

In his last book, Hayek wrote an epitaph for planning ambition. Here it is.

"The curious task of economics is to demonstrate to men how little they really know about what they imagine they can design."

[pause]

How little they really know.

Fourth, Milton Friedman, who supplies the policy. His book of 1962, Capitalism and Freedom, argued that economic freedom is a precondition of political freedom. And then Friedman did something philosophers rarely do. He proposed mechanisms. School vouchers. The negative income tax. Floating exchange rates. The all-volunteer military. All of them trace to him.

Let's test the third move before we take the last two.

A senator argues: "Industrial policy will fail not because subsidies are unjust, but because no agency can gather the dispersed, local, constantly changing information that prices already transmit."

Whose argument is she channeling? And is it a moral argument, or something else? Think it through.

[long pause]

It is Hayek's argument, and it is not a moral one. It is a claim about what planners cannot know, not a claim about rights. The name for that kind of argument is epistemic: an argument about knowledge.

Keep it distinct from the argument that opened this lecture. Hayek says intervention fails even with the best intentions. Nozick says patterned redistribution wrongs people even if it works.

> Hayek's argument is about knowledge. Nozick's argument is about rights.

The two pillars are independent. You can accept one and reject the other.

Fifth, Robert Nozick himself, who supplies the philosophical confrontation. His opponent is John Rawls.

In 1971, in A Theory of Justice, Rawls had rebuilt liberal egalitarianism. Inequalities are permissible, Rawls argued, only if they benefit the least advantaged. That is called the difference principle.

Nozick answers with the entitlement theory. A distribution is just if holdings were justly acquired, justly transferred, and rectified where past injustice can be shown. Full stop. There is no further pattern to satisfy.

In plainer words: for Nozick, justice lies in how holdings came about, not in the pattern they form.

His book opens with the tradition's most quoted sentence. "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them, without violating their rights."

Sixth, and last, a pair.

Murray Rothbard supplies the radical edge. In 1973, in For a New Liberty, he pushes the logic past Nozick. If aggression is wrong, then the state is wrong at any size, because it is funded by taxation and sustained by monopoly. On his account, courts, police and defense should be supplied by competing private providers.

James Buchanan supplies the institutional realism. He founded public choice economics. Its method is to analyze politicians and bureaucrats as self-interested actors, and not as benevolent correctors of market failure. Buchanan summarized the program in three words: "politics without romance."

[pause]

Six moves, each a different kind of argument. And in the last of them, two registers: Rothbard's moral absolutism, and Buchanan's institutional skepticism.

## One question, three streams

Within that shared core, the family divides over a single question. How much state, if any?

Picture the answers laid along a line, with no state at one end and more state toward the other. Libertarianism is not a point on that line. It is a band. It runs from Rothbard's no-state position, through Nozick's night-watchman state, to the classical liberals. And at its outer edge, bleeding-heart libertarians reach toward the welfare liberalism of Rawls, without joining it.

Three streams have names you should know. For each: what it holds, and who carries it.

Minarchists hold that the state is legitimate at minimal scope. Rights protection. Courts. Contract enforcement. National defense. Perhaps a few genuine public goods. This is the tradition's philosophical center of gravity. It is Nozick's position, and roughly the Cato Institute's.

> The minarchist answer: a state, at minimal scope.

Anarcho-capitalists hold that even that much is illegitimate. Every governmental function, on their account, can be supplied by competing private providers. This is the Rothbardian line.

> The anarcho-capitalist answer: no state at all.

Bleeding-heart libertarians hold that libertarian institutions need moral preconditions: a welfare floor, and real equality of opportunity. Markets, on their account, are justified partly by what they do for the worst-off. Their manifesto is John Tomasi's book of 2012, Free Market Fairness.

> The bleeding-heart answer: markets, a welfare floor, and real equality of opportunity.

Three answers to one question. Choose the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

The next lecture gives each stream its full case.

## What libertarianism is not

That is the family. Now clear away four mistakes about it.

It is not selfishness. The argument is about the moral difference between chosen and coerced cooperation, plus an empirical bet on which institutions produce flourishing. And the tradition's history runs through anti-slavery and anti-conscription causes.

It is not Republican economics. The Republican coalition includes social conservatives and national conservatives who favor tariffs, industrial policy, immigration restriction and morals legislation. Libertarians reject all four. And where civil liberties or war powers are at stake, libertarians frequently vote and litigate alongside progressives.

It is not anti-government in the cartoon sense. Minarchists and classical liberals are emphatically for courts and for the rule of law. Their objection is to scope and discretion, not to the existence of legal order. Anarcho-capitalists do reject the state, but in favor of competing legal institutions, not chaos.

And it is not uniformly opposed to redistribution. Friedman designed the negative income tax. Hayek endorsed a guaranteed minimum against severe deprivation. Bleeding-heart libertarians defend a basic-income floor outright. What the family jointly opposes is the discretionary, paternalistic welfare state. It does not oppose, in every stream, transfers as such.

Now a question that reaches back across this whole lecture. Your uncle says libertarians are "just Republicans who smoke weed."

You have already heard what you need to answer him. Which two civil-liberties positions, and which one economic position, break that equation?

[long pause]

On civil liberties. Libertarians backed the repeal of victimless-crime laws, including laws against same-sex relations, starting with the party's platform of 1972. And libertarians oppose mass surveillance. Both positions run against the conservative grain.

On economics. Libertarians oppose tariffs and industrial policy, which much of the Republican coalition now supports.

And here is the deeper point.

> Libertarianism is a principle applied consistently, not a partisan bundle.

The principle is to minimize coercion. Applied consistently, it keeps cutting across both parties' platforms.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Nozick's moral: liberty upsets patterns. Start from a distribution you call just, let people choose freely, and the pattern breaks. On Nozick's argument, restoring it takes continuous interference.

[beat]

Two. The core. Liberty is the default, and restriction carries the burden of proof. No one may initiate force or fraud, not even the state. And you own yourself, with property as the extension.

[beat]

Three. Six moves, each a different kind of argument. Locke gives the rights. Mill, the harm principle. Hayek, the knowledge problem. Friedman, the policy mechanisms. Nozick, the entitlement theory. Rothbard, a state that is wrong at any size. Buchanan, politics without romance. And keep one pair apart: Hayek's argument is about knowledge, Nozick's argument is about rights.

[beat]

Four. One question divides the family: how much state, if any? The minarchist answer: a state, at minimal scope. The anarcho-capitalist answer: no state at all. The bleeding-heart answer: markets, a welfare floor, and real equality of opportunity.

[beat]

And five. Libertarianism is a principle applied consistently, not a partisan bundle. That is why it is not Republican economics.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Two arguments are left open, on purpose.

The first is the family's own fight. Should the state be small, or should it not exist? And is social justice a libertarian concern, or a category error?

The second comes from outside. Rawlsians argue that the Chamberlain argument ignores background conditions. Communitarians argue that the unencumbered chooser is a fiction. Socialists argue that property itself is the coercion.

The next lecture takes up the family's fight, and lets each stream make its best case against the others. The lecture after that maps the borders with the critics.

For now, go back to the box with Chamberlain's name on it. A million quarters, each one freely paid. [beat] You know now what a libertarian sees in that box, and why. A defensible position requires hearing the rest of the argument first.
