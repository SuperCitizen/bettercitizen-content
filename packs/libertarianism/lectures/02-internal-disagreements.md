May 2022. A convention hall in Reno. The Libertarian Party is tearing itself in half, in public.

An insurgent group called the Mises Caucus sweeps the party's leadership elections. The caucus is Rothbardian, anti-establishment, and allergic to respectability politics. Its candidate for national chair wins more than 69 percent of the delegates, on the first ballot.

Several state affiliates walk away. So do longtime donors.

[pause]

To outsiders, it looked like a personality fight in a small party. [beat] It wasn't.

It was the surfacing of the oldest argument in libertarianism. That argument has run continuously since the early 1970s, when two men published dueling answers to the same question. Their names were Robert Nozick and Murray Rothbard, and they knew each other from New York libertarian circles.

> The oldest question: if coercion is presumptively wrong, how much state survives?

Last time, we met the core that the whole libertarian family shares. This time, the family argues. Everything in this lecture is a version of that old fight, plus a newer one: whether libertarianism owes anything to social justice.

The streams agree on most fundamentals. They diverge, sometimes bitterly, on what those fundamentals imply.

By the end, you should be able to state each stream's best case in its own voice. We will take it in four steps.

First, the founding duel: Rothbard against Nozick.

Second, the three streams, each at full strength.

Third, the streams against each other.

And last, where each stream lands on the issues, and what the fight has done to the party.

## The founding duel

Begin with Rothbard, because his position is brutally simple.

The state claims a territorial monopoly on legitimate force. And it funds itself by taxation that no one individually consented to. Judge it by the standards we apply to everyone else, Rothbard argued in 1973, and it is indistinguishable from a protection racket with better stationery.

Nothing about courts, police or defense requires a monopoly provider, on his account. All of them can be supplied the way food and insurance are supplied: by providers competing for willing customers.

[pause]

Nozick took that challenge seriously. His book of 1974, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, spends its entire first part answering it. And his reply is a story. Follow it in order.

Start where Rothbard starts: in a stateless market for protection. Now watch what rational, rights-respecting people do.

First, protection agencies form.

Then clients cluster toward the strongest agency in each territory, because protection is more valuable the more your neighbors share your enforcer.

Then a dominant agency emerges.

And last, the dominant agency forcibly brings the holdouts under its procedures, and compensates them. [beat] At that point, it has become a minimal state.

No one designed it. And no one's rights were violated along the way, Nozick argues. That is why his reply is called an invisible-hand story.

Here is the point of the exercise. On Nozick's account, the night-watchman state is not a betrayal of libertarian principle. It is the principle's natural equilibrium.

But go one step beyond the night-watchman state, and Nozick rejects it as emphatically as Rothbard does. That is what his Wilt Chamberlain argument is for, from the last lecture.

Now test the duel. Remember the non-aggression principle, the core rule we met last time.

A classmate says: "Nozick and Rothbard disagree about whether the state is legitimate, so one of them must reject the non-aggression principle."

What is wrong with that? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Both men accept the principle. Rothbard reads it as forbidding any tax-funded monopoly on force. Nozick argues that a minimal state can emerge from a stateless market without anyone's rights being violated. If he is right, the principle and a night-watchman state are compatible.

> Nozick and Rothbard share the principle. They disagree about what it permits.

Put another way: the dispute is over what the principle implies for institutions, not over the principle itself. That is why the family resemblance survives the fight.

There is a third voice in the duel, and it opens a different front.

David Friedman is Milton Friedman's son. In 1973, in a book called The Machinery of Freedom, he drops the natural-rights scaffolding entirely, and asks the economist's question. Would competing legal systems actually work?

His case is about consequences. It draws on historical episodes like medieval Iceland, where law enforcement ran for centuries through private arbitration and compensation, and not through a central executive.

So here are two men who want no state at all, for two different reasons.

> Rothbard says the state is wicked. Friedman says the state is inefficient.

That is a difference with teeth. Evidence could change Friedman's mind. It could not change Rothbard's. Hold on to that. You will need it in a few minutes.

## Three streams, at full strength

Now the three streams as they stand today. Each gets the same four things: its core commitment, who carries it now, its strongest logic, and its program. The attacks come afterward.

The minarchist stream.

Its core commitment: the state is legitimate at minimum scope. Protecting rights against force and fraud. Enforcing contracts. Providing courts and national defense. Perhaps narrow public goods that cannot be supplied privately. Beyond that, state action is presumptively illegitimate.

Who carries it now? The Cato Institute mainstream. The legal scholar Richard Epstein. The editorial line of Reason magazine. And most academic libertarians working in Nozick's wake.

Its strongest logic is a concession, made with open eyes. National defense and a final court of appeal are hard to provide by subscription, because of free-rider problems. So the state may provide them. Everything else on the modern state's menu fails the test: welfare programs, occupational licensing, drug prohibition, most economic regulation, foreign adventurism.

And its program is to unwind all of that through constitutional politics. Litigation. Legislation. Persuasion.

> The minarchist case: a state at minimum scope, reached through constitutional politics.

Put another way, the minarchist project is reformist in method, even when it is radical in aim. That is why minarchists dominate the tradition's think tanks, and its presence in the law schools.

The anarcho-capitalist stream.

Its core commitment: the state is illegitimate at any scale. Every function it performs can and should be supplied by competing private providers. Courts. Police. Defense. Money.

Who carries it now? The orbit of the Mises Institute, with figures like Walter Block and Hans-Hermann Hoppe. David Friedman's wing, which argues from consequences. And since Reno, the governing faction of the Libertarian Party.

Its strongest logic comes as three arguments.

One, consent. If taxation without consent is wrong for one service, it is wrong for all of them. And the reply that you can leave the country proves too much, because it would license any abuse.

Two, incentives. A monopoly provider of law judges its own disputes, including disputes about its own power. History, on this account, shows exactly the growth pattern that such a conflict of interest predicts.

Three, existence proofs. Commercial arbitration already resolves most contract disputes privately. Private security employs more people than public police in much of the world. And medieval Iceland, among other historical orders, shows that law without a state is not a fantasy.

And its program is gradualist. Privatize functions outward, until what remains of the state is small enough to be dissolved.

> The anarcho-capitalist case: the state is illegitimate at any scale.

Before the third stream, reach back to the founding duel. I said the difference between Rothbard and David Friedman has teeth. Here is the bite.

Suppose a rigorous historical study concludes that competing private legal systems consistently collapse into cartels, within a generation. Whose case for anarcho-capitalism does that most directly threaten?

[long pause]

Friedman's. His case rests on an empirical claim: that competing providers of law outperform a monopoly. Evidence of cartels strikes that claim directly.

Rothbard's case is a moral one. The state is wrong because taxation is aggression. And no finding about efficiency touches a moral claim.

The bleeding-heart stream.

Its core commitment: libertarian institutions are justified partly by what they do for the least advantaged. And they need moral preconditions: a basic floor, and genuine equality of opportunity. Libertarians should affirm those preconditions, on this view, and not grudgingly tolerate them.

Who carries it now? John Tomasi and Matt Zwolinski, among others. And among institutions, the Niskanen Center, which has drifted from libertarianism toward a market-friendly centrism.

Its strongest logic is the boldest move in Tomasi's book of 2012, Free Market Fairness. Take Rawls seriously. Rawls's test of justice is that institutions must work to the benefit of the least advantaged. And the market-friendly regime wins, the bleeding heart argues, on Rawls's own test.

Two reasons. Thick economic liberty, the liberty to own, to trade and to hire, is itself among the basic liberties that respect citizens as "responsible self-authors". And market economies, on this argument, have the strongest record of raising the floor.

And its program follows. A universal basic income, in place of the conditional welfare bureaucracy. Open immigration, as the single largest anti-poverty lever available. And acceptance of anti-discrimination law, where exclusion is systemic enough to function as coercion.

> The bleeding-heart case: markets are justified partly by what they do for the worst-off.

## The streams against each other

The disagreements come out sharpest in attack. So for the next few minutes, each stream makes its case against the other two, at full strength, in its own voice.

The minarchist goes first. Against the anarcho-capitalists.

A market for force is a market that eats itself. Protection agencies that disagree about procedure have no neutral arbiter, and the strongest agency's competition ends in conquest or cartel. The state's monopoly on force is not an unfortunate add-on. It is what makes rights enforceable at all.

[beat]

And against the bleeding hearts.

Admitting social justice as a standard concedes the central question. Once the state may redistribute to correct background unfairness, the limiting principle is gone. And the property rights that make independence from the state possible become renegotiable by every legislature.

[pause]

Now the anarcho-capitalist. Against the minarchists.

Minarchy is unstable by its own logic. A state strong enough to monopolize force is strong enough to grow, and the American founding is Exhibit A. The most carefully limited government ever designed now runs entitlement programs its framers could not have imagined.

[beat]

And against the bleeding hearts.

They negotiate with an institution whose legitimacy is the entire question. They trade the movement's radical clarity for a seat at a table the state owns.

[pause]

Last, the bleeding heart. Against the minarchists.

A theory with nothing to say about the worst-off will deserve the marginality it gets. And rectifying historical injustice is in Nozick's own entitlement theory. It is the rectification principle. Minarchists just decline to apply it.

[beat]

And against the anarcho-capitalists.

The state is not the only thing that coerces. Destitution, caste and private domination can do it too. Institutions that ignore that are not neutral, merely blind.

[pause]

Six attacks, each in its own voice. This lecture does not rank them.

Choose the stream you find least convincing. State its best case against the other two, as its own adherents would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Where the streams land

Arguments over principle become positions on issues. The lesson page sets ten issues side by side, stream by stream. Here is what that comparison shows.

On drugs, on marriage and on war, the family is nearly unanimous. All three streams strongly support drug legalization and marriage equality. All three are non-interventionist in foreign policy. That is why outsiders often cannot see the fault lines at all.

> Three issues carry the heat: welfare, anti-discrimination law, and the Constitution.

Take them one at a time.

Welfare programs. The minarchist wants them minimal, with private charity first. The anarcho-capitalist would abolish them. The bleeding heart would replace them with a universal basic income.

Anti-discrimination law. The minarchist is skeptical, on grounds of freedom of association. The anarcho-capitalist rejects it. The bleeding heart generally supports it, against systemic exclusion.

And the Constitution. The minarchist strongly supports constitutional originalism. The anarcho-capitalist rejects the Constitution's authority. The bleeding heart is mixed.

Let's put that to work. A think-tank fellow writes: "The state should be cut to courts, police, and defense, and we'll get there through constitutional litigation. Also, a universal basic income would be a catastrophe of dependency."

Which stream is speaking? And what gave it away? Think it through.

[long pause]

The minarchist. And two things gave it away.

The first is the method. A night-watchman state, reached by constitutional litigation, rules out anarcho-capitalism, which rejects the Constitution's authority. The second is the hard rejection of a universal basic income. That rules out the bleeding-heart stream, which often supports one.

Notice what would have told you nothing: a line about drugs, or about marriage. All three streams agree there.

Those fault lines run through the organized movement's whole history. It is short enough to walk in order.

December 1971. David Nolan and a small group of Colorado activists found the Libertarian Party.

1972. The party's first ticket appears on just two state ballots. Yet a faithless Republican elector votes for it, and Tonie Nathan becomes the first woman ever to receive an electoral vote.

2016. The high-water mark. Gary Johnson wins 3.3 percent of the national popular vote, the best result in party history.

2022. Reno, where this lecture began.

2024. Donald Trump is invited to speak at the party's convention, and is loudly booed when he asks for its votes. The party's own nominee finishes well under half a percent.

And January 2025. Trump delivers a full pardon for Ross Ulbricht, the founder of Silk Road. A longstanding libertarian cause is won outside the ballot box.

[pause]

Which leaves a paradox. By vote share, libertarianism looks negligible. By policy influence, it has won arguments far above its electoral weight: drug-law liberalization, school choice, occupational-licensing reform, the all-volunteer military. And it has won them mostly by routing ideas through both major parties and the courts, not through its own ballot line.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The oldest question: if coercion is presumptively wrong, how much state survives? Rothbard answers: none. Nozick answers: a minimal state, which emerges by an invisible hand.

[beat]

Two. Nozick and Rothbard share the principle. They disagree about what it permits. And among those who want no state, Rothbard says the state is wicked, while Friedman says the state is inefficient. Only Friedman's case can be moved by evidence.

[beat]

Three. Each stream's case. The minarchist case: a state at minimum scope, reached through constitutional politics. The anarcho-capitalist case: the state is illegitimate at any scale. The bleeding-heart case: markets are justified partly by what they do for the worst-off.

[beat]

Four. Each stream's charge against its rivals. From the minarchist: a market for force eats itself. From the anarcho-capitalist: a state strong enough to monopolize force is strong enough to grow. From the bleeding heart: the state is not the only thing that coerces.

[beat]

And five. Three issues carry the heat: welfare, anti-discrimination law, and the Constitution. On drugs, marriage and war, the family is nearly unanimous.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Three questions.

Is minarchy a resting point, or a way station? Is private law a historical curiosity, or a scalable institution? And does social justice complete the tradition, or dissolve it?

Each stream has a serious answer to the others, and a research program still at work. That is why this subject is marked as contested.

The next lecture turns outward: to where this family sits among conservatism, progressivism and socialism, and to what its ideas have changed in American law. It takes up the paradox we just reached.

For now, go back to that convention hall in Reno. [beat] It was not a personality fight in a small party. It was the oldest argument in libertarianism, surfacing again. And you can now state all three sides of it, each in its own voice.
