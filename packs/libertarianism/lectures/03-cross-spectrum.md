December 2023. For the first time anywhere, a self-described libertarian takes office as president of a G20 country.

His name is Javier Milei. He is an economist, and he calls himself an anarcho-capitalist in theory and a minarchist in practice. He campaigned waving a chainsaw, to symbolize what he would do to the state.

Argentina became the tradition's first experiment at national scale. And both its admirers and its critics got real data. Here are four early numbers, verified as of mid-2026.

Inflation. In December 2023, after an initial devaluation, prices were rising 25.5 percent a month. By late 2025, the monthly rate had fallen to between 2 and 2.5 percent.

The budget. The government posted Argentina's first primary fiscal surplus since 2010.

Poverty. The poverty rate spiked brutally, to nearly 53 percent, in the first half of 2024. Then it fell, to just under 32 percent by mid-2025. That was its lowest level since 2018.

And the voters. In the midterm elections of October 2025, Milei's party won about 41 percent of the national vote, and more than doubled its seats in the lower house.

[pause]

Supporters say: stabilization, vindicated. Critics say: a recovery purchased with a year of severe and avoidable pain, and propped up by U.S. financial support. And the experiment is young.

Hold both readings. We will come back to Argentina at the end.

Milei is a useful place to begin, because he scrambles the map most Americans carry. Libertarianism's borders are strange. Which neighbor looks closest depends entirely on which question you ask.

> Libertarians meet conservatives on economics, progressives on civil liberties, socialists on war and corporate power.

Last time, the libertarian family argued with itself. This time, we look outward. By the end, you should be able to say what libertarianism shares with each neighbor, where each alliance breaks, and what the evidence can and cannot show.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the conservative alliance, and why it is cracking.

Second, the overlaps on the left: with progressives, and with socialists.

Third, the scoreboard: what libertarian ideas have changed in American law.

And last, back to Argentina.

## The conservative alliance

The alliance between libertarians and conservatives was no accident. It was a deliberate Cold War construction, and it had an architect.

Frank Meyer was a senior editor at National Review. He spent the 1950s and 60s arguing that traditionalist conservatism and libertarian economics were not rivals. They were halves of one position. Virtue must be freely chosen, Meyer argued, so a free economy is the precondition of a virtuous society.

His synthesis was dubbed fusionism. Think of it as traditionalist ends, reached by libertarian means. It supplied the Republican coalition's operating system from Goldwater through Reagan: free markets, anti-communism and traditional morals, riding the same ticket.

The shared planks are real. Free trade. Skepticism of state power. Federalism. Property rights. Constitutional limitation.

But notice what kind of thing fusionism always was.

> Fusionism was a treaty, not a theory.

A treaty is an agreement between two parties, and it lasts only while both of them keep it. This one is now visibly cracking, from the conservative side.

National conservatives, and the right-populism now in the ascendant, favor tariffs, industrial policy, immigration restriction, and aggressive use of state power against cultural adversaries. All of that is anathema to libertarians.

The Libertarian Party's convention of 2024 staged the divorce as theater. Donald Trump was invited to speak. When he asked for the party's votes, he was loudly booed. He won his only sustained cheer by promising to free Ross Ulbricht. And he kept that promise, with a full pardon on January 21, 2025.

That episode captures the current relationship: transactional, issue by issue, and mutually suspicious. American conservatism still contains a classical-liberal wing that is functionally libertarian on economics. It no longer controls the coalition.

Let's test that. A candidate in 2026 runs on four things: tariffs, industrial policy, immigration restriction, and using state power against what the candidate calls woke corporations.

Inside the old fusionist coalition, what is this program? Is it fusionism intact, or something else? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is the traditionalist half governing without the libertarian half. The treaty, broken.

Meyer's fusionism paired traditionalist ends with libertarian means: free markets and limited government, as the precondition of freely chosen virtue. This program keeps the ends and discards the means. That is why libertarians read the national-conservative turn as fusionism's collapse, and not as its evolution.

## The overlaps on the left

Now turn to the progressives. Libertarians share more with them than either side's partisans like to admit. And the overlap is structural, not coincidental. It shows in five places.

Civil liberties: speech, due process, privacy, sexual autonomy. The ACLU and Cato file overlapping briefs on the Fourth Amendment and on surveillance.

Criminal-justice reform. The First Step Act of 2018 passed through an alliance of progressives, libertarians and reform conservatives.

Drug policy. Libertarians supplied the argument for half a century. Progressives supplied the votes.

Immigration. Most libertarians favor radical liberalization, which puts them well to the left of the median Democrat.

And skepticism of collusion between corporations and the state. The libertarian critique of regulatory capture and the progressive critique of corporate influence converge on the same revolving doors.

[pause]

What divides the two is just as structural. Progressives treat market outcomes as politically constructed, and correctable. Libertarians treat market exchange as presumptively legitimate, and treat correction as the thing that requires justification. On taxation, regulation and labor law, the two are simply incompatible. So the alliance assembles and dissolves, issue by issue.

Here is a puzzle. The same libertarian organization lobbies one year alongside the ACLU, against a surveillance bill. The next year it lobbies alongside the Chamber of Commerce, against a minimum-wage increase.

Is that incoherent? Think it through.

[long pause]

Not by its own lights. The consistent principle is to minimize coercion, as the libertarian defines it. Warrantless surveillance is state coercion of personal life, and there a progressive ally is available. A wage mandate is state coercion of voluntary contract, and there a business ally is available.

The partners change because the other traditions sort issues by different principles: equality for progressives, order and tradition for conservatives.

> Coalitions track overlapping conclusions, not shared premises.

Put another way: two groups can arrive at the same vote without agreeing on why.

Now the socialists. Here libertarianism shares less, but the overlaps are revealing. There are three.

War. Both traditions denounce the military-industrial complex and serial intervention. Libertarian Republicans like Rand Paul, and progressive Democrats like Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna, have co-sponsored war-powers resolutions against the same deployments.

Corporate power. Both treat the alliance of concentrated wealth and state power as corrupt. The libertarian calls it regulatory capture. The socialist calls it state-monopoly capitalism. They are pointing at the same lobbyists.

And ancestry. A history called The Individualists documents libertarianism's nineteenth-century origins, among radicals who fought slavery and empire alongside the left.

The unbridgeable divide is property.

The sharpest socialist engagement with Nozick is a book of 1995 by G. A. Cohen. Cohen argues that taking self-ownership seriously does not yield capitalist property in the external world. And he argues that liberty for the propertyless is formal, not real. Jason Brennan answers in kind, with a defense of laissez-faire, in a book of 2014.

That exchange is the deepest single fault line on the spectrum, and both sides are at their best in it. The two traditions can share an enemy. They cannot share a view of property.

## Winning without winning

Here is the strangest fact about American libertarianism. Its party is electorally negligible. Gary Johnson's 3.3 percent, in 2016, is the all-time high. Yet its policy agenda keeps becoming law, carried by other parties.

Run down the scoreboard as it stood in mid-2026. Five lines.

The drug war. The Libertarian Party's platform called for retreat in 1972. Since then, 24 states have legalized adult-use cannabis, along with two territories and D.C.

School choice. Friedman, the author of Capitalism and Freedom, wrote his voucher essay in 1955. By the end of 2025, 19 states had private-choice programs with universal eligibility.

Occupational licensing. Friedman made that case in the book itself, in 1962. Now 28 states recognize licenses from other states, in a wave that Arizona started in 2019.

Conscription. Friedman and the Gates Commission made that case from 1969. The all-volunteer force has stood since 1973, and it is now politically untouchable.

And criminal-justice reform. The First Step Act, and the Ulbricht pardon.

[pause]

Now, what does that scoreboard mean? Each stream of the family sees something different in it, and so do the tradition's critics.

Minarchists see vindication of the long game through institutions.

Anarcho-capitalists see crumbs. The administrative state grew the whole time.

Bleeding-heart libertarians see proof that libertarian wins come fastest when they are packaged with concerns other traditions share: racial disparities in drug enforcement, or poor families trapped in failing schools.

And critics of the tradition see wins that are real, but selective. The same decades brought expansions of surveillance, debt and executive power. Libertarians opposed all three, and lost.

[pause]

So has libertarianism ever won anything? That is an empirical question, with a checkable answer.

> Libertarianism has never won the presidency. It has repeatedly won the argument.

There is a general rule in that. When you evaluate any small ideological movement, separate its electoral scoreboard from its policy scoreboard. The two can diverge for decades.

## Back to Argentina

Return to Argentina now, with the streams in mind. The experiment is contested within the libertarian family, and outside it. So here are four readings, each built the same way.

Begin with how Milei governs. He governs as a stabilizer: slashing ministries, deregulating, running a surplus. He does not govern as an abolitionist of the state.

The anarcho-capitalist points to what has not happened. Nothing about courts, money or police has been privatized.

> The anarcho-capitalist reading: nothing has been privatized, so the core thesis remains untested.

The minarchist points to the broken inflationary spiral: on this reading, the most successful real-world demonstration in decades. And to the midterm result of October 2025, as evidence that voters will reward it.

> The minarchist reading: fiscal discipline and deregulation can break an inflationary spiral.

The bleeding-heart libertarian points to both the disinflation and the poverty spike of 2024.

> The bleeding-heart question: were transition costs of that size morally priced in?

And the skeptic of the whole tradition points to the extraordinary external support that the experiment required.

> The skeptic's reading: extraordinary external support, and one electoral cycle proves little.

As of mid-2026, every one of those readings is defensible.

Choose the reading you find least convincing, and state its best case, as the person who holds it would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

One last test, and it reaches back to the numbers that opened this lecture.

An op-ed declares: "Argentina proves anarcho-capitalism works." A reply declares: "Argentina proves libertarianism means mass impoverishment."

Using only the verified record, what is wrong with each? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first overclaims the regime. Milei has governed as a fiscal minarchist. There are no private courts, no private police, no private currency. So anarcho-capitalism's distinctive claims are untested.

The second overclaims the trend. Poverty spiked to nearly 53 percent in early 2024. But by mid-2025 it had fallen to just under 32 percent, below where Milei found it. And that came alongside the steep disinflation.

The defensible statements are narrower. Severe transition costs occurred. Stabilization followed. Long-run verdicts await.

Good ideological analysis says what a case can show, and what it cannot.

One more question about Argentina. Why there, and not in Europe?

European politics has no direct equivalent of American libertarianism. The nearest relatives are classical-liberal parties, such as Germany's FDP. They are market-friendly and civil-libertarian, but reconciled to substantial welfare states. And Europe's anarchist traditions run left, not toward anarcho-capitalism.

The deeper difference is structural. EU regulatory frameworks, parliamentary coalitions and constitutional welfare commitments leave little institutional space for small-state politics. So the first head of state anywhere to claim the label emerged from Latin America's inflationary politics.

Libertarianism in power, so far, looks like crisis medicine. It has been adopted where the status quo had already failed catastrophically.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Libertarians meet conservatives on economics, progressives on civil liberties, socialists on war and corporate power. Which neighbor looks closest depends on the question.

[beat]

Two. Fusionism was a treaty, not a theory. It paired traditionalist ends with libertarian means, and it is cracking from the conservative side.

[beat]

Three. Coalitions track overlapping conclusions, not shared premises. With progressives, the divide is over market outcomes: correctable, or presumptively legitimate. With socialists, the divide is property.

[beat]

Four. Libertarianism has never won the presidency. It has repeatedly won the argument. So keep the electoral scoreboard and the policy scoreboard apart.

[beat]

And five. Argentina, in four readings. The anarcho-capitalist: the core thesis remains untested. The minarchist: discipline and deregulation can break an inflationary spiral. The bleeding heart: were the transition costs morally priced in? The skeptic: one electoral cycle proves little.

[pause]

This series ends here, and it does not end with a verdict. So here is where the argument goes next.

A defensible position on libertarianism requires four things, whether you identify with the tradition or not.

A view on which stream best fits current conditions, or on what synthesis is achievable.

A view on the strongest critiques, and on where they bite and where they miss. Cohen, on self-ownership. Rawlsians, on background justice. Communitarians, on the unencumbered chooser.

A view on the evidence: the policy scoreboard and the Argentina experiment.

And an honest accounting of what evidence would change your position.

When you are back at a screen, the lesson invites you to declare a position, and to construct the strongest version of a view you reject.

For now, go back to December 2023, and a president who campaigned with a chainsaw. [beat] The experiment is young, and the game is still being played. Resist the urge to score it at halftime.
