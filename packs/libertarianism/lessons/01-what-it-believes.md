# What libertarianism actually believes

Libertarianism is often conflated with conservative economic policy or "Republican Lite." Both characterizations miss what makes it a distinct tradition with substantively different commitments. This lesson presents the core, in language adherents would themselves recognize.

## A core that holds across the family

Most libertarians, regardless of more specific orientation, share commitments roughly as follows:

**Liberty as the central political value.** Individual liberty is not one value among several; it's the foundational political value. Other values (equality, community, security, virtue) matter, but libertarians believe they are best pursued through free choice rather than imposed through coercion. The presumption is for individual self-determination, with the burden of proof on those who would restrict it.

**The non-aggression principle.** Initiating force or fraud against another person or their property is wrong. This applies to private actors and to the state — and arguably more strongly to the state, because state coercion is systematic and inescapable. Most libertarians treat taxation, drug prohibition, occupational licensing, mandatory military service, and many other state activities as forms of aggression that require strong justification.

**Self-ownership and property rights.** Individuals own themselves — their bodies, labor, and the products of their labor. Property rights are extensions of self-ownership: when you mix your labor with the world (Locke's formulation), you acquire a property right that the state should protect, not redistribute. This is the basis for both economic-freedom claims and personal-autonomy claims (drug policy, sexual ethics, end-of-life choice).

**Suspicion of concentrated power.** The state is uniquely dangerous because it claims a monopoly on legitimate violence. Concentrated economic power can be problematic, but it generally requires state collusion (regulatory capture, occupational licensing, eminent domain) to do significant harm. The libertarian solution is to limit state power so it can't be captured at scale, not to expand state power to "balance" private power.

**Voluntary cooperation as the alternative.** The libertarian response to coordination problems is markets, voluntary associations, mutual-aid societies, and contract — not state action. This is sometimes caricatured as "selfishness"; the libertarian response is that voluntary cooperation has produced enormous flourishing (markets, scientific research, civil society) and is morally distinct from state-imposed cooperation.

**Civil liberties as central, not peripheral.** Free speech, religious liberty, sexual autonomy, privacy, and due process are core libertarian commitments. Libertarians often align with progressives on civil-liberties questions (criminal justice reform, drug-policy liberalization, surveillance limitation, immigration liberalization) and against the conservative coalition. Libertarianism is not a "right-wing" position on these questions.

## The foundational thinkers

John Locke (1632-1704) — *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) provides the foundational case for natural rights, property as an extension of labor, and government as legitimately deriving from individual consent. Locke is foundational for both classical liberalism and libertarianism.

John Stuart Mill (1806-1873) — *On Liberty* (1859) gives the canonical defense of individual liberty against both state and majoritarian coercion. The "harm principle" — that liberty may be limited only to prevent harm to others — has become the libertarian benchmark for legitimate state action.

Friedrich Hayek (1899-1992) — *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960) and other works develop classical-liberal arguments about price signals, the limits of central planning, and the rule of law. Hayek himself rejected the "libertarian" label in favor of "old Whig" or "classical liberal" but is heavily influential on libertarian thought.

Milton Friedman (1912-2006) — *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962) brought free-market ideas to a popular audience. School vouchers, the negative income tax, the volunteer military, and floating exchange rates are all Friedman-influenced policy ideas. Friedman represented the "Chicago school" classical-liberal stream more than philosophical libertarianism, but the boundary is blurry.

Robert Nozick (1938-2002) — *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) is the canonical philosophical defense of minimal-state libertarianism. Nozick's "entitlement theory of justice" — that holdings are just if they were acquired justly and transferred justly — directly challenges Rawls's theory of justice. The book is the foundational philosophical text for academic libertarianism.

Murray Rothbard (1926-1995) — *For a New Liberty* (1978) is the canonical anarcho-capitalist statement. Rothbard argued that the state is illegitimate at any scale and that all functions traditionally assigned to government (courts, police, defense, currency) can be provided by competing private entities. Foundational for the anarcho-capitalist (or "ancap") stream.

## The major streams

**Minarchists** (limited-state libertarians). The state has legitimate but minimal functions: protecting individual rights against force and fraud, enforcing contracts, providing courts, defense against external aggression, and possibly providing certain narrow public goods. Beyond this, state action is presumptively illegitimate. Most academic libertarians (Nozick, Tomasi, the Cato Institute mainstream) are minarchist.

**Anarcho-capitalists** ("ancaps"). Even the minimal state is illegitimate; all government functions can be provided by competing private entities — private courts, private defense, private currencies. The Rothbardian tradition (David Friedman's *The Machinery of Freedom*, Hans-Hermann Hoppe's *Democracy: The God That Failed*) is the most-developed version. Smaller in number than minarchists but intellectually distinct.

**Bleeding-heart libertarians (BHLs).** Libertarianism plus serious commitment to social-justice goals. John Tomasi's *Free Market Fairness* argues that libertarian and Rawlsian premises produce overlapping conclusions about basic structures. The BHL position accepts that markets need certain moral preconditions (basic welfare floors, equal opportunity, anti-discrimination protections) and that libertarians should embrace them rather than treat all redistributive policy as inherently illegitimate. Foundational for libertarian-progressive engagement.

## What libertarianism is NOT

A few common misconceptions worth addressing:

**Not "selfishness."** The libertarian critique of state action is not based on self-interest at the expense of others. It's based on the moral status of voluntary vs. coerced action, and on empirical claims about which institutional arrangements actually produce flourishing. Many libertarians give substantial portions of income to charity, advocate for the global poor through immigration liberalization, and support effective-altruism-adjacent movements.

**Not "Republican economic policy."** Most American Republicans are not libertarians. The Republican coalition includes social conservatives (who oppose libertarian positions on drugs, sexuality, immigration, and foreign policy) and national conservatives (who support tariffs, industrial policy, and immigration restriction). Libertarians often support Democratic positions on these questions and Republican positions on others.

**Not "anti-government."** Most libertarians are pro-rule-of-law, pro-property rights, pro-courts. The position is anti-*concentrated*-government, not anti-*all*-government (except for anarcho-capitalists, who are pro-private institutions that perform similar functions).

**Not opposed to all redistribution.** Bleeding-heart libertarians support some redistribution (universal basic income, school vouchers, food stamps in some forms) when it's targeted, transparent, and minimally distortionary. The objection is to large-scale, opaque redistribution that creates rent-seeking and dependency, not to all transfers.

**Not "Reagan."** Reagan was a self-described conservative with significant libertarian sympathies on economic questions. He was not libertarian on social-conservative questions, drug policy, immigration (more open than current Republicans but not libertarian), or foreign policy. The libertarian-Reagan overlap is real but partial.

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson sharpens:

- **Minarchists** treat the state as legitimate at minimum scope. The tradition's center of gravity philosophically.
- **Anarcho-capitalists** treat any state as illegitimate. A coherent, demanding position with real adherents.
- **Bleeding-heart libertarians** treat libertarianism as compatible with significant social-justice commitments. The bridge to progressive engagement.
