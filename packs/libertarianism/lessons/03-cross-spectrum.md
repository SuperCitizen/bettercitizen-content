# How libertarianism relates to other traditions

In December 2023, for the first time anywhere, a self-described libertarian — Javier Milei, an economist who calls himself an anarcho-capitalist in theory and a minarchist in practice, and who campaigned waving a chainsaw to symbolize what he would do to the state — took office as president of a G20 country. Argentina became the tradition's first national-scale experiment, and both its admirers and its critics got real data. The early numbers, verified as of mid-2026: monthly inflation, which peaked at 25.5 percent in December 2023 after an initial devaluation, fell to roughly 2 percent by late 2025, with the annual rate down from a peak near 289 percent (April 2024) to around 32 percent; the government posted Argentina's first primary fiscal surplus since 2010; the poverty rate spiked brutally to 52.9 percent in the first half of 2024 before falling to 31.6 percent by mid-2025 — its lowest level since 2018; and in the October 2025 midterms Milei's party won over 40 percent of the national vote and tripled its seats in the lower house. Stabilization vindicated, say supporters. A recovery purchased with a year of severe and avoidable pain, and propped up by U.S. financial support, say critics — and the experiment is young. Hold both readings; the case study returns below.

Milei is a useful opening because he scrambles the map most Americans carry. Libertarianism's borders are genuinely strange: it overlaps with conservatism on economics, with progressivism on civil liberties, and with socialism on war and corporate power. Which neighbor looks closest depends entirely on which question you ask.

## What libertarianism shares with conservatism — and where the fusion is cracking

The libertarian–conservative alliance was a deliberate Cold War construction. Frank Meyer, a senior editor at *National Review*, spent the 1950s and 60s arguing that traditionalist conservatism and libertarian economics were not rivals but halves of one position — virtue must be freely chosen, so a free economy is the precondition of a virtuous society. His synthesis, dubbed **fusionism**, supplied the Republican coalition's operating system from Goldwater through Reagan: free markets, anti-communism, and traditional morals riding the same ticket. The shared planks are real — free trade, skepticism of state power, [[term:federalism]], property rights, constitutional limitation.

But fusionism was always a treaty, not a theory, and it is now visibly cracking from the conservative side. National conservatives and the [[ideology:right-populism]] ascendancy favor tariffs, industrial policy, immigration restriction, and aggressive use of state power against cultural adversaries — all anathema to libertarians. The 2024 Libertarian Party convention staged the divorce as theater: invited to speak, Donald Trump was loudly booed when he asked for the party's votes, and won his only sustained cheer by promising to free Ross Ulbricht — a promise he kept with a full pardon on January 21, 2025. The episode captures the current relationship: transactional, issue-by-issue, and mutually suspicious. [[ideology:american-conservatism]] still contains a classical-liberal wing that is functionally libertarian on economics; it no longer controls the coalition.

## What libertarianism shares with progressivism

More than either side's partisans like to admit, and the overlap is structural, not coincidental:

- **Civil liberties.** Speech, due process, privacy, sexual autonomy. The ACLU, the EFF, Cato, and *Reason* file overlapping briefs on Fourth Amendment and surveillance questions.
- **Criminal-justice reform.** The First Step Act of 2018 — sentencing reductions, earned early release — passed through an alliance of progressives, libertarians, and reform conservatives, with Koch-network groups supplying much of the advocacy infrastructure.
- **Drug policy.** Libertarians supplied the argument for half a century; progressives supplied the votes; the map below shows the result.
- **Immigration.** Most libertarians favor radical liberalization — bleeding-heart libertarians treat open borders as the single largest available anti-poverty program — putting them well to the *left* of the median Democrat.
- **Skepticism of corporate–state collusion.** The libertarian critique of regulatory capture and the progressive critique of corporate influence converge on the same revolving doors.

What divides them is just as structural: progressives treat market outcomes as politically constructed and correctable; libertarians treat market exchange as presumptively legitimate and "correction" as the thing requiring justification. On taxation, regulation, and labor law, the two are simply incompatible — which is why the alliance assembles and dissolves issue by issue.

## What libertarianism shares with socialism

Less — but the overlaps are revealing. Both traditions denounce the military-industrial complex and serial intervention; libertarian Republicans like Rand Paul and progressive Democrats like Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna have co-sponsored war-powers resolutions against the same deployments. Both treat the alliance of concentrated wealth and state power as corrupt — the libertarian calls it regulatory capture, the socialist calls it state-monopoly capitalism, and they are pointing at the same lobbyists. And historically the two share more ancestry than either advertises: Zwolinski and Tomasi's *The Individualists* (2023) documents libertarianism's nineteenth-century origins among radicals who fought slavery and empire alongside the left.

The unbridgeable divide is property. G. A. Cohen's *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995) — the sharpest socialist engagement with Nozick — argues that taking self-ownership seriously does not yield capitalist property in the external world, and that "liberty" for the propertyless is formal, not real. Jason Brennan's *Why Not Capitalism?* (2014) answers in kind. A serious student should read the exchange; it is the deepest single fault line on the spectrum, and both sides are at their best in it.

## Winning without winning: the scoreboard

Here is the strangest fact about American libertarianism: its party is electorally negligible — Gary Johnson's 3.3 percent in 2016 is the all-time high — yet its policy agenda keeps becoming law, carried by other parties. The table tracks verified outcomes as of mid-2026.

| Policy | Libertarian position since | Where it stands (mid-2026) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug-war retreat | LP platform, 1972 | 24 states, two territories, and D.C. have legalized adult-use cannabis (NCSL); a Florida initiative is on the November 2026 ballot |
| School choice | Friedman's voucher essay, 1955 | 23 universal-eligibility private-choice programs across 19 states by the end of 2025 (EdChoice); Texas's 2025 ESA launched at $1 billion |
| Occupational-licensing reform | Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom*, 1962 | 28 states recognize out-of-state licenses, a wave Arizona started in 2019 |
| End of conscription | Friedman and the Gates Commission, 1969–70 | All-volunteer force since 1973 — now politically untouchable |
| Criminal-justice reform | Long-standing | First Step Act (2018); Ulbricht pardon (Jan. 2025) |

Read the table's lesson carefully, because each tradition reads it differently. Minarchists see vindication of the long game through institutions. Anarcho-capitalists see crumbs — the administrative state grew the whole time. Bleeding-heart libertarians see proof that libertarian wins come fastest when packaged with concerns other traditions share (racial disparities in drug enforcement; poor families trapped in failing schools). And critics of the tradition note, fairly, that the wins are real but selective: the same decades saw expansions of surveillance, debt, and executive power that libertarians opposed and lost.

> **Why this matters:** "Has libertarianism ever won anything?" is an empirical question with a checkable answer. The honest version is: never the presidency, repeatedly the argument. When you evaluate any small ideological movement, separate its electoral scoreboard from its policy scoreboard — they can diverge for decades.

## The Milei test, continued

Return to Argentina with the streams in mind, because the experiment is genuinely contested *within* the family. Milei governs as a stabilizer — slashing ministries, deregulating, running a surplus — not as an abolitionist of the state; an anarcho-capitalist can fairly say nothing about courts, money, or police has been privatized, so the core thesis remains untested. A minarchist sees the most successful real-world demonstration in decades that fiscal discipline and deregulation can break an inflationary spiral — and the October 2025 midterm result as evidence voters will reward it. A bleeding-heart libertarian sees both the disinflation *and* the 2024 poverty spike, and asks whether transition costs of that size were morally priced in. A skeptic of the whole tradition notes the experiment required extraordinary external support and that one electoral cycle proves little. As of mid-2026, every one of those sentences is defensible. Resist the urge to score the game at halftime.

## "American" libertarianism vs. European liberal democracy

European politics has no direct equivalent. The nearest relatives are **classical-liberal parties** (Germany's FDP, parts of the U.K. Liberal Democrat tradition) — market-friendly and civil-libertarian, but reconciled to substantial welfare states — and **liberal-conservative** blocs that treat the social-insurance state as settled. Europe's anarchist traditions run left, not toward anarcho-capitalism. The deeper difference is structural: EU regulatory frameworks, parliamentary coalitions, and constitutional welfare commitments leave little institutional space for small-state politics, which is why the purer American positions read as exotic there — and why Milei, the first head of state anywhere to claim the label, emerged from Latin America's inflationary politics rather than from Europe. The pattern's exception proves the rule: libertarianism in power so far looks like crisis medicine, adopted where the status quo had already failed catastrophically.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position about libertarianism — whether you identify with the tradition or not — requires:

1. A view on **which stream best fits current conditions** — or what synthesis is achievable.
2. A view on **the strongest critiques** — Cohen on self-ownership, Rawlsians on background justice, communitarians on the unencumbered chooser — and where they bite versus miss.
3. A view on **the evidence**: the policy scoreboard and the Argentina experiment, read without halftime triumphalism in either direction.
4. An honest accounting of **what evidence would change your position**.

The Coach in this app will engage you on each of these. The Mirror will save your declared position; the steel-man drill will push you to construct the strongest version of the views you don't hold.
