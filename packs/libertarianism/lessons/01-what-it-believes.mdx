# What libertarianism actually believes

Start with the thought experiment that has opened a thousand political-philosophy seminars. In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), Robert Nozick asks you to imagine your favorite theory of fair distribution fully realized — perfect equality, or distribution by need, or whatever pattern you endorse. Call it D1. Now suppose Wilt Chamberlain — the most electrifying basketball draw of the era — signs a contract under which every home-game ticket includes an extra twenty-five cents that drops into a box with his name on it. Fans know the deal; they pay cheerfully. A million of them turn out over the season, and Chamberlain ends the year $250,000 richer than everyone else. Call the result D2. Here is Nozick's question: if D1 was just, and every step from D1 to D2 was a free choice by people spending shares you yourself certified as fairly theirs, what exactly is unjust about D2? And if you insist on restoring the pattern, notice what that requires: continuous interference with what free people do with their own holdings — taxing the quarter back out of Chamberlain's box, forbidding what Nozick called "capitalist acts between consenting adults." His section title states the moral: *liberty upsets patterns*.

You do not have to accept the argument — Lesson 3 will give you the strongest replies — but you cannot understand [[ideology:libertarianism]] without feeling its pull. The tradition is built on the intuition the Chamberlain case isolates: that voluntary exchange between rights-respecting adults is presumptively just, and that maintaining any preferred social pattern against it requires ongoing coercion that itself demands justification. Libertarianism is often shelved as "conservative economics" or "Republican Lite." Both labels miss what makes it a distinct tradition — one that follows that single intuition wherever it leads, including places where neither major party will go.

## A core that holds across the family

Libertarians disagree among themselves about plenty (Lesson 2 is entirely about that), but a recognizable core holds across the family.

**Liberty is the political value, not one value among several.** Equality, community, security, and virtue all matter to libertarians as human goods — but they are to be pursued through free choice, not imposed through coercion. The structural commitment is a presumption: individual self-determination is the default, and the burden of proof always sits on whoever proposes to restrict it. Most political traditions ask "what should the state do about X?" Libertarians ask first, "what entitles the state to act on X at all?"

**The non-aggression principle.** Initiating force or fraud against a person or their property is wrong — for individuals, and equally for the state. Indeed many libertarians say *especially* for the state, because state coercion is systematic, legally privileged, and inescapable. On this accounting, drug prohibition, occupational licensing, [[term:conscription]], and (for many) redistributive taxation are not policy choices to be weighed casually; they are standing acts of aggression that require strong justification or repeal. Defensive force is another matter entirely — the principle restricts *initiating* force, not resisting it.

**Self-ownership and property as its extension.** You own yourself: your body, your labor, your time. Property in external things, in the Lockean account, arises when you mix your labor with the unowned world — Locke's *Second Treatise* (1689, §27) argues that the laborer "hath mixed his labour with" the thing and thereby made it his. This single move underwrites both halves of the libertarian agenda: the economic half (your earnings are yours) and the personal half (your body, your medical choices, your consensual relationships are yours too).

**Suspicion of concentrated power, with the state as the paradigm case.** The state is uniquely dangerous because it claims a monopoly on legitimate violence. Concentrated *private* power can be dangerous too — but libertarians argue it usually needs state help to do durable harm: regulatory capture, licensing moats, subsidies, eminent domain. The libertarian remedy is to shrink the prize. A government that cannot dispense favors cannot be captured at scale.

**Voluntary cooperation is the alternative, not atomism.** The caricature has libertarians wanting everyone alone on a homestead. The tradition's actual answer to coordination problems is the dense web of voluntary institutions — markets, contracts, mutual-aid societies, charities, open-source projects, civil society generally. The claim is partly moral (cooperation you chose differs in kind from cooperation extracted at legal gunpoint) and partly empirical (voluntary orders have produced most of what we call prosperity).

**Civil liberties are core, not garnish.** Free speech, due process, privacy, sexual autonomy, religious liberty. The Libertarian Party's very first platform, in 1972, called for repealing all laws creating "crimes without victims," including laws against voluntary sexual relations and drug use — decades before either major party would touch those positions. On surveillance, criminal-justice reform, and drug policy, libertarians routinely line up with progressives against conservatives.

<Checkpoint question="Three things happen in your town: (a) a shop owner refuses to stock a book he finds offensive; (b) the state fines an unlicensed hair braider $2,000 for braiding without 1,500 hours of cosmetology training; (c) activists organize a boycott that bankrupts a bakery. Which does the non-aggression principle condemn?">
  Only **(b)**. The shop owner and the boycotters are making non-violent choices about their own property and patronage — perhaps objectionable, but not aggression. The licensing fine is state-initiated force against a peaceful exchange between willing adults, which is exactly what the NAP targets. Notice the principle cuts at *coercion*, not at outcomes: the bakery in (c) is just as bankrupt, but no one initiated force against it.

## The canon in six moves

The tradition's intellectual spine can be walked in six steps, each adding a distinct kind of argument.

**John Locke (1632–1704)** supplies the rights foundation: natural rights that precede government, property as an extension of labor, and political authority as legitimate only by consent. The *Two Treatises* (1689) is the common ancestor of [[ideology:classical-liberalism]] and libertarianism alike — and of the Declaration of Independence's "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness," which tracks Locke's "life, liberty, and estate."

**John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)** supplies the liberty principle. *On Liberty* (1859) argues that "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." The harm principle remains the standard libertarian test for legitimate state action — and notice what it excludes: protecting adults from themselves, and enforcing the majority's moral tastes.

**Friedrich Hayek (1899–1992)** supplies the epistemic argument. *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) — a wartime warning that central economic planning erodes political liberty — became a mass phenomenon when *Reader's Digest* ran a condensation in April 1945 and the Book-of-the-Month Club distributed over 600,000 copies. But the deeper contribution is a twelve-page article, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (*American Economic Review*, 1945, later named one of the journal's twenty best articles of its first century). The knowledge a modern economy runs on, Hayek argued, exists only as millions of dispersed, local, often tacit fragments that no planning board could ever assemble. Prices solve the problem no committee can: the price system works as "a kind of machinery for registering change, or a system of telecommunications" that tells each actor what they need to know — and nothing more — about events they will never directly observe. Hayek, who called himself an "Old Whig" and pointedly declined the libertarian label, is nonetheless the tradition's indispensable economist.

> **In the primary sources:** Hayek's epitaph for planning ambition comes in his last book, *The Fatal Conceit* (1988): "The curious task of economics is to demonstrate to men how little they really know about what they imagine they can design."

**Milton Friedman (1912–2006)** supplies the policy entrepreneurship. *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962) argued that economic freedom is a precondition of political freedom — "The great advances of civilization, whether in architecture or painting, in science or literature, in industry or agriculture, have never come from centralized government" — and then did something philosophers rarely do: proposed mechanisms. [[issue:school-vouchers]], the negative income tax (ancestor of today's EITC and UBI debates), floating exchange rates, and the all-volunteer military all trace to Friedman, who served on the Gates Commission whose work ended the draft in 1973. He won the Nobel Prize in 1976.

<Checkpoint question="A senator argues: 'Industrial policy will fail not because subsidies are unjust, but because no agency can gather the dispersed, local, constantly changing information that prices already transmit.' Whose argument is she channeling — and is it a moral argument or something else?">
  That is **Hayek's knowledge-problem argument** from "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), and it is *epistemic*, not moral — a claim about what planners *cannot know*, not about rights. Keep it distinct from Nozick's rights-based case (the Chamberlain argument that opened this lesson): Hayek says intervention fails even with the best intentions; Nozick says patterned redistribution wrongs people even if it works. The two pillars are independent — you can accept one and reject the other.
</Checkpoint>

**Robert Nozick (1938–2002) — against John Rawls (1921–2002)** — supplies the philosophical confrontation. Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) had rebuilt liberal egalitarianism: inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged (the "difference principle"). *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) answers with the entitlement theory: a distribution is just if holdings were justly acquired, justly transferred, and rectified where past injustice can be shown — full stop, with no further pattern to satisfy. The book opens with the tradition's most quoted sentence — "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)" — defends a state "limited to the narrow functions of protection against force, theft, fraud, enforcement of contracts, and so on," and won the 1975 National Book Award. Nozick versus Rawls is still the load-bearing argument of every distributive-justice syllabus.

**Murray Rothbard (1926–1995) and James Buchanan (1919–2013)** supply the radical edge and the institutional realism, respectively. Rothbard's *For a New Liberty* (1973) pushes the logic past Nozick: if aggression is wrong, the state — funded by taxation, sustained by monopoly — is wrong at any size, and courts, police, and defense should be supplied by competing private providers. Buchanan, working the opposite temperament, founded public choice economics (*The Calculus of Consent*, 1962, with Gordon Tullock): analyze politicians and bureaucrats as the self-interested actors they are — "politics without romance," as he summarized the program — rather than as benevolent correctors of market failure. He won the Nobel in 1986. Between them they define the tradition's two non-philosophical registers: moral absolutism and institutional skepticism.

Before moving on, make sure the six moves stay attached to their movers — each thinker contributes a *different kind* of argument, and the streams in Lesson 2 divide partly over which arguments they lean on.

**Minarchists** hold that the state is legitimate at minimal scope: rights protection, courts, contract enforcement, national defense, perhaps a few genuine public goods. This is the tradition's philosophical center of gravity — Nozick's position, and roughly the Cato Institute's. **Anarcho-capitalists** hold that even that much is illegitimate, and that every governmental function can be supplied by competing private providers — the Rothbardian line. **Bleeding-heart libertarians** argue that libertarian institutions need moral preconditions — a welfare floor, real equality of opportunity — and that markets are *justified* partly by what they do for the worst-off; John Tomasi's *Free Market Fairness* (2012) is the manifesto, and the Bleeding Heart Libertarians blog (2011–2020) was the movement's seminar room. The streams' histories are tangled: Matt Zwolinski and Tomasi's *The Individualists* (2023) traces the tradition to nineteenth-century radicals — abolitionists and anti-imperialists — before its Cold War turn rightward.

The organized movement is younger than the ideas: the Libertarian Party was founded on December 11, 1971, by David Nolan and a small group meeting in a Colorado Springs living room, and the Cato Institute — named for *Cato's Letters*, the eighteenth-century essays on liberty — followed in 1977.

## What libertarianism is not

> **Common misconception:** "As Jefferson said, 'That government is best which governs least.'" Jefferson never said it — Monticello's researchers list it among his spurious quotations. The motto appears in Thoreau's "Civil Disobedience" (1849), where Thoreau says he heartily accepts it, and Thoreau likely borrowed it from the *United States Magazine and Democratic Review*. If you quote it, quote Thoreau — and note that his essay is a case for conscience against unjust law, not a libertarian tract.

**Not selfishness.** The argument is about the moral difference between chosen and coerced cooperation, plus an empirical bet on which institutions actually produce flourishing. The tradition's own intellectual history runs through anti-slavery and anti-conscription causes, and its modern policy agenda (immigration liberalization, drug-war abolition, criminal-justice reform) is heavily about people other than its median adherent.

**Not Republican economics.** The Republican coalition includes social conservatives and national conservatives who favor tariffs, industrial policy, immigration restriction, and morals legislation — all positions libertarians reject. Where [[term:civil-liberties]] or war powers are at stake, libertarians frequently vote and litigate alongside progressives.

**Not anti-government in the cartoon sense.** Minarchists and classical liberals are emphatically pro-courts, pro-rule-of-law, pro-property registry. The objection is to scope and discretion, not to the existence of legal order. (Anarcho-capitalists do reject the state — but in favor of competing legal institutions, not chaos.)

**Not uniformly opposed to redistribution.** Friedman designed the negative income tax; Hayek endorsed a guaranteed minimum against severe deprivation in *The Road to Serfdom*; bleeding-heart libertarians defend a basic-income floor outright. What the family jointly opposes is the discretionary, paternalistic, bureaucratically intermediated welfare state — not, in every stream, transfers as such.

<Checkpoint question="Your uncle says libertarians are 'just Republicans who smoke weed.' Name two civil-liberties positions and one economic one that break the equation.">
  Civil liberties: libertarians backed repeal of victimless-crime laws (including laws against same-sex relations) starting with the LP's 1972 platform, and oppose mass surveillance — both against the conservative grain. Economics: libertarians oppose tariffs and industrial policy, which much of the Republican coalition now supports. The deeper point: libertarianism is a *principle* (minimize coercion) applied consistently, not a partisan bundle — which is why it keeps cutting across both parties' platforms.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson did not settle

Two arguments are left deliberately open. First, the family's internal fight: whether the state should be small or should not exist, and whether social justice is a libertarian concern or a category error — Lesson 2 lets each stream make its best case against the others. Second, the outside critics: Rawlsians who say the Chamberlain argument ignores background conditions, communitarians who say the unencumbered chooser is a fiction, socialists who say property itself is the coercion. Lesson 3 maps those borders. A defensible position requires hearing all of it first.
