# Federal vs. state: who can do what?

Walk into a licensed cannabis dispensary in Denver or Detroit, buy a product the state has inspected and taxed, and you have just committed a federal felony. Nothing about that sentence is a glitch. As of mid-2026, twenty-four states and the District of Columbia have legalized recreational marijuana under state law, while federal law still classifies it as a Schedule I controlled substance — a spring-2026 rescheduling moved state-regulated *medical* cannabis to Schedule III, but recreational use remains federally prohibited, and both governments are acting within their lawful authority. Two sovereigns, one storefront. That is federalism: not a tidy division of labor but a permanent, structured argument over who governs what. This lesson gives you the architecture — the text, the handful of cases that built the doctrine, and the disputes running as you read.

## Two governments, one citizen

The Constitution's design is asymmetric by intention. The federal government is one of **enumerated powers**: it may do only what the text grants — most of the list sits in Article I, Section 8 (tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, coin money, naturalize citizens, raise armies, declare war), capped by the Necessary and Proper Clause for carrying those powers into execution. The states are governments of **reserved powers**: they inherited general authority to govern — what lawyers call the police power, covering health, safety, morals, and welfare — and kept everything the Constitution didn't take away. The Tenth Amendment states the rule of the whole system in twenty-eight words: "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

In between lies a broad band of **concurrent powers** both levels exercise — taxing, borrowing, spending, building roads, chartering banks, running courts, defining crimes. Conflict inside that band is resolved by Article VI's [[term:supremacy-clause]]: where the federal government acts *within its enumerated powers*, valid federal law preempts contrary state law. The italics carry the whole subject. Supremacy is conditional — it answers "who wins a collision," not "who may act" — which is why nearly every federalism fight is really a fight about whether some federal statute fits inside an enumerated power.

Madison sold this design in [[term:federalist-papers]] No. 45 with a prediction: federal powers would be "few and defined," state powers "numerous and indefinite." Whether that prediction survived the twentieth century is the rest of this lesson.

## McCulloch: the terms of engagement

The first great test came in 1819, and it set the interpretive rules every later fight has been played under. Congress had chartered a national bank — nowhere mentioned in the Constitution — and Maryland taxed its Baltimore branch, aiming to cripple it. *McCulloch v. Maryland* asked two questions. Could Congress charter a bank at all? Yes, held Chief Justice Marshall: the enumerated powers carry **implied powers** with them, and "necessary and proper" means convenient and appropriate, not indispensable — "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end... are constitutional." Could Maryland tax the bank? No — "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and no state may destroy an instrument of the national government, because the federal government, within its sphere, is supreme over the states. Five years later, *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) gave the [[term:commerce-clause]] its first broad reading, defining commerce as all "commercial intercourse" and sinking New York's steamboat monopoly under a federal coasting license. The federal floor was set: powers read generously, supremacy enforced.

<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> A state, unhappy about a new federal facility in its territory, enacts a special surtax that applies only to federal installations, arguing that taxing property within its borders is a core reserved power. How does <em>McCulloch</em> answer?</summary>
<p>The surtax falls. Taxation is indeed a concurrent power — but <em>McCulloch</em> holds that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and a state may not aim that power at the instruments of the federal government, which is supreme within its sphere. The tell is discrimination: Maryland's tax hit only the national bank, just as this surtax hits only federal installations. A general, nondiscriminatory tax would be a different and harder question.</p>

## The commerce clause century

For most of the twentieth century, the commerce power *was* the story of federal growth — the constitutional engine behind the [[term:new-deal]], labor law, civil-rights legislation, and the federal criminal code. Its outer limit was marked in 1942 by a dairy farmer from Montgomery County, Ohio, named Roscoe Filburn. Under New Deal wheat quotas, Filburn's allotment was 11.1 acres; he planted 23, intending the excess mostly to feed his own livestock. The extra 239 bushels never left his farm — yet the penalty (49 cents a bushel, $117.11) was upheld unanimously. *Wickard v. Filburn*'s logic: one farmer's home-grown wheat is trivial, but the **aggregate** effect of every farmer doing likewise substantially affects the national wheat market — and that is enough. For the next half-century, no federal statute failed a commerce-clause challenge.

| Case | Year | What Congress did | Outcome and rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Gibbons v. Ogden* | 1824 | Licensed coastal navigation | Upheld — commerce is "intercourse," not just trade in goods |
| *Wickard v. Filburn* | 1942 | Capped wheat production, even home-consumed | Upheld — aggregation: cumulative effects count |
| *United States v. Lopez* | 1995 | Banned gun possession near schools | Struck — first limit since 1937; non-economic activity can't be aggregated |
| *United States v. Morrison* | 2000 | Federal civil remedy for gender-motivated violence | Struck — violent crime isn't economic activity |
| *Gonzales v. Raich* | 2005 | Banned homegrown medical marijuana | Upheld — Wickard's aggregation survives for economic activity within a comprehensive scheme |

Read the table's rhythm: expansion, expansion, then a brake. In *United States v. Lopez* (1995) the Court struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act — the first time since 1937 it had held that Congress exceeded the commerce power — because possessing a gun near a school is not economic activity, and a Constitution of enumerated powers must have *some* subject Congress cannot reach. *Morrison* (2000) applied the same line to the Violence Against Women Act's civil remedy. Then *Raich* (2005) showed the brake's limits: growing marijuana at home for personal medical use, legal under California law, was still reachable — it is economic activity (production and consumption of a commodity with a national market), and *Wickard* controls. The doctrine as of mid-2026: economic activity aggregates; non-economic activity does not; and in *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) five justices added that Congress may regulate existing economic *activity* but may not use the commerce power to compel commerce — the insurance mandate survived only as a tax.

<details class="checkpoint">
<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> Congress passes a comprehensive federal tomato-market statute with production quotas, and fines a hobbyist who grows backyard tomatoes solely for her own table. Separately, it makes shoplifting from any store a federal crime because retail theft, in aggregate, raises national prices. Which statute is on firmer constitutional ground?</summary>
<p>The <strong>tomato quota</strong>, comfortably. It is <em>Wickard</em> and <em>Raich</em> on the nose: growing a commodity for home consumption is economic activity that, aggregated across thousands of growers, substantially affects a national market Congress is comprehensively regulating. The shoplifting statute looks like <em>Lopez/Morrison</em> bait — garden-variety violent or acquisitive crime has traditionally been the states' police-power domain, and the Court has refused to let "everything affects prices eventually" reasoning erase the enumerated-powers line for local conduct.</p>
</details>

## What Washington cannot make states do

Even where Congress clearly *can* regulate, a separate Tenth Amendment doctrine controls *how*. The **anticommandeering** rule holds that the federal government may not issue direct orders to state governments as governments. *New York v. United States* (1992): Congress cannot force a state legislature to enact a federal program (there, "take title" to radioactive waste). *Printz v. United States* (1997): Congress cannot conscript state executive officers — the Brady Act's command that county sheriffs run federal gun background checks fell. *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018): the rule also bars Congress from *forbidding* states to legislate — the federal ban on state-authorized sports gambling (PASPA) fell, and the sports books now operating in most states are the visible result. The through-line, as Justice Alito put it in *Murphy*: Congress may regulate the people directly, but it may not treat state governments as field offices.

So how does Washington get states to run federal priorities? It buys them. Under the spending power, Congress attaches conditions to money states are free to refuse — highway funds conditional on a 21 drinking age (*South Dakota v. Dole*, 1987). The sums make "free to refuse" complicated: federal grants to state and local governments totaled about $1.1 trillion in fiscal 2023 — roughly 4 percent of GDP — with Medicaid alone over half. *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) finally located a limit: the Affordable Care Act's threat to pull *all* of a state's existing Medicaid funding unless it expanded the program was, in Chief Justice Roberts's phrase, "a gun to the head" — coercion, not inducement — and seven justices made the expansion effectively optional. Where persuasion ends and coercion begins between Dole's 5 percent and NFIB's 10-plus percent of state budgets is still unmarked road.

<details class="checkpoint">
<summary><strong>Checkpoint:</strong> Congress wants every state to adopt a drone-registration law. Bill A orders state legislatures to enact one and directs state police to enforce it. Bill B creates a federal registration system enforced by the FAA. Bill C offers states a 5 percent aviation-grant bonus if they adopt one. Which bills survive?</summary>
<p><strong>B and C.</strong> Bill A is a double anticommandeering violation — ordering a state legislature to legislate (<em>New York</em>, 1992) and conscripting state officers (<em>Printz</em>, 1997). Bill B is ordinary direct federal regulation of private conduct, which commandeering doctrine never touches. Bill C is a conditional grant in <em>Dole</em> territory — modest, germane, and refusable — comfortably short of <em>NFIB</em>'s coercion line. Same policy goal, three constitutional fates: in federalism, the <em>mechanism</em> is everything.</p>
</details>

## The scholar's lens: layer cake, marble cake — and Riker's warning

Generations of students learned **dual federalism**: federal and state governments as separate layers, each supreme in its own sphere — a layer cake. In 1960, political scientist Morton Grodzins looked at how American government actually worked and pronounced the image false. The real system, he wrote in "The Federal System" (in *Goals for Americans*), is a **marble cake**: "an inseparable mingling of differently colored ingredients," with federal, state, and local functions swirled together — the local health inspector enforcing federal standards with state training money. His student Daniel Elazar pushed the point historically: in *The American Partnership* (1962) he showed the mingling was there from the start — land grants, internal improvements, shared institution-building — meaning cooperative federalism wasn't a New Deal corruption of the design but the design's normal operation. Look back at this lesson's grant numbers and you can see the marbling priced out in dollars.

The same decade produced the tradition's darkest assessment. William Riker's *Federalism: Origin, Operation, Significance* (1964) argued that federalism's celebrated "laboratories" had one overwhelming historical product: the shelter of local tyranny — slavery, then [[term:jim-crow]]. His conclusion was designed to sting: "if in the United States one disapproves of racism, one should disapprove of federalism." Riker later softened after the civil-rights revolution, and his critics note that states have also been first movers on abolition, suffrage, and marriage equality. But every honest account of American federalism has to hold both facts — laboratory and shelter — at once.

## Federalism, live from 2026

The architecture above is the operating system of current headlines. Three running case studies, presented without verdicts:

- **Marijuana** is the *Raich* settlement lived out daily: the federal prohibition remains valid law the federal government mostly declines to enforce against state-legal conduct, while states build billion-dollar regulated markets that federal banking and tax law still treat as contraband commerce. The 2026 partial rescheduling narrowed, but did not close, the gap.
- **"Sanctuary" jurisdictions** are anticommandeering in action: under *Printz*, Washington cannot conscript state and local police into federal immigration enforcement, so it has turned to funding conditions instead — and federal courts (*San Francisco v. Trump*, 2025, and successor suits) have invoked the Tenth Amendment and spending-power limits against orders stripping funds from non-cooperating cities. The same doctrine that shields a red state's sheriffs from federal gun mandates shields a blue city's police from federal immigration mandates; anticommandeering has no party.
- **Abortion** became a federalism question again when *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled [[term:roe-v-wade]] and *Casey*, holding that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and that regulation is "returned to the people and their elected representatives." The result, as of mid-2026, is the widest state-by-state divergence on any major policy — near-total bans in some states, broad protections in others, and a new generation of interstate conflicts (travel, telehealth, shield laws) that the courts have only begun to sort.

## What this lesson is not

This lesson built the structure: enumerated versus reserved powers, the commerce arc, anticommandeering, and the spending workaround. It did not referee the normative fight — whether power *should* sit in Washington or the states — on which principled positions exist on every side, and which most people answer, inconveniently, depending on who currently holds which lever. Nor did it cover preemption doctrine's fine grain, the dormant commerce clause, or horizontal federalism (state-versus-state conflict), each a course of its own. And the live examples are moving targets — each will have new chapters by the time you read this. For a video companion, see [Crash Course Government and Politics #4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J0gosGXSgsI) — then test any week's news against the question this lesson trained you to ask: *which government is acting, under which power, and who says so?*
