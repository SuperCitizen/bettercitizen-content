# Federal–state conflict today: preemption, marijuana, sanctuaries, and the AG wars

In 2010, Arizona passed a law whose stated purpose was to help enforce federal immigration law — making it a state crime to violate federal registration rules, criminalizing unauthorized work, letting police arrest people they believed removable. The federal government's response was not gratitude. It sued Arizona, and in *Arizona v. United States* (2012) the Supreme Court struck down three of the law's four challenged provisions — not because Arizona's policy judgment was wrong, but because immigration was Washington's field, and even a state marching in the *same direction* as federal law was trespassing in it. Hold that oddity up against this one: states have legalized a drug federal law flatly prohibits, and no court has struck those laws down. A state can be forbidden to help and free to defy? Both results follow from doctrine you now have most of. This lesson supplies the last piece — preemption — and then walks the three loudest fronts of the current federal–state war: marijuana, sanctuaries, and the state attorneys general.

## Preemption: the Supremacy Clause's fine print

Lesson 1 left the [[term:supremacy-clause]] as a one-liner: valid federal law beats contrary state law. Preemption doctrine is the fine print — the rules courts use to decide what "contrary" means. The categories matter because nearly every modern regulatory fight ends up sorted into one of them.

| Type | The trigger | Classic example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Express** | Congress wrote a preemption clause into the statute | ERISA's sweeping clause displacing state laws that "relate to" employee benefit plans |
| **Field** | Federal regulation so pervasive that Congress left no room for states | Alien registration (*Hines*, 1941; *Arizona* §3, 2012) |
| **Conflict — impossibility** | Complying with both laws is literally impossible | A federal rule requires a drug label that state law forbids |
| **Conflict — obstacle** | State law "stands as an obstacle" to Congress's full purposes (*Hines*) | Arizona's work-crime: Congress chose employer sanctions, not worker prosecutions |

Two background rules shape everything in the table. First, the presumption against preemption: in fields the states have traditionally occupied — health, safety, land use — courts assume Congress did *not* displace state law unless that was its "clear and manifest purpose" (*Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator*, 1947). Second, preemption is statutory interpretation all the way down: the fights are about what Congress meant, which is why identical-sounding cases come out differently depending on a statute's text, structure, and savings clauses. *Arizona* shows the full toolkit in one opinion — §3 (state registration crime) fell to field preemption, §5(C) (work crime) and §6 (warrantless arrests) fell as obstacles to congressional design, while §2(B), the notorious "show me your papers" status-check provision, survived a facial challenge precisely because checking status with the federal government *assists* rather than obstructs the federal scheme.

## Marijuana: a valid law nobody fully enforces

Now the puzzle from the cold open. The Controlled Substances Act of 1970 put marijuana in Schedule I, *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) confirmed Congress had the power to do it, and the Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law supreme. So how do two dozen state recreational markets exist? Walk the sequence:

The sequence shows a standoff with three load-bearing walls. The first is anticommandeering: Washington may enforce its own ban with its own agents, but after *Printz* and *Murphy v. NCAA* it cannot order state police to enforce it or order state legislatures to keep prohibition on the books. The second is arithmetic: the federal government has nowhere near the personnel to police retail drug conduct in fifty states without state help — the DEA is a rounding error next to state and local law enforcement. The third is Congress's own checkbook: the appropriations rider makes federal interference with state *medical* programs not just impolitic but unfunded.

<Checkpoint question="The Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law supreme, and Raich holds the federal marijuana ban valid. Why hasn't any court struck down Colorado-style legalization as preempted?">
  Because preemption displaces state law that *conflicts* with federal law — and a state repealing its own criminal penalties hasn't enacted a conflicting law; it has enacted nothing. Nobody is forced to violate federal law (dual compliance is easy: don't sell marijuana), and forcing states to recriminalize would commandeer their legislatures (*Murphy*). The harder question is the state *licensing and taxing* apparatus — arguably an obstacle to Congress's prohibition — but courts have let it stand, and the federal government has never seriously pressed the theory. Supremacy means Washington wins collisions it chooses to have; it does not mean states must supply the collision.
</Checkpoint>

The price of the standoff lands on the industry: state-legal businesses largely cannot bank normally, face punishing federal tax treatment, and operate one enforcement-policy change away from felony exposure. The 2026 partial rescheduling eased the research and medical side but left the recreational gap — and the [[issue:federal-marijuana-legalization]] debate — exactly where it was: a valid federal law, a visible national market, and a Congress that has chosen, year after year, to resolve nothing.

## Sanctuaries: the same shield, opposite politics

A "sanctuary" jurisdiction is one that limits its own participation in federal immigration enforcement — declining to hold people past their release date on federal detainer requests, restricting information-sharing, keeping local police out of immigration work. Whatever one thinks of the policy, its legal architecture is the marijuana standoff with the parties reversed: the federal government retains full power to enforce immigration law with federal officers; what it cannot do, under *Printz*, is conscript state and local government into the job. So Washington reached for the other lever — money. A 2017 executive order threatened to strip federal funds from [[issue:sanctuary-cities]]; the Ninth Circuit affirmed an injunction against it, and parallel fights over conditions on Byrne JAG law-enforcement grants split the circuits before the issue went dormant — then reignited in 2025, when new executive orders drew new injunctions (*San Francisco v. Trump*, N.D. Cal. 2025) on the same Tenth Amendment and spending-power grounds, now reinforced by *NFIB*'s coercion holding from Lesson 2.

The doctrine's party-blindness is the point to carry away. The same years produced "Second Amendment sanctuary" states and counties limiting cooperation with federal gun-law enforcement — legally identical posture, mirror-image politics. The line between the lawful and unlawful versions is the one *Murphy* drew: a state may *withhold its own hands* from federal enforcement, but it may not purport to *nullify* federal law. Missouri found the line the hard way: its Second Amendment Preservation Act, which declared various federal gun laws "invalid to this state" and penalized local police for helping enforce them, was struck down in federal court (2023–24) — not because states must help Washington, but because declaring federal law void is the one move the Supremacy Clause has always categorically forbidden.

## The AG wars: states as plaintiffs

The newest front is fought in courtrooms by an officer most voters could not name twenty years ago. The modern era of state-attorney-general litigation against the federal government runs through *Massachusetts v. EPA* (2007), where the Supreme Court granted states "special solicitude" in [[term:standing]] analysis — easing their path into federal court to challenge federal action — and ordered EPA to reconsider regulating greenhouse gases. Political scientist Paul Nolette, whose *Federalism on Trial* (2015) documented the transformation, showed AGs evolving from consumer-protection lawyers into coordinated national-policy litigators, organized by party, recruiting test plaintiffs and shopping favorable districts. The pattern since is almost mechanical: Republican AG coalitions sued the Obama administration (blocking the DAPA immigration program in 2015–16, when an equally divided Supreme Court left the injunction standing) and the Biden administration (*West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022, which curbed EPA's power-plant authority); Democratic coalitions sued both Trump administrations — by the second administration's first anniversary, trackers counted roughly seventy multistate suits, challenging everything from funding freezes to tariffs to birthright-citizenship orders.

Whether this is federalism working or failing is genuinely contested. The defense: AG suits are checks-and-balances by other means — states are the only plaintiffs with the resources, standing advantages, and political mandate to test executive action at speed, and they win often enough to prove the suits aren't frivolous. The critique: policy by [[term:injunction]] makes a single district judge a national veto point and converts legal doctrine into a partisan relay race — and the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. CASA* (2025), curbing "universal" injunctions, raised rather than settled the stakes, since states argue their injuries still require relief of statewide or national breadth. Either way, the AG wars complete an arc this pack has traced from the start: a federal system designed to *divide* power now also *organizes the opposition* — whichever party loses the White House immediately re-bases in the statehouses.

<Checkpoint question="Your state's AG wants to block a new federal regulation. Name two litigation advantages the AG holds over a private business making the identical legal argument — and one 2025 decision that complicated the second advantage.">
  First, standing: under *Massachusetts v. EPA*, states get "special solicitude" — injuries to their fiscs, their territory, or their quasi-sovereign interests open courthouse doors that stay closed to many private plaintiffs. Second, remedial breadth: states have routinely won injunctions protecting the whole state — or the nation — rather than one company. *Trump v. CASA* (2025) complicated the second by curbing universal injunctions, leaving open how much breadth a *state's* complete relief actually requires — which is now itself a battleground.
</Checkpoint>

## Who should win? Three honest answers

Everything above described the war. Whether centralization or divergence *should* prevail is a real argument with serious people on every side — and your answer to it deserves stress-testing against all three of these.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Nationalist">
    National problems do not respect state lines, and neither should their solutions. A patchwork of fifty rules is a tax on everything that moves — firms re-engineer products state by state, citizens' rights expand and contract at borders, and states race to the bottom whenever competition rewards laxity. The Founders knew this failure mode intimately: the Articles of Confederation died of it, and Madison arrived at Philadelphia proposing a federal veto over state laws. The Supremacy Clause and a vigorous preemption doctrine are not affronts to the design — they are the design, the cure for the "mutability" and parochialism of state legislation that Madison catalogued in his 1787 memorandum "Vices of the Political System of the United States" and returned to in Federalist No. 62. Where Congress has spoken, courts should enforce uniformity without embarrassment; where states obstruct — whether a licensing scheme for a federally prohibited drug or a refusal to share law-enforcement information — the national interest, expressed by the only legislature every American elects, should prevail.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="States'-rights constitutionalist">
    The federal government is one of enumerated powers, and every doctrine in this lesson exists because Washington strains against that fact. Brandeis's image still carries the argument: a "single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country" (*New State Ice Co. v. Liebmann*, 1932, dissenting). Divergence is not a bug to be preempted but the system's method — marijuana policy, where states moved decades ahead of a frozen Congress, is the laboratory functioning exactly as advertised. Anticommandeering and the presumption against preemption are not technicalities; they preserve the accountability the Tenth Amendment promises, so voters know which government to blame. And the doctrine's party-blindness is its vindication: the same shield protects sanctuary cities and Second Amendment counties. A Constitution that centralized every contested question would not end the conflicts — it would simply make every election a winner-take-all national emergency.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Functionalist">
    Both flags miss the question, which is never "who is sovereign?" but "which level does this job best?" Match the scale of the problem to the scale of the government: interstate externalities (air pollution, immigration, drug trafficking's national market) need national floors; locally variable preferences (land use, policing styles, marijuana retail) belong with governments close enough to be watched. By that metric the current battle lines look scrambled — full federal marijuana prohibition over a conduct market that is overwhelmingly local, but also fifty-state product patchworks where uniformity is plainly cheaper. The doctrines this lesson taught earn their keep not as sovereignty totems but as good institutional design: anticommandeering keeps accountability legible, preemption analysis asks what Congress's scheme actually needs, and the spending power lets the level with the money hire the level with the administrative capacity. The marble cake of Lesson 1 was never a failure of the system. It is the system — the task is keeping each swirl assigned, visibly, to the government that can be fired for botching it.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## What this lesson is not

This lesson mapped the live fronts — preemption's categories, the marijuana standoff, the sanctuary fights, and the AG wars — and steel-manned the three standing answers to who should win them. It did not predict outcomes: the broader marijuana rescheduling, the post-*CASA* shape of state-sought injunctions, and the next round of funding-condition litigation were all in motion as this was written, and each will have moved by the time you read it. It also left whole theaters uncovered — interstate compacts, the dormant commerce clause, and state constitutional law as an independent rights frontier. The discipline to keep: when the next federal–state collision hits the headlines, run the questions in order. Which government is acting? Under which power? Through which mechanism — command, condition, or preemption? And who, exactly, says so?
