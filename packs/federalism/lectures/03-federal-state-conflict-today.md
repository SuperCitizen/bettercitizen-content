In 2010, Arizona passed a law to help Washington.

[beat]

Its stated purpose was to help enforce federal immigration law. It made it a state crime to violate federal registration rules. It criminalized unauthorized work. It let police arrest people they believed to be removable.

The federal government's response was not gratitude. It sued Arizona. And in 2012, in Arizona v. United States, the Supreme Court struck down three of the law's four challenged provisions.

Not because Arizona's policy judgment was wrong. Because immigration was Washington's field. Even a state marching in the same direction as federal law was trespassing in it.

[pause]

Now hold that oddity up against another. States have legalized a drug that federal law flatly prohibits. And no court has struck those laws down.

So a state can be forbidden to help, [beat] and free to defy?

[pause]

Both results follow from doctrine, and you already have most of it. Last time, the two sovereigns were bargaining. This lecture is about what happens when they stop bargaining and start suing.

By the end, you will be able to explain both halves of that puzzle. And you will hold the strongest version of three different answers to who should win.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the missing piece of doctrine: preemption.

Second, two standoffs that turn out to be one: marijuana, and sanctuary jurisdictions.

Third, the newest front: states as plaintiffs, led by their attorneys general.

And last, the question under all of it. Who should win? Three honest answers.

## Preemption

In the first lecture, we left the Supremacy Clause as one line: valid federal law beats contrary state law.

Preemption is the fine print under that line. It is the set of rules courts use to decide what "contrary" means. And it comes in three kinds.

The first is express preemption. Congress writes a preemption clause into the statute itself.

[beat]

The second is field preemption. Here, federal regulation is so pervasive that Congress has left no room for the states. The classic field is the registration of aliens.

[beat]

The third is conflict preemption, and it has two forms. One is impossibility: complying with both laws is literally impossible. A federal rule requires a drug label that state law forbids. The other is obstacle: the state law, in the Court's phrase, "stands as an obstacle" to the full purposes of Congress.

> Preemption comes in three kinds: express, field, and conflict.

Say them once more, in plain words. Congress said so. Congress filled the field. Or the two laws conflict: either nobody can obey both, or the state law stands in Congress's way.

Two background rules shape all of it.

One is a presumption against preemption. In fields the states have traditionally occupied, such as health, safety and land use, courts assume that Congress did not displace state law, unless that was its "clear and manifest purpose."

The other rule: preemption is statutory interpretation, all the way down. That is why cases that sound identical come out differently, depending on a statute's text and structure.

> Every preemption fight is a fight about what Congress meant.

Now go back to Arizona, and watch the full toolkit work in one opinion.

The state registration crime fell to field preemption. Registration was Washington's field.

The work crime fell as an obstacle. Congress had chosen sanctions on employers. It had not chosen prosecutions of workers.

And the fourth provision? [beat] That was the status check, the notorious one known as "show me your papers." It survived a facial challenge. Why? Because checking a person's status with the federal government assists the federal scheme. It does not obstruct it.

So that is the first half of our puzzle. Arizona was not forbidden to agree with Washington. It was forbidden to legislate in Washington's field, and to stand in the way of the design Congress chose.

[pause]

Let's test the categories. A federal aviation statute sets noise limits for drones. It is silent about state law. Your state then enacts a stricter limit, and a manufacturer sues, claiming preemption.

The manufacturer's weakest argument is impossibility. Why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Because impossibility requires that obeying both sovereigns at once cannot be done. [beat] And here it can. A drone that meets the stricter state limit automatically satisfies the federal one. Comply with the state, and you have complied with both.

The manufacturer's real arguments are the other two. Field: is the federal regulation pervasive? Or obstacle: did Congress intend a uniform ceiling, and not a floor?

## Marijuana

Now the second half of the puzzle: the drug that federal law prohibits, and states license.

In 1970, the Controlled Substances Act put marijuana in Schedule One. In 2005, in Gonzales v. Raich, the Supreme Court confirmed that Congress had the power to do it. And the Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law supreme.

So how do two dozen state recreational markets exist? Walk the sequence with me.

In 2012, Colorado, and the state of Washington, legalized recreational use. Look at exactly what they did. They did not purport to block federal enforcement. They withdrew their own prohibitions, and licensed what federal law still forbids.

[beat]

In 2013, the Justice Department issued the Cole Memorandum. It would not prioritize enforcement against state-legal conduct that avoided a list of harms. Discretion became the policy, in practice.

In 2014, Congress made its own move. It began barring the Justice Department from spending funds to prevent states from implementing medical-marijuana laws. That rider has been renewed in spending bills since.

In 2018, Attorney General Sessions withdrew that memorandum. Prosecutions of state-legal markets did not meaningfully resume. The truce held without the paper.

And in 2026 came a partial rescheduling. Marijuana under qualifying state medical licenses moved to Schedule Three. Recreational marijuana stayed in Schedule One.

[pause]

That sequence shows a standoff, and it rests on three load-bearing walls.

The first wall is anticommandeering. Washington may enforce its own ban with its own agents. But it cannot order state police to enforce it. And it cannot order state legislatures to keep prohibition on the books.

The second wall is arithmetic. The federal government has nowhere near the personnel to police retail drug conduct in fifty states without state help. Next to state and local law enforcement, the DEA is a rounding error.

The third wall is Congress's own checkbook. That spending rider makes federal interference with state medical programs not just impolitic, but unfunded.

Anticommandeering. Arithmetic. The checkbook.

[pause]

Now the hard question, and you have what you need to answer it. The Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law supreme. Raich holds the federal marijuana ban valid. So why hasn't any court struck down Colorado-style legalization as preempted? Think it through.

[long pause]

Because preemption displaces state law that conflicts with federal law. And a state that repeals its own criminal penalties has not enacted a conflicting law. [beat] It has enacted nothing.

[pause]

Nobody is forced to violate federal law. Obeying both is easy: don't sell marijuana. And forcing a state to recriminalize would commandeer its legislature.

There is a harder version of the question. The state apparatus of licensing and taxing is, arguably, an obstacle to the prohibition Congress enacted. But courts have let it stand, and the federal government has never seriously pressed the theory.

> Washington wins the collisions it chooses to have. States need not supply the collision.

Put the two halves of the puzzle side by side. Arizona enacted a law, inside a federal field. Colorado withdrew a law, and left federal law nothing to collide with.

[pause]

What remains is a valid federal law, a visible national market, and a Congress that has chosen, year after year, to resolve nothing.

## Sanctuaries

Now change the subject, and keep the structure.

A sanctuary jurisdiction is one that limits its own participation in federal immigration enforcement. It declines to hold people past their release date at federal request. It restricts the sharing of information. It keeps local police out of immigration work.

Whatever one thinks of the policy, its legal architecture is the marijuana standoff, [beat] with the parties reversed.

Before I say how, try it yourself. A few minutes ago, I named three walls that hold up the marijuana standoff. Which was the first?

[long pause]

Anticommandeering. And it is the same wall here. The federal government retains full power to enforce immigration law with federal officers. What it cannot do is conscript state and local government into the job.

So Washington reached for the other lever: [beat] money. In 2017, an executive order threatened to strip federal funds from sanctuary cities, and it drew an injunction. In 2025, new orders drew new injunctions, on the same grounds: the Tenth Amendment, and the limits of the spending power. Those grounds are now reinforced by the coercion holding from last time.

Carry this point away: the doctrine is blind to party. The same years produced Second Amendment sanctuaries: states and counties limiting their cooperation with the enforcement of federal gun laws. The legal posture is identical. The politics are a mirror image. The same shield protects sanctuary cities and Second Amendment counties.

But the shield has an edge, and one state found it the hard way.

Missouri passed a Second Amendment Preservation Act. It declared various federal gun laws "invalid to this state," and it penalized local police for helping to enforce them. It was struck down in federal court.

[beat]

Not because states must help Washington. Because declaring federal law void is the one move the Supremacy Clause has always, categorically, forbidden.

> A state may withhold its own hands. It may not nullify federal law.

## States as plaintiffs

The newest front is fought in courtrooms, by an officer most voters could not have named twenty years ago: [beat] the state attorney general.

The modern era runs through a 2007 case, Massachusetts v. EPA. There the Supreme Court granted states what it called "special solicitude" in the analysis of standing. In plainer words, it eased their path into federal court to challenge federal action.

The political scientist Paul Nolette documented the transformation that followed. Attorneys general evolved from consumer-protection lawyers into coordinated litigators of national policy, organized by party.

The pattern since is almost mechanical. Republican coalitions sued the Obama administration, and then the Biden administration. Democratic coalitions sued both Trump administrations. By the first anniversary of the second one, trackers counted roughly seventy multistate suits.

[pause]

So a federal system designed to divide power now also organizes the opposition.

> Whichever party loses the White House regroups in the statehouses.

Is that federalism working, or federalism failing? It is contested, and each side gets its best case.

The defense argues that these suits are checks and balances by other means. States are the only plaintiffs with the resources, the standing advantages and the political mandate to test executive action at speed. And it points to the record: states win often enough to prove the suits are not frivolous.

[beat]

The critique argues that policy by injunction makes a single district judge a national veto point. And it points to what the suits have become: legal doctrine, converted into a partisan relay race.

[pause]

The ground is still moving. In 2025, in Trump v. CASA, the Supreme Court curbed what are called universal injunctions. That raised the stakes. It did not settle them. States argue that their injuries still require relief of statewide, or national, breadth.

## Who should win?

Everything so far described the war. Whether centralization or divergence should prevail is a real argument, with serious people on every side. Here are three answers, each in its own strongest terms.

First, the nationalist. The nationalist wants to protect uniformity: one rule for a national problem. The nationalist argues that national problems do not respect state lines, and neither should their solutions. A patchwork of fifty rules is a tax on everything that moves. Citizens' rights expand and contract at borders. And states race to the bottom whenever competition rewards laxity.

The nationalist points to the founding. The Articles of Confederation died of this failure, and Madison arrived at Philadelphia proposing a federal veto over state laws. So the Supremacy Clause and a vigorous preemption doctrine are not affronts to the design. They are the design. Where states obstruct, the national interest should prevail, as expressed by the only legislature every American elects.

> The nationalist case: national problems do not respect state lines.

Second, the states'-rights constitutionalist. The constitutionalist wants to protect the limits on federal power, and the accountability the Tenth Amendment promises. The constitutionalist argues that the federal government is one of enumerated powers, and that divergence is not a bug to be preempted. It is the system's method. A Constitution that centralized every contested question would not end the conflicts. It would make every election a winner-take-all national emergency.

The constitutionalist points to an image from Brandeis, in a 1932 dissent. A "single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory." The constitutionalist points to marijuana policy, where states moved decades ahead of a frozen Congress: the laboratory, functioning exactly as advertised. And to the doctrine's blindness to party, as its vindication.

> The states'-rights case: divergence is the system's method, not a bug.

Third, the functionalist. The functionalist wants to protect good institutional design, and argues that both flags miss the question. The question is never who is sovereign. It is which level does this job best. Match the scale of the government to the scale of the problem. Harms that spill across state lines, such as air pollution, need national floors. Preferences that vary locally, such as land use, belong with governments near enough to be watched.

The functionalist points to how scrambled the current battle lines look by that measure. A full federal prohibition of marijuana, over a market that is overwhelmingly local. But also fifty-state patchworks for products, where uniformity is plainly cheaper. On this view, the marble cake was never a failure of the system. It is the system. The task is keeping each swirl assigned, visibly, to the government that can be fired for botching it.

> The functionalist case: ask which level does the job best.

Three answers. This lecture does not choose among them.

Now pick the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

If that advocate would recognize the case you just made, you have done the work. Your own answer deserves stress-testing against all three.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Preemption comes in three kinds: express, field, and conflict. Conflict means impossibility, or obstacle. And every preemption fight is a fight about what Congress meant.

[beat]

Two. State marijuana legalization stands because a state that repeals its own ban has enacted nothing to preempt. Washington wins the collisions it chooses to have. States need not supply the collision.

[beat]

Three. Sanctuary cities and Second Amendment counties stand behind the same shield, and it is anticommandeering. A state may withhold its own hands. It may not nullify federal law.

[beat]

Four. State attorneys general now carry these fights into court. Whichever party loses the White House regroups in the statehouses. Whether that is federalism working or failing is contested.

[beat]

And five. Three honest answers to who should win. The nationalist: national problems do not respect state lines. The states'-rights constitutionalist: divergence is the system's method. The functionalist: ask which level does the job best.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has predicted no outcomes. The broader rescheduling of marijuana, the shape of the injunctions states may seek, and the next round of fights over funding conditions were all in motion as this was written. Each will have moved by the time you hear it.

Whole theaters remain uncovered, too. Interstate compacts. The dormant commerce clause. And state constitutional law, as an independent frontier for rights. That is where the argument goes from here.

So go back to where we started. Arizona, forbidden to help. Colorado, free to defy. [beat] You can now say why.

[pause]

And when the next federal and state collision hits the headlines, run the questions in order. Which government is acting? Under which power? Through which mechanism: command, condition, or preemption? [beat] And who, exactly, says so?
