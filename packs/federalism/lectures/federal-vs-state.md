Walk into a licensed cannabis dispensary in Denver, or in Detroit. Buy a product that the state has inspected, and the state has taxed.

[beat]

You have just committed a federal crime.

[pause]

Nothing about that is a glitch. As of mid-2026, twenty-four states and the District of Columbia have legalized recreational marijuana under state law. Federal law still classifies it as a Schedule One controlled substance. A rescheduling in the spring of 2026 moved state-regulated medical cannabis to Schedule Three. Recreational use remains federally prohibited.

And both governments are acting within their lawful authority.

Two sovereigns, one storefront.

That is federalism. It is not a tidy division of labor. It is a permanent, structured argument over who governs what.

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to take a scene like that one and ask the right question of it. Which government is acting, under which power, and who says so?

We will take it in four steps.

First, the map: three kinds of power, and the rule for when they collide.

Second, two cases that stretched the federal side of the map: a bank in Baltimore, and a wheat field in Ohio.

Third, what Washington cannot make a state do, and how it gets its way all the same.

And last, two pictures of the whole system, and one warning.

## Three kinds of power

Start with the design. The Constitution treats the two levels of government differently, and it does so on purpose.

The federal government may do only what the text grants. Most of the list sits in one place: Article One, section eight. Tax. Borrow. Regulate commerce among the states and with foreign nations. Coin money. Naturalize citizens. Raise armies. Declare war. And then one clause more, the Necessary and Proper Clause, for carrying those powers into execution.

Powers granted in the text have a name: enumerated powers. The federal government works from a list.

The states are built the other way around. Nobody handed them a list. They inherited a general authority to govern, over health, safety, morals and welfare. Lawyers call it the police power. And the states kept everything the Constitution did not take away. [beat] Those are the reserved powers.

The Tenth Amendment states the rule of the whole system in twenty-eight words. Listen for the word "reserved."

"The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, [beat] are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

[pause]

Reserved to the States, or to the people.

> The federal list is closed. The state list is open-ended.

Between the two lies a broad band of powers that both levels exercise. Both tax. Both borrow and spend. Both build roads, charter banks, run courts and define crimes. These are the concurrent powers.

So, three kinds. Enumerated: the federal list. Reserved: everything the states kept. Concurrent: what both hold at once.

And when the two levels collide inside that shared band? Article Six answers, in the Supremacy Clause. Where the federal government acts within its enumerated powers, valid federal law overrides contrary state law. The lawyer's word for that is preemption.

Listen to the condition again. Within its enumerated powers. [beat] Supremacy is conditional.

> Supremacy says who wins a collision. It does not say who may act.

Put another way, the Supremacy Clause never tells you whether Washington held the power in the first place. That is why nearly every federalism fight is really a fight about one thing: does this federal statute fit inside an enumerated power?

Let's make sure those three categories are yours. Here are three everyday powers. Sort each one: enumerated, reserved, or concurrent.

Declaring war. [beat] Licensing doctors and lawyers. [beat] Taxing income.

Take a moment.

[long pause]

Declaring war is enumerated. It is on the federal list, and the Constitution expressly denies it to the states.

Licensing doctors and lawyers is reserved. That is classic police power: the regulation of health and safety.

And taxing income is concurrent. Both levels do it, every April.

Madison sold this design with a prediction. In Federalist 45, he wrote that federal powers would be "few and defined," and state powers "numerous and indefinite."

Few and defined. [beat] Keep that prediction in mind, and watch what happens to it.

## The bank

The first great test came in 1819. It set the rules that every later fight has been played under.

Congress had chartered a national bank. A bank is mentioned nowhere in the Constitution. And Maryland taxed the bank's Baltimore branch, aiming to cripple it.

The case was McCulloch v. Maryland, and it asked two questions.

Question one. Could Congress charter a bank at all?

[beat]

Yes, held Chief Justice Marshall. The enumerated powers carry implied powers with them. And "necessary and proper" means convenient and appropriate. It does not mean indispensable.

[beat]

In plainer words: if the goal is within the Constitution's scope, Congress may choose fitting means of reaching it, even means the text never names.

Question two. Could Maryland tax the bank?

[beat]

No. Marshall's reason fits in one line. "The power to tax involves the power to destroy."

[pause]

No state may destroy an instrument of the national government. Within its sphere, the federal government is supreme over the states.

Five years later, in 1824, a case called Gibbons v. Ogden gave the commerce power its first broad reading. A federal license sank New York's steamboat monopoly. Commerce, the Court said, is not just trade in goods. It is all "commercial intercourse."

So by 1824, the terms were fixed.

> The federal floor was set: powers interpreted generously, supremacy enforced.

## The wheat field

That was the bank. Now the wheat field.

For most of the twentieth century, the commerce power was the story of federal growth. It was the constitutional engine behind the New Deal, labor law, civil-rights legislation, and the federal criminal code.

Its outer limit was marked in 1942, by a dairy farmer from Ohio named Roscoe Filburn.

Under the New Deal's wheat quotas, Filburn was allotted about 11 acres. He planted 23. He meant the excess mostly to feed his own livestock. And the extra wheat, 239 bushels of it, [beat] never left his farm.

The penalty came anyway: about 117 dollars. And the Supreme Court upheld it, unanimously, in a case called Wickard v. Filburn.

How can wheat that never leaves a farm be commerce among the states?

[beat]

Here is the Court's logic. One farmer's home-grown wheat is trivial. But add up every farmer doing likewise, and the effect on the national wheat market is substantial. And that is enough.

[pause]

That logic has a name: aggregation. The Court does not ask what one person's wheat does to the market. It asks what happens when you add everyone together.

For the next half-century, no federal statute failed a commerce-clause challenge. [beat] Hold that against Madison's prediction: few and defined.

[pause]

Then, in 1995, came the brake.

The case was United States v. Lopez. The Court struck down a federal law, the Gun-Free School Zones Act. It was the first time since 1937 that the Court had held that Congress exceeded the commerce power.

The reasoning had two parts. Possessing a gun near a school is not economic activity. And a Constitution of enumerated powers must have some subject that Congress cannot reach.

Ten years later, the Court showed the limits of that brake. The case was Gonzales v. Raich, in 2005. Growing marijuana at home, for personal medical use, was legal under California law. [beat] Congress could still reach it.

Why? Because growing a crop is economic activity: the production and consumption of a commodity with a national market. And for economic activity, the wheat case controls.

> Economic activity adds up. Non-economic activity does not.

That is the doctrine as of mid-2026. And it explains the storefront where we began. The federal prohibition on marijuana remains valid law, on the logic of a farmer's wheat.

Let's test that line. Congress passes two laws.

The first is a comprehensive statute for the national tomato market, with production quotas. Under it, a hobbyist is fined for growing backyard tomatoes, solely for her own table.

The second makes shoplifting, from any store, a federal crime. The theory: retail theft, in aggregate, raises national prices.

Which law stands on firmer constitutional ground? Think it through.

[long pause]

The tomato quota, comfortably. It is the wheat case and the marijuana case over again. Growing a commodity for your own table is economic activity. Add it up across thousands of growers, and it substantially affects a national market that Congress is regulating comprehensively.

The shoplifting law looks like Lopez. Ordinary crime has traditionally been the states' domain, under the police power. And the Court has refused to let the reasoning that "everything affects prices eventually" erase the line around enumerated powers.

## What Washington cannot command

So the federal list stretches a long way. Before we go on, look back.

At the start, I gave you three kinds of power. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Enumerated, reserved, and concurrent. Everything since has been about the first kind: how far the federal list can stretch. Now we cross to the states' side of the line.

Here is the rule. Even where Congress clearly can regulate, a separate doctrine controls how. It comes from the Tenth Amendment, and it is called the anticommandeering rule. The federal government may not issue direct orders to state governments, as governments.

Three cases built it.

One. New York v. United States, in 1992. Congress cannot force a state legislature to enact a federal program.

Two. Printz v. United States, in 1997. Congress cannot conscript a state's executive officers. A federal law, the Brady Act, commanded county sheriffs to run federal gun background checks. That command fell.

Three. Murphy v. NCAA, in 2018. Congress cannot forbid a state to legislate, either. A federal ban on state-authorized sports gambling fell, and the sports books now operating in most states are the visible result.

Justice Alito, in Murphy, gave the thread that runs through all three. "Conspicuously absent from the list of powers given to Congress," he wrote, "is the power to issue direct orders to the governments of the States."

> Washington may regulate the people. It may not treat the states as field offices.

So how does Washington get states to carry out federal priorities?

[beat]

It buys them.

Under the spending power, Congress attaches conditions to money that states are free to refuse. The classic case is South Dakota v. Dole, from 1987: federal highway funds, on the condition of a drinking age of 21.

> What Washington cannot command, it buys.

But look at the sums, because the sums make "free to refuse" complicated. In fiscal 2023, federal grants to state and local governments came to about 1.1 trillion dollars. Medicaid alone was over half.

In 2012, the Court finally located a limit. The case was NFIB v. Sebelius. The Affordable Care Act threatened to pull all of a state's existing Medicaid funding, unless the state expanded the program. Chief Justice Roberts had a phrase for that threat: "a gun to the head." It was coercion, not inducement. And seven justices made the expansion, in effect, optional.

[pause]

Where persuasion ends and coercion begins, between those two cases, is still unmarked road. The next lecture walks it.

Now put both halves to work. Congress wants every state to adopt a drone-registration law, and it is weighing three bills.

Bill A orders state legislatures to enact one, and directs state police to enforce it.

Bill B creates a federal registration system, enforced by the FAA.

Bill C offers states a 5 percent bonus on their aviation grants, if they adopt one.

An order. A federal program. An offer. [beat] Which bills survive? Take your time.

[pause 5s]

B and C.

Bill A breaks the anticommandeering rule twice. It orders a state legislature to legislate, and it conscripts state officers.

Bill B is ordinary federal regulation of private conduct. Commandeering doctrine never touches that.

Bill C is a conditional grant, in Dole territory: modest, refusable, and comfortably short of the coercion line.

Same policy goal. Three constitutional fates.

> In federalism, the mechanism is everything.

## Two cakes and a warning

One step left: how scholars picture the whole system.

Generations of students learned a picture called dual federalism. Federal and state governments sit in separate layers, each supreme in its own sphere. A layer cake.

In 1960, the political scientist Morton Grodzins looked at how American government actually worked, and pronounced that image false. The real system, he wrote, is a marble cake: "an inseparable mingling of differently colored ingredients."

Federal, state and local functions, swirled together. Picture the local health inspector, enforcing federal standards, with state training money.

The same decade produced the darkest assessment in this tradition. The states are celebrated as laboratories. In 1964, William Riker argued that those laboratories had one overwhelming historical product: the shelter of local tyranny. [beat] Slavery, and then Jim Crow.

His conclusion was designed to sting. "If in the United States one disapproves of racism, one should disapprove of federalism."

[pause]

Riker later softened, after the civil-rights revolution. And his critics note that states have also been first movers: on abolition, on suffrage, on marriage equality.

> Every honest account of American federalism holds both facts: laboratory and shelter.

All of this is the operating system of current headlines. You have met one running case, marijuana. Here are two more, without verdicts.

Take sanctuary jurisdictions. Under Printz, Washington cannot conscript state and local police into federal immigration enforcement. So it has turned to funding conditions. And federal courts have invoked the Tenth Amendment, and the limits on the spending power, against orders stripping funds from cities that do not cooperate.

Notice who else that doctrine protects. The same rule shields a blue city's police from federal immigration mandates, and a red state's sheriffs from federal gun mandates. [beat] Anticommandeering has no party.

And take abortion. In 2022, a case called Dobbs overruled Roe v. Wade, and held that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion. Regulation, the Court said, is "returned to the people and their elected representatives." As of mid-2026, the result is the widest state-by-state divergence on any major policy.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Three kinds of power. Enumerated: the federal list, and it is closed. Reserved: what the states kept, and it is open-ended. Concurrent: what both levels hold.

[beat]

Two. Supremacy is conditional. It says who wins a collision. It does not say who may act. Valid federal law wins only when it fits inside an enumerated power.

[beat]

Three. The federal floor was set early: powers interpreted generously, supremacy enforced. The power to tax involves the power to destroy. And under the commerce power, economic activity adds up. Non-economic activity does not.

[beat]

Four. Washington may not treat the states as field offices, and anticommandeering has no party. What it cannot command, it buys, up to the point where an offer becomes a gun to the head. In federalism, the mechanism is everything.

[beat]

And five. The system is a marble cake, not a layer cake. And an honest account of it holds both facts: laboratory and shelter.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. We built the structure. We did not referee the larger fight: whether power should sit in Washington, or in the states. Principled positions exist on every side. And most people answer, inconveniently, depending on who currently holds which lever.

The running cases are moving targets, too. Each will have new chapters by the time you hear this.

The next lecture follows the money. How does Washington buy what it cannot command? And where does buying become coercion?

For now, go back to the storefront. Two sovereigns, one storefront. The next time a headline sets a state against Washington, ask what you practiced here. Which government is acting? Under which power? [beat] And who says so?
