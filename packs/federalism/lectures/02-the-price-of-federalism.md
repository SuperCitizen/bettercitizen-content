The drinking age in every state is 21. And there is no federal law that says it must be.

[beat]

Search the U.S. Code, and the closest thing you will find is an instruction to the Secretary of Transportation. Withhold a percentage of federal highway money from any state that lets people under 21 buy alcohol.

South Dakota sold low-alcohol beer to nineteen-year-olds, and wanted to keep doing so. So it sued. Congress, it argued, was regulating drinking ages by laundering a command through a checkbook.

It lost, seven to two, in a 1987 case called South Dakota v. Dole. Then it did the arithmetic. And it raised its drinking age, like everyone else.

[pause]

Nobody was ordered to do anything.

> Washington most often governs the states by contract, not by command.

Last time, we saw why. A direct command to a state is unconstitutional. So Washington pays. This lecture follows the money.

By the end, you will be able to take any federal offer to the states and say three things about it. What kind of deal it is. Which strings are legal. And where the strings become a leash.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the money itself: where federal grants came from, how large they are, and the forms they take.

Second, the law of strings: a four-part test from the drinking-age case.

Third, what happens when Congress skips the checkbook: mandates without money.

And last, the day the Supreme Court said a deal had gone too far.

## The money

Federal money for state purposes is older than the income tax.

In 1862, the Morrill Act granted federal land to the states to endow colleges. Those are the land-grant universities, and they still carry the name.

In 1916, the Federal-Aid Road Act set the modern template. Cash instead of land. A matching requirement, so that states had skin in the game. And conditions: to qualify, each state had to create a highway department.

[beat]

Cash, a match, and conditions. The New Deal scaled that template up. The Great Society multiplied it, and in 1965 added Medicaid, the program that now dominates the whole system.

These transfers have a name: grants-in-aid. A grant-in-aid is money from the national treasury, to a state or local government, for a defined purpose. Today the Congressional Research Service counts them at over 1 trillion dollars a year. Medicaid alone is the majority of it.

[pause]

What does that much money buy? Dependence is a loaded word, so let's measure it.

In fiscal 2024, the fifty states together drew about 34 percent of their revenue from Washington. [beat] About a third.

The most reliant state was Louisiana, where the federal government supplied more than half. The least reliant was North Dakota. Even there, nearly a quarter of state revenue came from Washington.

[pause]

So the floor is high everywhere. And here is the constitutional point: every condition Congress attaches rides on money of this magnitude.

"States are free to refuse federal funds." That is a true sentence. [beat] It is a true sentence about sums no state can actually refuse.

[pause]

Hold that thought. The Supreme Court eventually had to.

Now the forms, because the politics is in the differences among them.

A categorical grant is money for a narrowly defined purpose: highway construction, special education, childhood vaccination. It comes hedged with conditions on how the money may be spent, who must be served, and what must be reported. Categorical grants are the dominant form by far. And Congress prefers them for a plain reason: they let national majorities buy specific outcomes.

A block grant runs the other way. It consolidates funding into a broad purpose, such as community development, and hands the states wide discretion within it. Governors of both parties love block grants. Appropriators of both parties distrust them.

> Categorical grants: narrow purpose, tight strings. Block grants: broad purpose, wide discretion.

There was once a third form, with no strings at all. It was called general revenue sharing: Washington, mailing checks. It existed from 1972 to 1986, and it died without a constituency in Congress. [beat] That is a lesson in itself. Politicians do not enjoy raising taxes so that other politicians can take credit for spending them.

Block grants carry a catch, and one program shows it. In 1996, welfare reform converted an older program into a block grant called TANF. The states won flexibility. Congress won a fixed price: [beat] 16.5 billion dollars a year.

That figure has never been adjusted for inflation. So its real value has eroded, by roughly half.

[pause]

Flexibility was real. So was the slow defunding that nobody ever had to vote for.

And the flexibility drifts, too. Congress, unable to resist, re-attaches strings over time.

Let's test the two forms. A federal program sends every state a fixed annual sum, set by formula, to spend on "services that strengthen families." The states choose the mix. They file an annual report. And that is all.

What kind of grant is that? Take a moment.

[long pause]

A block grant. A broad purpose, formula funding, and wide state discretion: that is the signature of a block grant. A bare report does not make it categorical. And it is not revenue sharing, because the money is still tied to a purpose.

## The law of strings

That is the money. Now the law of strings.

Here is the constitutional question under all of it. Congress cannot order the states to do something. That was the anticommandeering rule, from last time. So how can Congress pay them to do the very same thing?

The drinking-age case gave the standard answer. Chief Justice Rehnquist upheld the condition, and organized the doctrine into four requirements. Count them with me.

One. The spending must serve the general welfare. Courts apply that test with near-total deference.

[beat]

Two. The condition must be unambiguous. States must know the deal when they take the money, because the legitimacy of the whole doctrine rests on the metaphor of a contract.

[beat]

Three. The condition must be related to the federal interest in the program. Drunk nineteen-year-olds driving across state lines relate to highway funds. This requirement is called germaneness, and it has teeth mostly in theory.

[beat]

Four. The condition may not induce a state to violate some independent constitutional bar. Congress cannot pay the states to establish a church.

General welfare. Unambiguous. Related. No independent bar.

[pause]

Then came a caveat. It would wait twenty-five years for a client.

The Court reached back to a case from 1937. A financial inducement, it allowed, might in some circumstances be so coercive that it passes the point at which "pressure turns into compulsion."

[pause]

Pressure turns into compulsion.

In Dole itself, the Court found no such problem. The threat was 5 percent of highway funds, and the Court called that "relatively mild encouragement."

One justice pressed on the soft spot. Justice O'Connor, in dissent, posed a dilemma. If germaneness means anything, then a drinking age is a condition on how states regulate their citizens. It is not a condition on how highway money is spent. And if germaneness means nothing, then the spending power can buy what the commerce power cannot reach.

[pause]

Her dissent is still the best short statement of the worry: that conditional spending is commandeering, with extra steps.

Now run the test yourself. Congress offers the states a 4 percent bonus on federal transit grants, if they lower their default urban speed limits to 20 miles an hour.

Does the condition survive? And which of the four requirements gives a challenger the best argument? Take your time.

[pause 5s]

It almost certainly survives.

[beat]

General welfare: easily, given the deference. Unambiguous: yes, if the statute states the deal clearly. No independent constitutional bar stands in the way. And a 4 percent bonus, on one grant program, is nowhere near coercion.

The challenger's best argument is germaneness. But street safety relates to transportation spending at least as closely as drinking ages related to highway funds. So the precedent forecloses it.

> Under Dole, modest and refusable conditions win, even on matters Congress could not command.

## Mandates without money

So far, Washington has been paying. Sometimes Congress skips the checkbook, and legislates costs onto the states.

Two examples. The Americans with Disabilities Act obliges state and local governments, like private employers, to make their facilities accessible, at their own expense. And the REAL ID Act of 2005 set federal standards for driver's licenses. No state was ordered to comply. [beat] But a noncompliant license would stop working at airport security.

Politics has one name for all of this: the unfunded mandate. It is worth being precise where the politics is not.

A direct requirement that states bear costs is constitutional, when Congress regulates the states the same way it regulates everyone else. A generally applicable wage law is the example. What the anticommandeering rule bars is narrower: conscripting states to govern third parties. It does not bar subjecting the states themselves to law.

And a condition on a grant is something else again.

> A condition on a grant is not a mandate, however mandatory it feels.

Governors and mayors revolted. In 1995, Congress responded with the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act. Notice what kind of law it is. It is a transparency statute. [beat] It is not a prohibition.

Here is what it does. The Congressional Budget Office must estimate the cost of any mandate on state and local governments above a threshold: about 103 million dollars, by 2025. And any member may raise a point of order against the bill.

That is all. And its exclusions are where the action went. Conditions of federal aid, civil-rights requirements, and the enforcement of constitutional rights are all outside its scope.

> The Unfunded Mandates Reform Act requires a cost estimate. It bans nothing.

The deepest grievance is one that law never touches: the underfunded promise. Congress passed what became the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act. In it, Congress authorized federal grants of up to 40 percent of the average per-pupil cost of educating children with disabilities.

Appropriations have never come near it. In recent years, the federal contribution has run at roughly a third of the promised share. States and school districts carry the rest, of a requirement they cannot decline.

Let's sort three federal moves. All three involve the same new safety codes for prisons.

In the first, a statute requires every state-run prison to meet the codes, with no money attached.

In the second, the codes are required only of prisons that accept federal corrections grants.

In the third, the codes are imposed on private prison companies.

Which one is the unfunded mandate, in the strict sense? Think it through.

[long pause]

The first. It is a direct federal requirement that states bear costs. It is lawful if Congress has an enumerated power behind it, and is regulating the states' own operations as it regulates others'. And it is exactly what the cost estimates of the 1995 act were built for.

The second is a Dole condition. Legally it is not a mandate at all, however coercive it feels, and the 1995 act expressly excludes it.

The third is ordinary regulation of private parties. It raises no federalism question whatsoever.

The political phrase gets applied to all three. The law treats them completely differently.

## The coercion line

Everything so far assumed that the state could walk away from the deal. In 2012, the Supreme Court confronted a deal designed so that no state could.

The Affordable Care Act expanded eligibility for Medicaid, with Washington paying the overwhelming share of the expansion's costs. And it provided that a state refusing the expansion could lose all of its existing Medicaid funding.

Not just the new money. [beat] All of it.

Before the ruling, reach back. In the drinking-age case, the Court left a caveat on the shelf. A financial inducement might pass a certain point. Can you still say what happens at that point?

[long pause]

Pressure turns into compulsion.

That caveat had waited twenty-five years for a client. Now it had one. The case was NFIB v. Sebelius, and the Court made the comparison with numbers.

In Dole, South Dakota stood to lose less than half of one percent of its budget. Here, Medicaid's federal funds amounted to more than 10 percent of most states' entire budgets.

Chief Justice Roberts called the threat "a gun to the head." [beat] He called it "economic dragooning that leaves the States with no real option but to acquiesce." And seven justices agreed that the condition crossed the line, from pressure into compulsion.

[pause]

The remedy rewrote the deal, and did not kill it. A state could take the expansion money or leave it. Either way, it kept its existing Medicaid.

What followed is an experiment in what "optional" means at scale. As of mid-2026, forty-one states, counting the District of Columbia, have adopted the expansion. [beat] Ten have not.

[pause]

And notice how little of the map the Court drew. It located one point. More than 10 percent of a state's budget, attached to an existing program, is too much. Then it declined to draw the rest of the line.

> The Court marked one point on the coercion line, and left the rest blank.

Every aggressive funding condition since has been litigated in that blank space.

## Peterson's ledger

One more idea, because it explains the pattern under this whole lecture. It comes from political science, and it belongs to Paul Peterson, in a 1995 book called The Price of Federalism.

Peterson sorted everything governments do into two functions.

The first is developmental: roads, schools, public safety. Developmental policy makes a place more attractive. States and localities do it well, because competition for residents and businesses rewards them for it.

The second is redistributive: welfare, and health care for the poor. At the state level, competition does the opposite. A state that is generous alone fears attracting the needy and repelling the taxed. So competition pushes every state to do less than its own voters might prefer.

And that explains who pays for what.

> Washington finances redistribution. The states deliver it.

Medicaid. TANF. Disability education. The fights over them are fights over which level absorbs the costs of redistribution.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Washington most often governs the states by contract, not by command. The contracts are grants-in-aid: over a trillion dollars a year, and about a third of what the states take in. Washington finances redistribution. The states deliver it.

[beat]

Two. Grants come in two main forms. Categorical grants: narrow purpose, tight strings, and Congress prefers them. Block grants: broad purpose, wide discretion, and a fixed sum that inflation quietly shrinks.

[beat]

Three. The Dole test has four requirements: general welfare, an unambiguous condition, a related condition, and no independent constitutional bar. Under Dole, modest and refusable conditions win.

[beat]

Four. A condition on a grant is not a mandate, however mandatory it feels. The true unfunded mandate is a direct requirement that states bear costs. And the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act requires a cost estimate. It bans nothing.

[beat]

And five. There is a coercion line, where pressure turns into compulsion. The Court has marked one point on it: a gun to the head, at more than 10 percent of a state's budget. The rest is blank.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled, because no court has. Where, between half a percent and 10 percent, does compulsion begin? Are conditions on new money ever coercive? And is the whole edifice of conditional spending what Justice O'Connor warned about: commandeering, on layaway?

We also have not asked what happens when the two sovereigns stop bargaining, and start suing each other. That open warfare is the next lecture: preemption, marijuana, sanctuary jurisdictions, and the state attorneys general.

For now, go back to the drinking age. Twenty-one, in every state, and no command anywhere. [beat] Only a price. And the price was enough.
