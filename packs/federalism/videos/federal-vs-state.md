## Two sovereigns, one storefront

Walk into a licensed cannabis dispensary in Denver. Buy a product the state has inspected and taxed. You have just committed a federal crime.

- Legal under state law
- A federal crime

That is not a glitch. As of mid-2026, twenty-four states and the District of Columbia have legalized recreational marijuana, and federal law still prohibits it. Both governments are acting within their lawful authority.

> Federalism is a permanent, structured argument over who governs what.

## Three kinds of power

The federal government has enumerated powers. It may do only what the Constitution's text grants, such as regulating interstate commerce, coining money and declaring war.

- Enumerated: the federal list

The states have reserved powers. They kept everything the Constitution did not take away, including health, safety and education. The Tenth Amendment states the rule: powers not delegated to the United States, nor prohibited to the states, are reserved to the states, or to the people.

- Reserved: the states' open-ended list

In between are concurrent powers that both levels use, such as taxing and building roads.

- Concurrent: held by both

## Who wins a collision

When the two collide, the Supremacy Clause says valid federal law wins. But only where the federal government acts within its enumerated powers. So nearly every federalism fight is really a fight about whether some federal law fits inside an enumerated power.

- Supremacy Clause: conditional

> Supremacy answers who wins a collision, not who may act.

## What Washington cannot make states do

Even where Congress can regulate, it may not issue direct orders to state governments. That is the anticommandeering rule. In Printz v. United States, in 1997, a federal command that county sheriffs run gun background checks fell.

- Anticommandeering: no orders to states
- Printz, 1997

So Washington buys what it cannot command. It attaches conditions to money that states are free to refuse, such as highway funds tied to a drinking age of 21.

- Conditions on money states may refuse

> Washington may not treat the states as its field offices.

## Not a tidy division

How far the federal list reaches is the story of the power to regulate commerce: expansion, expansion, then a brake. And where persuading states with money becomes coercing them is still unmarked road.

- Commerce power: expansion, then a brake
- Persuasion, or coercion?

The lesson walks the cases, from a national bank in 1819 to one farmer's wheat, has everyday powers to sort into the three kinds, and shows federalism as both laboratory and shelter. It does not referee where power should sit.
