# How war remade America

The previous lesson followed war as a narrative through American history. This lesson is thematic: it asks *how* war changed the country's institutions, economy, and ideas about citizenship. The recurring lesson is that wars do not end when the fighting stops — they leave durable marks on government, society, and memory.

## Bigger government and new taxes

War has been the single greatest driver of federal growth. To finance the Civil War, Congress passed the first federal income tax in 1861 and created the office of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue. The modern income tax followed the Sixteenth Amendment (1913) and expanded dramatically during World War II, when payroll withholding was introduced in 1943 and the number of Americans paying income tax rose from a few million to more than 40 million. Wars also enlarged the bureaucracy: the draft, war production boards, price controls, and rationing all extended federal authority into daily life. Much of this apparatus shrank after each war but rarely returned to its prewar size.

## Industry and technology

Mobilization repeatedly accelerated American industry and invention. The Civil War spurred railroads and mass production of standardized goods. World War II turned the United States into what President Roosevelt called the "arsenal of democracy," and government-funded research produced advances in radar, aviation, synthetic materials, and — through the Manhattan Project — nuclear weapons. The Cold War sustained federal research spending that helped create computing, the interstate highway system (justified partly on defense grounds), satellites, and the network that became the internet.

## Civil rights: advanced and limited

War expanded opportunities for marginalized Americans, often unevenly. Roughly 180,000 Black soldiers served in the Union Army during the Civil War, strengthening the case for emancipation and citizenship. In both World Wars, women entered industrial and military jobs in large numbers, though many were pushed out afterward. The G.I. Bill of 1944 funded college and home loans for millions of veterans, fueling the postwar middle class — but local administration and discriminatory lending meant Black veterans often received far fewer benefits.

A landmark step came in 1948, when President Truman ordered the integration of the military:

> "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the President that there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion, or national origin."

Historians widely credit wartime service and military desegregation with helping to build momentum for the broader civil rights movement, while also noting the limits and delays in how these gains reached civilian life.

## The national-security state and the "military-industrial complex"

After 1945 the United States did not demobilize as it had after earlier wars. The National Security Act of 1947 created a permanent defense and intelligence establishment, and Cold War budgets sustained a large standing military in peacetime. In his 1961 farewell address, President Eisenhower — himself a five-star general — warned about the consequences:

> "In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex. The potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist."

Scholars such as Michael Sherry and Andrew Bacevich describe how this permanent war footing reshaped politics, the economy, and culture. The *degree* to which the military-industrial complex distorts American policy is a matter of ongoing debate among historians and political scientists, not a settled conclusion.

## Citizenship and memory

War also shaped who counts as a full citizen and how the nation remembers itself. Military service has repeatedly been tied to claims for fuller citizenship, from Black Union soldiers to immigrants who served for a path to naturalization. Wars produced enduring institutions of memory — national cemeteries, Memorial Day and Veterans Day, war memorials on the National Mall — and recurring debates over how conflicts should be commemorated and interpreted. These debates, like the meaning of the Civil War or the Vietnam War, remain genuinely contested in public life.

## Conclusion

Taken together, these themes show that war did not merely punctuate American history; it built much of the modern American state and reshaped the rights and obligations of citizenship. Understanding the United States requires understanding how deeply war is woven into its institutions and its self-image.
