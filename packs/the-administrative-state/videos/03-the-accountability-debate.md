## The headless fourth branch

The most famous insult ever aimed at the administrative state came from inside the house. In 1937, Franklin Roosevelt's own committee, tasked with making the New Deal government work, described the independent commissions as a headless fourth branch of the government. The question it put on the table ninety years ago is still the question.

- 1937: the Brownlow Committee

> To whom should the rulemakers answer?

## To expertise

The expertise institutionalist argues that modern problems are technical, continuous and fast, and that only standing bodies of professionals can meet them. James Landis made the case in 1938: insulation is the point. This position points to a 1984 article on oversight. Congress does not patrol like police. It relies on fire alarms, interest groups and constituents who sound off when an agency misbehaves. Silence means no alarm has rung.

- Landis, 1938: insulation is the point
- Fire alarms, not police patrols

> The institutionalist: not unaccountable, but differently accountable.

## To the Constitution

The constitutionalist critic argues that binding rules made outside the legislature are extralegal power, however elaborate the procedure. Philip Hamburger made the case in 2014. This position points to Article Two: the executive power is vested in a President, so officers who wield it must be removable by him. Its two strands pull in different directions. One wants delegated power more tightly controlled by the President. The other wants less power delegated at all.

- Hamburger, 2014: extralegal power
- Two strands: presidential control, less delegation

> The constitutionalist critic: process is not pedigree.

## To Congress

The democratic-accountability reformer argues that delegation is a transfer of blame: a legislator votes for clean air, and lets the agency set the painful particulars. David Schoenbrod made the case in 1993. This position points to the REINS Act, proposed in every Congress since 2009 and not law. No major rule could take effect unless both chambers approved it. Silence would kill the rule, not bless it.

- Schoenbrod, 1993: a transfer of blame
- REINS Act: silence kills a major rule

> The reformer: fewer rules with owners, not more rules with alibis.

## What no opinion can settle

Every camp agrees the administrative state must answer to the people. They disagree about which intermediary counts as the people's voice: profession, President, or Congress. The lesson gives each position in full, with the evidence on what politicizing the civil service costs, and what that evidence cannot tell you.
