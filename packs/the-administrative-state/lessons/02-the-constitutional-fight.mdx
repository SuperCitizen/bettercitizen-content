# The constitutional fight: delegation, deference, and removal

On the morning of May 27, 1935 — "Black Monday," New Dealers called it — the Supreme Court handed down two unanimous decisions within minutes of each other, and between them built the constitutional architecture this lesson is about. In *Schechter Poultry*, the Court struck down the National Industrial Recovery Act's "codes of fair competition" because Congress had handed its legislative power to the executive with essentially no instructions. In *Humphrey's Executor*, it held that President Roosevelt could *not* fire a Federal Trade Commissioner over policy disagreement, because Congress may insulate expert, multimember commissions from presidential control. Read together, the two cases drew the deal that governed for ninety years: Congress may delegate broadly (after *Schechter*, nondelegation went dormant), and in exchange may park some of that delegated power in agencies the President does not fully command. As this lesson is written, in mid-June 2026, both halves of that deal are on the operating table at once — one held open by *Loper Bright* and the major questions cases, the other awaiting the Court's decision in *Trump v. Slaughter*, argued in December 2025, on whether *Humphrey's Executor* should be overruled outright. The *Three Branches* pack mentions this terrain in a sentence; here we walk it case by case, on three fronts: can Congress give the power away, who says what the statutes mean, and who controls the people who wield it.

## Front one: can Congress give legislative power away?

Article I vests "All legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress, and the **nondelegation doctrine** holds that Congress cannot hand that power to anyone else. The operative test is older than the [[term:new-deal]]: in *J.W. Hampton* (1928), Chief Justice Taft wrote that a delegation stands so long as Congress supplies "an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." The bar that phrase sets has proven almost unclearable from the government's perspective — by which I mean, almost no delegation has ever flunked it. The two exceptions came in a single year, 1935: *Panama Refining* (the "hot oil" case) and *Schechter*, both aimed at the NIRA, a statute so standardless that Justice Cardozo — the Court's most committed defender of the New Deal — called it "delegation running riot." Since then: nothing. In *Whitman v. American Trucking* (2001), Justice Scalia, no friend of broad government, upheld the Clean Air Act's instruction to set air standards "requisite to protect the public health," observing that the Court had "found the requisite 'intelligible principle' lacking in only two statutes" in its entire history and that it had "almost never felt qualified to second-guess Congress regarding the permissible degree of policy judgment" left to agencies.

The dormancy was supposed to end in *Gundy v. United States* (2019). The Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act let the Attorney General decide whether registration requirements applied to offenders convicted *before* the Act — arguably a naked grant of legislative choice to a prosecutor. A four-justice plurality, per Justice Kagan, read the statute narrowly and upheld it, warning that "if SORNA's delegation is unconstitutional, then most of Government is unconstitutional." Justice Gorsuch's dissent, joined by Roberts and Thomas, opened with the counter-creed: "The Constitution promises that only the people's elected representatives may adopt new federal laws restricting liberty." Kavanaugh, not yet seated for argument, later signaled sympathy for Gorsuch's framework. Court-watchers spent five years expecting the revival; the test arrived in **FCC v. Consumers' Research (2025)**, a challenge to the universal-service fund, where the FCC sets the "contribution factor" telecom carriers pay — a power to raise billions, guided by statutory aspirations like "just, reasonable, and affordable." Decided June 27, 2025: the Court upheld the scheme, **6–3**, in an opinion by Justice Kagan holding that Congress had "sufficiently guided and constrained" the agency's discretion, that there is no stricter nondelegation rule for revenue-raising statutes, and that the private role of the fund's administrator was advisory only. Gorsuch dissented, joined by Thomas and Alito. The scoreboard as of mid-2026, then: the intelligible-principle test survives, the revival has three declared votes, and the doctrine's real work — this is the scholars' consensus and the dissenters' complaint — is done elsewhere, by the interpretive doctrines of front two.

## Front two: who says what an ambiguous statute means?

When Congress writes "requisite to protect the public health," someone must decide what counts. For forty years the answer was **Chevron deference**. *Chevron U.S.A. v. NRDC* (1984) — which, in a detail too perfect for fiction, upheld the *deregulatory* "bubble" policy of Reagan's EPA under Administrator Anne Gorsuch, whose son would help bury the doctrine four decades later — established a two-step framework: if Congress has "directly spoken to the precise question," its intent controls; if the statute is silent or ambiguous, courts must accept any "permissible construction of the statute" the agency offers, even one the judges would not have chosen. The theory: ambiguity is an implicit delegation, agencies have expertise and political accountability that courts lack, and uniform national administration beats circuit-by-circuit judicial improvisation. *Chevron* became the most-cited case in administrative law, applied in thousands of decisions.

*Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) ended it. The plaintiffs were herring fishermen ordered by a rule — resting on statutory silence — to pay the salaries of the federal observers riding their boats. Chief Justice Roberts, for a 6–3 majority, grounded the overruling in the APA itself, whose §706 directs that "the reviewing court" shall "decide all relevant questions of law." The holding, in the opinion's own words: **"Chevron is overruled. Courts must exercise their independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority, as the APA requires."** Precision matters about what that sentence did and did not do. Courts may still give an agency's interpretation *respect* proportional to its persuasiveness — the older *Skidmore* (1944) posture — and statutes that expressly delegate discretion ("as the Secretary deems appropriate") still confer it; the courts' job there is policing the delegation's "outer statutory boundaries." And under [[term:stare-decisis]], *Loper Bright* expressly declined to "call into question prior cases that relied on the Chevron framework" — the forty years of specific holdings stand, even as their method is repudiated. What changed is the default: ambiguity is no longer agency space. It is judicial space.

> **Common misconception:** "Loper Bright means agencies can no longer regulate." It means agencies no longer win *because the statute is unclear*. An agency acting within a clear grant, or an express delegation, regulates exactly as before — and its factual and policy judgments still get only State Farm reasonableness review, not de novo second-guessing. The transfer of power effected by Loper Bright runs from agencies to *judges*, not from agencies to nobody.

## The major questions doctrine: the clear-statement hammer

Between *Gundy*'s dormant doctrine and *Loper Bright*'s live one sits the tool doing the most visible work: the **major questions doctrine**. In *West Virginia v. EPA* (2022), the Court confronted the Clean Power Plan, which read §111(d) of the Clean Air Act — a provision historically used for measures at individual plants — to authorize restructuring the nation's electricity generation away from coal. Chief Justice Roberts, for the 6–3 majority, named explicitly a practice visible in earlier cases (the FDA's tobacco claim, the CDC's eviction moratorium, OSHA's vaccine-or-test mandate): in "extraordinary cases," where "the history and the breadth of the authority that the agency has asserted, and the economic and political significance of that assertion," give a court "reason to hesitate," the agency must point to **"clear congressional authorization"** — ordinary ambiguity will not do. The Clean Power Plan failed that test: "A decision of such magnitude and consequence rests with Congress itself, or an agency acting pursuant to a clear delegation from that representative body." A year later, *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023) applied the doctrine to the Education Secretary's cancellation of roughly $430 billion in student loans under the HEROES Act's power to "waive or modify" loan provisions in emergencies — "staggering by any measure," wrote Roberts, and not clearly authorized.

What kind of rule is this? The justices themselves disagree. Justice Gorsuch's concurrences cast it as a substantive clear-statement rule *enforcing* Article I — nondelegation by interpretive means. Justice Barrett, concurring in *Nebraska*, offered a thinner account: just common-sense context, the way no reasonable reader thinks a babysitter authorized to "make sure the kids have fun" may take them on an overnight trip. The difference matters: on Gorsuch's account the doctrine should grow; on Barrett's it polices only true outliers. Either way, its practical effect pairs with *Loper Bright* into a one-two: novel, economy-scale agency assertions need clear text (major questions), and the courts, not the agencies, decide what the text means (*Loper Bright*).

## Front three: who controls the people who hold the power?

The third front is personnel. Article II vests "the executive Power" in the President and commands that he "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" — which, the Court has long inferred, requires power to remove subordinates. *Myers v. United States* (1926), written by Chief Justice (and former President) Taft, struck down a statute requiring Senate consent to fire postmasters: removal is the President's alone. *Humphrey's Executor* (1935) then carved the great exception — FTC commissioners, performing "quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial" duties as "a body of experts," could be protected by a for-cause standard ("inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office"); the FTC, Justice Sutherland wrote, "cannot in any proper sense be characterized as an arm or an eye of the executive." On that exception the entire architecture of independent agencies — the Fed, the SEC, the FCC, the NLRB — was built. *Morrison v. Olson* (1988) stretched the exception to cover even a prosecutor, over Justice Scalia's now-canonical solo dissent ("this wolf comes as a wolf") insisting Article II grants the President *all* executive power.

Then the wolf started winning. *Free Enterprise Fund* (2010): two stacked layers of for-cause protection are unconstitutional. *Seila Law* (2020): the CFPB's single director must be removable at will — and Chief Justice Roberts recharacterized *Humphrey's Executor* as a narrow exception for "multimember expert agencies that do not wield substantial executive power," a description, scholars instantly noted, that fits almost no modern agency, including the 1935 FTC itself. *Collins v. Yellen* (2021) extended *Seila* to the FHFA. The 2025 collision was therefore engineered, not accidental: President Trump removed, without cause, Democratic members of the NLRB, MSPB, FTC, and other commissions, in open defiance of the statutes and in explicit reliance on the *Seila* line. In **Trump v. Wilcox** (May 22, 2025), the Court stayed lower-court orders reinstating NLRB member Gwynne Wilcox and MSPB member Cathy Harris — reasoning that the government was "likely to show" such officials "exercise considerable executive power" — while carving out the institution markets cared about: the Federal Reserve, it assured, "is a uniquely structured, quasi-private entity that follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States." Justice Kagan's dissent called the carve-out a tell: a "bespoke" exception for the one agency whose independence the markets would punish the Court for ending.

Here is the precise state of the law as of mid-June 2026 — write it carefully, because it will change within weeks. ***Humphrey's Executor* has not been overruled.** But on September 22, 2025, the Court granted certiorari before judgment in **Trump v. Slaughter** — the suit of fired FTC commissioner Rebecca Slaughter, whom the district court had ordered reinstated — and directed briefing on whether the FTC's removal protections violate the [[term:separation-of-powers]] "and, if so, whether *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* should be overruled." Argument was heard December 8, 2025; most observers read the majority as ready to overrule or drastically narrow the [[term:precedent]]; the decision is due by the end of the term, expected late June 2026. Meanwhile the interim orders have settled the practical question in the President's favor: every contested removal has been allowed to stand pending decision, so for fifteen months the independent agencies have operated, functionally, as if *Humphrey's Executor* were already gone. Whichever way *Slaughter* comes down, remember the adjacent fronts: the Appointments Clause requires that "Officers of the United States" be properly appointed — *Lucia v. SEC* (2018) held SEC administrative law judges are such officers — and *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) moved a slice of agency enforcement from in-house tribunals to Article III juries. Control of the [[term:administrative-state]] is being re-litigated joint by joint.

## What this lesson does not settle

Three things, in ascending order of consequence. First, the doctrine in motion: *Trump v. Slaughter* will land weeks after this lesson's mid-June 2026 vantage point, and you should read the opinion itself rather than trust any summary written before it — note whether the Court overrules *Humphrey's Executor*, merely narrows it to its facts, and what it says about the Federal Reserve carve-out, because that footnote will be the next decade's litigation. Second, the empirical question the cases keep asserting answers to: whether judges deciding statutory meaning de novo produces *better* or merely *different* regulation than expert agencies enjoying deference — political scientists are only beginning to measure post-*Loper Bright* outcomes. Third, the deepest question, which doctrine cannot answer because it is about what we want from government: whether the 1935 settlement — broad delegation to partially insulated experts — was a betrayal of the Constitution's design or its intelligent adaptation to industrial modernity. That is an argument among Landis, Hamburger, and the democratic reformers, and it is Lesson 3.
