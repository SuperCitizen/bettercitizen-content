# The accountability debate: experts, the Constitution, and the people

The most famous insult ever aimed at the administrative state came from inside the house. In 1937, Franklin Roosevelt's own Committee on Administrative Management — the Brownlow Committee, staffed by the country's leading public-administration scholars and tasked with making the New Deal government *work* — described the independent regulatory commissions as "a headless 'fourth branch' of the Government, a haphazard deposit of irresponsible agencies and uncoordinated powers." Not a senator on a stump. Not a litigant's brief. The architect's own engineers, reporting that part of the building stood outside the Constitution's three-branch floor plan and answered to no one in particular. Hold that fact close: the accountability anxiety did not arrive from outside the administrative project in 2016, or 1980. It is original to the project, and the three positions this lesson steel-mans — expertise institutionalist, constitutionalist critic, democratic reformer — are three answers to the question FDR's committee put on the table ninety years ago: *to whom should the rulemakers answer?* Lessons 1 and 2 gave you the machinery and the case law. This one gives you the argument, which the case law is now deciding at speed.

## The terms of the fight

Be precise about what is and is not contested. Nobody here disputes that a modern state needs administration — the sharpest critics concede Congress cannot set particulate standards by floor vote, and the strongest defenders concede that unelected officials wielding binding power need *some* accountability circuit. The fight is over which circuit bears the load: accountability to **expertise and procedure** (insulate professionals, bind them with process — the APA settlement), to the **President** (elections control the executive, so the executive must control the agencies), or to **Congress** (the delegating branch should vote, on the record, for the rules that bind). Each circuit has a scholarship, a line of cases from Lesson 2, and a live 2025–26 policy fight — and a story about why the other two fail. That symmetry is why the debate does not resolve.

## Accountability to expertise: the Landis settlement

The classic statement belongs to James Landis — FDR's securities regulator, dean of Harvard Law School before he turned forty — whose 1938 Storrs Lectures, published as *The Administrative Process*, remain the most confident defense ever written. Landis embraced exactly the feature critics call the defect: the agency *combines* legislative, executive, and judicial functions on purpose, because the [[term:separation-of-powers]], whatever its virtues for high politics, is a terrible way to run a railroad. The administrative process, he argued, was "our generation's answer" to legislatures that lack time and knowledge and courts that lack initiative and continuity; a commission that writes rules, monitors markets daily, and adjudicates violations compounds expertise no committee or court can match. Insulation from at-will removal is not a bug but the point — it buys time horizons longer than a news cycle and judgment less purchasable by the regulated.

The institutionalist position has since acquired two reinforcements Landis lacked. First, a procedural one: the APA of 1946 and the hard-look review you met in Lesson 1 mean the modern agency is anything but unbound — it legislates inside a cage of notice, comment, reason-giving, and [[term:judicial-review]] that Congress itself never faces. Adrian Vermeule's review of Philip Hamburger's indictment (next section) carried the thesis in its one-word title — "No" — and pressed precisely this point: the critique attacks a lawless caricature, not the procedure-saturated administrative law the United States actually has. Second, an empirical one: the political-science literature on oversight. In a canonical 1984 article, Mathew McCubbins and Thomas Schwartz observed that Congress rationally declines "police patrol" oversight (continuous, centralized monitoring) in favor of "fire alarms" — decentralized tripwires of interest groups, constituents, and procedural rights who sound off when an agency misbehaves, triggering hearings, appropriations riders, and CRA resolutions. Apparent congressional passivity, on this account, is not abdication; it is an efficient design in which silence means no alarm has rung.

  The fire-alarm rebuttal: patrols are expensive and mostly find nothing, so Congress outsources monitoring — to regulated firms, NGOs, and affected constituents armed with comment rights, petition rights, and lawsuits — and acts when alarms ring. No hearing may simply mean no alarm. The two stories come apart on testable ground: when alarms DO ring (mass comments, GAO findings, court losses), does Congress respond with hearings, riders, or CRA action? If yes, the system is working as designed; if alarms ring into silence — or if the only "alarms" Congress hears come from one side's donors — the senator's abdication story regains force.

## Accountability to the Constitution: the indictment

The deepest modern attack is Philip Hamburger's *Is Administrative Law Unlawful?* (2014), and its force comes from its historical frame. The English constitutional settlement of the seventeenth century, Hamburger argues, was forged *against* exactly this: the Crown's claim to bind subjects through prerogative instruments — proclamations, the Star Chamber's decrees — outside acts of Parliament and judgments of the law courts. Administrative power is that "extralegal" power returned in modern dress: binding rules made outside the legislature, adjudication outside the courts, and a body of "administrative law" that functions as the new prerogative's apologetics. On this view the administrative state is not a policy choice to debate but a constitutional regression to be undone — and the judicial campaign you traced in Lesson 2 (nondelegation's three votes, *Loper Bright*, the removal cases) is the undoing in progress.

Alongside Hamburger's Article I/Article III indictment runs the **unitary executive** strand, associated above all with Steven Calabresi (with Christopher Yoo, *The Unitary Executive*, 2008): Article II vests *all* executive power in one President, so every officer exercising it must answer to him — removal protections are unconstitutional whether the official is "expert" or not. This is the scholarship that traveled from Scalia's lone *Morrison* dissent to *Seila Law* to the *Wilcox* stay to the pending *Slaughter* case. But notice — and this is the analytical point a careful reader should carry — the two strands pull in different directions. The unitary-executive claim wants delegated power *more* tightly controlled by the President; the nondelegation claim wants less power delegated to the executive *at all*. A victory for the first concentrates the administrative state under presidential command; a victory for the second shrinks it in favor of Congress. They share an enemy in the insulated expert agency, which is why they travel together politically — the [[term:deep-state]] framing fuses them — but as constitutional theories they are rivals, and the post-*Slaughter* era will force the choice into the open.

## Accountability to Congress: the democratic-reform position

The third position locates the original sin not in agencies or presidents but in Congress itself. Its sharpest statement is David Schoenbrod's *Power Without Responsibility* (1993): delegation is not a transfer of power so much as a **transfer of blame**. A legislator who votes for "clean air" in the abstract, then lets the EPA set the painful particulars, claims credit for the goal and disclaims the costs — calling the agency overzealous in one breath and underfunded in the next. The remedy is to make Congress own the rules it causes. The flagship vehicle is the **REINS Act** ("Regulations from the Executive in Need of Scrutiny"), proposed in every Congress since 2009: where the CRA (Lesson 1) lets Congress veto a rule by acting, REINS flips the default — no *major* rule ($100 million or more in annual effect) could take effect unless both chambers passed, and the President signed, a joint resolution of approval within roughly 70 session days. Silence would kill the rule, not bless it. Status, as of mid-2026: reintroduced in January 2025, written into the House Judiciary Committee's reconciliation draft, stripped before passage as the Senate's Byrd-rule machinery loomed — and never passed by a Senate where it would itself face the [[term:filibuster]], an irony its sponsors feel acutely. Critics' standing objection: a Congress that passes 72 laws a year cannot vote on hundreds of major rules, so REINS would not democratize rulemaking but freeze it — which, supporters answer candidly, is partly the point.

The reform position's second front is **personnel**, and here 2025–26 supplied the live experiment. The Pendleton Act of 1883 — enacted two years after a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President Garfield — began converting federal jobs from a [[term:patronage-system]] of spoils into a merit civil service with tenure protections, on the theory that professional continuity should outlast electoral turnover. The counter-theory holds that those protections now insulate a workforce that can slow-walk elected presidents of one party — the "deep state" claim in its strong form, or in its measured form, what political scientists call the bureaucracy's ideological drift. The first Trump administration's Schedule F order (October 2020, rescinded by President Biden before use) proposed stripping civil-service removal protections from career employees in "policy-influencing" positions. The second administration revived it on inauguration day 2025 under a new name, **Schedule Policy/Career**: OPM's implementing rule was published for public inspection on February 5, 2026, and on June 3, 2026 — eight days before this lesson's vantage point — an [[term:executive-order]] made the first transfers, moving roughly 8,000 employees in more than 4,800 positions into the new schedule, where they serve, in effect, at will. Union and watchdog litigation (AFGE, NTEU, PEER) is proceeding now that transfers are concrete, with due-process claims — tenure as a property interest — at the front. Read it both ways honestly: as democratic accountability (personnel is policy, and a president stymied by his own employees is a democratic failure) and as the spoils system's return with better paperwork (the at-will logic that lets a president remove obstruction also lets him demand loyalty — and the Pendleton settlement exists because that trade had been run before, and failed).

> **The scholar's lens:** The politicization question has data. David Lewis (*The Politics of Presidential Appointments*, 2008; "Testing Pendleton's Premise," *Journal of Politics*, 2007) exploited the Bush-era PART program ratings to compare federal programs run by political appointees against those run by career senior executives: careerist-run programs scored systematically higher, with appointee experience and tenure — not education or private-sector pedigree — driving the gap; Nick Gallo and Lewis (2012) found appointees drawn from campaigns and party organizations scored lower still. Paul Verkuil's *Valuing Bureaucracy* (2017) builds the institutional case on such evidence. Mind the data's limits, though: PART measured management quality, not democratic responsiveness — and Lewis's own framing is that presidents politicize *knowingly*, accepting a competence cost to buy control. The numbers tell you the trade exists and its sign. They cannot tell you whether the trade is worth it; that is a judgment about what government is for.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Expertise institutionalist">
    Modern problems are technical, continuous, and fast; legislatures are generalist, intermittent, and slow. The Landis insight stands: only standing bodies of professionals can regulate securities markets or drug safety with the necessary knowledge and time horizon, and insulation is what makes expertise usable — it buys decisions that outlast news cycles and resist purchase by the regulated. The system is not unaccountable; it is differently accountable: the APA's notice-and-comment, hard-look review, inspectors general, GAO audits, and fire-alarm oversight (McCubbins & Schwartz) form a denser web of checks than any cabinet department faced in 1900 — and the Lewis data show the professionals outperform the loyalists on every management measure we have. The constitutional attack mistakes the Constitution's silence about administration for prohibition, when the first Congress itself delegated broadly (veterans' pensions, patents, trade with tribes). Dismantle insulation and you do not get democracy — you get regulation by whoever last briefed the President.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Constitutionalist critic">
    Process is not pedigree. However elaborate the APA's procedures, binding rules made outside Article I and adjudication outside Article III reproduce exactly the extralegal power — prerogative legislation, prerogative courts — that Anglo-American constitutionalism spent the seventeenth century extinguishing (Hamburger). The Vermeule rejoinder that agencies are procedure-bound concedes the point: the procedures are the administration's own, revisable by the administration, which is what "a law unto itself" means. And the personnel question answers itself from Article II's first sentence: the executive power is vested in a President, so officers wielding it must be removable by him (Calabresi & Yoo; Scalia's Morrison dissent, vindicated in Seila Law and likely in Slaughter). Where the expertise case is strongest, the remedy is honest: Congress should legislate the hard choices itself, and what it cannot legislate it should not empower others to impose. The 2024–26 Court is not revolutionizing; it is restoring — returning interpretation to judges, major choices to Congress, and the executive branch to the executive.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Democratic-accountability reformer">
    Both other camps defend an indefensible bargain struck by Congress with itself: pass the applause lines, delegate the pain, blame the agency (Schoenbrod). The expertise camp answers a question nobody asked — competence was never the complaint; consent was — and the constitutionalist camp would hand the administrative state to the President, which replaces one unaccountable principal with another and makes every election a regime change for the entire regulatory order. The durable fix runs through the first branch: make Congress vote on the rules that bind (REINS for major rules, a CRA used as more than transition-year score-settling), staff agencies with professionals but let elected principals set direction, and fund a Congress — committees, CBO/GAO-grade analytic capacity — able to write specific statutes again. We would rather have fewer rules with owners than more rules with alibis. A republic does not need its citizens to locate the author of a binding command in a flowchart; the author should be the people they elected.
  </Perspective>

## What this lesson does not settle

Almost everything — which is the point of steel-manning. Within weeks of this writing, *Trump v. Slaughter* will say which theory owns the removal question, but no opinion can settle the regress beneath the doctrine: every camp *agrees* the administrative state must answer to the people and disagrees about which intermediary — profession, President, or Congress — counts as the people's voice. The empirics cannot close it either: the Lewis data price politicization, but a democrat may rationally pay a competence price for responsiveness, just as an institutionalist may accept a responsiveness deficit for the FDA's sake. What you should refuse, after three lessons, is the lazy version of every position: "unelected bureaucrats" (procedurally caged and judicially reviewed — Lesson 1), "the Court is just deregulating" (it upheld the universal-service delegation in 2025 — Lesson 2), and "this was all settled until recently" (FDR's own committee said "headless fourth branch" in 1937). The argument is ninety years old, currently being re-decided, and yours to join.
