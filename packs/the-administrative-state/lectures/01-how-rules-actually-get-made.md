Picture the ladder in your garage. There is a federal safety standard behind it.

Now think of your car. An emission limit stands behind that. And the mortgage on the house came with disclosures that somebody required.

[beat]

Who wrote those rules?

[pause]

Start with some arithmetic. In 2025, Congress enacted 72 public laws. Two chambers, 535 members, every committee, every hearing, every floor vote. [beat] Seventy-two laws.

In the same year, federal agencies published 2,441 final rules.

[pause]

That is 34 rules for every law. And here is the detail that should reset your sense of scale: 2025 was the quiet year.

The Federal Register, where those rules are published, ran to about 61,000 pages. That was its lightest year since 1992, under an administration that had openly set out to deregulate. The year before, it passed 106,000 pages, the highest count ever.

And a rule is not advice. Once it is final, it carries the force of law, courts enforce it, and violating it can cost you money or liberty.

> A final federal rule binds you exactly as a statute does.

So who writes the law that Americans are actually bound by? The honest arithmetic says: mostly not Congress.

This lecture is about the machinery that produces those rules. By the end, you should be able to follow one rule from the statute behind it to the courtroom, and name everyone who can stop it on the way.

We will take it in four steps. First, where agencies get the power. Second, the procedure they must follow, which is called notice and comment. Third, the president's checkpoint. And last, the two checks from outside: Congress, and the courts.

Whether this machinery is constitutional, and whether it is legitimate, are the fights of the next two lectures. The machinery comes first, because every position in those fights claims to be describing it.

## Two assembly lines

Begin with a delegation. Congress passes a law that creates an agency and hands it power. Administrative lawyers call that law an organic statute.

Here is what an organic statute sounds like. The Clean Air Act tells the EPA to set air-quality standards "requisite to protect the public health." The Federal Trade Commission Act, of 1914, tells the FTC to prevent "unfair methods of competition."

[beat]

Notice what those phrases leave out. Which pollutant? How many parts per billion? Measured how? The organic statute does not say.

> Congress states the goal and the boundaries. The agency writes the binding specifics.

The result is two assembly lines, running side by side. What Congress makes is compiled in the Statutes at Large, and then codified by subject into the United States Code. What agencies make appears daily in the Federal Register, and is then codified into the Code of Federal Regulations.

Two lines. Two shelves. And both shelves are law.

And one line out-produced the other, 34 to 1. Take that gap the way a political scientist would, and not as an indictment. Congress designed it. A body of 535 generalists, meeting in two-year increments, cannot set ladder standards or recompute particulate thresholds. So it builds institutions that can, and then it oversees them.

Is that oversight real? That question waits for the third lecture. For now, the machinery has its own answer to an old worry: who guards the guardians? It answers in three layers. Procedure. Presidential review. And two checks from outside.

## Notice and comment

The first layer is procedure, and it comes from the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946, the APA. It is the constitution of the administrative state. Administrative lawyers call it the armistice between the New Deal's enthusiasts and its enemies.

Its working heart is Section 553, which sets out three moves.

First, the agency publishes its proposal in the Federal Register. That is the notice.

Second, "interested persons" submit comments. Interested persons means anyone, and it includes you.

Third, the agency issues the final rule, together with its reasons. The Act calls that "a concise general statement of their basis and purpose." And the rule generally cannot take effect for at least thirty days.

> Notice. Comment. Then a final rule, with its reasons.

A rule made that way is called a legislative rule. It went through the process, and it carries the force of law.

[pause]

Three refinements have turned that plain sequence into doctrine.

The first concerns the concise statement, which has become anything but concise. Courts demand that agencies respond to significant comments, so final rules now ship with explanations running hundreds of pages. And if the final text strays too far from the proposal, the agency must propose it again.

Comments can arrive by the million, but agencies are not vote-counters. A comment counts for the arguments and the data it contains, and not for how many others say the same thing.

The second refinement is a road not taken. The APA also sketches formal rulemaking: hearings that run like trials, with witnesses and cross-examination. In 1973, the Supreme Court held that it applies only when a statute demands rules "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing." Those are magic words, and Congress almost never uses them. So formal rulemaking is functionally extinct.

Nor can judges bring it back. In 1978, a unanimous Court rebuked lower judges for inventing extra procedural requirements. The APA sets the ceiling as well as the floor.

The third refinement is the most contested in practice. It is about guidance.

Guidance is what an agency says without making a rule: interpretive rules, policy statements, pages of frequently asked questions, "Dear Colleague" letters. All of it is exempt from Section 553 entirely. No notice, and no comment.

The price of that freedom is that guidance is supposed to lack the force of law, and formally it does. But consider who is speaking. An agency that licenses you, funds you, or prosecutes you has announced how it reads its own rules. At that point, "nonbinding" is a lawyer's word. Regulated parties comply as if it were law.

So keep three things apart. A legislative rule takes notice and comment, and carries the force of law. Formal rulemaking takes a trial-like hearing, and is almost never required. Guidance takes no process at all, and formally binds no one.

Let's put that to work. An agency posts a webpage "clarifying" that an existing workplace-safety rule covers warehouse mezzanines, and inspectors begin citing firms accordingly. A trade association sues, arguing that the page is really a new legislative rule.

What turns on that label? And what should the court ask? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Everything turns on it. A legislative rule needed notice and comment, and this page had none. Guidance needed no process, but guidance cannot create new binding obligations by itself.

So the court should ask one question. Does the page interpret duties the old rule already imposed, or does it add new ones? If mezzanines were not fairly within the original rule, the agency has legislated without process, and its clarification does not stand.

And the label the agency chose? That is evidence. It is not the answer.

Behind that lawsuit sits a wider argument, about guidance itself.

> Critics say guidance is regulation without process. Agencies say it is transparency about enforcement.

The same documents, under two descriptions. Choose the description you find less convincing, and make the best case for it that you can. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The president's checkpoint

The second layer is one you will not find in the APA. Nothing in that Act mentions the White House.

Yet since 1981, every significant rule from an executive agency has passed through a presidential checkpoint. It is a small office of career analysts: the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, known as OIRA.

Ronald Reagan created that centralized review. In 1993, an executive order of Bill Clinton's rewrote it, in the form that has anchored the system under every administration since.

Here is how it works. An agency must submit its significant rules to OIRA twice: once before it proposes them, and again before it makes them final. Significant means, above all, a rule with an economic effect of 100 million dollars or more a year. The agency must show, with numbers, that the benefits justify the costs. Then OIRA can clear the rule, negotiate changes, or return it.

That is the machine. The dial on top of it moves with each president, and the settings for 2025 and 2026 matter. There were three changes.

One. President Biden had raised the threshold to 200 million dollars. On January 20, 2025, President Trump rescinded that order, and the line went back to 100 million. That pulled more rules into review.

Two. Eleven days later came a ten-for-one requirement: for each new rule, agencies must identify at least ten to repeal.

Three, and for the structure of government the most consequential. In February 2025, an order extended OIRA review to the independent regulatory commissions: the SEC, the FCC, the FTC and their siblings. Every earlier version of centralized review, back to 1981, had exempted them.

Now a question. All three changes arrived within weeks of one another. Why could they arrive so fast? Think about where this review comes from.

[long pause]

It comes from presidential orders, and from nowhere else. The APA never mentions OIRA, or cost-benefit analysis. So the threshold, the ten-for-one requirement and the reach into independent agencies all arrived without a single vote in Congress.

> White House review of rules rests on presidential orders, not on any statute.

That is why the regime can change with each inauguration. An executive order cannot amend the APA. But within the executive branch, it decides whose rules need the White House's signature. And as of mid-2026, the answer is: almost everyone's.

Can independent agencies be commanded this way, under the Constitution? Hold that question. It belongs to the next lecture.

## Congress's recall button

Now the checks from outside. Congress kept a lever for itself: a law of 1996 called the Congressional Review Act, or CRA.

Under it, every rule must be submitted to both chambers before it takes effect. And Congress gets a fast track for repeal, a joint resolution of disapproval. Within a window of roughly 60 days, that resolution is privileged in the Senate: ten hours of debate, a simple majority, no filibuster.

If the resolution is enacted, the rule is dead. And the agency may never reissue it in "substantially the same form" without new authority from Congress.

That sounds like a powerful weapon. So why did it overturn exactly one rule in its first twenty years?

[beat]

The catch sits in plain sight. A joint resolution requires the president's signature, and presidents do not sign death warrants for their own agencies' rules.

So the lever works when the rule belongs to the previous president. A lookback provision makes sure there are such rules to reach. For every rule submitted within the final 60 legislative days of the old Congress, the clock opens again for the next one.

> The Congressional Review Act lies dormant mid-administration, and detonates at transitions.

The history makes the point by arithmetic. One rule overturned in the first twenty years. Sixteen in 2017. Three in 2021. Then 2025 broke the scale: 22 resolutions signed in a single year, more than every previous year combined.

And notice the symmetry this lever carries. The same window will face whoever wins in 2028.

Let's test the mechanics. A major rule is finalized in October of a presidential election year. The incumbent's party loses the White House. In January, the new president's party holds 51 Senate seats.

What can opponents of that rule do, that they could not do to a rule finalized two years earlier? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The October rule falls inside the lookback window, so the new Congress gets a fresh 60-day clock. A disapproval resolution needs only simple majorities, with no Senate filibuster, plus the new president's signature. And that signature is now gettable.

The rule from two years earlier enjoyed no such window. Disapproval would have met a veto.

There is a long tail, too. If the opponents succeed, the agency is barred from issuing anything "substantially the same" without new legislation. No court has authoritatively said what that phrase means, which is why agencies treat a CRA kill as salted earth.

## When courts check the work

The last station is judicial review. Under the APA, courts set aside agency action that is "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law."

Arbitrary and capricious. What does that mean in practice? The canonical answer is a case from 1983, known as State Farm.

Here is the story. A rule required cars to have passive restraints: airbags, or automatic seatbelts. In 1981, the new Reagan administration's highway-safety agency rescinded that rule. Its reasoning was that detachable automatic belts would mostly be detached.

The Supreme Court struck the rescission down, and on the key point it was unanimous. The agency had not even considered requiring airbags alone, an alternative sitting inside its own record.

The standard the Court set out governs to this day. An agency must "examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action." And that explanation must include, in the Court's phrase, a "rational connection between the facts found and the choice made."

[pause]

A rational connection, between the facts found and the choice made. An agency fails that test if it ignores an important aspect of the problem, or offers an explanation that runs counter to the evidence.

Two features of State Farm deserve a star.

First, look at what the agency was doing. It was not regulating. It was deregulating. And it lost.

> State Farm polices reasoning, not outcomes.

Regulation and deregulation get the same hard look. That is why rescissions died in court from 2017 to 2020, and again in 2025 and 2026, when agencies rushed the paperwork.

Second, this review survives every doctrinal storm. The next lecture tells the story of deference to agencies on questions of law. Whatever happens to that deference, the requirement of a reasoned explanation, on questions of fact and policy, stands untouched.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull the whole line back. A major rule passes a series of stations, from the statute that starts it to the court that may end it. Can you name them, in order?

[long pause]

Congress delegates, in an organic statute. The agency drafts, and the White House reviews the draft. The proposal is published, and the public comments. The agency answers the significant comments and publishes the final rule. The rule goes to Congress, which can disapprove it. And challengers can take it to court. Most rules now take two to four years to run that line.

Every station on it is also a veto point, and veto points are where politics happens. An administration that cannot move Congress can still move the regulatory state: by rule, by guidance, by returning a rule to its agency, by declining to defend a rule in court.

So, five things to keep.

One. A final federal rule binds you exactly as a statute does. And agencies write most of them: 2,441 final rules in 2025, against 72 laws.

[beat]

Two. Congress states the goal and the boundaries. The agency writes the binding specifics, by notice, comment, and a final rule with its reasons. Guidance skips that process, and formally lacks the force of law.

[beat]

Three. White House review of rules rests on presidential orders, not on any statute. So it changes with the president, and as of mid-2026 it reaches the independent agencies too.

[beat]

Four. The Congressional Review Act lies dormant mid-administration, and detonates at transitions. It needs a president willing to sign.

[beat]

And five. State Farm polices reasoning, not outcomes. A rescission gets the same hard look as a new rule.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. You know how the machine runs. We have not asked whether the machine fits the Constitution's design.

May Congress hand its legislative power away in the first place? Must the president be able to fire the people who run the agencies? And who gets the last word on what an ambiguous statute means?

The stakes are high, because the pipeline we just walked concentrates lawmaking, law-execution and, when agencies adjudicate, law-judging. And it concentrates them inside bodies the Constitution never mentions.

Those questions have split the Supreme Court's last decade wide open. The next lecture walks them case by case.

For now, go back to the garage. The ladder. The car. The mortgage on the house. [beat] Congress stated the goals. Now you know who wrote the specifics, and who was in a position to stop them.
