It is the morning of May 27, 1935. New Dealers will call it Black Monday.

The Supreme Court hands down two decisions, within minutes of each other, and both are unanimous.

In the first, Schechter Poultry, the Court strikes down the "codes of fair competition" of the National Industrial Recovery Act. Its reason: Congress had handed its legislative power to the executive, with almost no instructions.

In the second, Humphrey's Executor, the Court holds that President Roosevelt could not fire a Federal Trade Commissioner over a policy disagreement. Its reason: Congress may insulate expert, multimember commissions from presidential control.

[pause]

Put those two cases side by side, and they draw a deal. After Schechter, the limit on delegation went dormant, so Congress may delegate broadly. And in exchange, Congress may park some of that delegated power in agencies the president does not fully command.

> The 1935 deal: broad delegation, and some agencies the president does not fully command.

That deal governed for ninety years. This lecture speaks from mid-June 2026, and at that moment both halves of the deal are on the operating table at once.

By the end, you should be able to say where each half stands, and to file each famous case where it belongs. We will walk three fronts, and each front is a question.

Front one: can Congress give the power away?

Front two: who says what the statutes mean?

Front three: who controls the people who hold the power?

## Front one: can Congress give the power away?

Article One vests "All legislative Powers herein granted" in Congress. From those words comes the nondelegation doctrine: Congress cannot hand that power to anyone else.

But the working test is older than the New Deal, and it has proved very hard to fail. In 1928, Chief Justice Taft wrote that a delegation stands so long as Congress supplies "an intelligible principle" for the agency to conform to.

An intelligible principle. Put plainly, the statute has to give the agency some standard to steer by.

Almost no delegation has ever flunked that test. The exceptions came in a single year, 1935. One was Panama Refining, the hot oil case. The other was Schechter. Both were aimed at the National Industrial Recovery Act, a statute so standardless that Justice Cardozo called it "delegation running riot." And Cardozo was the Court's most committed defender of the New Deal.

Since then: nothing.

The dormancy was supposed to end in 2019, in a case called Gundy. The Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act let the Attorney General decide whether its requirements applied to offenders convicted before the Act. Arguably, that was a naked grant of legislative choice to a prosecutor.

Four justices gave the statute a narrow reading, and upheld it. And each side set down its creed in a sentence.

Justice Kagan wrote for the four. If this delegation is unconstitutional, she wrote, "then most of Government is unconstitutional."

Justice Gorsuch wrote for the dissent, joined by Roberts and Thomas. "The Constitution promises that only the people's elected representatives may adopt new federal laws restricting liberty."

[pause]

Hold both sentences. One says: strike this down, and most of the government goes with it. The other says: the Constitution promised that new laws restricting liberty would come only from the people's elected representatives.

Court-watchers spent five years expecting the revival. The test arrived in June 2025, in a case called FCC v. Consumers' Research. The FCC sets what telecom carriers pay into the universal-service fund. That is a power to raise billions, guided by statutory aspirations like "just, reasonable, and affordable."

The Court upheld the scheme, six to three. Justice Kagan wrote that Congress had "sufficiently guided and constrained" the agency's discretion. Justice Gorsuch dissented again, this time joined by Thomas and Alito.

Let's test that. Suppose someone tells you that the Supreme Court revived nondelegation, in Gundy and again in Consumers' Research. From what you have just heard, what is wrong with that claim? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It has both cases backwards. Gundy upheld the delegation. Consumers' Research upheld the scheme. Those cases are famous for refusing to strike a delegation, while three justices announced they were ready to.

> Only twice has a statute failed the intelligible-principle test. Both times, in 1935.

So here is the scoreboard, as of mid-2026. The intelligible-principle test survives. The revival has three declared votes. And the doctrine's real work is done elsewhere, by the interpretive doctrines of front two. That last point is the scholars' consensus, and the dissenters' complaint.

## Front two: who says what the statute means?

Take one phrase from the Clean Air Act: air standards "requisite to protect the public health." Someone has to decide what counts as requisite. Who?

For forty years, the answer was Chevron deference, named for a case of 1984. It worked in two steps.

Step one. Has Congress "directly spoken to the precise question"? If so, its intent controls.

Step two. If the statute is silent or ambiguous, courts must accept any "permissible construction of the statute" the agency offers, even one the judges would not have chosen.

Put more plainly: where the statute is unclear, the agency's reasonable reading wins.

The theory had three parts. Ambiguity is an implicit delegation. Agencies have expertise and political accountability that courts lack. And uniform national administration beats judicial improvisation, circuit by circuit.

Chevron became the most-cited case in administrative law, applied in thousands of decisions.

[pause]

In 2024, a case called Loper Bright ended it.

The plaintiffs were herring fishermen. A rule ordered them to pay the salaries of the federal observers riding their boats, and that rule rested on statutory silence.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote for a majority of six to three. He grounded the overruling in the Administrative Procedure Act itself, the APA, which directs that "the reviewing court" shall "decide all relevant questions of law."

Here is the holding, in the opinion's own words.

"Chevron is overruled. Courts must exercise their independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority, as the APA requires."

[pause]

Independent judgment. Now be precise about what that sentence did, and what it did not do.

Courts may still give an agency's interpretation respect, in proportion to its persuasiveness. A statute that expressly hands an agency discretion still confers it. And the forty years of specific holdings that relied on Chevron still stand, even as their method is repudiated.

What changed is the default.

> Under Chevron, ambiguity was agency space. After Loper Bright, it is judicial space.

One misconception to set aside: that Loper Bright means agencies can no longer regulate. It means agencies no longer win because the statute is unclear. An agency acting within a clear grant regulates exactly as before, and its judgments of fact and policy still get only a review for reasonableness.

So the power did not disappear. It runs from agencies to judges, not from agencies to nobody.

## The major questions doctrine

Between the dormant doctrine of front one and the new default of front two sits the tool doing the most visible work. It is called the major questions doctrine.

The case that named it came in 2022, West Virginia v. EPA. The EPA's Clean Power Plan rested on one provision of the Clean Air Act, historically used for measures at individual plants. The Plan took that provision to authorize restructuring the nation's electricity generation, away from coal.

Chief Justice Roberts, again for a majority of six to three, named a practice already visible in earlier cases. In "extraordinary cases," an agency must point to "clear congressional authorization." Ordinary ambiguity will not do.

And the Court gave its reason. "A decision of such magnitude and consequence rests with Congress itself, or an agency acting pursuant to a clear delegation from that representative body."

> Extraordinary claims of agency power need clear congressional authorization.

A year later, in Biden v. Nebraska, the Court applied the doctrine again. The Education Secretary had cancelled roughly 430 billion dollars in student loans, under a power to "waive or modify" loan provisions in emergencies. "Staggering by any measure," wrote Roberts, and not clearly authorized.

What kind of rule is this? The justices themselves disagree, and there are two accounts.

On Justice Gorsuch's account, it is a substantive rule that enforces Article One. It is nondelegation, by interpretive means.

On Justice Barrett's account, it is something thinner: common sense about context. Tell a babysitter to "make sure the kids have fun," and no reasonable reader thinks you authorized an overnight trip.

The difference matters. On Gorsuch's account, the doctrine should grow. On Barrett's, it polices only true outliers.

Either way, it pairs with Loper Bright into a one-two. A novel assertion on the scale of the economy needs clear text. And the courts, not the agencies, decide what the text means.

Let's run a case through that sequence. This one takes some working out.

A statute from 1970 lets an agency regulate "substances posing risks to water quality in interstate commerce." In 2027, citing that clause, the agency orders a phase-out of a class of pesticides, used on roughly 40 percent of American cropland. The projected effect is 90 billion dollars a year. And the agency has never before claimed this authority.

What does a court ask first? And how much weight does the agency's own reading get?

[pause 5s]

Step one is now major questions, not deference. The assertion is novel, economically vast and politically charged. So the court asks whether Congress clearly authorized pesticide phase-outs in a general clause about water quality. Under West Virginia v. EPA, almost certainly not.

And suppose the court treated it as an ordinary question of interpretation. After Loper Bright, the agency's reading gets no deference. The court decides the best reading independently.

Before 2022, the same rule might have survived. The clause is ambiguous, and Chevron's second step asked only whether the agency's reading was permissible.

The statute did not change. The sequence of doctrines did.

## Front three: who controls the people who hold the power?

The third front is personnel. Article Two vests "the executive Power" in the president, and commands that he "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." From that, the Court has long inferred a power to remove subordinates.

The story runs in four moves.

Move one, 1926. In a case called Myers, the Court struck down a statute that required the Senate's consent to fire postmasters. The opinion came from Chief Justice Taft, a former president himself. Removal is the president's alone.

Move two, 1935: Humphrey's Executor, the great exception. FTC commissioners, the Court said, perform "quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial" duties, as "a body of experts." So Congress could protect them with a for-cause standard. The FTC, Justice Sutherland wrote, "cannot in any proper sense be characterized as an arm or an eye of the executive."

On that exception, the entire architecture of independent agencies was built: the Fed, the SEC, the FCC, the NLRB.

The opposing view found its voice in 1988, when the Court stretched the exception to cover even a prosecutor. Justice Scalia dissented alone. Article Two, he insisted, grants the president all executive power. One phrase of his became canonical: "this wolf comes as a wolf."

Move three. The wolf started winning. In 2020, in a case called Seila Law, the Court held that the single director of the CFPB must be removable at will. And Chief Justice Roberts recast Humphrey's Executor as a narrow exception, for "multimember expert agencies that do not wield substantial executive power." Scholars noted instantly that the description fits almost no modern agency, including the FTC of 1935 itself.

Move four, 2025. President Trump removed, without cause, Democratic members of the NLRB, the FTC and other commissions. The statutes said for cause. The president relied explicitly on the Seila Law line.

In May 2025, in Trump v. Wilcox, the Court stayed the lower-court orders that had reinstated two of those members. The Court reasoned that the government was "likely to show" that such officials "exercise considerable executive power."

It also set one institution apart: the Federal Reserve. The Fed, the Court said, "is a uniquely structured, quasi-private entity," in the tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States.

Justice Kagan's dissent called that carve-out a tell: a "bespoke" exception, for the one agency whose independence the markets would punish the Court for ending.

So where does the law stand? Take this part carefully, because it is the part that will change.

> As of mid-June 2026, Humphrey's Executor has not been overruled.

But in September 2025, the Court took up Trump v. Slaughter, the suit of a fired FTC commissioner whom a district court had ordered reinstated. It directed briefing on whether the FTC's removal protections violate the separation of powers. And, if so, whether Humphrey's Executor should be overruled.

The case was argued in December 2025. Most observers took the majority to be ready to overrule the precedent, or to narrow it drastically. As of mid-June 2026, the decision had not come down.

Meanwhile, interim orders have settled the practical question in the president's favor. Every contested removal has been allowed to stand. So for fifteen months, the independent agencies have operated, functionally, as if Humphrey's Executor were already gone.

Not overruled on paper. [beat] And not operating in practice.

## Filing the cases

The three fronts are easy to blur, and the classic mistake is to cite a case on the wrong one. So pull something back. At the start I gave you one question for each front. Can you still name the three questions?

[long pause]

Can Congress give the power away? Who says what the statutes mean? And who controls the people who hold the power?

> Three fronts: what Congress may give away, who interprets, who controls personnel.

Now file the cases. Schechter, Gundy and Consumers' Research are delegation cases. Chevron and Loper Bright are deference cases. West Virginia v. EPA sits between those two fronts, as a rule about how clearly Congress must speak. And Myers, Humphrey's Executor, Seila Law, Wilcox and Slaughter are removal cases.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The 1935 deal: broad delegation, and some agencies the president does not fully command. One morning built both halves, and by mid-2026 both were under challenge.

[beat]

Two. Only twice has a statute failed the intelligible-principle test, both times in 1935. The revival of nondelegation has three declared votes.

[beat]

Three. Under Chevron, ambiguity was agency space. After Loper Bright, it is judicial space. The power ran from agencies to judges.

[beat]

Four. Extraordinary claims of agency power need clear congressional authorization. That is the major questions doctrine.

[beat]

And five. As of mid-June 2026, Humphrey's Executor has not been overruled. But every contested removal has been allowed to stand.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Three things, in rising order of consequence.

First, the doctrine is in motion. From mid-June 2026, Trump v. Slaughter was expected within weeks. When you look for it, go to the opinion itself, and not to any summary written before it. Does the Court overrule Humphrey's Executor, or only narrow it to its facts? And what does it say about the Federal Reserve? That footnote will be the next decade's litigation.

Second, a question of evidence. When judges decide what statutes mean, is the regulation that results better, or merely different? Political scientists are only beginning to measure.

Third, the deepest question. Doctrine cannot answer it, because it is about what we want from government. It asks what the settlement of 1935 was, and there are two readings.

> One reading: the 1935 settlement betrayed the Constitution's design.

> The other reading: the settlement adapted the Constitution's design, intelligently, to industrial modernity.

You have now heard arguments, in the justices' own words, that serve each reading. Choose the reading you find less convincing, and build its best case out of those cases. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

That argument has names attached: Landis, Hamburger, and the democratic reformers. The next lecture gives each of them a full hearing.

For now, go back to that morning in 1935. Two decisions, minutes apart, and a deal that held for ninety years. [beat] Both halves of it are open again.
