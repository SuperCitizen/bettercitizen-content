The most famous insult ever aimed at the administrative state came from inside the house.

The year is 1937. Franklin Roosevelt has his own Committee on Administrative Management, known as the Brownlow Committee. It is staffed by the country's leading scholars of public administration, and its task is to make the New Deal government work.

Here is how that committee describes the independent regulatory commissions.

"A headless 'fourth branch' of the Government, a haphazard deposit of irresponsible agencies and uncoordinated powers."

[pause]

A headless fourth branch.

Not a senator on a stump. Not a litigant's brief. The architect's own engineers, reporting that part of the building stood outside the Constitution's three-branch floor plan, and answered to no one in particular.

Hold on to that fact. The anxiety about accountability did not arrive from outside the administrative project, in 2016 or in 1980. It is original to the project. And the committee put a question on the table that is still there.

> The question is ninety years old: to whom should the rulemakers answer?

The last two lectures gave you the machinery and the case law. This one gives you the argument. There are three answers to that question. By the end, you should be able to state each one as its best advocate would, and to say what the evidence can settle between them, and what it cannot.

We will take it in three steps. First, the terms of the fight. Then the three answers, each in its turn and each built the same way: who holds it, what it protects, its strongest argument, and the evidence it points to. And last, an experiment now in progress, and the data.

## The terms of the fight

Be precise about what is contested, and what is not.

Nobody in this argument disputes that a modern state needs administration. The sharpest critics concede that Congress cannot set particulate standards by floor vote. And the strongest defenders concede that unelected officials wielding binding power need some circuit of accountability.

The fight is over which circuit bears the load, and there are three.

One is accountability to expertise and procedure: insulate the professionals, and bind them with process.

Another is accountability to the president: elections control the executive, so the executive must control the agencies.

The third is accountability to Congress: the branch that delegates should vote, on the record, for the rules that bind.

Three circuits, then: expertise and procedure, the president, or Congress.

Each circuit has a scholarship, a line of cases, and a policy fight under way in 2025 and 2026. Each also has a story about why the other two fail. That symmetry is why the debate does not resolve.

## Accountability to expertise

Start with the expertise institutionalist. The classic statement belongs to James Landis: Roosevelt's securities regulator, and dean of Harvard Law School before he turned forty. His lectures of 1938, published as The Administrative Process, remain the most confident defense ever written.

What this position protects is expertise that can be used: knowledge, a long time horizon, and judgment the regulated cannot easily buy.

Its argument embraces the very feature that critics call the defect. The agency combines legislative, executive and judicial functions on purpose. Modern problems are technical, continuous and fast, while legislatures are generalist, intermittent and slow. Landis called the administrative process "our generation's answer" to legislatures that lack time and knowledge, and to courts that lack initiative and continuity.

A commission writes the rules, monitors the markets daily and adjudicates the violations. So it builds up an expertise that no committee or court can match. And insulation from removal at will is not a bug. It is the point.

> The expertise case: insulation is what makes expertise usable.

The evidence it points to comes in three kinds.

First, procedure. The system, the institutionalist argues, is not unaccountable. It is differently accountable. The modern agency legislates inside a cage of notice, comment, reason-giving and judicial review, and Congress itself never faces that cage.

Adrian Vermeule pressed precisely this point, in a reply to the leading critic. Its title was one word: "No."

The critique, he argued, attacks a lawless caricature, and not the procedure-saturated administrative law the country actually has.

Second, oversight. In a canonical article of 1984, Mathew McCubbins and Thomas Schwartz observed that Congress rationally declines police patrols, meaning continuous, centralized monitoring. It favors fire alarms: interest groups, constituents and procedural rights, a set of tripwires that sound off when an agency misbehaves. Then come the hearings, the appropriations riders, the resolutions of disapproval.

> On the fire-alarm account, silence means no alarm has rung.

Third, history and performance. The first Congress itself delegated broadly: veterans' pensions, patents, trade with tribes. So the Constitution's silence about administration, on this view, is not a prohibition. And the institutionalist points to data, which we will come to, showing professionals outperforming loyalists on every management measure we have.

The position ends on a warning. Dismantle insulation, and you do not get democracy. You get regulation by whoever last briefed the president.

Let's test the fire-alarm account. A senator argues: "Congress hasn't held a single oversight hearing on this agency's flagship rule." To the senator, that is proof that delegation has escaped democratic control.

How would McCubbins and Schwartz answer? And what evidence would tell you which story is true? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Their answer: patrols are expensive, and mostly find nothing. So Congress outsources the monitoring to regulated firms and affected constituents, armed with comment rights, petition rights and lawsuits. No hearing may simply mean no alarm.

And the two stories come apart on testable ground. Watch what happens when alarms do ring: mass comments, GAO findings, court losses. If Congress responds with hearings or riders, the system is working as designed. If alarms ring into silence, or if the only alarms Congress hears come from one side's donors, the senator's story regains force.

## Accountability to the Constitution

Now the constitutionalist critic. The deepest modern statement is Philip Hamburger's book of 2014, and its title is a question: Is Administrative Law Unlawful?

What this position protects is the Constitution's own channels for power: binding rules from the legislature, judgments from the courts, and execution under the president.

Its argument takes its force from history. The English constitutional settlement of the seventeenth century, Hamburger argues, was forged against exactly this. The Crown claimed to bind its subjects through prerogative: proclamations, and the decrees of the Star Chamber, outside acts of Parliament and judgments of the law courts. Administrative power is that extralegal power, returned in modern dress. Binding rules are made outside the legislature, and cases are adjudicated outside the courts.

And the cage of procedure? The critic has an answer.

> The constitutionalist case: process is not pedigree.

To the critic, Vermeule's rejoinder concedes the point. Agencies may be bound by procedure, but the procedures are the administration's own, revisable by the administration. And that is what "a law unto itself" means.

So on this view the administrative state is not a policy choice to debate. It is a constitutional regression, to be undone.

Beside Hamburger's indictment runs a second strand, the unitary executive, associated above all with Steven Calabresi and Christopher Yoo. Article Two vests all executive power in one president. So every officer who exercises it must answer to him, and removal protections are unconstitutional, whether the official is an expert or not.

The evidence it points to is history, text and a line of cases. The history is the seventeenth-century settlement. The text is the first sentence of Article Two. And the cases run from Scalia's lone dissent to Seila Law, to the Wilcox stay, to the pending Slaughter case.

The position ends on a remedy. Congress should legislate the hard choices itself, and what it cannot legislate, it should not empower others to impose. On this view the Court of 2024 to 2026 is not revolutionizing. It is restoring: interpretation to judges, major choices to Congress, and the executive branch to the executive.

There is one analytical point to carry out of this section. Those two strands pull in different directions.

> Unitary-executive theory wants presidential control. Nondelegation theory wants less power delegated at all.

A victory for the first concentrates the administrative state under presidential command. A victory for the second shrinks it, in favor of Congress. The two share an enemy in the insulated expert agency, which is why they travel together politically. But as constitutional theories they are rivals.

Let's test that on a case. A president directs the EPA administrator, whom she can fire at will, to issue an aggressive rule. The rule stretches the ambiguous text of an old statute.

Which critic has an objection, and to what? Think it through.

[long pause]

The unitary-executive theorist has no objection here. An at-will administrator carrying out the president's direction is the theory working, not failing.

The objection to the rule comes from the other strand. Nondelegation says a choice this big belongs to Congress, regardless of who commands the agency.

So consolidating agencies under presidential control satisfies one critique, while aggravating the other.

## Accountability to Congress

The third position is the democratic-accountability reformer. It locates the original sin not in agencies or presidents, but in Congress itself. Its sharpest statement is David Schoenbrod's book of 1993, Power Without Responsibility.

What this position protects is consent: a binding command should have an author the people elected.

Its argument is that delegation is not a transfer of power so much as a transfer of blame. A legislator votes for clean air in the abstract, and lets the EPA set the painful particulars. Then the legislator claims credit for the goal and disclaims the costs, calling the agency overzealous in one breath and underfunded in the next.

That is an indefensible bargain, the reformer argues, and both other camps defend it. The expertise camp answers a question nobody asked.

> The reform case: competence was never the complaint. Consent was.

And the constitutionalist camp would hand the administrative state to the president. That replaces one unaccountable principal with another, and makes every election a regime change for the entire regulatory order.

The evidence it points to is the conduct of Congress itself: pass the applause lines, delegate the pain, blame the agency.

The position ends on a remedy too: make Congress own the rules it causes. The flagship is the REINS Act, proposed in every Congress since 2009. The Congressional Review Act lets Congress veto a rule by acting. REINS flips the default. A major rule is one with 100 million dollars or more in annual effect. No such rule could take effect unless both chambers approved it, and the president signed, within roughly 70 session days.

> Under REINS, silence would kill a major rule, not bless it.

As of mid-2026, it is not law. It was reintroduced in January 2025, stripped from that year's reconciliation bill before passage, and has never passed the Senate, where it would itself face the filibuster.

The rest of the program runs through the first branch as well. Staff agencies with professionals, but let elected principals set direction. And fund a Congress able to write specific statutes again. The reformer would rather have, in the position's own words, "fewer rules with owners than more rules with alibis."

Here the test comes from the critics, and their objection is a standing one. A Congress that passes 72 laws a year cannot vote on hundreds of major rules. So REINS would not democratize rulemaking. It would freeze it. And supporters answer candidly: that is partly the point.

[pause]

Three positions, each in its own terms. Before any data, choose the one you find least convincing. State its case the way its best advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## An experiment in progress, and the data

One front of this fight is personnel, and here 2025 and 2026 supplied an experiment in progress.

The background is the Pendleton Act of 1883, enacted two years after a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President Garfield. It began converting federal jobs from spoils into a merit civil service, with tenure protections. The theory was that professional continuity should outlast electoral turnover. The counter-theory holds that those protections now insulate a workforce that can slow-walk elected presidents of one party.

On inauguration day 2025, the second Trump administration revived a plan from the first, under a new name: Schedule Policy/Career. Career employees in policy-influencing positions lose their civil-service removal protections. In June 2026, an executive order made the first transfers, moving roughly 8,000 employees into the new schedule. There they serve, in effect, at will. Unions and watchdog groups are litigating.

Take it both ways, honestly.

One way, it is democratic accountability. Personnel is policy, and a president stymied by his own employees is a democratic failure.

The other way, it is the spoils system's return, with better paperwork. The at-will logic that lets a president remove obstruction also lets him demand loyalty. And the Pendleton settlement exists because that trade had been run before, and failed.

Is there evidence on that trade? There is. David Lewis took management ratings from a Bush-era program, called PART, and compared federal programs run by political appointees with those run by career senior executives.

The finding: careerist-run programs scored systematically higher. A later study found that appointees drawn from campaigns and party organizations scored lower still.

Now the limits. Those ratings measured management quality, not democratic responsiveness. And Lewis's own framing is that presidents politicize knowingly: they accept a competence cost, to buy control.

So the numbers tell you that the trade exists, and which way it runs.

> The data price the trade. They cannot say whether the price is worth paying.

That is a judgment about what government is for.

One more test, and it reaches back. Early on I gave you three circuits of accountability. Now take two levers from this lecture: the REINS Act, and Schedule Policy/Career. Which circuit does each one load?

[long pause]

REINS loads Congress, with a vote before a major rule takes effect. Schedule Policy/Career loads the president, through personnel.

And every lever on the reform menu works that way. Each is somebody's accountability triumph, and somebody else's accountability disaster.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The question is ninety years old: to whom should the rulemakers answer? There are three circuits of accountability: expertise and procedure, the president, or Congress.

[beat]

Two. The expertise case, from Landis: insulation is what makes expertise usable. The agency is not unaccountable, on this account, but differently accountable. And silence means no alarm has rung.

[beat]

Three. The constitutionalist case, from Hamburger: process is not pedigree. And remember its two strands. Unitary-executive theory wants presidential control. Nondelegation theory wants less power delegated at all.

[beat]

Four. The reform case, from Schoenbrod: competence was never the complaint. Consent was. Delegation transfers blame, and under REINS, silence would kill a major rule, not bless it.

[beat]

And five. Lewis found that programs run by career executives outscored those run by political appointees. The data price the trade. They cannot say whether the price is worth paying.

[pause]

What has this series not settled? Almost everything, and that is the point of stating each case at its strongest.

From mid-June 2026, Trump v. Slaughter was due within weeks to say which theory owns the removal question. But no opinion can settle what lies beneath the doctrine. Every camp agrees that the administrative state must answer to the people. They disagree about which intermediary counts as the people's voice: a profession, the president, or Congress.

The evidence cannot settle it either. A democrat may rationally pay a competence price for responsiveness. An institutionalist may accept a responsiveness deficit, for the FDA's sake.

What you can set aside, after these three lectures, is the lazy version of every position.

"Unelected bureaucrats"? They are procedurally caged, and judicially reviewed.

"The Court is just deregulating"? It upheld the universal-service delegation in 2025.

"This was all settled until recently"? [beat] Roosevelt's own committee said headless fourth branch, in 1937.

So where does the argument go from here? To the Court, which is deciding it again at speed. And to you.

The architect's own engineers asked the question first. The argument is ninety years old, it is being decided again now, and it is yours to join.
