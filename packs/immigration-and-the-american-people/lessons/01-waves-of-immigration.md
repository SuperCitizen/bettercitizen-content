# Waves of immigration

The United States is often described as a nation of immigrants. The phrase compresses a long and uneven history: people arrived in distinct waves, from changing parts of the world, under laws that grew steadily more restrictive before opening again. This lesson traces that demographic record.

## Colonial peoples and the founding stock

The population of the colonies that became the United States was already mixed before independence. English settlers predominated in New England and the Chesapeake, but the middle colonies drew Dutch, German, Scots-Irish, Swedish, and French Huguenot settlers. Enslaved Africans were carried to North America by force; by 1790 they numbered roughly 700,000 of a national population of about 3.9 million in the first federal census. Native peoples, of course, long predated all of these arrivals. The first census counted a population that was already plural in origin, even as political and legal power rested with English-descended whites.

## The "old" immigration: Germans and Irish

The first great voluntary wave came in the 1840s and 1850s. Crop failures and political upheaval in the German states pushed millions toward American ports. The most catastrophic single cause was the Irish Potato Famine (roughly 1845-1852), which killed about a million people in Ireland and drove a million more to emigrate. Between 1845 and 1855, more than three million immigrants arrived, a staggering number for a country of about 23 million in 1850. The Irish, overwhelmingly Catholic and poor, settled in eastern cities; Germans spread into the Midwest. This "old immigration" came largely from northern and western Europe and provoked the first organized nativist backlash, discussed in the next lesson.

## The "new" immigration: Southern and Eastern Europe

After about 1880 the sources shifted. Italians, Poles, Russians, Greeks, Slavs, and Eastern European Jews arrived in unprecedented numbers, peaking in the first years of the twentieth century. In 1907 alone roughly 1.3 million immigrants were admitted. Many passed through the federal inspection station at Ellis Island, opened in 1892 in New York Harbor, which processed roughly twelve million arrivals before it closed to immigrant processing in 1954. By 1910 the foreign-born share of the U.S. population reached about 14.7 percent, near its historical high. Contemporaries labeled these Catholic, Orthodox, and Jewish newcomers the "new immigration" and often regarded them as less assimilable than earlier arrivals, a judgment that would later be written into law.

## Asian immigration and the Chinese Exclusion Act

On the West Coast a different stream arrived. Chinese laborers came in large numbers after the 1849 Gold Rush and built much of the western half of the transcontinental railroad. Anti-Chinese agitation, especially in California, produced the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the first federal law to bar a group by nationality. It suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers and barred Chinese immigrants from naturalization; it was renewed and tightened for decades and not repealed until 1943. Japanese immigration that followed was curtailed by the 1907-1908 "Gentlemen's Agreement." These measures established the principle that the federal government could select immigrants by national origin.

## The national-origins quota system

Restrictionist sentiment culminated in the Immigration Act of 1924 (the Johnson-Reed Act). It imposed numerical quotas tied to "national origins," capping each nationality's annual admissions at a small percentage of that group's share of the U.S. population recorded in an earlier census. The formula was deliberately calibrated to favor northern and western Europeans and to sharply limit southern and eastern Europeans; it excluded most Asians entirely. The system reduced overall immigration dramatically. Combined with the Great Depression and the Second World War, it drove the foreign-born share down to about 4.7 percent by 1970, the lowest in the nation's recorded history.

## Hart-Celler and the modern era

The civil-rights climate of the 1960s made the explicitly racial quota system politically untenable. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, known as the Hart-Celler Act, abolished the national-origins quotas and replaced them with a system based on family reunification and occupational skills, with per-country caps applied more evenly. Its sponsors expected little demographic change, but the effect was profound. Immigration rose, and its sources shifted decisively away from Europe toward Latin America and Asia. By 2020 the foreign-born share had climbed back to roughly 13.7 percent, with Mexico, China, India, and the Philippines among the leading countries of origin. The waves continued, but their geography had been remade.
