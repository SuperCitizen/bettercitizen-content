# The industrial transformation

In 1790 the United States was an overwhelmingly agrarian nation; most goods were made in homes and small workshops. Within little more than a century it had become the largest manufacturing economy on earth. This lesson traces how that happened: the arrival of the factory, the standardization of parts, the giant industries of steel and oil, and the assembly line that put manufacturing at the center of American life.

## The factory system arrives

American industrialization began in textiles. Samuel Slater, an English mechanic, memorized the design of Richard Arkwright's water-powered spinning machinery and carried that knowledge to Rhode Island, where in 1793 he opened a successful water-powered cotton mill at Pawtucket. Slater's mill is conventionally treated as the start of the American factory system, and he is often called the "father of the American factory."

A generation later the model grew far larger. The Boston Associates built integrated textile mills at Lowell, Massachusetts, beginning in the 1820s, combining spinning and weaving under one roof and powered by the Merrimack River. The Lowell mills were notable for their labor force: thousands of young women, the "Lowell girls," recruited from New England farms and housed in company boardinghouses. Their work was disciplined by the clock and the factory bell — a sharp break from the rhythms of farm and household labor.

## The "American system" of manufactures

A second innovation proved even more consequential. Eli Whitney — better known for the cotton gin — promoted in 1798 the idea of manufacturing muskets from interchangeable parts, so that any component would fit any weapon. Whitney's own demonstrations were partly staged, and the genuine breakthroughs came in the federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry and in the work of inventors such as John Hall, who by the 1820s and 1830s achieved real interchangeability through precise machine tools and gauges.

This approach — standardized, machine-made, interchangeable parts assembled into finished products — became known abroad as the "American system of manufactures" after British observers admired it at the 1851 Crystal Palace Exhibition. It spread from firearms to clocks, sewing machines, agricultural equipment, and bicycles, and it laid the technical groundwork for everything that followed.

## The second industrial revolution

After the Civil War, American manufacturing entered a new and explosive phase, often called the second industrial revolution. Three developments stand out.

**Steel.** Andrew Carnegie applied the Bessemer process — which blew air through molten iron to burn off impurities cheaply and at scale — to build enormous integrated steelworks around Pittsburgh. Carnegie drove down costs relentlessly, and his enterprise was folded into the United States Steel Corporation in 1901, the world's first billion-dollar company.

**Oil.** John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil consolidated refining through aggressive pricing, rebate deals with railroads, and the trust form of organization, controlling the great majority of American refining by the 1880s.

**Railroads.** The railroads tied the system together, creating a national market, demanding vast quantities of steel, and — as Alfred Chandler argued — pioneering the large, multi-unit business enterprise run by salaried managers. Coordinating thousands of miles of track and complex schedules required a "visible hand" of professional management that replaced the "invisible hand" of the market in coordinating production and distribution.

## Mass production and scientific management

The culminating innovation was mass production. Henry Ford's engineers installed a moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant in 1913. By bringing the work to the stationary worker and breaking assembly into small repeated tasks, Ford cut the time to build a Model T from over twelve hours to about ninety minutes. Prices fell, output soared, and in 1914 Ford introduced the five-dollar day, partly to reduce crippling worker turnover. "Fordism" came to mean high-volume, standardized production paired with relatively high wages and mass consumption.

Alongside it ran "scientific management," associated with Frederick Winslow Taylor, whose Principles of Scientific Management (1911) urged employers to study tasks with stopwatches, break them into measured motions, and prescribe the "one best way" to do each. Taylorism promised efficiency but also intensified managerial control over how workers used their time, and it remained controversial among labor.

## What it did to American life

The consequences were sweeping. Output per worker rose dramatically, and manufactured goods that had once been luxuries became affordable. Industry pulled people from farms and from abroad into rapidly growing cities, fueling the urbanization of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Work itself changed — paced by machines, organized by managers, and increasingly performed for wages rather than as independent craft. By the eve of World War II, the factory and the corporation had reshaped where Americans lived, what they bought, and how they earned a living, setting the stage for the wartime mobilization examined in the next lesson.
