Picture a book. It is 500 pages long. It mentions a candidate for office exactly once. And its last line reads: "and so vote for X."

Could the federal government ban that book?

[beat]

In March 2009, a lawyer for the government stood at the lectern of the Supreme Court and answered. If a corporation published that book with its own treasury money, he said, the government could prohibit it.

[pause]

That answer was given in a case about a movie. It turned a small dispute over one film into a constitutional earthquake. The case is Citizens United.

Almost everyone has an opinion about it. Far fewer can say what it held. By the end of this lecture you will be able to state, in mechanical terms, what the ruling changed and what it left standing. Whether it was right is the next lecture's argument, and you cannot have that argument honestly without this one.

We will take it in four steps. First, the story: a film, a book, and an answer at the lectern. Second, the one distinction the ruling did not touch. Third, the ledger: what fell, and what stood. And last, two things people think the ruling did, and it didn't, along with the three camps still arguing over it.

## A film, and then a book

Start in January 2008. A conservative nonprofit corporation called Citizens United has a problem with a release date.

It has produced a film called Hillary: The Movie. It runs 90 minutes, and it is a documentary-style attack on Hillary Clinton, who is then the front-runner for the Democratic presidential nomination.

A cable company has made an offer. For a payment of 1.2 million dollars, it will carry the film on a video-on-demand channel, free to any subscriber who chooses it.

Here is the catch. A federal statute, the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, is also known as McCain–Feingold. Its section 203 makes it a federal crime for a corporation to spend treasury money on what the law calls an electioneering communication. That means a broadcast that names a federal candidate, within 30 days of a primary.

Citizens United is a corporation. The primaries are 30 days away.

A three-judge federal court agrees with the government that the film is, in effect, a feature-length "vote against her." And the video-on-demand plan dies.

[pause]

That could have stayed a small case about one film. What changed it happened at the Supreme Court, on March 24, 2009.

The lawyer defending the statute was the Deputy Solicitor General, Malcolm Stewart. He followed the statute's logic wherever the Justices pushed. And they pushed toward print.

Justice Alito asked whether the government's theory would let Congress restrict the same advocacy in a book. Stewart allowed that it could. "That's pretty incredible," Alito answered.

Then Chief Justice Roberts built the example that stuck. The 500-page book. One mention of a candidate. The last line: "and so vote for X."

Stewart offered that the corporation could be required to publish the book through a PAC. Roberts pressed him. "And if they didn't, you could ban it?"

Listen to the answer.

"If they didn't, we could prohibit the publication of the book using the corporate treasury funds."

[pause]

Prohibit the publication of the book.

Nearly every publisher in America is a corporation. The government had just claimed the power to ban their books.

The Court then took an unusual step. It ordered the case argued a second time, on a bigger question: whether to overrule its own precedents that permitted bans on corporate spending at all.

The second argument came on September 9, 2009. This time the government's lawyer was the Solicitor General, Elena Kagan, making her first argument before the Court. Justice Ginsburg asked whether the government stood by its answer about books.

"The government's answer has changed," Kagan replied, and there was laughter in the courtroom.

Books were now safe, she suggested. Pamphlets were another matter. In her words: "a pamphlet would be different. A pamphlet is pretty classic electioneering."

For five Justices, that settled something. A statute whose constitutionality depended on which printed formats the government promised not to ban was already lost.

[pause]

The decision came on January 21, 2010. The vote was five to four. Justice Kennedy wrote for the majority, and Justice Stevens answered in a dissent of 90 pages.

Here is what the Court held. The government may not ban political speech because the speaker is a corporation or a union.

> No ban on political speech because the speaker is a corporation or a union.

That sentence is the holding. Not "corporations are people." Not "money is speech." Not "unlimited donations." [beat] The rest of this lecture is the mechanics of that one sentence: exactly what fell, and exactly what stood.

## Two tracks

To see what changed, you need one distinction. Campaign finance law has rested on it since 1976, and a case called Buckley v. Valeo.

Buckley sorts political money onto two tracks.

The first track is the direct contribution: money given to a candidate or a committee. Buckley held that contributions can be capped. The candidate knows who paid, so there is a risk of a bargain, a favor in return for the money. Lawyers call that quid pro quo corruption. That risk, and even the appearance of it, justifies regulation.

The second track is the independent expenditure: money you spend on your own, arguing for or against a candidate, without coordinating with anyone's campaign. Buckley held that for individuals, this spending cannot be capped. No coordination, no bargain. And to cap that spending is to cap speech itself.

[pause]

So, two tracks. Money you give, which can be capped. And money you spend yourself, which cannot.

Now, which track did Citizens United move?

Not the first. Corporations and unions were already barred from giving treasury money to federal candidates, under two old statutes. The Tillman Act, of 1907. And the Taft–Hartley Act, of 1947. Those contributions were illegal before the ruling, and they are illegal today.

The limits on what an individual may give survived too. In the cycle that began in 2025, that limit is 3,500 dollars per candidate, per election.

What the case did was extend Buckley's second track. The freedom an individual already had, to spend independently without a cap, now belongs to corporations and unions as well.

Here is what that means in practice. After January 2010, a corporation may spend any amount of treasury money on ads that say "defeat Senator X." The one condition is that the spending is not coordinated with any campaign.

> Citizens United widened who may spend. It did not change who may give.

Let's test that. After Citizens United, may Apple write a check for 1 million dollars to a presidential campaign? And may it spend 1 million dollars airing its own ads, attacking that campaign's opponent? Take a moment.

[long pause]

No to the first. Yes to the second.

The check is a contribution. Corporate contributions to candidates remain criminal under the Tillman Act, and Citizens United never touched them.

The ads are an independent expenditure. The Court held that the First Amendment protects that spending whatever the speaker's corporate form, provided there is no coordination with a campaign.

## What fell

Now the ledger, and first, what fell. Three things, each one cutting deeper than the one before.

One. Section 203 itself: the ban on broadcast ads, paid for by a corporation or a union, that name a candidate in the weeks before an election.

The Court struck it down facially. That word matters. It means the ban fell not just as applied to this one film, but for everyone.

The Court's own precedents offered narrower exits, and it declined them. Its reasoning was that years of case-by-case litigation would themselves chill speech, while the FEC ran what Kennedy derided as an effective licensing regime.

When government uses its full power, including the criminal law, to command where a person may get information, Kennedy wrote, "it uses censorship to control thought."

Two. A precedent. In 1990, in Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, the Court had upheld a Michigan ban on corporate independent expenditures. Austin's rationale has a name: anti-distortion. A state, Austin said, may counter "the corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth." It meant wealth built up with the help of the corporate form, with little or no connection to public support for the corporation's political ideas.

Citizens United rejected that rationale root and branch. The First Amendment, Kennedy wrote, does not permit government to handicap speakers because they are wealthy or institutional.

So the Court overruled Austin outright. Courts normally stand by what they have already decided, a practice called stare decisis. An explicit abandonment of it, like this one, is rare.

Three. Part of a third case. In 2003, in McConnell v. FEC, the Court had upheld section 203 on Austin's logic. To that extent, McConnell was overruled as well.

[pause]

Now go back to where this lecture began: the book, and the government's answer about it. That exchange was good theater. But why did it matter as law? Why would it move a Court toward striking the statute for everyone, when it could have carved out one more exception? Think it through.

[long pause]

Because it showed that the statute's logic had no limiting principle the government itself could state.

Look at where the government ended up. Books were exempt. Pamphlets were "classic electioneering." If the statute's constitutionality turned on promises like those, about which formats prosecutors would leave alone, then protected speech survived only at the regulators' grace. The majority treated that as the definition of a censorship regime, and as grounds for a facial ruling.

The book concession turned a case about one film into a case about the government's theory of power.

## What stood

That is what fell. Now the list that popular accounts skip, and it is half the case. Five things stood.

First, the ban on direct contributions from corporations and unions. Intact.

Second, the limits on what individuals may give to candidates, parties and PACs. Intact.

Third, the ban on foreign nationals. Foreign money remains barred from American elections, contributions and expenditures alike.

Fourth, the coordination doctrine. An expenditure that is coordinated with a campaign is not independent. It is treated as a contribution, and capped. That rule stood in principle, and how it holds up in practice is a question for a later lecture.

And fifth, the statute's disclosure and disclaimer rules. The Court upheld them eight to one, with only Justice Thomas dissenting on that point.

Here is Kennedy again, this time for that near-unanimous majority. "The First Amendment protects political speech; and disclosure permits citizens and shareholders to react to the speech of corporate entities in a proper way."

[pause]

Think about who is in that eight. Four of the five Justices who freed corporate spending joined the four dissenters in blessing mandatory transparency about it. Even celebrating it.

> The same ruling upheld disclosure, eight to one.

Hold on to that vote. It is why the fight over disclosure, which closes this series, is a fight about statutes and agencies, and not about the Constitution.

So for a corporation's treasury money, the before and after is short. Contributions are still banned. The company PAC, funded by employee money, is unchanged. One channel opened: independent expenditures, unlimited, with disclosure attached.

> Citizens United moved exactly one wall.

## Two things it did not do

Two things people believe about this case are not in it.

The first is that the Court ruled that corporations are people. It did not. Corporate personhood is the doctrine that corporations hold legal rights, such as contract and property. It runs back through a case called Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific, in 1886. That is a century before Citizens United, and the doctrine does most of its work far from election law.

The majority's claim was about speech, not personhood. The First Amendment protects political speech. And that speech, the opinion said, "does not lose First Amendment protection simply because its source is a corporation."

A newspaper corporation's editorial had always been protected. The Court extended the same logic to every other corporate form. And that extension is precisely the move the dissent contested.

The second belief is that Citizens United created the Super PAC. It did not. That was a different court, two months later: the D.C. Circuit, in a case called SpeechNow.org v. FEC. It took the premise that independent spending cannot corrupt, and extended it from spending to contributions. Contributions, that is, to groups that do nothing but independent spending.

One more correction belongs here. Citizens United did not legalize anonymous spending. The donor anonymity of what is called dark money comes from IRS rules about certain nonprofits. It does not come from this opinion. On disclosure, remember, this opinion went eight to one in favor.

Before we go on, a quick check. Some minutes ago I gave you the holding in a single sentence. Can you still say it?

[long pause]

The government may not ban political speech because the speaker is a corporation or a union.

## Why the case stays contested

Those are the mechanics, and none of the camps disputes them. What they dispute is whether the Court was right. Three positions are alive, and the next lecture builds each one at full strength. Here is only the map, with each position stated the same way: who holds it, and where it says the error lies.

First, the First Amendment maximalists. They argue that the holding is the only one consistent with a categorical text. The government had claimed the power to criminalize a film about a presidential candidate. And book-banning, they argue, was the logical endpoint of the government's own argument.

> The maximalist case: the text is categorical, and book-banning was the endpoint.

Second, the anti-corruption advocates. They argue that the majority rested on an empirical premise: that independent spending cannot corrupt, or even appear corrupt. That premise, they argue, was asserted and not demonstrated. And it has been falsified, they argue, by fifteen years of single-candidate Super PACs run by the candidates' former aides.

> The anti-corruption case: the majority's premise was asserted, not demonstrated.

Third, the democratic-equality advocates. They argue that the deepest error comes before this case. It is Buckley's refusal to let political equality count as a constitutional interest. On this view, Citizens United is a faithful application of a wrong premise.

> The democratic-equality case: Citizens United faithfully applied a wrong premise.

Notice how differently the three are aimed. The first thinks Stevens's dissent was wrong. The second thinks Kennedy's facts were wrong. The third thinks the whole frame was wrong.

[pause]

You have heard each of them for only a few sentences. Even so, choose the one you find least convincing. From what you know so far, what is the best case for it?

[pause 5s]

Keep your answer. The next lecture will put it to work.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The holding. The government may not ban political speech because the speaker is a corporation or a union. That is not a ruling that corporations are people.

[beat]

Two. The two tracks, from Buckley. Contributions can be capped, and for corporations and unions they are banned. Independent spending cannot be capped. Citizens United widened who may spend. It did not change who may give.

[beat]

Three. What fell. Section 203, for everyone. Austin, overruled outright. And the matching part of McConnell. Austin is dead. Buckley is alive.

[beat]

Four. What stood. The contribution ban and the contribution limits. The ban on foreign money. The coordination rule. And disclosure, upheld eight to one. Citizens United moved exactly one wall.

[beat]

And five. What it did not do. It did not create the Super PAC: that was SpeechNow, two months later, in a different court. And it did not legalize anonymous spending.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled, and did not try to. Was the ruling right? One camp says the dissent was wrong. One says the facts were wrong. One says the whole frame was wrong. Those are three different quarrels, and all three are open.

The next lecture takes them up: the strongest case for each of the three positions, built so that you can argue with the position and not with a caricature of it.

For now, go back to the lectern, and the 500-page book. One answer about that book changed what the case was about. [beat] You know now what the Court did with it. And you know what the Court left alone.
