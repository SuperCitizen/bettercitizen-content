Two numbers.

In 2008, the last federal election held before Citizens United, every outside group in the country, combined, spent 338 million dollars.

In 2024, one donor spent 290 million dollars. [beat] By himself.

The donor was Elon Musk. His year-end filings with the FEC showed 239 million dollars of it flowing through a single Super PAC, called America PAC.

Among other things, America PAC took over much of the Republican presidential ground operation: the canvassers, the voter registration, the get-out-the-vote work. And it did that for a campaign it was legally forbidden to coordinate with.

[pause]

Every part of that story was lawful. And no part of it was possible fifteen years earlier.

This lecture explains the machine that produced it. You already know the first piece: the holding of January 2010. There are three more. A case in a lower court, that same March. A sequel at the Supreme Court, in 2014. And a regulator that has, step by step, stopped regulating. Each piece matters, because the argument over Citizens United today is really an argument over the whole assembly.

We will take it in four steps. First, the two cases that came after. Second, the wall between a Super PAC and a campaign, on paper and in practice. Third, the two places where the system has gone quiet: the money that is never disclosed, and the referee that no longer referees. And last, what the record means, which is where the camps divide.

## Two more cases

Citizens United said nothing about what a group could collect.

There was an association called SpeechNow.org. It wanted to pool donations from individuals, and to spend them solely on independent ads for and against candidates. But federal law still capped what any individual could give a political committee, at 5,000 dollars a year.

SpeechNow sued. And two months after Citizens United, the full D.C. Circuit, all nine of its judges, finished the syllogism.

Here is the syllogism. The Supreme Court had just declared, as a matter of law, that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." If the spending cannot corrupt, wrote Chief Judge Sentelle, then contributions to a group that does nothing but that spending cannot corrupt either. And a limit that serves no anti-corruption interest serves nothing. In his words: "the First Amendment cannot be encroached upon for naught."

[pause]

So the cap fell. The decision is SpeechNow.org v. FEC, of March 26, 2010. What it created has a formal name, the independent-expenditure-only committee, and a nickname that everyone uses. The Super PAC.

> Citizens United opened the spending. SpeechNow opened the fundraising.

Two details get lost in the shorthand, so hold on to both.

First, the same opinion upheld the registration and reporting requirements. SpeechNow had to organize as a political committee, and itemize its donors publicly. That will matter a great deal when we reach disclosure.

Second, the FEC never asked the Supreme Court to review its loss. So the Super PAC rests on a circuit court precedent that the highest court has never examined.

Let's test that. Suppose a future Supreme Court formally repudiates the premise that independent expenditures cannot corrupt. Congress passes nothing. What happens to the Super PAC? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Its legal foundation dissolves.

SpeechNow is a pure syllogism, built on that single premise. No corruption from independent spending. So no anti-corruption interest in capping the contributions that fund only such spending. So the cap falls. [beat] Knock out the premise, and the old cap of 5,000 dollars has no constitutional obstacle left.

That is why fifteen years of litigation and scholarship have aimed at the premise, and not at the vehicle.

> The Super PAC rests on one premise: independent spending cannot corrupt.

Now the sequel. It arrived four years later.

Federal law capped two things. It capped what a donor could give each candidate: those are the base limits. And it capped the donor's total federal giving, across every candidate and committee. In the cycle of 2013 and 2014, that total was 123,200 dollars. That is the aggregate limit.

Shaun McCutcheon, an electrical engineer from Alabama, had given to sixteen candidates. He wanted to support a dozen more, at the legal rate for each one. The aggregate limit stood in the way, and he sued.

In 2014, in McCutcheon v. FEC, the Court struck the aggregate limits, five to four. Chief Justice Roberts opened on the note the case is remembered for. "There is no right more basic in our democracy than the right to participate in electing our political leaders."

[pause]

His reasoning ran mechanically from Buckley's own definition. Each base limit already polices the risk of a quid pro quo with each individual candidate. So a ceiling on how many candidates a donor may support, at the lawful rate, adds no protection against corruption. It only restricts participation.

The base limits survive. And the case is narrower than Citizens United: it concerns what individuals give directly, not what corporations spend. But it extends the same choice of definition.

> For the Court, corruption means a bargained exchange, and nothing broader counts.

Five cases now carry the whole structure, and the popular shorthand routinely scrambles them. Two are older. Buckley held that contributions may be capped, and an individual's independent expenditures may not. Austin upheld a state ban on corporate independent spending, and was overruled in 2010.

The other three you have now met. So before we go on, sort them. Which decision opened the spending? Which opened the fundraising? And what did the 2014 case remove?

[long pause]

Citizens United opened the spending: the government may not ban independent political spending because the speaker is a corporation or a union.

SpeechNow, in the D.C. Circuit, opened the fundraising: no cap on contributions to committees that make only independent expenditures.

And McCutcheon struck the aggregate ceiling on one donor's total giving. The base limits survive.

## The wall, on paper and in practice

On paper, the wall between a Super PAC and a campaign is what makes the entire structure constitutional. In practice, the wall has been engineered around from the start. The methods are public. They are well documented. And under current enforcement, they are effectively lawful. There are four.

One: staffing. The first Super PACs of a presidential cycle set the pattern, in 2012. Restore Our Future was founded by veterans of Mitt Romney's prior campaign. Priorities USA Action was founded by former aides from the Obama White House. Single-candidate Super PACs, run by the candidate's recent staff, have been standard equipment ever since.

Two: solicitation. Under FEC guidance issued in 2011, a federal candidate may appear at a fundraiser for the Super PAC that supports them, and personally ask for money. The candidate may ask for no more than 5,000 dollars. After that, the host may ask for the rest.

Three: redboxing. A campaign posts its message scripts, its footage and its guidance on which voters to target, on a public webpage. Often the material sits inside a literal red box, addressed to no one, beginning "voters need to know." Then the allied Super PAC carries it out.

Why does that work? The coordination rules require that information be conveyed privately. This material is public, so it defeats them. A study in the Election Law Journal found the practice across more than two hundred congressional campaigns, in the 2022 cycle alone.

Four: the canvassing loophole. The FEC's coordination rules govern coordinated communications, which means paid public advertising. Knocking on doors is not a public communication under the regulations. That is how America PAC could lawfully run the Republican ground game in 2024, and remain, for legal purposes, independent of the campaign it was turning out voters for.

[pause]

Let's test the wall. A Super PAC is devoted to one Senate candidate. It is run by her campaign manager from two cycles ago. It hires her former pollster. And it lifts its ad scripts from a "voter education" page on her campaign website.

Which of those, if any, violates the coordination rules as currently enforced? Think it through.

[long pause]

Almost certainly none.

The FEC's regulation turns on the private conveyance of material, nonpublic campaign information. Former staff and shared vendors draw scrutiny only if they carry inside information across the wall. And a public webpage is, by definition, not nonpublic. That is the entire point of redboxing. Add a Commission that deadlocks on coordination complaints, and the wall functions mostly as a formality.

Here the two positions from last time collide, and they collide on facts, not doctrine. Justice Kennedy's opinion assumed a world of genuinely independent spending, policed by a functioning regulator. The anti-corruption advocate argues that this assumption has failed, and with it the empirical premise of the holding. The maximalist argues that these adaptations are ordinary politics that corrupt no one, and that public messaging is speech, not conspiracy.

## Where disclosure stops, and the referee

Now the money itself. Citizens United upheld disclosure, eight to one, and Super PACs do itemize their donors. Even so, the system after 2010 built a detour.

The detour runs through a kind of nonprofit called a 501(c)(4), a social welfare organization. That is an IRS category, not an FEC one. Such a nonprofit may take unlimited money. It never publicly discloses its donors. And it may spend on politics, so long as politics is not its primary purpose, a term the IRS has never rigorously defined.

It may also pass unlimited sums to a Super PAC. And the Super PAC then discloses exactly what the law requires: [beat] the nonprofit's name.

So picture two routes from a donor to the same election ad. On the first, the donor gives straight to the Super PAC, and the name and the amount are public. On the second, the donor gives to the nonprofit, and the nonprofit gives to the Super PAC. The filing shows only the nonprofit, and a sum.

> Disclosure follows the dollar to the nonprofit. There the trail legally ends.

Set the vehicles side by side. A traditional PAC itemizes its donors to the FEC, and each donor is capped at 5,000 dollars a year. A Super PAC itemizes its donors too, with no cap. A 527 organization discloses to the IRS. The social welfare nonprofit is the one that does not disclose its donors.

Money that travels this way is called dark money, and its scale is no longer speculative. Outside spending in 2024 reached a record 4.5 billion dollars, and more than half came from groups that do not fully disclose their funding.

A fuller accounting, by the Brennan Center, put the dark money of 2024 at a record 1.9 billion dollars. And about 1.3 billion of it was passed into Super PACs, which disclosed only the name of the intermediary.

Notice what that means for the doctrine. Every member of the Citizens United Court except Justice Thomas blessed one regulatory tool, and it is the tool the system now routes around. Not by breaking the law. By stacking two legal regimes, the IRS and the FEC, that were never designed to interlock. The eight to one holding blessed transparency. It did not require Congress or the IRS to close this route.

[pause]

That leaves the referee. Every assumption in the majority opinion points, in the end, at one agency.

The FEC has six seats, with no more than three for either party. It needs four votes to investigate, to make rules, or to find a violation. That design makes deadlock a feature.

One analysis measured it. From 1975 to 2007, the Commission deadlocked on about 5 percent of its votes on enforcement requests, per year. From 2008 to 2019, it was about 24 percent. That is precisely the era when the questions about coordination and dark money arrived. Coordination complaints, including redboxing complaints, have died this way for a decade.

Then the referee left the field entirely. On May 1, 2025, the Commission dropped below its quorum, the four members it needs to act. By early 2026 it was down to two sitting commissioners. It could not open investigations, issue advisory opinions, or resolve cases. Reporters in Washington described an effective shutdown of more than 200 days, and counting.

Two replacement commissioners were nominated. As of mid-2026, the Senate had not confirmed them. And the 2026 midterms were being contested with no functioning federal regulator of campaign finance.

So a complaint about coordination can be filed. It cannot be voted on. No quorum means no investigation and no penalty, while the statute of limitations keeps running.

## What the record means

That is the record: the cases, the wall, the detour, the referee. Now, what does it mean? Was the wall ever real?

Three positions answer, and each gets the same treatment: what it points to, and how it reads the record.

The anti-corruption advocate points to the structure of the Super PAC. The canonical early diagnosis is Richard Briffault's article of 2012. The single-candidate Super PAC, he argued, achieves formal independence without functional independence. It is staffed by the candidate's people, funded by the candidate's donors, and dedicated to the candidate's race. So Buckley's line between expenditures and contributions becomes a pricing rule, unlimited money one step removed, and not a protection against corruption.

Richard Hasen presses a deeper point. The majority resolved an empirical question, whether independent money can corrupt, "as a matter of law." That, he argues, is why no accumulation of practice since 2010 ever forces the doctrine to update.

> The anti-corruption reading: formal independence, without functional independence.

The maximalist points to the results. The strongest statement belongs to Bradley Smith and the Institute for Free Speech school. Fifteen years on, they argue, federal prosecutions for bribery by way of a Super PAC remain rare to nonexistent. Voters receive more information, from more sources, than in any prior era. And the predicted wave of provable corruption has not materialized.

As for the referee, on this reading a weak FEC is a feature. An agency that polices political speech should find it structurally difficult to act, because an aggressive one would be a weapon that incumbents aim at challengers.

> The maximalist reading: adaptation is not evidence of disease.

The disclosure-first reformer points to the detour and to the referee. The regime Kennedy described in 2010 had two parts: vigorous disclosure, and enforced independence. On this reading, that regime was a counterfactual.

> The reformer reading: the holding assumed an enforcement world that has never existed since.

And the data counsel humility in both directions. The measurable effect of money, on outcomes and on roll-call votes, remains smaller and murkier than the rhetoric of either camp implies.

[pause]

Three readings of one record. Choose the one you find least convincing. Then make its best case, from the facts you now have.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Three decisions, two courts. Citizens United opened the spending. SpeechNow, in the D.C. Circuit, opened the fundraising, and that made the Super PAC. McCutcheon struck the aggregate ceiling, and left the base limits standing.

[beat]

Two. The Super PAC rests on one premise: independent spending cannot corrupt. And it rests on a circuit court precedent that the Supreme Court has never examined.

[beat]

Three. The wall. Staffing, solicitation, redboxing, canvassing. All public, all documented, and all, under current enforcement, effectively lawful.

[beat]

Four. The detour. Disclosure follows the dollar to the nonprofit, and there the trail legally ends. In 2024, dark money reached a record 1.9 billion dollars.

[beat]

And five. The referee. The FEC needs four votes out of six. From 2008, it deadlocked far more often than before. And on May 1, 2025, it lost its quorum.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Does the system since 2010 vindicate the majority, or falsify it?

Vindicate: speech expanded, Super PAC donors are disclosed, and there is no proven epidemic of quid pro quo. Falsify: independence engineered into fiction, disclosure laundered through nonprofits, and the referee absent.

Both accounts fit the verified record. They disagree about what the record means. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

One thread remains. Disclosure is the one tool that everyone's Constitution permits. So why has Congress not closed the nonprofit gap, in sixteen years of trying? That fight is the next lecture: the DISCLOSE Act, a case called NAACP v. Alabama, the argument for donor privacy, and the states acting where Washington cannot.

For now, go back to the two numbers. 338 million dollars, from every outside group in the country. 290 million, from one donor. [beat] You can now name every step between them. What those steps add up to is the argument.
