January 2010. In the same opinion that freed corporate spending, Justice Kennedy describes the safety mechanism. It is what made the rest of Citizens United tolerable to its own majority. Listen to the promise in it.

"With the advent of the Internet, prompt disclosure of expenditures can provide shareholders and citizens with the information needed to hold corporations and elected officials accountable."

Citizens, he went on, "can see whether elected officials are 'in the pocket' of so-called moneyed interests."

[pause]

Eight Justices upheld the disclosure rules on that theory: the four dissenters, plus four of the five in the majority.

Now move forward fifteen years. The elections of 2024 ran on a record 1.9 billion dollars whose original sources no citizen can see.

Both things are true. And the distance between them is the most active front in campaign finance politics today. It is also the strangest one.

> On disclosure, the obstacle so far has been Congress, not the Constitution.

This last lecture maps the fight over that gap: whether to close it, or to defend it. By the end you should be able to state three positions at full strength, and to say what the next court case will test.

Four steps. First, what the law requires now, because each camp's argument lives in the mechanics. Second, the three positions, each built the same way. Third, the states, where the experiment is running. And last, a question that cuts across all three: what is disclosure for?

## What the law requires now

Start with the mechanics.

Candidate committees, parties, PACs and Super PACs itemize every donor above 200 dollars, in public filings with the FEC. Outside groups that run election ads report the spending.

But the donors behind the spending of a 501(c)(4), a social-welfare nonprofit, surface only in a narrow case. Under the FEC's regulation, an outside group discloses only those contributions "made for the purpose of furthering" the specific ad it reports.

Made for the purpose of furthering. [beat] A donor avoids that language by simply not earmarking the gift.

So give 10 million dollars to a social-welfare nonprofit, for its general purposes. No federal filing will ever connect your name to the ads it buys, or to the Super PACs it funds.

> Dark money rests on one regulatory phrase, more than on any court holding.

That makes the dispute precise. Should the law trace election money back to its original human source? It already does so for a gift of 201 dollars to a candidate.

Three positions answer. Each gets the same treatment: where it starts, what it protects, what it argues, what it points to, and what would change it.

## The reformer case

Begin with the disclosure-first reformer. This position takes Citizens United at its word: spend what you like, but the public sees who is speaking. And it reads the eight to one holding as a standing invitation, which Congress has declined for sixteen years.

What this position protects is the voter's ability to know who is speaking.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Three steps.

First, the constitutional runway is real. Buckley, in 1976, upheld disclosure on three interests: informing voters, deterring corruption, and enforcing other rules. Citizens United reaffirmed it almost unanimously, in the very opinion that deregulated the spending. And months later, a case called Doe v. Reed upheld the disclosure of who had signed a referendum petition. Again, eight to one.

So no current doctrine, the reformer argues, forbids original-source disclosure of electoral spending. The binding constraints are statutes and regulations.

> The reformer case: the Constitution already said yes.

Second, the legislative fix has been written for sixteen years. It is called the DISCLOSE Act, introduced in 2010 as a direct answer to Citizens United. It would require any group that spends meaningfully on elections to reveal its large donors, and to trace money through intermediary shells.

It passed the House in June 2010, by 219 votes to 206. Then it died by filibuster. In the Senate, the vote to end debate failed that July, with 57 in favor. It failed again that September, with 59. [beat] One vote short.

Its descendants have failed the same way every time. The most recent was folded into the Freedom to Vote Act, of 2021 and 2022.

So the reformer's point is this. A policy with majority support in both chambers has been blocked for a decade and a half by a supermajority rule, and not by the First Amendment.

Third, the evidence this position points to. The standing objection is that disclosure chills speech. The best causal study is by Wood and Spencer, from 2016. It exploited the variation in state disclosure regimes, and it found the chilling effect to be negligible.

On average, less than one donor per candidate stops giving when contributions become publicly visible. And there was no outsized effect on small donors, or on ideological outliers. Disclosure, on this evidence, informs voters without silencing donors.

What would change this position? A showing that modern disclosure produces retaliation at scale. Not awkwardness. Boycotts, threats and job loss, systematic enough to deter ordinary participation. Honest reformers concede that such a line exists. The argument is about whether today's donors are anywhere near it.

Let's test this. A senator says: "We can't pass the DISCLOSE Act. The Supreme Court held in Citizens United that political spending is protected speech."

What is wrong with that sentence, as a matter of record? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It collapses two separate holdings.

The five to four holding protected the spending. A separate holding, eight to one, upheld disclosure of that spending as constitutional, and four of the five Justices in the majority joined it. Kennedy wrote that transparency "enables the electorate to make informed decisions and give proper weight to different speakers and messages."

So whatever blocks a law on original-source disclosure, it is not Citizens United. Since 2010 the obstacle has been the Senate's 60-vote threshold, never a court.

## The donor-privacy case

Now the donor-privacy advocate, built the same way. And first, what this case is not. It is not the claim that rich people deserve secrecy. It starts from a precedent of the civil rights era, about what majorities do to dissenters they can identify.

The case is NAACP v. Alabama, from 1958. Alabama's attorney general demanded the NAACP's membership lists. A unanimous Supreme Court refused. Members who were identified faced economic reprisal, threats and violence. And compelled disclosure of association, the Court held, would abridge rights that the state could not touch directly.

What this position protects, then, is the identified dissenter. Anonymous association, the Court held, can be the condition of free association. The American tradition has honored that principle since the Federalist Papers ran under the name Publius.

> The donor-privacy case: anonymity can be the condition of free association.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Three steps.

First, the logic is viewpoint-neutral. Take a donor to an LGBT-rights group, in a hostile county. Take a donor to a traditional-marriage group, in a hostile industry. They face the same mechanism: disclosure converts giving into a target list. And privacy advocates note that doxxing and retaliation at work are cheaper and faster in the internet era Kennedy celebrated than they were in 1958.

Second, the doctrine has been re-armed. In 2021, in Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, the Court struck down a California rule, six to three. California had made a blanket demand: charities must file their lists of major donors with the state attorney general. The filings were nominally confidential. The Court struck the rule down anyway.

Chief Justice Roberts's opinion requires what is called exacting scrutiny, with narrow tailoring, for any compelled donor disclosure. It found a dragnet collection unconstitutional on its face.

Bonta concerned the regulation of charities, not elections. But its tailoring requirement is the template that every future challenge to a DISCLOSE-style law will invoke.

Third, the line between election speech and issue speech will not hold. DISCLOSE-style bills reach groups that mix election ads with issue advocacy. The Institute for Free Speech argues that the practical effect is to force membership lists out of advocacy nonprofits, whose core work is constitutionally protected issue speech. That, it argues, is exactly the NAACP scenario, with modern paperwork.

What would change this position? Data showing that disclosed electoral donors do not, in fact, suffer meaningful retaliation. That is why the Wood and Spencer finding is the study privacy advocates must answer. And they answer it by pointing to the tails: the rare but severe cases, and not the average.

## The maximalist case

Third, the First Amendment maximalist. On this question the position is an outlier, and on the Court it belongs to one Justice. Justice Thomas, alone in 2010 and consistent since, rejects the compromise that both of the other camps accept.

What this position protects is anonymous political speech itself.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Disclosure itself chills speech. So the Court, on this view, should have struck down the statute's disclosure section, section 201, along with section 203.

> The maximalist case: disclosure itself chills speech.

And here is what this position points to. In his partial dissent in Citizens United, Thomas cited the harassment of donors to California's Proposition 8. For the tradition, there is his concurrence in a 1995 case, McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission, which protected anonymous political pamphleteering. The political culture of the Framers, on this account, prized anonymous speech.

It remains a minority position. But Bonta moved the median Justice toward it. And the coalition that decided disclosure eight to one, in 2010, no longer exists on today's Court.

[pause]

Six decisions have now gone by, some for transparency and some for anonymity. Can you put them in order, and say which way each one pointed? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Lay them on one rail.

In 1958, NAACP v. Alabama: for anonymity. In 1976, Buckley: for disclosure, with exemptions kept for groups that are genuinely threatened. In 1995, McIntyre: for anonymity. In 2010, Citizens United and Doe v. Reed, twin votes of eight to one: for transparency. And in 2021, Bonta: for anonymity.

The anonymity cases and the transparency cases alternate, which is why each camp claims momentum. And the most recent word belongs to anonymity.

Which better describes the current majority: Doe v. Reed, or Bonta? Candidly, that is unknown. It is precisely what the next case will find out.

Three positions, then. Choose the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its strongest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## The states move first

With the FEC lacking a quorum, and the Senate stuck at 60, the experiments are in the states. The most ambitious is in Arizona.

Proposition 211, the Voters' Right to Know Act, was adopted by ballot initiative in 2022, with about 72 percent of the vote.

Its mechanism is original-source tracing. Any group that spends more than 50,000 dollars on a statewide race must publicly identify the true original contributors of more than 5,000 dollars. And it must do so no matter how many nonprofit intermediaries the money passed through.

It is, in miniature, the DISCLOSE Act model: aimed at the pipeline, not at the speech.

And the litigation is the doctrinal test in motion. The challengers are two Arizona groups, represented by the Goldwater Institute, on behalf of donors who say they fear harassment. They argue that Proposition 211 violates the anonymity rights of NAACP and Bonta, and Arizona's own constitution.

As of mid-2026, the law had survived its early rounds and remained in effect, with proceedings in the state supreme court still unresolved.

Watch it. A square ruling for Proposition 211 gives every state a blueprint. A ruling against it, on Bonta grounds, would for the first time turn the donor-privacy doctrine loose on electoral disclosure. And it would put the question on a path back to a Supreme Court far friendlier to anonymity than the one that voted eight to one in 2010.

So here is a question for you. Arizona's Proposition 211 and California's scheme in Bonta both compelled information about donors. One survives, so far. One was struck down. What is the difference that a court would seize on? Think it through.

[long pause]

Tailoring, and interest.

California collected the donor list of every large charity, confidentially, for general convenience in preventing fraud. That is a dragnet with no connection to elections, and it failed Bonta's requirement of narrow tailoring.

Proposition 211 discloses donors only above high thresholds, and only for money actually spent on campaign media. There, three decisions recognize a distinct interest, the voters' interest in information: Buckley, Citizens United, and Doe v. Reed.

The open question is how today's Court will take Bonta. As confined to that distinction? Or as a general charter of donor anonymity, which swallows it?

## What is disclosure for?

One more lens, and it cuts across all three positions. It comes from Richard Briffault, in an article of 2010 on campaign finance disclosure.

Disclosure, he argues, serves different masters, and they pull in different directions.

One is voter information. That rationale justifies publicizing the big spenders behind ads. And it implies high thresholds, because voters gain nothing from a database of 25-dollar donors.

The other is anti-corruption. That rationale justifies tracing large money to the benefactors of officeholders.

Regimes get into trouble, Briffault argues, when they pursue one rationale with the other's tools. Itemizing the addresses of schoolteachers serves no voter. And letting eight-figure gifts hide behind nonprofit shells serves no theory at all.

That design point quietly indicts the maximal versions of both camps. And most serious proposals since bear his fingerprints, including the high thresholds of Proposition 211.

Under the whole debate sits the Wood and Spencer finding, as an empirical floor: the average chill is negligible. And above that floor, the argument is about the tails.

> The unresolved question: whether average effects or tail risks should drive constitutional doctrine.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The mechanics. Dark money rests on one regulatory phrase, more than on any court holding. A donor who does not earmark the gift is never named.

[beat]

Two. The reformer case: the Constitution already said yes. Eight to one, in the very opinion that freed the spending. The fix has been written for sixteen years, and the Senate's 60-vote threshold has stopped it.

[beat]

Three. The donor-privacy case: anonymity can be the condition of free association. It runs from NAACP v. Alabama, in 1958, to Bonta, in 2021, and its demand for narrow tailoring.

[beat]

Four. The maximalist case: disclosure itself chills speech. One Justice holds it. But the Court that voted eight to one is not today's Court.

[beat]

And five. The test in motion is Arizona's Proposition 211, which traces election money to its original source. As of mid-2026 it was in effect, with challenges pending.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled.

It has not settled whether original-source disclosure would, in fact, change the behavior of voters or of officeholders. The informational benefit is better theorized than measured.

It has not settled where NAACP's protection ends, and the electorate's right to know begins. Bonta guaranteed that the line will be relitigated, and the Court that draws it is not the Court of 2010.

And it has not settled the deepest irony of this whole series. Go back to Kennedy's promise, in 2010: spending without limit, and citizens who can see.

> Unlimited but transparent independent spending is a regime no American election has yet conducted.

That is the regime Citizens United actually authorized.

So this is where the lectures end, and where the argument goes next. A defensible position on Citizens United does four things. It states the holding mechanically. It picks a frame, and names its empirical bets. It reckons with fifteen years of evidence. And it says what it would do about disclosure, and what evidence about chill, retaliation or voter information would change its mind.

[beat]

That last step is yours.
