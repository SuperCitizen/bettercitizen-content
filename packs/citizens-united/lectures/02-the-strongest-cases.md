Two sentences. Both were written on the same day, in January 2010. Both are still carried into battle.

The first is Justice Kennedy's, for the majority. When government uses "censorship to control thought," it has claimed a power that no free society grants. And a statute that could reach films, and by the government's own early argument books, was exactly that.

The second is Justice Stevens's, closing a dissent of ninety pages. "While American democracy is imperfect, few outside the majority of this Court would have thought its flaws included a dearth of corporate money in politics."

[pause]

Censorship to control thought. A dearth of corporate money in politics.

Each sentence is devastating against a weak version of the other side. Neither one touches the strong version.

So this lecture builds the strong versions. Last time, the mechanics. This time, the argument. By the end you should be able to state each position the way its smartest adherents state it, and to say which claims about the evidence are settled and which are not.

Five stops. The First Amendment maximalist case. The anti-corruption case. The democratic-equality case. Then a body of scholarship that refuses all three. And last, where the evidence stands.

Each position gets the same treatment: where it starts, what it protects, what it argues, and what would change it.

## The First Amendment maximalist case

Begin with the First Amendment maximalist. The starting move is the text.

The First Amendment says "Congress shall make no law," and then the words that matter here: "abridging the freedom of speech." It does not say "the freedom of natural-person speech." It makes no exception for speakers whose speech has political consequences. The text is categorical, and the strongest version of this position takes a categorical text seriously.

What this position protects is political speech itself, whoever the speaker is.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Three steps.

First, discriminating among speakers is the textbook First Amendment violation. Section 203 banned political speech by corporations and unions, while it permitted the same speech by individuals and by media corporations. That treats speakers differently because of who they are. A long line of cases makes such discrimination presumptively unconstitutional.

And this ban did not punish falsehoods after the fact. It silenced speech in advance, under threat of criminal penalty, in the weeks before the vote, when political speech matters most.

Second, Austin's anti-distortion rationale had no limiting principle. Austin let government suppress speech because corporate wealth might distort the marketplace of ideas. But The New York Times is a corporation. Random House is a corporation. So are the ACLU, the Sierra Club and Planned Parenthood.

If government may suppress political speech because the speaker commands too much institutional wealth, then the media exemption Congress wrote into the statute is a press license. And a license is something the government may someday decline to renew. On this view, the book-banning concession at oral argument was not a gaffe. It was the rationale's honest endpoint.

Third, independent spending does not corrupt, because there is no quid pro quo. Buckley accepted that for individuals in 1976, and the maximalist argues that a corporate form adds nothing. No coordination, no exchange. No exchange, no quid pro quo corruption in the constitutional sense. Stretch the appearance of corruption to cover influence and access, and it would license regulating any speech an incumbent finds too persuasive.

[pause]

Now the strongest version. It is not that money is speech. That is the critics' caricature. It is this.

> The maximalist case: the means of amplification are inseparable from the speech itself.

A leaflet costs paper. A documentary costs production. Airtime costs money. Tell a citizen "You may speak, but you may not spend to be heard," and you have hollowed out the right for every speaker who is not already famous.

Bradley Smith, a former chairman of the FEC, makes this case at book length, in Unfree Speech. He adds a twist that the dissents never fully answered. Campaign finance rules are written by incumbents. And they reliably burden challengers, who need to buy name recognition, more than the officeholders who are already known.

What would change this position? Documented quid pro quo corruption, traceable to spending that was genuinely independent, at a volume that suggests systemic capture. Maximalists point to the record since 2010, in prosecutions, in FEC matters and in journalism, and hold that it has not shown this.

Let's put this case to work. A state, alarmed by the wealth of the tech industry, bans political ads funded by any company with more than 1 billion dollars in revenue. But it exempts what it calls "bona fide news organizations."

Which of the maximalist's arguments does that law walk into? And which precedent would it need, to survive? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It walks into the first argument: discrimination among speakers. The law silences some speakers, the large companies outside the media, and licenses others, the media corporations. That is exactly the line Citizens United said cannot be drawn.

To survive, the state would need Austin's anti-distortion rationale. And Austin is the one precedent that Citizens United explicitly overruled.

For the maximalist, the exemption is the tell. A government that must define bona fide news, to make its ban workable, has become a licensor of the press.

## The anti-corruption case

Now the anti-corruption advocate, built the same way. The starting move is to refuse the maximalist's premise: that independent means independent.

The line between expenditures and contributions looked clean in Buckley. In practice, this position argues, it has collapsed. And the majority's holding rests on the empirical claim that it holds.

What this position protects is the integrity of representation, and the public's confidence in it.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Four steps.

First, Super PACs in practice are not independent. They are founded by candidates' former aides. They share consultants, vendors and pollsters. They are blessed at the candidates' own fundraisers. And each dedicates itself to a single candidate, behind a wall that exists mainly on paper.

Set that beside the pivotal sentence of Kennedy's opinion: that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." That was an empirical assertion, offered "as a matter of law."

Second, the appearance of corruption is no late invention. It is in Buckley itself. The Court of 1976 treated public confidence in the system as a constitutionally sufficient interest. Democracy is harmed when citizens reasonably conclude that access and outcomes are for sale, whether or not a prosecutor could prove the sale. The majority narrowed that standard, to the appearance of quid pro quo. The dissent argues that the harm to legitimacy is real at the level of the system, even when every single transaction is lawful.

Third, the record. Stevens's dissent runs through a century of congressional fact-finding, from the Tillman Act of 1907 forward. Congress repeatedly studied corporate election spending, and concluded that it corrupts. Stevens faults the majority for resolving a contested empirical question without a factual record.

Fourth, a wider name for the harm. In a 2011 book called Republic, Lost, Lessig calls it dependence corruption. Representatives become dependent "on the funders" rather than, in the phrase of Federalist 52, "on the people alone." No bribe occurs. The distortion is in whom officeholders spend their days courting, and so in which problems exist for them at all.

> The anti-corruption case: dependence on funders corrupts, even when no bribe occurs.

On this view, the majority did not merely get its facts wrong. It wrote into the Constitution a definition of corruption too narrow to describe the actual disease.

What does this position point to? Richard Briffault and Richard Hasen document the slippage between independence on paper and independence in practice. And the FEC's failure to enforce its coordination rules, the advocate argues, has effectively ratified it.

And what would change it? A showing that the spending since 2010 has produced better-informed voters, and no measurable shift in access or agenda. Or a showing that the corruption it fears cannot be told apart, even in principle, from responsiveness to constituents.

The strongest advocates concede that telling them apart is hard. A 2005 review of the research, by Stratmann, found the claim that money buys votes far shakier than reform rhetoric implies. And a 2003 study by Ansolabehere and two colleagues found campaign money behaving more like ideological consumption than like investment.

So the honest version of the case has migrated. It no longer says that money buys roll-call votes. It says that money buys the agenda, the access and the candidate pool. Those claims are harder to test, and harder to dismiss.

## The democratic-equality case

Third, the democratic-equality advocate. Same treatment. And the starting move is to change the battlefield.

This position does not fight on Citizens United. It fights on Buckley v. Valeo. It holds that the Citizens United majority was largely right, given Buckley, and that Buckley was wrong. Being right from a wrong premise produced the system we have.

What this position protects is political equality: the principle that a citizen's political influence should not scale with wealth.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Three steps.

First, political equality is itself a constitutional value. One person, one vote, the rule of a 1964 case called Reynolds v. Sims, expresses that commitment in the structure of government. Buckley famously retorted the other way. Listen for two words: wholly foreign.

"The concept that government may restrict the speech of some elements of our society in order to enhance the relative voice of others [beat] is wholly foreign to the First Amendment."

[pause]

Wholly foreign to the First Amendment. The equality school answers that it is not foreign to the Constitution, which guarantees a republican form of government, and equal protection. Treating the First Amendment as the only clause in the room, it argues, was the original error.

> The democratic-equality case: political equality is itself a constitutional value.

Cass Sunstein develops the position in a 1993 book, and Justice Breyer's writing on active liberty carries it onto the Court.

Second, the free-speech tradition of the Framers protected dissent, not markets in amplification. The move from "government may not punish your views" to "government may not limit what anyone spends amplifying views" happened in 1976, with little founding-era warrant either way. So the question, on this view, is one of democratic design, not of text.

Third, the trade-off is a judgment about values, and other democracies strike it differently. Here is what this position points to. Canada caps election advertising by third parties. The U.K. caps campaign spending, and bans paid political ads on television. Both hold competitive elections, with robust dissent.

The point of the comparison is not that those systems are right. It is that the American arrangement is a choice, and not a logical entailment of free expression.

And what would change this position? A showing that rules motivated by equality, in practice, entrench incumbents. That is the standing conservative critique, and a serious one: a spending cap freezes the advantage of name recognition in place. Or a showing that such rules suppress speech that voters demonstrably needed.

The strongest equality advocates accept the risk of entrenching incumbents, and design around it. They propose small-donor vouchers, or public matching funds of the kind New York City uses.

Now set the second and third positions side by side, because they are easy to blur. The anti-corruption advocate accepts Buckley, and says Citizens United misapplied it. The democratic-equality advocate says Buckley itself is the error. So what different remedies do the two positions imply? Think it through.

[long pause]

The anti-corruption position needs only a repair inside Buckley. Real enforcement of the coordination rules. A broader reading of appearance, still centered on corruption. Disclosure with teeth. Overrule the empirical premise of Citizens United, and the rest of the framework stands.

The democratic-equality position needs Buckley itself displaced. That could come by constitutional amendment. It could come from a Court willing to accept political equality as a compelling interest. Or it could route around the doctrine entirely, through public financing, which adds voices instead of capping them, and is constitutional today.

> Diagnosis determines prescription.

## Three bets

Three positions. Hold them side by side, each compressed to the version its adherents would sign. Each one ends in a bet about the evidence.

You have heard what would change each one's mind. So what is each one betting on? See whether you can say all three.

[long pause]

The maximalist bet: genuinely independent spending does not produce quid pro quo corruption at any systemic scale. Fifteen years of prosecutions and FEC files have not shown it.

[beat]

The anti-corruption bet: access and agendas measurably track big independent money. The wall is a fiction that the record keeps confirming.

[beat]

The democratic-equality bet: rules that protect equality can be designed, with vouchers and matching funds, so that they neither entrench incumbents nor starve voters of information.

[pause]

Now choose the position you find least sympathetic. State its best case, the way its smartest adherent would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## A fourth voice

One influential body of scholarship refuses all three framings. Call it the institutionalist case. Its authors are Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner, in a book of 2015.

They argue that the great post-Watergate project backfired. That project capped contributions, and above all it throttled the money of the parties. But the money did not leave politics.

> The institutionalist case: money did not leave politics. It rerouted.

It moved away from party committees, which are pragmatic and minded toward electability. It moved to ideological outside groups and to donors the authors call purists, who fund the most polarizing candidates.

What they point to is the variation across fifty states. They find that finance systems centered on parties are associated with less polarized legislatures.

And here is their heresy. Citizens United is not the disease. It is a symptom, of a regime that starved the institutions best at moderating politics. So the cure is to deregulate the parties, not to cap the outside groups again.

What would change it? Evidence that party-centered finance does not moderate, or that polarization is driven by demand.

You do not have to accept the argument. You do have to locate it, because it scrambles the usual sides. It agrees with reformers that the system since 2010 is pathological. And it agrees with maximalists that the cure is less restriction, at least for parties.

## What the evidence settles

All four positions invoke the evidence. So where is the evidence, in fact?

Three things are reasonably settled. Independent spending has grown enormously since 2010, and the 2024 cycle set the record. Its donor base is extraordinarily concentrated. And spending routed through certain nonprofits is far less transparent than spending by committees.

Three things are contested. Whether outside spending changes who wins, relative to the fundamentals of an election. Whether dependence corruption is real, and measurable apart from quid pro quo. And whether the rules before 2010 were preventing the harms now blamed on their absence, or merely hiding them, in soft money and sham issue ads.

Those are contested questions, not settled ones.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The maximalist case: the means of amplification are inseparable from the speech itself. A ban that turns on who is speaking is censorship.

[beat]

Two. The anti-corruption case: dependence on funders corrupts, even when no bribe occurs. The independence is a fiction in practice.

[beat]

Three. The democratic-equality case: political equality is itself a constitutional value. Its quarrel is with Buckley itself. The anti-corruption case would repair Buckley. This one would displace it. Diagnosis determines prescription.

[beat]

Four. The institutionalist case: money did not leave politics. It rerouted, from the parties to the purists. Its cure is to deregulate the parties.

[beat]

And five. The evidence. It is reasonably settled that independent spending has grown enormously since 2010. It is contested whether that spending changes who wins.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled: which frame is right. So what does a defensible position look like? It picks a frame, and says why. It names the empirical claims it is betting on. And it identifies the evidence that would change it.

> A defensible position names its bets, and the evidence that would change it.

The next lecture supplies the record: fifteen years of it, since 2010. It is the record that every one of these bets is betting against.

For now, go back to the two sentences we began with. Censorship to control thought. A dearth of corporate money in politics. [beat] You can now meet each of them with the strong version of the other side.
