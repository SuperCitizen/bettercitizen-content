## A 500-page book

On March 24, 2009, a government lawyer stood before the Supreme Court to defend a law. It made it a federal crime for a corporation to pay for a broadcast naming a federal candidate within thirty days of a primary. The case was about a film, Hillary: The Movie, made by a nonprofit corporation called Citizens United.

Chief Justice Roberts asked about a 500-page book that ends "and so vote for X." Could the government ban that? The answer: "we could prohibit the publication of the book using the corporate treasury funds."

- March 24, 2009: a film, then a book

## The holding

On January 21, 2010, by five votes to four, with Justice Kennedy writing for the majority and Justice Stevens in dissent, the Court held that the government may not ban political speech because the speaker is a corporation or a union.

- January 21, 2010: 5–4

That sentence is the holding. Not "corporations are people," not "money is speech," not "unlimited donations."

> Government may not ban political speech because the speaker is a corporation or union.

## One wall moved

Since Buckley v. Valeo, in 1976, the law has run on two tracks. Contributions, money given to a candidate, can be capped. Independent expenditures, money spent on your own advocacy without coordination, cannot be capped for individuals.

Citizens United did not move the first track. Corporate and union treasury contributions to federal candidates were illegal before, and they are illegal today. The case extended the second track to corporations and unions. They may spend any amount on their own ads, so long as the spending is not coordinated with any campaign.

- Contributions to candidates: still banned
- Independent spending: now unlimited

> It widened who may spend independently, not who may give to candidates.

## What it did not do

The same opinion upheld disclosure and disclaimer rules, 8 to 1, with only Justice Thomas dissenting on that point.

- Disclosure upheld 8–1

And it did not create the Super PAC. That was SpeechNow.org v. FEC, from a different court, two months later.

- Super PAC: SpeechNow.org, March 2010

## Why it stays contested

Three positions dispute the ruling. First Amendment maximalists argue the holding is the only one consistent with a categorical text. Anti-corruption advocates argue the majority's premise, that independent spending cannot corrupt, was asserted and not demonstrated. Democratic-equality advocates argue the deepest error came earlier, in Buckley.

- Maximalists: Stevens's dissent was wrong
- Anti-corruption: Kennedy's facts were wrong
- Democratic equality: the whole frame was wrong

None of them disputes the mechanics. The lesson has the rest: the precedents that fell, everything that stood, and how one answer about books turned a small case about a film into this one.
