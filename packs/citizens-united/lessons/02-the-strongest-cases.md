# The strongest case for and against

Two sentences, both from the same day in January 2010, are still doing duty as battle standards. Justice Kennedy, for the majority: when government uses "censorship to control thought," it has claimed a power no free society grants — and a statute that could reach films, and by the government's own early argument books, was exactly that. Justice Stevens, closing his ninety-page dissent: "While American democracy is imperfect, few outside the majority of this Court would have thought its flaws included a dearth of corporate money in politics." Each sentence is devastating against a weak version of the other side. Neither touches the strong version. This lesson builds the strong versions — three positions, each stated the way its smartest adherents state it, so you can engage the argument rather than a caricature.

## 1. The First Amendment maximalist case

The starting move is textual. The First Amendment says "Congress shall make no law … abridging the freedom of speech." It does not say "the freedom of natural-person speech," or "the freedom of speech of those whose speech does not have political consequences." The text is categorical, and the strongest version of this position takes the categorical text seriously.

The doctrinal scaffolding:

- **Speaker-based discrimination is the textbook First Amendment violation.** Banning corporate and union political speech while permitting individual and media-corporation speech treats speakers differently based on identity. A long doctrinal line — *Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley* (1972) through *Reed v. Town of Gilbert* (2015) — makes such discrimination presumptively unconstitutional. BCRA §203 was speaker-based by design; strict scrutiny applies, and near-zero laws survive it.

- **The "anti-distortion" rationale of *Austin* had no limiting principle.** *Austin* let government suppress speech because corporate wealth might "distort" the marketplace of ideas. But The New York Times is a corporation. Random House is a corporation. The ACLU and the Sierra Club and Planned Parenthood are corporations. If government may suppress political speech because the speaker commands too much institutional wealth, the media exemption Congress wrote into the statute is a press license the government may someday decline to renew. The book-banning concession at oral argument (Lesson 1) was not a gaffe; it was the rationale's honest endpoint.

- **Independent expenditures don't corrupt, because there is no quid pro quo.** *Buckley* accepted this for individuals in 1976. The maximalist claim is that the speaker's corporate form adds no corruption: no coordination, no exchange; no exchange, no quid-pro-quo corruption in the constitutional sense. The "appearance of corruption" gloss, stretched to cover *influence* and *access*, would license regulating any speech an incumbent finds too persuasive.

- **Censorship's mechanics matter.** §203 did not punish falsehoods after the fact; it silenced speech *in advance*, in the precise window — the weeks before the vote — when political speech matters most, under threat of criminal penalty, subject to an FEC clearance process the majority likened to licensing.

The strongest version of this position is not "money is speech" — that's the critics' caricature. It is: *the means of amplification are inseparable from the speech itself.* A leaflet costs paper; a documentary costs production; airtime costs money. "You may speak, but you may not spend to be heard" hollows the right for every speaker who is not already famous. Bradley Smith — a former FEC chairman — makes this argument at book length in *Unfree Speech* (2001), adding a public-choice twist the dissents never fully answered: campaign-finance rules are written by incumbents, and they reliably burden challengers, who need to buy name recognition, more than the already-known officeholders who regulate them.

What evidence would change this position? Documented quid-pro-quo corruption traceable to genuinely *independent* expenditures, at a volume suggesting systemic capture. Maximalists hold that the record since 2010 — prosecutions, FEC matters, journalism — has not shown this.

## 2. The anti-corruption case

The starting move is to refuse the maximalist's premise that "independent" means independent. The categorical distinction between expenditures and contributions, however clean it looked in *Buckley*, has collapsed in practice — and the majority's holding rests on the empirical claim that it holds.

The scaffolding, steel-manned:

- **Super PACs in practice are not independent.** They are founded by candidates' former aides, share consultants, vendors, and pollsters, are blessed at candidate "fire-side" fundraisers, and dedicate themselves to a single candidate — with a "wall" between committee and campaign that exists mainly on paper. Kennedy's pivotal sentence — that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption" — was an empirical assertion offered "as a matter of law." Richard Briffault's "Super PACs" (2012) and Richard Hasen's "Citizens United and the Illusion of Coherence" (2011) document the slippage; the FEC's non-enforcement of coordination rules (Lesson 3) has effectively ratified it.

- **"Appearance of corruption" is not category creep — it is in *Buckley* itself.** The 1976 Court treated public confidence in the system as a constitutionally sufficient interest: democracy is harmed when citizens reasonably conclude access and outcomes are for sale, whether or not a prosecutor could prove the sale. The majority read "appearance" down to appearance-of-quid-pro-quo; the dissent's argument is that legitimacy harms are real at the system level even when every individual transaction is lawful. Samuel Issacharoff's "On Political Corruption" (2010) sharpens the point: the constitutional question was never only bribery, but the integrity of representation.

- **Stevens marshaled a record the majority did not engage.** His dissent runs through a century of congressional fact-finding — from the Tillman Act (1907) forward — in which Congress repeatedly studied corporate election spending and concluded it corrupts, and faults the majority for resolving a contested empirical question without a factual record, in a case the parties had originally litigated on narrow grounds.

- **Lessig's reframe: dependence corruption.** *Republic, Lost* (2011) names the harm *Buckley*'s definition cannot see: representatives who are dependent "on the funders" rather than — Federalist 52's phrase — "on the people alone." No bribe occurs; the distortion is in whom officeholders spend their days courting and which problems therefore exist for them. On this view the majority did not merely err on facts; it constitutionalized a definition of corruption too narrow to describe the actual disease.

What evidence would change this position? A showing that post-2010 spending has produced better-informed voters and no measurable shift in access or agenda — or that the corruption it fears cannot be distinguished, even in principle, from responsiveness to constituents. The strongest advocates concede the discrimination problem is hard: Stratmann's literature review (2005) finds the "money buys votes" claim far shakier than reform rhetoric implies, and Ansolabehere, de Figueiredo, and Snyder (2003) found campaign money behaving more like ideological consumption than investment. The honest anti-corruption case has migrated accordingly — from "money buys roll-call votes" to "money buys the agenda, the access, and the candidate pool," claims harder to test and harder to dismiss.

## 3. The democratic-equality case

The starting move is to fight not on *Citizens United* but on *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976). This position holds that the *Citizens United* majority was largely right *given Buckley* — and that *Buckley* was wrong. Being right from a wrong premise produced the system we have.

The argument:

- **Political equality is itself a constitutional value.** One person, one vote (*Reynolds v. Sims*, 1964) expresses a structural commitment: citizens' political influence should not scale with their wealth. *Buckley* famously retorted that "the concept that government may restrict the speech of some elements of our society in order to enhance the relative voice of others is wholly foreign to the First Amendment." The equality school answers: it is not foreign to the *Constitution* — which guarantees a republican form of government and equal protection — and treating the First Amendment as the only clause in the room was the original error. Cass Sunstein's *Democracy and the Problem of Free Speech* (1993) develops the position; Justice Breyer's writing on "active liberty" carries it onto the Court.

- **The Framers' free-speech tradition protected dissent, not amplification markets.** Doctrinally, the move from "government may not punish your views" to "government may not limit what anyone spends amplifying views" happened in 1976, with little founding-era warrant either way. The equality school wants courts to admit the question is one of democratic design, not text.

- **The trade-off is a values judgment, and other democracies strike it differently.** Canada caps third-party election advertising; the U.K. caps campaign spending and bans paid political TV ads; France bans paid broadcast political advertising and caps candidate spending, while Germany leans on public funding and allocated free airtime. All hold competitive elections and robust dissent. The comparative point is not that those systems are right — it's that the American arrangement is a *choice*, not a logical entailment of free expression.

What evidence would change this position? A showing that equality-motivated rules in practice entrench incumbents (the standing conservative critique, and a serious one: spending caps freeze the name-recognition advantage in place), or that they suppress speech voters demonstrably needed. The strongest equality advocates accept the entrenchment risk and design around it — Lessig's small-donor vouchers, public matching à la New York City — rather than waving it off.

## A fourth voice: the institutionalists

One influential body of scholarship refuses all three framings. Raymond La Raja and Brian Schaffner (*Campaign Finance and Political Polarization: When Purists Prevail*, 2015) argue the great post-Watergate project — capping contributions, throttling *party* money above all — backfired: money did not leave politics, it **rerouted** from pragmatic, electability-minded party committees to ideological outside groups and "purist" donors, who fund the most polarizing candidates. Drawing on fifty states' worth of variation, they find party-centered finance systems associated with less polarized legislatures. Their heresy: *Citizens United* is not the disease but a symptom of a regime that starved the institutions best at moderating politics — and the cure is to *deregulate the parties*, not to re-cap the outside groups. You do not have to accept the argument; you do have to locate it, because it scrambles the usual sides — agreeing with reformers that the post-2010 system is pathological, and with maximalists that the cure is less restriction, at least for parties. The Supreme Court's pending party-spending case, *NRSC v. FEC* (argued December 2025), is this literature's day in court.

## What's settled and what isn't

All three (or four) positions invoke "the evidence." Here is where the evidence actually is:

| Position | Core claim | Key authority | Would be falsified by… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maximalist | Speaker-identity bans are censorship; independence defuses corruption | First Amendment text; *Buckley*; Smith (2001) | Proven quid-pro-quo via genuinely independent spending, at scale |
| Anti-corruption | "Independence" is fictional in practice; dependence corrupts without bribery | Stevens dissent; Lessig (2011); Briffault (2012) | Evidence access/agendas don't track big donors |
| Democratic-equality | *Buckley* wrongly excluded political equality as a constitutional value | Sunstein (1993); comparative practice | Evidence equality rules entrench incumbents or starve voters of speech |
| Institutionalist | Limits rerouted money from moderating parties to polarizing purists | La Raja & Schaffner (2015) | Evidence party-centered finance doesn't moderate, or polarization is demand-driven |

**Reasonably settled:** independent spending has grown enormously since 2010 (the 2024 cycle set the record — Lesson 3 has the verified numbers); its donor base is extraordinarily concentrated; 501(c)(4) spending is far less transparent than committee spending. **Genuinely contested:** whether outside spending changes electoral *outcomes* relative to fundamentals (Stratmann 2005; Ansolabehere et al. 2003 counsel humility); whether dependence corruption is real and measurable apart from quid pro quo; whether pre-2010 rules were preventing the harms now attributed to their absence — or merely hiding them in soft money and sham issue ads.

A defensible position picks a frame, says why, names the empirical claims it is betting on, and identifies what evidence would change it. The next lesson supplies the fifteen years of record you'll be betting against.
