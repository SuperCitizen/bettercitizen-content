# The strongest case for and against

This lesson presents three positions at their strongest. The goal is not to balance them as if all three are equally true; it's to make each version one its adherents would themselves endorse, so a learner can engage the position rather than a caricature.

## 1. The First Amendment maximalist case

The starting move is textual. The First Amendment says "Congress shall make no law … abridging the freedom of speech." It does not say "the freedom of natural-person speech," or "the freedom of speech of those whose speech does not have political consequences." The text is categorical, and the strongest version of this position takes the categorical text seriously.

The doctrinal scaffolding:

- **Speaker-based discrimination is the textbook First Amendment violation.** Striking down corporate political speech while permitting individual political speech, or media-corporation political speech, or union political speech, treats different speakers differently based on identity. *Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley* (1972), *Reed v. Town of Gilbert* (2015), and a long line of cases hold this is presumptively unconstitutional. BCRA §203 was speaker-based — it banned corporate and union speech but not individual or media speech. Strict scrutiny applies; near-zero laws survive it.

- **The "anti-distortion" rationale Austin relied on is dangerous.** Austin let government suppress speech because corporate wealth might "distort" the marketplace of ideas. The maximalist case is that this rationale has no limiting principle. The New York Times is a corporation. Random House is a corporation. The ACLU is a corporation. If the government can suppress political speech because the speaker has too much wealth or institutional power, it can suppress *any* speech.

- **Independent expenditures don't corrupt because there's no quid pro quo.** Buckley v. Valeo accepted this in 1976 for individuals. The maximalist's claim is that the same logic applies to corporations: if there's no coordination, there's no exchange, and without an exchange there's no corruption in the constitutional sense. The "appearance of corruption" gloss the dissents reach for is a category-creep that, if accepted, would let the government regulate any speech it thinks looks too influential.

- **Prior restraint is the worst kind of First Amendment violation.** BCRA §203 didn't punish bad speech after the fact; it banned political speech *before* it could be heard, in the most consequential window — the thirty days before a primary, when voters are actually paying attention. Justice Kennedy's opinion calls this out directly (slip op. at 49–50): "When the Government seeks to use its full power, including the criminal law, to command where a person may get his or her information or what distrusted source he or she may not hear, it uses censorship to control thought."

The strongest version of this position is not "money is speech" — that's a slogan critics use to caricature it. It's "the means by which speech is amplified are inseparable from the speech itself, and government regulation of those means is regulation of speech." A leaflet costs paper; a TV ad costs airtime; a documentary costs production. To say "you may speak but you may not spend money to be heard" is to render the right hollow for any speaker who isn't already heard. Brad Smith makes this argument cleanly in *Unfree Speech*.

What evidence would change this position? Documented quid-pro-quo corruption directly traceable to *independent* expenditures (not coordinated ones), in volume sufficient to suggest the system is being captured rather than informed. Maximalists generally hold that the empirical record so far does not show this.

## 2. The anti-corruption case

The starting move is to refuse the maximalist's premise that "independent" means independent. The claim is that the categorical distinction between independent expenditures and contributions, however clean it looks in *Buckley*, has collapsed in practice.

The empirical scaffolding (steel-manned):

- **Super PACs in practice are not independent.** They're staffed by former campaign aides; they share consultants, vendors, polling, even physical office space (with a "wall" between the Super PAC and the campaign that exists on paper). Justice Kennedy's confidence that "independent expenditures … do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption" (slip op. at 42) was an empirical claim about how independent the spending actually is. Briffault (2011) and Hasen (2011) document the slippage; the FEC's enforcement has effectively conceded it (we'll trace that in Lesson 3).

- **The "appearance of corruption" standard isn't categorical creep — it's in Buckley.** Buckley itself recognized that public confidence in democratic legitimacy is harmed when the public *perceives* that money buys access. CU sidelines this by reading "appearance" narrowly. The dissent's argument is that the harms to legitimacy are real even when no quid-pro-quo would be findable in court — and that the constitutional question Buckley framed is not just "is there corruption" but "is there appearance of corruption that erodes the system."

- **Stevens's 90-page dissent made detailed empirical arguments the majority skipped.** Justice Stevens marshaled evidence on (a) the long history of corporate-spending regulation going back to the Tillman Act (1907), (b) the fact that Congress had repeatedly studied this and drawn the conclusion that corporate independent expenditures *do* corrupt, and (c) the absence of any majority engagement with that record. From his dissent: "While American democracy is imperfect, few outside the majority of this Court would have thought its flaws included a dearth of corporate money in politics." (Slip op., dissent at 1.)

- **Lessig's reframe.** Larry Lessig's *Republic, Lost* argues that the right concept is not quid-pro-quo corruption but **dependence corruption** — the way representatives, even with no specific quid-pro-quo, become dependent on a small donor class and shape policy to keep them happy. CU dismisses this concept; the anti-corruption case insists it's the actual problem and that Buckley's narrow definition of corruption was always inadequate to it.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that the post-2010 system is in fact producing better-informed voters, more competitive elections, and equal political voice for non-wealthy citizens. Anti-corruption advocates concede the empirical case is harder to make than the maximalist literature implies; Stratmann's literature review (2005) finds the "money buys votes" claim is more contested than reformers often state. The strongest version of this position acknowledges that.

## 3. The democratic-equality case

The starting move is to fight not on Citizens United but on Buckley v. Valeo (1976), the precedent CU built on. This position thinks the majority in CU was largely right *given Buckley*, but Buckley was wrong, and the result of being right-given-wrong-premises is the system we have.

The argument:

- **Political equality is itself a constitutional value.** The democratic-equality case grounds itself in the text and structure of the Constitution — the Fourteenth Amendment's equal-protection guarantee, the republican-form-of-government clause (Art. IV §4), and the broader idea that representative democracy presupposes citizens having equal political voice. Cass Sunstein's *Democracy and the Problem of Free Speech* (1993) develops this position at book length. Justice Breyer made versions of it in his dissents.

- **The First Amendment was not designed to protect spending.** This is the doctrinal claim. The Founders' free-speech tradition was about protecting *individuals* from government punishment for unpopular *expression*. Buckley's move — treating campaign expenditures as core First Amendment speech — extended the doctrine into a domain (political-economic distribution of voice) the original framers did not address and the early Republic regulated heavily without controversy.

- **The trade-off CU settles is a values judgment, not a textual one.** The majority's opinion treats "free speech" as a single value and assumes more of it is always better. The democratic-equality position says: the question is not how much speech, but whose speech, in a system where amplification scales with wealth. Treating these as equivalent — as if a billionaire's $1B ad campaign is the same kind of speech as a citizen's letter to the editor — collapses a distinction the Republic depends on.

- **Other democracies regulate this without First Amendment problems.** The U.K., Canada, France, and Germany all have stricter campaign-finance regimes (often including ad-buying caps and shorter campaign windows) and remain free societies. The democratic-equality case takes this comparative evidence seriously: it suggests political equality and free expression can coexist, and that the U.S. Constitution's doctrine has drifted in a way that makes that balance unreachable.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that political-equality regulations in fact suppress speech the public would benefit from hearing, or that they entrench incumbents (a common conservative critique of campaign-finance regulation). The strongest version of this position acknowledges the entrenchment risk and tries to design rules that mitigate it (Lessig's vouchers, for instance) rather than dismiss it.

## What's settled and what isn't

It's worth ending with where the empirical record actually is, because all three positions invoke it.

**Reasonably settled:**
- Independent expenditures by Super PACs and 501(c)(4)s have grown enormously since 2010 (OpenSecrets data is uncontested on the magnitudes).
- The donor base for Super PAC spending is concentrated in a small number of wealthy individuals and organizations.
- Disclosure of donors to 501(c)(4) "social welfare" organizations is sharply lower than for traditional PACs (this is what "dark money" refers to in policy literature).

**Genuinely contested:**
- Whether Super PAC spending changes electoral *outcomes* relative to candidate quality and macro political conditions (Stratmann 2005 and successors find smaller effects than the rhetoric implies).
- Whether independent expenditures create *dependence corruption* in the Lessig sense, separate from quid-pro-quo.
- Whether stricter pre-CU regimes were actually preventing the problems CU is now blamed for, or whether the post-CU world is the same problem with different vehicles.

A defensible position on Citizens United picks one of the three frames, says why, and acknowledges what's contested. The next lesson traces what's happened in the fifteen years since.
