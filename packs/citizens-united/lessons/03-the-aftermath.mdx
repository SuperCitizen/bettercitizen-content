# Where the law has gone since

In 2008 — the last federal election held before *Citizens United* — every outside group in the country combined spent \$338 million. In 2024, one donor spent \$290 million by himself. Elon Musk's year-end FEC filings showed \$239 million of it flowing through a single [[term:super-pac]], America PAC, which among other things took over much of the Republican presidential ground operation — canvassers, voter registration, get-out-the-vote — for a campaign it was legally forbidden to "coordinate" with. Every part of that sentence was lawful, and no part of it was possible fifteen years earlier. This lesson explains the machine that produced it: the January 2010 holding you already know, plus a D.C. Circuit case from that March, plus a 2014 sequel, plus a regulator that has progressively stopped regulating. Each piece matters, because the argument over *Citizens United* today is really an argument over this whole assembly.

## March 2010: SpeechNow.org and the birth of the Super PAC

*Citizens United* said nothing about what a *group* could collect. SpeechNow.org — an unincorporated association that wanted to pool individual donations and spend them solely on independent ads for and against candidates — was still bound by FECA's \$5,000-per-year cap on what any individual could give a political committee. It sued, and two months after *Citizens United*, the full D.C. Circuit — all nine judges, sitting en banc — finished the syllogism. The Supreme Court had just declared, as a matter of law, that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." If the *spending* cannot corrupt, Chief Judge Sentelle wrote in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (March 26, 2010), then *contributions to a group that does nothing but that spending* cannot corrupt either — and a limit that serves no anti-corruption interest serves nothing: "the First Amendment cannot be encroached upon for naught."

Two details get lost in the shorthand. First, the same opinion **upheld** the registration and reporting requirements — SpeechNow had to organize as a political committee and itemize its donors publicly, a holding that matters enormously in Lesson 4. Second, the FEC never asked the Supreme Court to review its loss. So the entity the decision created — the "independent-expenditure-only committee," universally nicknamed the Super PAC — rests on a circuit-court precedent the highest court has never examined. *Citizens United* opened the spending; *SpeechNow* opened the fundraising; the combination is the post-2010 landscape.

<Checkpoint question="Suppose a future Supreme Court formally repudiates the premise that independent expenditures cannot corrupt. What happens to the Super PAC — without Congress passing anything?">
  The Super PAC's legal foundation dissolves. *SpeechNow* is a pure syllogism built on that single premise: no corruption from independent spending → no anti-corruption interest in capping contributions that fund only such spending → cap falls. Knock out the premise and the old \$5,000 contribution cap on political committees has no constitutional obstacle left — which is why fifteen years of litigation and scholarship have aimed at the *premise* ("as a matter of law, independence defuses corruption") rather than at the vehicle. The doctrinal load-bearing wall is an empirical claim.
</Checkpoint>

## 2014: McCutcheon and the aggregate ceiling

The third doctrinal event arrived four years later. FECA capped not only what a donor could give each candidate (the **base limits**) but the donor's *total* federal giving — \$123,200 per two-year cycle in 2013–14, split between candidates (\$48,600) and committees (\$74,600). Shaun McCutcheon, an Alabama electrical engineer, had given \$33,088 across sixteen candidates, wanted to support a dozen more at the legal per-candidate rate, and sued.

In ***McCutcheon v. FEC* (2014)**, 5–4, the Court struck the aggregate limits. Chief Justice Roberts's plurality opened on the register the case is remembered for — "There is no right more basic in our democracy than the right to participate in electing our political leaders" — and reasoned mechanically from *Buckley*'s own definition: each base limit already polices the quid pro quo risk with each individual candidate, so a ceiling on *how many* candidates a donor may support at the lawful rate adds no anti-corruption protection; it only restricts participation. Justice Thomas supplied the fifth vote while complaining the plurality had not gone far enough — he would overrule *Buckley* and strike the base limits too. Base limits survive (\$3,500 per candidate per election in 2025–26). The case is narrower than *Citizens United* — it concerns individuals' direct giving, not corporate spending — but it extends the same definitional choice: corruption means a bargained exchange, and nothing broader counts.

## The independence fiction

On paper, the wall between a Super PAC and a campaign is what makes the entire structure constitutional. In practice, the wall has been engineered around from the start, by methods that are public, well documented, and — under current enforcement — effectively lawful:

- **Staffing.** The first presidential-cycle Super PACs set the pattern in 2012: Restore Our Future was founded by veterans of Mitt Romney's prior campaign; Priorities USA Action by former Obama White House aides. Single-candidate Super PACs run by the candidate's recent staff have been standard equipment ever since.
- **Solicitation.** Under FEC guidance issued in 2011, federal candidates may appear at and personally solicit for "their" Super PAC's fundraisers, so long as the candidate asks for no more than \$5,000 — after which the host may ask for the rest.
- **Redboxing.** Campaigns post message scripts, B-roll footage, and voter-targeting guidance on public webpages — often inside a literal red box, addressed to no one, beginning "voters need to know…" — and the allied Super PAC executes it. Because the material is *public*, it defeats the coordination rules' requirement of privately conveyed information. A 2024 *Election Law Journal* study by Foy-Sutherland and Ghosh found the practice across more than two hundred congressional campaigns in the 2022 cycle alone.
- **The canvassing loophole.** The FEC's coordination rules govern coordinated *communications* — paid public advertising. Door-to-door canvassing is not a "public communication" under the regulations, which is how America PAC could lawfully run the Republican ground game in 2024 while remaining, for legal purposes, independent of the campaign it was turning out voters for.

> **Why this matters:** Justice Kennedy's opinion assumed a world of genuinely independent spending policed by a functioning regulator. Whether that assumption failed — or whether these adaptations are just ordinary politics that corrupt no one — is precisely where Lesson 2's maximalist and anti-corruption positions collide on facts rather than doctrine.

<Checkpoint question="A Super PAC devoted to one Senate candidate is run by her campaign manager from two cycles ago, hires her former pollster, and lifts its ad scripts from a 'voter education' page on her campaign website. Which element, if any, violates the coordination rules as currently enforced?">
  Almost certainly none. The conduct standards in the FEC's coordination regulation turn on the private conveyance of *material, nonpublic* campaign information — former staff and shared vendors trigger scrutiny only if they carry inside information across the wall, and a public webpage is by definition not nonpublic (that is the entire point of redboxing). Add a Commission that deadlocks on coordination complaints, and the "wall" functions mostly as a formality. The anti-corruption advocate reads this as the holding's empirical premise collapsing; the maximalist answers that public messaging is speech, not conspiracy.
</Checkpoint>

## The pipeline: where disclosure stops

*Citizens United* upheld disclosure 8–1, and Super PACs do itemize their donors. The post-2010 system nonetheless built a detour. A **501(c)(4)** "social welfare" nonprofit — an IRS category, not an FEC one — may take unlimited money, never publicly discloses its donors, and may spend on politics so long as politics is not its *primary purpose* (a term the IRS has never rigorously defined). It may also pass unlimited sums to a Super PAC. The Super PAC then discloses exactly what the law requires: the nonprofit's name.

The scale of the detour is no longer speculative. Outside spending in 2024 reached a record **\$4.5 billion**, and more than half came from groups that do not fully disclose their funding (OpenSecrets). The Brennan Center's fuller accounting — adding television ad tracking and platform archives to FEC data — put 2024 [[term:dark-money]] at a record **\$1.9 billion**, nearly double 2020, with about **\$1.3 billion** of it passed into Super PACs that disclosed only the intermediary's name. Note what this means doctrinally: the one regulatory tool every member of the *Citizens United* Court except Justice Thomas blessed is the tool the system now routes around — not by breaking the law, but by stacking two legal regimes (IRS and FEC) that were never designed to interlock.

## The referee that stopped refereeing

Every assumption in the majority opinion ultimately points at one agency. The FEC has six seats, no more than three per party, and needs four affirmative votes to investigate, to make rules, or to find a violation. That design makes deadlock a feature: one analysis found the Commission deadlocked on about 4.9 percent of its votes on enforcement requests per year from 1975 to 2007 — and 24.1 percent per year from 2008 to 2019, precisely the era when coordination and dark-money questions arrived. Coordination complaints, including redboxing complaints, have died this way for a decade.

Then the referee left the field entirely. On **May 1, 2025**, the Commission dropped below its four-member [[term:quorum]]; by early 2026 it was down to two sitting commissioners, had canceled the public meetings remaining on its calendar, and could not open investigations, issue advisory opinions, or resolve cases — a state Washington reporters described as an effective shutdown of more than 200 days and counting. Two replacement commissioners were nominated in February 2026; as of mid-2026 the Senate had not confirmed them, and the 2026 midterms were being contested with no functioning federal campaign-finance regulator.

Two honest readings coexist. The **maximalist** reading: a weak FEC is a feature — an agency that polices political speech *should* find it structurally difficult to act, because an aggressive one would be a weapon incumbents aim at challengers. The **reformer** reading: the regime Kennedy described in 2010 — vigorous disclosure, enforced independence — was a counterfactual; the Court built its constitutional holding on an enforcement world that has never existed since. Both descriptions are true; which one matters more is one of the live questions.

<Checkpoint question="The 2026 midterms are underway with the FEC unable to act. A Senate campaign believes a rival's Super PAC is illegally coordinating. What, concretely, can happen to that complaint — and which side of the Lesson 2 argument does this fact pattern arm?">
  Procedurally: the complaint can be filed but cannot be voted on — no quorum means no investigation, no conciliation, no penalty, and the statute of limitations keeps running. (Courts can sometimes deem a complaint "dismissed" by inaction, letting the complainant sue, but that is slow and rare.) It arms the anti-corruption advocate's empirical point — the independence on which the doctrine rests is unpoliced in exactly the years it is being tested — while the maximalist counters that the absence of enforcement has produced no proven quid pro quo either, which is their standing falsification test from Lesson 2.
</Checkpoint>

## The scholar's lens: was the wall ever real?

Richard Briffault's "Super PACs" (*Minnesota Law Review*, 2012) is the canonical early diagnosis: the single-candidate Super PAC, he argued, achieves "formal" independence without "functional" independence — staffed by the candidate's people, funded by the candidate's donors, dedicated to the candidate's race — and so converts *Buckley*'s expenditure/contribution line into a pricing rule (unlimited money, one step removed) rather than an anti-corruption protection. Richard Hasen's "Citizens United and the Illusion of Coherence" (2011) presses the deeper point from Lesson 2: the majority resolved an empirical question — can independent money corrupt? — "as a matter of law," which is why no accumulation of post-2010 practice ever forces the doctrine to update. The strongest reply belongs to Bradley Smith and the Institute for Free Speech school: fifteen years on, federal prosecutions for Super-PAC-mediated bribery remain rare to nonexistent, voters receive more information from more sources than in any prior era, and the predicted wave of provable corruption has not materialized — adaptation, on this view, is not evidence of disease. The data from Ansolabehere and colleagues (Lesson 2) counsel humility in both directions: money's measurable effect on outcomes and roll calls remains smaller and murkier than either camp's rhetoric implies.

## What this lesson did not settle

It did not settle whether the post-2010 system vindicates the majority — speech expanded, Super PAC donors disclosed, no proven quid pro quo epidemic — or falsifies it: independence engineered into fiction, disclosure laundered through nonprofits, the referee absent. Both accounts fit the verified record above; they disagree about what the record *means*, which is the Lesson 2 argument continued on fifteen years of evidence. What it should have settled is the mechanics and the numbers: which court did what, what each vehicle must reveal, and what 2024 actually cost. One thread remains: if disclosure is the one tool everyone's Constitution permits, why has Congress not closed the 501(c)(4) gap in sixteen years of trying? That fight — the DISCLOSE Act, *NAACP v. Alabama*, donor privacy, and the states acting where Washington cannot — is Lesson 4.
