# The strongest cases in the regulation debate

Three positions on how banking and money should be governed. Each presented at its strongest. They do not map cleanly onto left and right: the financial-stability and market-discipline camps disagree about bank *regulation*, while the monetary-reform camp reframes the argument around the *money supply itself*.

## 1. The financial-stability case

The starting move is to take the 2008 crisis seriously as evidence about what unregulated finance does. A lightly-constrained financial system, on this view, periodically produces catastrophic crises whose costs fall on the whole society, not just on the bankers who took the risks. Regulation is the price of preventing the next one.

The argument:

- **Bank runs are real and self-fulfilling, so a public backstop is necessary.** Diamond & Dybvig (1983) — work that won the 2022 Nobel — formalized why even a *solvent* bank can be destroyed by a run, and why government-backed deposit insurance can eliminate the bad equilibrium. The near-total absence of classic depositor runs in the U.S. from 1934 until 2008 is treated as the strongest natural experiment in the case *for* the FDIC.

- **Banks are too thinly capitalized, and that's the core problem.** Admati & Hellwig (*The Bankers' New Clothes*) argue the central fix is simple and underused: require banks to fund far more of their assets with equity rather than debt. Highly leveraged banks gamble with other people's money — they keep the upside and socialize the downside. Higher capital requirements make losses fall on shareholders first and dramatically reduce the chance of failure, at little real cost to lending. The industry's resistance, they argue, is about private profit, not public benefit.

- **"Too big to fail" is a subsidy that must be regulated away.** When a bank is so large or interconnected that the government cannot let it fail, it enjoys an implicit guarantee: it can borrow cheaply because creditors assume a bailout. That subsidy encourages exactly the size and risk-taking that make the firm dangerous. The financial-stability answer is structural: higher capital surcharges on the biggest banks, living wills, stress tests, and an orderly-resolution mechanism so even a giant can be wound down. The point is not to love regulation; it is that the alternative to regulating too-big-to-fail is *paying* for it.

- **Crises have huge external costs.** The 2008 collapse cost trillions in lost output and millions of jobs — costs borne overwhelmingly by people who never touched a mortgage-backed security. When the downside of private risk-taking lands on the public, the public has a legitimate claim to constrain the risk. This is a standard externality argument, and finance is a textbook case.

- **Deregulation has a track record.** The savings-and-loan crisis followed 1980s deregulation; the 2008 crisis followed the erosion of Glass-Steagall and the growth of a lightly-regulated "shadow" banking system; the 2023 failures (Lesson 3) followed the 2018 loosening of rules for mid-size banks. On this reading, each major deregulatory episode was followed by a crisis, which is not a coincidence.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that the regulatory apparatus itself *causes* the instability it claims to prevent — by inducing moral hazard, by pushing risk into unregulated corners, or by being systematically captured. The financial-stability camp concedes these are real risks and tries to answer them with simpler, harder-to-game rules (especially high equity capital) rather than ever-more-complex supervision.

## 2. The market-discipline case

The starting move is to notice that the same governments that regulate banks also *guarantee* them — and to argue that the guarantee is the disease, not the cure. Deposit insurance, the lender of last resort, and the expectation of bailouts remove the incentive for anyone to monitor risk, so banks take more of it. The fix is more market accountability, not more rules.

The argument:

- **Moral hazard is the central mechanism.** If depositors and creditors know they'll be made whole, they have no reason to avoid risky banks, and risky banks have no reason to be careful. Insurance and bailouts therefore *manufacture* the recklessness that more regulation is then deployed to suppress — a ratchet in which each rescue requires the next round of rules. The honest fix is to let creditors (and sometimes uninsured depositors) bear losses, so that risk is priced again.

- **Crises are caused by bad government policy as much as by markets.** The market-discipline camp disputes the "deregulation caused 2008" narrative. They point to government policy that *fueled* the housing bubble: ultra-low Fed interest rates in the early 2000s, federal mandates and subsidies pushing mortgage credit to weak borrowers, and Fannie/Freddie's implicit guarantee. John Taylor's *Getting Off Track* argues Fed policy that deviated from a stable rule was a primary cause. On this view 2008 is an indictment of intervention, not of free markets.

- **Banking crises are political, not natural.** Calomiris & Haber (*Fragile by Design*) compare countries and find that crisis-prone banking systems are the product of political bargains — between governments that want cheap credit for favored constituencies and bankers who want protection from competition. Canada, with lighter formal regulation but fewer protective bargains, went ~150 years without a systemic banking crisis; the heavily-regulated U.S. has had many. The lesson is that *more* regulation often means more politicized credit, not more safety.

- **Regulators are captured and always behind.** Supervisors are outgunned, revolve through the firms they oversee, and write rules for the last crisis while the next one builds somewhere they aren't looking. Basel's risk-weighting, for instance, told banks that mortgage-backed securities and sovereign debt were ultra-safe — right up until they weren't. Complex regulation gives a false sense of safety and a real compliance moat that entrenches the biggest incumbents.

- **Simpler, skin-in-the-game rules beat complex supervision.** Where this camp overlaps with the stability camp is on equity: make owners and creditors bear losses. But it draws the opposite conclusion about the rest — strip back the detailed rulebook, shrink or price the safety net, and let the prospect of failure do the disciplining that regulators cannot.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that market discipline simply does not work fast enough — that by the time creditors flee, the run is already systemic and the externalities already imposed — so that some public backstop is unavoidable and the only real question is how to regulate alongside it. The market-discipline camp concedes the run dynamic is real and is strongest when it pairs deregulation with much higher capital, so that banks rarely reach the point of needing rescue.

## 3. The monetary-reform case

The starting move is to step back from the bank-regulation argument entirely and ask a prior question: *who should control the money supply, and by what rule?* From this view, both camps above are arguing about how to manage the symptoms of a deeper problem — a discretionary, [[term:fiat-currency]] system run by a central bank that creates booms and busts in the first place.

The argument:

- **Discretionary monetary policy is the source of the cycle.** If the Fed sets interest rates too low for too long, it inflates asset bubbles (housing in the 2000s, arguably assets again in the 2010s) that inevitably burst. Boom-and-bust, on this view, is not a market failure to be regulated away but a *monetary* phenomenon generated by the central bank's own discretion. Regulating banks harder treats the hangover instead of the alcohol.

- **Rules beat discretion.** A softer version (Taylor) keeps the Fed but binds it to a transparent rule, so policy is predictable and not subject to political pressure or improvisation. The stronger version wants to constrain money growth by a fixed rule or anchor. Either way the target is *discretion* — the idea that a committee can fine-tune a $25-trillion economy in real time, with long lags and imperfect data, better than a rule can.

- **Sound money protects savers.** A system that can create money without limit, the argument goes, structurally favors debtors (including the government) over savers, and erodes the currency over time. The dollar has lost most of its purchasing power since 1913. Critics of [[term:fiat-currency]] — from gold-standard advocates to some [[term:cryptocurrency]] proponents — want money whose supply cannot be expanded at the discretion of officials, precisely so it can serve as a stable store of value.

- **The backstop and the printing press are one system.** This camp connects to the market-discipline argument: the lender of last resort can rescue banks at scale only *because* the Fed can create money without limit. End or constrain that capacity and "too big to fail" loses its ultimate funding source. The deepest version of the deregulation argument is therefore monetary, not regulatory — you cannot have a hard budget constraint for banks while the central bank has a soft one.

- **Independence is power without accountability.** A central bank insulated from elections makes profoundly distributional choices — who gets cheap credit, whose assets are propped up, how the costs of inflation are shared — with no direct democratic check. Reformers across the spectrum (sound-money on the right, some post-Keynesians on the left) agree the current arrangement concentrates enormous, lightly-accountable power, even as they disagree about what should replace it.

What evidence would change this position? A persuasive showing that discretionary central banking, for all its flaws, has *outperformed* rule-based or commodity-based alternatives — that the post-1913 record (despite the Depression and 2008) beats the pre-Fed era of frequent panics, and that the macroeconomic stability of the 1980s–2000s "Great Moderation" was real and Fed-made. The monetary-reform camp's strongest opponents argue exactly this, and the historical comparison is genuinely contested.

## What's settled and what's contested

**Settled (or near-settled):**
- Bank runs are real, can hit solvent banks, and can be self-fulfilling (Diamond-Dybvig).
- Higher equity capital makes individual banks safer and shifts losses from the public to owners; the disagreement is about *how high* and at *what cost to credit*, not about the direction.
- Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort both calm runs *and* create moral hazard. The trade-off is real; the dispute is about where to set the dial.
- "Too big to fail" confers a real funding subsidy on the largest banks.

**Genuinely contested:**
- Whether 2008 was primarily a failure of markets (under-regulation) or of policy (intervention and easy money).
- Whether more regulation reduces crises or merely politicizes credit and pushes risk into the shadows.
- Whether discretionary central banking outperforms a rules-based or commodity-anchored alternative.
- Whether central-bank independence is a feature (insulation from short-term politics) or a defect (power without accountability).

A defensible position picks a frame, says why, and is honest about what it would take to be wrong. Lesson 3 turns to the policy fights where these arguments are actually playing out.
