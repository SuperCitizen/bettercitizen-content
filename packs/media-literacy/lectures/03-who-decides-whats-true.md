February 19, 2020. The Lancet publishes a statement from 27 scientists. It condemns, in these words, "conspiracy theories suggesting that COVID-19 does not have a natural origin."

[beat]

Within a year, that sentence had hardened into infrastructure. Outlets called the lab-leak hypothesis debunked. And Facebook, a platform with billions of users, was removing claims that the virus was man-made.

[pause]

Then the story turned. In May 2021, eighteen scientists wrote in the journal Science to demand a genuine investigation. Investigative reporting revived the question. President Biden ordered a 90-day intelligence review. Facebook stopped removing the claim. And outlets quietly re-graded it, from debunked, to unresolved.

In 2023, the agencies that reviewed it split. Two leaned toward a lab-related origin. Several others leaned toward a natural one, mostly at low confidence. There was no smoking gun on either side.

And in January 2025, the CIA assessed that a research-related origin was "more likely" than a natural one. Listen to the conditions. [beat] Low confidence. No new evidence. Both scenarios still plausible.

As of mid-2026, the question remains unresolved.

> A hypothesis changed its status without the evidence changing much.

Go over that arc a second time, because partisans of every camp misread it once. Two things are true together.

One. The gatekeepers really did enforce a premature consensus. The letter itself, it later emerged, was organized by a researcher with undisclosed ties to collaboration in Wuhan. A legitimate hypothesis was suppressed. And something was labeled misinformation that was, at worst, unproven.

[beat]

Two. The skeptics' favorite ending, that the lab leak was true all along, is also not what happened. Most published virological analyses still favor a market origin.

> The honest status: contested, low confidence, evidence incomplete.

So the episode indicts everyone's preferred referee at once. That is why it is our case study. And it leaves a question. [beat] When citizens disagree about facts, at scale, whose call should count?

[pause]

Three serious answers compete. By the end of this lecture you will be able to state each one at full strength. You will be able to say what the research finds about each, and what evidence would move you from one to another.

We will take it in four steps. First, the three answers, each from its smartest advocate. Second, what the evidence shows. Third, what happens to each answer when it is held as a tribe. And last, what stays unsettled.

## Three answers

Begin on ground that all three share. None of them denies the story you just heard. All three are positions about design: given that referees err, what should a society build?

The first answer is the institutionalist view, also called the professional-gatekeeping view.

What it protects is a common baseline of fact, vetted by professionals. Institutionalists call that baseline civic infrastructure: it is what makes disagreement about values possible, without disagreement about reality.

Its strongest argument is about machinery. Verification is skilled labor, performed inside accountable systems, and no society has found a substitute. A reporter's claim passes through editors, fact-checkers and libel lawyers. A published error triggers a correction, owed in public, under a masthead whose reputation takes decades to build and one scandal to destroy. That machinery, and not anyone's virtue, is why retractions exist at all.

The evidence it points to comes from experiments. Across four countries, corrections by professional fact-checkers measurably and durably improved the accuracy of belief. And they did so across the political aisle.

On the lab-leak story, institutionalists argue that the record supports them. Yes, the gatekeepers blew the call. But notice who produced the reversal: scientists publishing in Science, investigative reporters at legacy outlets, an institutional intelligence review. The system's misses are real, loud, and self-documented. [beat] Name the alternative that even has a corrections page.

> The institutionalist case: fire the referees, and errors have no mechanism for ending.

The second answer is the anti-establishment skeptic's.

What it protects is the chance for a legitimate hypothesis to be judged on evidence, and not shut out by consensus.

Its strongest argument starts from the same case study. The lab-leak hypothesis, skeptics argue, was not felled by evidence. [beat] It was strangled by consensus enforcement: a letter with undisclosed conflicts, laundered through prestige media into the label debunked, and enforced by platforms at the scale of billions.

The evidence it points to is a record. The same machinery throttled a New York Post story about a candidate's son's laptop, weeks before the 2020 election. Twitter blocked the link, its chief executive later called that a mistake, and other outlets eventually verified key contents. Add the WMD consensus of 2002 and 2003: unanimous, prestigious, and catastrophically wrong. And add the experts behind the gatekeepers. When psychology's flagship replication project examined landmark findings in the field, only 36 percent replicated significantly.

The novelist Michael Crichton named the everyday version: Gell-Mann amnesia. You open the paper to a story about your own field, and you see that it's wrong. Then you turn the page, and trust the next story.

The remedy, skeptics argue, is not to trust no one. It is correction that is decentralized and adversarial, with skin in the game. Their prototype is Community Notes, on X. A note publishes only when raters who usually disagree both find it helpful: a bridging algorithm, not a committee. And a 2025 study of some 40,000 noted posts found that an attached note cut reposts of a flagged post nearly in half, in the moment.

> The skeptic's case: concentrated gatekeeping is a single point of failure.

The third answer is the civic-empowerment view, also called the literacy-first view.

What it protects is the citizen's own capacity to judge.

Its strongest argument is that the contest itself is the mistake. The first two views argue about who should hold the referee's whistle. But any central arbiter, whether editorial, corporate or crowd-sourced, becomes a target to capture and a grievance to mobilize against. And labels do nothing for the citizen who has already stopped trusting labelers. The durable defense is distributed competence: a population that verifies the way the last lecture taught.

The evidence it points to comes from experiments too. Instruction in lateral reading improves source evaluation, in randomized studies and in classrooms. And prebunking works at the scale of a platform. Ninety-second inoculation videos improved people's recognition of manipulation techniques, and their judgment about sharing. That held in six randomized trials, and in a field study of 22,000 people on YouTube. Its advocates point to Finland too, with decades of media education in its schools, which treats this as civil defense. That example carries a caveat, and we will come to it.

Its deepest argument is civic. Outsource the deciding, and the capacity atrophies exactly when you need it most. [beat] The referee you trust today will someday be staffed by people you don't.

> The civic-empowerment case: deciding what's true is the citizen's job description.

[pause]

Now put all three to work on our case study. Each one claims the lab-leak story as evidence for itself. You have heard two of those readings. Can you say them back? And can you work out the third?

[long pause]

The institutionalist reading: the system erred, and then corrected itself in public. Scientists, reporters and an intelligence review reopened the question. That is what working error-machinery looks like.

[beat]

The skeptic's reading: the error was not random. Gatekeepers enforced a conflicted consensus, and platforms suppressed a legitimate hypothesis for a year. That is concentrated refereeing, failing dangerously.

[beat]

The civic-empowerment reading: citizens equipped to weigh confidence levels were never at the mercy of either mistake. Not the premature label, debunked. And not the later overclaim, proven true.

[pause]

All three readings are defensible. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

One more step, and this one has no answer from me. Take the view you find least convincing of the three. State its best case, as its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## What the evidence shows

Making each case at full strength does not mean refusing to keep score. Each position makes empirical claims, and some have been tested. Take them in the same order, and ask of each the same two things. What works? And where does it stop?

Professional fact-checking first. The experimental literature is unusually consistent. Corrections improve factual beliefs. The improvement persists for weeks. And backfire effects, where a correction entrenches the false belief, are rare: much rarer than early lore claimed.

Where it stops is reach and trust. Fact-checks disproportionately reach the people who least need them. And the label "fact-checker" itself polarizes. That is partly why Meta, in January 2025, ended its eight-year U.S. program of third-party fact-checking, and announced a move to a Community Notes model. Call it an experiment, now running, in replacing the first answer with the second.

[pause]

Community Notes second. Two findings must be held together. When a note reaches a post, it works. One study found roughly 46 percent fewer reposts immediately after a note attached, with effects across the political spectrum.

Where it stops is speed. Over the whole life of a post the drop was about 12 percent, because notes arrive in mid-flight. And a study of the full rollout found no detectable reduction, at the level of the platform, in engagement with misleading posts. The consensus mechanism takes hours and virality takes minutes, so most misleading impressions happen before any note exists.

Two more limits. Because raters who usually disagree rarely agree on politically charged posts, many accurate notes there never publish. And notes lean heavily on professional fact-checks and news organizations as their evidence. In practice, the crowd aggregates the gatekeepers it was supposed to replace. [beat] Symbiosis, not substitution.

[pause]

Literacy and prebunking third. The inoculation trials and the classroom studies show genuine, replicated gains in skill: recognizing manipulation, and rating sources more accurately. These are the best-validated tools we have at the level of the individual.

Where it stops is size and staying power. The effects are modest. They fade in weeks to months without boosters. And nobody has yet shown that they scale into herd immunity: that a gain in skill changes real-world belief or sharing across a population.

Here is the caveat on Finland. Its famous rankings come from an index that scores countries on inputs: education quality, press freedom, social trust. It does not score how citizens perform against misinformation. Finland's curriculum is real and seriously implemented. But the headline "Finland defeats fake news" outruns what the index can show.

[pause]

So here is the ledger, in one line.

> All three tools work. Corrections reach poorly, notes arrive late, training fades.

Let's test that. A friend says: "Community Notes proved crowds beat fact-checkers. Even Meta agreed, and fired theirs."

Which correction does the research support? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not that notes fail. Not that notes win in every measured way. And not that the studies cancel out.

The supported correction keeps both findings. Notes work on the posts they reach. But they usually arrive after the viral peak, they often fail to publish on contested political claims, and they lean on professional fact-checks for their evidence. Two findings at two levels are not a contradiction.

As for Meta: its switch was a policy choice, made amid this mixed evidence. It was not a verdict delivered by it.

## A position, not a tribe

These three views are positions about institutional design. And every one of them curdles into something lesser when it is held tribally.

The institutionalist view curdles into "my outlets are the real ones." That is bias, wearing a press badge.

[beat]

The civic-empowerment view curdles into "everyone just needs my workshop." That is a non-answer, at viral speed.

[beat]

The skeptic's view curdles into conspiracism: the style of thought in which every institutional error is intentional, every absence of evidence is suppression, and every correction is the cover-up. It has ended with a man carrying a rifle into a Washington pizzeria, in December 2016, to investigate pizzagate: a claim that had been checked and refuted.

[pause]

Two traps guard the exits. One is false equivalence: every referee has failed, so all claims are equal. That treats a low-confidence intelligence assessment and a peer-reviewed literature as tied. The other is the cynic's surrender, from the last lecture: nothing is true. That is the propagandist's finish line.

The discipline that avoids both has three parts. Proportion belief to track record and corroboration. State what would change your mind. And check whether your complaint about referees survives the cases where your own side benefited from refereeing.

Here is a self-test on that last part. Suppose you cheered when platforms suppressed the claim you hated, and raged when they suppressed the one you liked. Then your position is not one of the three. [beat] It is "referees for thee."

So a last question, about your own lean. What near-term, observable evidence would move you toward each of the other two positions?

[long pause]

Here is one fair list.

Toward the institutionalist: decentralized systems persistently failing on contested political claims, with notes never publishing and hoaxes outrunning crowds. And meanwhile, the corrections of professional outlets demonstrably landing.

[beat]

Toward the skeptic: another episode on the scale of the lab leak, in which gatekeepers enforce a consensus that collapses under undisclosed conflicts. Or bridging-based systems beginning to beat newsrooms on speed and on accuracy.

[beat]

Toward civic empowerment: prebunking and lateral-reading programs showing durable, population-scale effects on real sharing behavior, and not just on lab tasks.

> If nothing would move you, you are holding an identity, not a position.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The lab-leak hypothesis changed its status without the evidence changing much. The first failure was a premature label: debunked. The second was an overclaim: true all along. The honest status: contested, low confidence, evidence incomplete.

[beat]

Two. Three answers to who should referee. The institutionalist case: fire the referees, and errors have no mechanism for ending. The skeptic's case: concentrated gatekeeping is a single point of failure. The civic-empowerment case: deciding what's true is the citizen's job description.

[beat]

Three. All three tools work, and each has a ceiling. Corrections reach poorly. Crowd notes arrive late. Literacy training is modest, and it fades.

[beat]

Four. Hold a position, not an identity. Proportion belief to track record and corroboration, and say what would change your mind.

[pause]

These three lectures have settled some things, as well as anything here can be settled. The attention economy shapes what reaches you. Local accountability journalism collapsed, and that has a cost. Reading laterally and tracing to the origin beat staring harder at the artifact. And reflexive cynicism is not a sophisticated stance. It is a manufactured one.

Here is what stays unsettled. Who, if anyone, should referee truth at scale? That question is value-laden. It trades error rates against capture risk, against civic capacity. And the evidence, honestly read, refuses to crown a winner.

[pause]

Unsettled too is the origin of the virus, which is exactly why it made a good case study.

One boundary, before we finish. We have not argued about what platforms may legally host, remove, or be sued over. That is a separate question, and the Free Speech pack takes it up. Keep the two apart. Who decides what is true would survive any legal regime.

The argument continues there. It also continues in your feed, tonight, where all three referees and their failures will be on display.

So go back to that sentence in The Lancet, and the label it became. A citizen who can say three things, about a virus and about a referee, has everything these lectures have to give. [beat] Contested. Low confidence. Here is what would change my mind.
