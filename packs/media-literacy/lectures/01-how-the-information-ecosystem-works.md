New York, August 1835. A two-year-old newspaper called the Sun begins a six-part exclusive.

Its story: the eminent astronomer Sir John Herschel, observing from South Africa through a giant new telescope, has discovered life on the moon. [beat] Unicorns. Beavers that walk on two legs. And winged bat-people, who build temples.

[beat]

None of it was true. The series was almost certainly written by one of the Sun's own reporters, and the scientific journal it cited had ceased to exist.

It didn't matter. Sales soared. A delegation of Yale scientists came hunting for the original articles. And on September 16 the Sun cheerfully acknowledged the fabrication, without ever quite retracting it.

[pause]

Why so cheerful? Because the Sun cost a penny a copy, and it had pioneered a new business model. Sell the paper cheap. Build a mass audience. Then sell that audience to advertisers.

> The hoax was not a glitch. It was the business model, working.

So the single most useful question in media literacy is not "is this true?" [beat] It is this one.

> Who pays for this, and what does that make them want from me?

That question is our way into the machine: the incentives, the pipes and the choke points that decide what reaches you. By the end of this lecture you will be able to follow a story from the event to your feed. And you will be able to say who handled it on the way, and what each of them wanted.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the money: who pays for news, and what that does to it.

Second, the feed: who chooses what you see.

Third, the reporting itself: where a story comes from, and what has happened to the people who gather it.

And last, something new: media of events that never occurred.

## The money

Begin before the Sun. For most of the 1800s, American newspapers were openly partisan. Many were funded, quite literally, by political parties and by printing contracts.

The penny press broke that dependency by substituting a different one: advertising.

The sociologist Michael Schudson has traced what came out of that swap. The commercial mass paper invented things we now treat as eternal: the interview, the beat reporter, and eventually the ideal of objectivity itself. That ideal, in his account, emerged less from virtue than from a need: to sell one paper to Democrats and Republicans alike.

[pause]

So the ethic of the fourth estate, the idea of journalism as a check on power, grew up inside a commercial machine. The tension between the two has never gone away.

The modern version has been put formally by the economist James T. Hamilton. News is a product, and its content is driven by the audiences that advertisers will pay to reach.

Hamilton analyzed the network evening news from 1969 to 1998. Across those years, coverage drifted from hard politics toward soft news, as competition from cable fragmented the audience. That was not because journalists got lazier. [beat] It was because the viewer the networks were fighting over, the one at the margin, preferred it.

Here is what that means in practice. The supply of news about your city council is thin. Not because the information doesn't matter, but because the audience that pays for content rarely demands it.

[pause]

The internet did not change this logic. It perfected it. Online, attention is measured the instant you give it, and sold one impression at a time. So the headline that almost tells you the thing beats the headline that tells you the thing. [beat] That is all clickbait is: the penny-press incentive, measured in real time.

Let's test that. Picture a free news app. It shows you no subscription screen, and no donation button.

Follow the money. What is the app most likely selling? To whom? And what does that make it want from you? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is selling your attention, and usually data about you, to advertisers. So its core incentive is time spent, and engagement: clicks, scrolls, shares. [beat] Not accuracy. Not importance.

Be careful about what that proves. Nothing about it shows that any given story is false. It tells you which errors the system will tend to make: toward the arresting, toward the emotional, and toward more of what you already liked.

> Engagement is not a synonym for importance.

## The feed

That is who pays. Now, who chooses what you see?

Through most of the twentieth century, an editor chose. An editor decided what went on the front page, or into the six-thirty broadcast, and everyone in town saw roughly the same thing. That is called institutional distribution.

Today, distribution is substantially social. Platforms rank items one user at a time, optimizing mostly for engagement. Your neighbors' feeds are not your feed.

Put the two side by side. Under institutional distribution, editors choose. Under social distribution, algorithms and sharing choose.

[pause]

How scattered has it become? In 2025 the Pew Research Center asked American adults where they regularly get news. The largest platform, Facebook, reached 38 percent. [beat] Not much more than a third.

So no single platform owns the news audience the way the six-thirty broadcast once did. The attention is scattered across feeds that each rank stories differently, for different people.

And many of the voices in those feeds are not outlets at all. About one in five American adults now regularly gets news from news influencers: individual accounts. Of the prominent ones Pew studied, 77 percent had never worked for a news organization.

So what does a feed ranked for engagement do to you? Here the popular story and the research have drifted apart, and honesty requires both.

The popular story is the filter bubble. The phrase is Eli Pariser's, and it holds that personalization seals each of us inside a separate reality: an echo chamber, built by an algorithm.

The largest test to date complicates that story. It is called the 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study. Its researchers switched tens of thousands of consenting users to a chronological feed, for three months. Those users spent less time on the platform, and they engaged less.

And their polarization? Their political knowledge? Their attitudes? [beat] No significant change. At least, not in those three months.

[pause]

So the honest summary has three parts.

Ranking for engagement demonstrably shapes what circulates: outrage travels, and corrections limp.

[beat]

The bubble's walls are real, but leakier than the slogan.

[beat]

And the deepest sorting may be the company we choose, not the ranking algorithm alone.

One caution, because a tempting shortcut is waiting here. It says: the algorithm shows people fake news, and that is why they believe wrong things.

But outright fabricated content is a small slice of most users' diets. The larger effects run through selection and emphasis: which true-ish things get amplified, framed and repeated.

> A feed can mislead you with nothing but accurate headlines.

It only has to choose which ones you see ten times.

## How a story travels

Now the third step: the reporting itself.

Most of the news you encounter was not reported by the account showing it to you. A typical story passes through a chain of hands, and each handoff adds value, or cost, or distortion. Let's walk the chain in order.

First, the event. Something happens: a vote, a verdict, a storm, a leak. At that instant almost nobody knows the full story, including the people in it.

Then, original reporting. A reporter gathers facts, calls sources, and files. It might be a local beat reporter, a specialist outlet, or a wire service like the AP. This is the expensive layer. It is also the shrinking one.

Then, editing. Editors challenge the sourcing, lawyers vet the risk of defamation, and someone writes a headline, often someone other than the reporter. The headline is what most people will ever see.

Then, syndication. Wire copy is licensed and reprinted by hundreds of outlets, which is why the same article appears everywhere under different logos.

Then, aggregation. Portals and rewrite shops repackage the reporting, adding reach, subtracting context, and paying the original reporter little or nothing. They write new headlines, built to be clicked. This is where a careful claim often becomes an incautious one.

Then, the feeds. They rank the story, and people share it: typically the headline, often unread.

And last, commentary. Influencers, podcasters and partisan accounts narrate the story to their own audiences. This is the fastest-growing layer, mostly staffed by people who have never worked in a newsroom. Some add genuine expertise, or firsthand documentation. Most add only voltage.

[pause]

Now walk any viral post backward down that chain. You will usually find a single piece of original reporting. [beat] It is holding up a skyscraper of derivative takes.

One distinction keeps this honest. Bystander video enters the chain at the reporting layer. That is genuine citizen journalism, and it is precious. A stranger's caption on someone else's clip enters at the commentary layer, and it is not.

So here is the habit.

> Identify which layer you are looking at before you decide what it is worth.

Let's put the habit to work. The same earthquake story appears, nearly word for word, on 200 small news sites within an hour.

Is that evidence the story is well confirmed? Or is it something else? Think it through.

[long pause]

It is something else. It is syndication: almost certainly one wire-service story, republished 200 times. That is one act of reporting, not 200 independent confirmations.

> Two hundred logos can equal exactly one source.

Keep that line. The next lecture's verification moves count independent origins, never raw repetitions.

## The layer that is caving in

Go back to the expensive layer, the one I called shrinking. Here is how fast it is going, at the local level.

Northwestern's Medill School counts the losses every year. Its report for 2025 counts roughly 3,500 newspapers lost since 2005. [beat] And closures continue, at more than two a week.

As of 2025, 213 American counties have no surviving local news source at all. They are called news deserts. In a news desert, the reporting step may never happen. And everything further down the chain inherits the gap.

[pause]

The cause is mostly economic. Classified advertising, the cash engine of every metro daily, went to free online marketplaces. Display advertising went overwhelmingly to platforms.

Why should a course in civics care? Because study after study finds that local accountability journalism was doing load-bearing civic work. Two findings.

One. After a local newspaper closes, the borrowing costs of its town rise. In plainer words:

> When a local paper closes, the town pays more to borrow.

Why would that be? Because the bond market prices the loss of the watchdog. Nobody is left reading the budget.

Two. Other studies link closures to lower local turnout, and to more party-line voting, as nationally polarized news takes the place of city hall.

[pause]

And into the vacuum has moved something worse than nothing.

In June 2024, NewsGuard counted 1,265 sites of a particular kind. Each is dressed as independent local news, but secretly funded by a partisan operation. Operations of both parties fund them. The name for such a site is pink slime. And that count slightly outnumbered the daily newspapers still surviving.

[pause]

Think of what a pink slime site is. It is the trusted form of the local paper, hollowed out, and refilled with someone's agenda.

That is why the identity of a source is the first thing to check. Who owns it? Who funds it? What is its track record? The answers won't tell you whether one article is true. They tell you what the outlet is for.

Let's test it. A site called the Madison County Courier covers your county, with a professional layout and bylined stories.

Here are four things you might learn about it. Its articles contain occasional typos and broken links. It is funded by a partisan PAC, and discloses no ownership. It is only a weekly, not a daily. Its reporters are young and inexperienced.

Which single fact should most change how you see that site? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The funding. [beat] Typos, frequency and junior staff are signals of quality. Undisclosed partisan funding is a signal of identity: it changes what the outlet is. Selection, framing and omission will serve the funder, even if every individual sentence is technically accurate.

## The synthetic layer

One layer is left, and it is new. Since roughly 2023 it has settled over the whole stack: media that no human witnessed, because no event occurred.

Two cases.

Slovakia, September 2023. Two days before an election, an audio clip goes viral. In it, a candidate apparently discusses vote-rigging with a journalist. The clip was synthetic: a deepfake. And it landed during the legal media blackout before the election, when corrections could not circulate. Researchers still caution against claims that it swung the result. But it demonstrated the playbook.

[beat]

New Hampshire, January 2024. An AI clone of President Biden's voice goes out by robocall to Democrats, urging them to skip the primary. The FCC proposed a forfeiture of 6 million dollars against the consultant who commissioned it. A New Hampshire jury later acquitted him on state charges. The law here is still being built.

[beat]

By late 2025, consumer tools like OpenAI's Sora app could render photorealistic people on demand, on video. Services that remove its watermark appeared within days of its launch.

Two consequences matter more than any single fake.

First, fabrication is now cheap in exactly the formats our brains treat as eyewitness evidence: a voice, a candid photo, a shaky video.

The second is more corrosive, and it has a name. Once everyone knows that convincing fakes exist, anyone caught on a real recording can shout "deepfake." The mere possibility of synthesis becomes a universal alibi. That is the liar's dividend.

> The liar's dividend: once fakes are convincing, any real recording can be called fake.

Be exact about what is new here. False content spread in error is misinformation. False content spread on purpose is disinformation. Both predate the technology. What AI changes is the unit cost.

And what the liar's dividend attacks is not your belief in any one clip. It attacks your confidence that anything can be settled by evidence.

[pause]

Holding the line against that last move is most of the job.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, reach back to the start. I gave you one question, in two halves. Can you still say both halves?

[long pause]

Who pays for this? And what does that make them want from me?

That question explains the Moon Hoax. It explains the free app. And it explains the Madison County Courier.

So. Five things to keep.

One. Follow the money. Advertising-funded media sells its audience to advertisers, so it is built to hold attention. And engagement is not a synonym for importance.

[beat]

Two. Editors once chose what a whole town saw. Now algorithms and sharing choose, one user at a time. That shapes what circulates, though the bubble's walls are leakier than the slogan. And a feed can mislead you with nothing but accurate headlines.

[beat]

Three. A story travels through layers, and most of what reaches you derives from a thin layer of original reporting. Identify which layer you are looking at. Two hundred logos can equal exactly one source.

[beat]

Four. That reporting layer is collapsing locally: roughly 3,500 newspapers lost since 2005. When a local paper closes, the town pays more to borrow. And impostors have moved into the ruins.

[beat]

Five. A synthetic layer now sits on top of everything. Fabrication is cheap. And the liar's dividend lets any real recording be called fake.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not told you how to evaluate any particular claim. That is the next lecture: the verification toolkit, with the research on which moves work.

And it has not told you who should referee truth at scale: editors, fact-checkers, platforms, crowds, or no one. That waits for the lecture after, where three views each get their strongest case: the institutionalist, the anti-establishment skeptic, and the civic-empowerment view.

Knowing how the machine works is necessary for both. It is sufficient for neither.

For now, go back to New York, in 1835. A paper for a penny, and a moon full of bat-people. [beat] You know now what the Sun was selling. It was never the moon.
