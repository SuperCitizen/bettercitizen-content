Early October, 2024. Hurricane Helene's floodwaters are standing in western North Carolina, and an image is tearing across social media.

A little girl in a life vest. She is sobbing, in a rescue boat, clutching a puppy.

[beat]

A sitting U.S. senator posted it with the caption "Caption this photo," inviting outrage at the federal response. He deleted it after users pointed out the obvious.

[beat]

The girl did not exist. Across different versions of the image her boat changed, her clothing changed, even her puppy's markings changed. The image was AI-generated.

[pause]

Here is the detail that matters most. Some prominent sharers, told exactly that, declined to take it down. One party official from Georgia told her followers that she didn't know where the photo came from. And that it didn't matter, because it captured how the storm's victims felt.

Real photographs of Helene's devastation existed in abundance. [beat] The fake won anyway.

It won because it was engineered to. A model had optimized it for plausibility. Feeds that optimize for emotion amplified it. And notice what it never needed.

> The fake never had to survive a check. It only had to outrun one.

You cannot stare at pixels hard enough to be safe in that world. The good news is that the skills that work are few, fast, teachable, and unusually well evidenced. The discipline is using them before you feel certain.

By the end of this lecture you will know what a professional fact-checker does with a claim, often in under a minute. We will take it in four steps.

First, why smart people get fooled, and what the professionals do instead.

Second, the four moves that came out of that.

Third, what can still go wrong: three ways.

And last, a limit: the point where doubt stops being a skill.

## Why smart people get fooled

The foundational study here watched experts fail.

Two Stanford researchers, Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, put three groups in front of unfamiliar websites. Ten historians, each with a PhD. Twenty-five Stanford undergraduates. And ten professional fact-checkers.

The historians and the students did what school had always rewarded. They stayed on the page, and they judged each site by its own evidence: the professional design, the footnotes, the dot-org address, the About page. Many of them were taken in by polished advocacy sites.

That habit has a name: vertical reading.

The fact-checkers did something that looked, at first, like cheating. [beat] Within seconds they left the page. They opened new tabs, and they checked what the rest of the web said about the source.

[pause]

They spent less time on the page, and more time learning about it. And they reached more accurate judgments, in a fraction of the time.

Wineburg and McGrew called it lateral reading. Vertical reading stays on the page. Lateral reading leaves it.

How deep does the problem run among beginners? The same Stanford group gave internet tasks to a national sample of more than 3,400 high-school students.

More than half judged an anonymous Facebook video to be "strong evidence" of U.S. voter fraud. [beat] The video had been shot in Russia.

And when they investigated a site about climate science, 96 percent never discovered its ties to the fossil-fuel industry.

[pause]

Those students were reading vertically. They were evaluating a performance that the site's authors controlled completely.

> A website's surface is testimony from the defendant. Lateral reading cross-examines.

Think of the checklists you may have learned in school. Does it look professional? Does it cite sources? Is the domain reputable? Every one of them evaluates a feature the deceiver controls. The pink slime sites from the last lecture pass those checklists by design.

There is one kind of evidence a fabricator cannot manufacture: what the rest of the web says, when asked about them.

## The four moves

A digital-literacy researcher named Mike Caulfield compressed the fact-checkers' practice into four moves. Their first letters spell a word: SIFT.

They are a habit, not a curriculum.

The first move is Stop. The moment a piece of content produces a surge in you, of outrage, or vindication, or fear, pause before you engage. Strong emotion is not proof that the thing is false. It is proof that you have been targeted, because emotion is what the distribution layer selects for.

Stop also applies to a page of search results. Don't dive into the first link. Scan the whole page first, the outlet names, the dates, the snippets, and map the territory before you enter it. That second kind of stopping is called click restraint.

[beat]

The second move is Investigate the source. This is lateral reading, applied. You open a new tab, and you ask what Wikipedia, or this app's source profiles, or a quick search says about the outlet or the account. You are not asking whether the site is nice. You are asking about its track record, its funding and its agenda. And you are asking whether it is in a position to know what it claims to know.

[beat]

The third move is Find better coverage. You usually don't need to verify this article. You need to verify the claim. So you search for the claim itself, and see whether outlets with verification machinery have covered it, and ideally fact-checking organizations too. If a bombshell exists on exactly one site, and nowhere else 48 hours later, that silence is data.

[beat]

The fourth move is Trace to the original. Most viral misinformation is not fabricated from nothing. It is real material, amputated from its context. A flood video from 2014, relabeled for this week's storm. A quote, truncated at the comma that reversed it. A caveat on page 38 of a study, promoted to a headline. So you get to the original, the full video, the transcript, the paper, the wire photo, and you judge that.

[pause]

> Stop. Investigate the source. Find better coverage. Trace to the original.

For images, tracing has a dedicated tool: the reverse image search. You feed it the picture, and it finds where that picture has appeared before.

That catches the commonest photo lie instantly: a real image, with a false caption and the wrong year. Often it catches AI images too, by surfacing the fact-check.

What it replaces is the hunt for flaws, like counting fingers. The generators improve every month. The better question is about provenance: where has this existed before?

> Provenance ages better than forensics.

Let's test the moves. A friend sends you a screenshot. It shows a CNN chyron, the on-screen caption, announcing a celebrity's arrest. The screenshot looks completely authentic.

You have four options. Zoom in on the font and the lighting, for signs of editing. Search whether CNN, or any other outlet, is reporting the arrest. Check your friend's reliability: have they shared fakes before? Or ask your friend where they got it.

Which is the highest-value first move? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Search for the arrest. That is the third move: find better coverage. A real CNN story exists on CNN, and competitors match it within minutes. A fabricated chyron exists only as a screenshot.

Zooming in fails for a reason you have met already: it evaluates evidence the faker controls. And the two questions about your friend are reasonable, but secondary. The claim, not the courier, is what can be checked.

## What kind of piece is this?

Now, what can still go wrong. The first way is the plainest: mistaking the kind of piece in front of you.

A surprising share of the grievances that begin "the media lied" dissolve into a confusion of genre. Analysis is treated as news. Opinion is treated as the institution. Sponsorship is treated as coverage.

The labels are real commitments, and four of them are worth knowing.

A news report promises verified facts, with sourcing, and it owes you a correction if it is wrong.

Analysis promises interpretation by the newsroom, anchored to reporting.

Opinion promises one writer's argument, or the editorial board's. The facts are checked. The conclusions are not.

And sponsored content is advertising, wearing the outlet's typography. Its labels say "paid post," or "presented by."

[pause]

Set the two ends side by side. A news piece commits the outlet to verified fact. An op-ed commits it to almost nothing but the author's identity. That is why the line matters.

A related skill is reading a correction. A serious outlet's correction states what was wrong, when, and how it was fixed. And the existence of a corrections page is evidence of a working system for handling error. It is not evidence of untrustworthiness. Outlets that never correct anything are not outlets that never err.

[beat]

So note exactly what was conceded. "We misstated the year of the audit" does not retract the audit story.

> A correction to one detail is not a retraction of the story.

Here is a scene. Your uncle texts you: "Even the New York Times admits masks did nothing. They printed a correction!"

Before you reply, what two things do you check? And what distinction will probably decide the argument?

[long pause]

First, trace to the original. Find the actual text of the correction. What specifically was corrected: a number, the attribution of a quote, a date? And on which article?

Second, find better coverage of the underlying claim, to see where the evidence stands.

The deciding distinction is usually correction versus retraction, and with it, news versus opinion. A correction to one detail gets retold as an admission that the whole thing was false. An op-ed's claim gets billed as what the Times said.

Most such arguments end when the correction's actual text arrives.

## When checking backfires

The second way is the uncomfortable one. Every debate about doing your own research now has to pass through it.

In late 2023 the journal Nature published five experiments. Researchers asked people to evaluate recent news articles, some true and some false. And they encouraged some of those people to search online before judging.

Here is what they found. Searching to evaluate the false stories increased belief in them, by a meaningful margin.

[pause]

Increased it. How can checking make things worse?

The mechanism is called a data void. A fresh or fringe false claim often exists online almost exclusively in the words of the people pushing it. So if you search the claim's own distinctive phrasing, the exact headline of a hoax, for instance, you get page after page of low-quality sources that corroborate it. You get the lie, mirrored.

Put another way: search is a tool for navigating the information ecosystem, and it inherits every distortion of that ecosystem.

The countermeasures belong to the toolkit, and the first is the one to memorize.

> Search the event in neutral terms, not the claim in its own words.

Then practice click restraint, and scan the results page before you enter it. Give weight to the sources you have vetted laterally, not to whatever ranked first. And respect recency. In the first hours of a breaking event the void is at its widest, and even good outlets are mostly relaying fragments.

Add one quiet statistical habit: base-rate awareness. Think of all the anonymous posts marked "BREAKING" that you have ever seen, making sensational claims with no named source. What fraction held up?

[long pause]

Whatever your number, that history is your starting probability. Not the vividness of today's specimen. An extraordinary claim that arrives through a low-accountability channel starts low. And a screenshot of a screenshot does not move it much.

The Sun's readers, in 1835, had no reference class for a newspaper that invents a civilization on the moon. [beat] You do.

## Name the move

The third way is different in kind. Plenty of misleading content contains no false facts at all. The deception lives in the move the argument makes. These moves have names, and naming them is the fastest way to stop them working on you.

Two earn special flags in modern feeds. The first is the Gish gallop: twenty dubious statistics in two minutes, each needing ten minutes to check. It thrives because volume is cheap online, and refutation is not. The second is whataboutism. Fine, our senator took bribes, but what about their party's scandals? It is the universal solvent of accountability.

Here are eight more, each in a breath.

The straw man refutes a weaker claim than the one actually made. "They want speed limits" becomes "they want to ban cars."

The red herring is a genuinely interesting irrelevance, introduced to move the argument somewhere safer.

The slippery slope asserts, without evidence, that step one makes a catastrophic step ten inevitable.

Begging the question smuggles the conclusion into the premises.

The appeal to authority cites status instead of evidence, especially status in the wrong field.

The bandwagon treats popularity as proof. Ten million people have shared this! But virality measures contagiousness, not accuracy.

Tu quoque treats hypocrisy as refutation. You drive a car, so your climate argument fails. But hypocrites can be right.

And the loaded question convicts whoever answers it. Why is the city hiding the report? Challenge the premise, not the question.

[pause]

One caution. Naming fallacies is for auditing arguments, including your own side's. Deployed as a gotcha, it becomes its own bad argument. The antidote is the steel-man habit, the opposite of the straw man.

## The limit

Last, a warning, because the evidence demands one.

Teach people that media can deceive them, and some of them draw a conclusion that sounds efficient: [beat] trust nothing.

That conclusion has a sponsor.

[beat]

Analysts at RAND studied a Russian propaganda model known as the firehose of falsehood: high in volume, spread across many channels, and shamelessly contradictory. Propaganda like that, they observed, doesn't need you to believe any particular story. Its strategic payoff is the conclusion that nothing can be known. After that, contests for power are decided by loyalty and volume, instead of evidence.

The journalist Peter Pomerantsev gave that end-state its name, in the title of a book: Nothing Is True and Everything Is Possible.

[pause]

So set two citizens side by side. A citizen who believes everything is a propagandist's tool.

> A citizen who believes nothing is the propagandist's product.

The two are equally manipulable. A person with no way to settle questions of fact decides them by tribe.

What the toolkit produces is neither belief nor armor. [beat] It is calibrated trust. That means confidence that is provisional, proportioned to a source's track record and to independent corroboration, and revisable on evidence. It is the working state of mind of every fact-checker in the Stanford study.

Reach back to those fact-checkers for a moment. What did they do, within seconds, that the historians did not?

[long pause]

They left the page, and they checked what the rest of the web said about the source. That was lateral reading. Their confidence came after the check, and in proportion to it.

Calibration has a hard edge. It means accepting verified things you wish were false. A toolkit applied only to the other side's claims is just confirmation bias, with better tools.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Read laterally. Leave the page, and ask the rest of the web about the source. A website's surface is testimony from the defendant.

[beat]

Two. The four moves. Stop. Investigate the source. Find better coverage. Trace to the original. And at the original, notice the genre, and notice exactly what a correction concedes.

[beat]

Three. Searching can make things worse. In a data void, the claim's own words return the lie, mirrored. So search the event in neutral terms, and start from the base rate.

[beat]

Four. Name the move. The Gish gallop and whataboutism. The straw man and the red herring. The slippery slope, and begging the question. The appeal to authority, and the bandwagon. Tu quoque, and the loaded question.

[beat]

Five. The goal is calibrated trust, not cynicism. A citizen who believes nothing is the propagandist's product.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. You can now do what the fact-checkers did. None of that answers whose verification should bind anyone but you.

When a platform labels a post, when a fact-checker rates a claim, when a crowd of volunteers appends a note, someone has been deputized to referee. Who deserves that power, given the documented failures of every candidate? That is a real controversy among serious people, and it is the subject of the next lecture.

For now, go back to the girl in the boat. She did not exist, and she won anyway, because she was shared faster than anyone checked. [beat] A check often takes under a minute. That is the whole contest.
