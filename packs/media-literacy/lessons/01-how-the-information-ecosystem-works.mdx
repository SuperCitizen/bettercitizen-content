# How the information ecosystem works

On August 25, 1835, the *New York Sun* began publishing a six-part exclusive: the eminent astronomer Sir John Herschel, observing from South Africa through a giant new telescope, had discovered life on the moon — unicorns, bipedal beavers, and winged bat-people who built temples. None of it was true. The series was almost certainly written by a *Sun* reporter, Richard Adams Locke; the "scientific journal" it cited had ceased to exist. It didn't matter. Sales soared, a delegation of Yale scientists came hunting for the original articles, and on September 16 the *Sun* cheerfully acknowledged the fabrication — without ever quite retracting it. The *Sun*, two years old and a penny a copy, had pioneered a new business model: sell the paper cheap, build a mass audience, and sell the *audience* to advertisers. The Great Moon Hoax was not a glitch in that model. It was the model, working.

Start here, because the single most useful question in media literacy is not "is this true?" but "**who pays for this, and what does that make them want from me?**" This lesson is about the machine — the incentives, pipes, and choke points that decide what reaches you. The skills for evaluating individual claims come in Lesson 2; the fight over who should referee it all comes in Lesson 3.

## The business model is the message

For most of the 1800s, American newspapers were openly partisan — many were literally funded by political parties and printing contracts. The penny press broke that dependency by substituting a different one: advertising. The sociologist Michael Schudson's *Discovering the News* (1978) traces how the commercial mass paper invented things we now treat as eternal — the interview, the beat reporter, eventually the ideal of "objectivity" itself, which emerged less from virtue than from the need to sell one paper to Democrats and Republicans alike. The ethic of the [[term:fourth-estate]] — journalism as a check on power — grew up *inside* a commercial machine, and the tension between the two has never gone away. Its lurid extreme had a name by the 1890s: [[term:yellow-journalism]], the Hearst–Pulitzer circulation war that taught the industry how reliably outrage and spectacle convert to sales.

The economist James T. Hamilton's *All the News That's Fit to Sell* (2004) puts the modern version formally: news is a product, and its content is driven by the audiences advertisers will pay to reach. Hamilton's analysis of network evening news from 1969 to 1998 documents the drift from hard political coverage toward soft news as cable competition fragmented audiences — not because journalists got lazier, but because the *marginal viewer* the networks were fighting over preferred it. The supply of news about your city council is thin not because the information doesn't matter but because the audience that pays for content rarely demands it.

The internet did not change this logic; it perfected it. When attention is measured per click and sold per impression, the headline that *almost* tells you the thing ("You won't believe what the CDC just admitted") beats the headline that tells you the thing — which is all [[term:clickbait]] is: the penny-press incentive, instrumented in real time.

<Checkpoint question="A free news app shows you no subscription screen and no donation button. Following the money, what is the app most likely selling, to whom — and what behavior does that make the app want from you?">
  It is selling **your attention** (and usually data about you) to **advertisers**. That makes its core incentive *time spent and engagement* — clicks, scrolls, shares — not accuracy or importance. Nothing about that proves any given story false; it tells you which errors the system will tend to make: toward the arresting, the emotional, and the more-of-what-you-already-liked.
</Checkpoint>

## What the feed optimizes for

Through most of the 20th century, distribution was **institutional**: an editor chose what went on the front page or the 6:30 broadcast, and everyone in town saw roughly the same thing. Distribution is now substantially **social**: platforms rank items one user at a time, optimizing mostly for engagement, and your neighbors' feeds are not your feeds. However your guesses landed, the structural point to notice is that no single platform owns the news audience the way the 6:30 broadcast once did: the largest platform reaches barely a third of adults, and the rest of the attention is scattered across feeds that each rank stories differently, for different people. Two more Pew numbers complete the picture. About one in five U.S. adults — and 37% of adults under 30 — now regularly gets news from **news influencers**, individual accounts rather than outlets; of 500 prominent news influencers Pew studied in 2024, 77% had never worked for a news organization. Trust runs the other way from usage: as of late 2025, 56% of U.S. adults express at least some trust in information from national news organizations, down roughly 20 percentage points from 2016, with local outlets higher (70%) but falling too.

What does engagement ranking actually do to you? Here the popular story and the research have drifted apart, and honesty requires both. The popular story — Eli Pariser's [[term:filter-bubble]], the algorithmic [[term:echo-chamber]] — holds that personalization seals each of us in a separate reality. The largest test to date, the US 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study, complicates it: when researchers (publishing independently in *Science* and *Nature*, 2023) switched tens of thousands of consenting users to a chronological feed for three months, they saw less time on platform and less engagement — but **no significant change in polarization, political knowledge, or attitudes** over the study window. Most users' diets were less hermetically sealed than the metaphor suggests, and like-minded content, while prevalent, wasn't doing the polarizing work the metaphor assigns it — at least not in three months. The honest summary: engagement optimization demonstrably shapes *what circulates* (outrage travels; corrections limp), the bubble's walls are real but leakier than the slogan, and the deepest sorting may be the company we choose, not the ranking algorithm alone.

> **Common misconception:** "The algorithm shows people fake news, and that's why they believe wrong things." Outright fabricated content is a small slice of most users' diets; the larger effects run through *selection and emphasis* — which true-ish things get amplified, framed, and repeated. A feed can mislead you with nothing but accurate headlines, simply by choosing which ones you see ten times.

## The collapse of local news

While national outlets fight for attention, the layer beneath them has been quietly caving in. Northwestern's Medill School has tracked the carnage annually; its *State of Local News 2025* report counts roughly **3,500 newspapers lost since 2005** — closures continuing at more than two per week — and more than **three-quarters of newspaper jobs gone** over the same period. As of 2025, **213 U.S. counties are "news deserts"** with no surviving local news source, over 1,500 more have only one (often a weekly), and about 50 million Americans live with limited or no access to local news. The cause is mostly economic: classified advertising — every metro daily's cash engine — went to free online marketplaces, and the display dollars that followed audiences online went overwhelmingly to platforms, not publishers.

Why should a civics curriculum care? Because the empirical literature keeps finding that local accountability journalism was doing load-bearing civic work. One widely cited finding, from the *Journal of Financial Economics* (Gao, Lee & Murphy 2020): after a local newspaper closes, the affected municipality's borrowing costs rise by 5 to 11 basis points — bond markets literally price the loss of the watchdog, because nobody is left reading the budget. Other studies link closures to lower local turnout and more party-line voting, as nationally polarized news substitutes for city hall.

Into the vacuum has moved something worse than nothing. In June 2024, NewsGuard counted **1,265 "pink slime" sites** — outlets dressed as independent local news but secretly funded by partisan operations of both parties — slightly *outnumbering* the roughly 1,213 surviving daily newspapers. A pink-slime site is [[term:astroturfing]] applied to journalism: the trusted *form* of the local paper, hollowed out and refilled with someone's agenda. This is why source identity — who owns it, who funds it, what's their track record — is the first thing to check, and why SuperCitizen maintains structured profiles of 80 news outlets (ownership, funding, leanings, corrections record) you can pull up whenever a story's origin matters. The profile won't tell you whether one article is true; it tells you what the outlet is *for*.

## How a story actually travels

Most "news" you encounter was not reported by the account showing it to you. A typical story passes through distinct hands, and each handoff adds value, cost, or distortion:

<ProcessFlow title="From event to your feed" steps={[
  {title: 'Event', detail: 'Something happens: a vote, a verdict, a storm, a leak. At this instant, almost nobody knows the full story — including the people in it.'},
  {title: 'Original reporting', detail: 'A reporter — wire service (the AP was founded in 1846 by five New York papers splitting the cost of covering the Mexican-American War), local beat reporter, or specialist outlet — gathers facts, calls sources, and files. This is the expensive, shrinking layer.', branch: 'In a news desert, this step may simply not happen — and everything downstream inherits the gap.'},
  {title: 'Editing and verification', detail: 'Editors challenge sourcing, lawyers vet defamation risk, headlines get written (often by someone other than the reporter — and the headline is what most people will ever see).'},
  {title: 'Publication and syndication', detail: 'The story posts; wire copy is licensed and reprinted by hundreds of outlets, which is why the same article appears everywhere with different logos.'},
  {title: 'Aggregation', detail: 'Aggregators and portals (Google News, Apple News, Yahoo) and rewrite shops repackage the reporting — adding reach, subtracting context, paying the original reporter little or nothing.', branch: 'Aggregators re-headline for clicks; this is where a careful claim often becomes an incautious one.'},
  {title: 'Social amplification', detail: 'Feeds rank it; people share it — typically the headline, often unread. Engagement optimization decides which version of the story multiplies.'},
  {title: 'Commentary and creators', detail: 'Influencers, podcasters, and partisan accounts narrate the story to their audiences — the fastest-growing layer, mostly staffed by people who have never worked in a newsroom. Some add genuine expertise or firsthand documentation; most add only voltage.'},
]} />

Walk any viral post backward down this chain and you will usually find a single piece of original reporting supporting a skyscraper of derivative takes. (Genuine [[term:citizen-journalism]] — bystander video, open-source investigation — enters at the *reporting* layer and is precious; a stranger's caption on someone else's clip enters at the *commentary* layer and is not.) The practical habit this teaches: **identify which layer you're looking at before you decide what it's worth.**

<Checkpoint question="The same earthquake story appears, nearly word-for-word, on 200 small news sites within an hour. Is that evidence the story is well-confirmed — or something else?">
  Something else: it's **syndication**, almost certainly one wire-service story republished 200 times. That's one act of reporting, not 200 independent confirmations. Two hundred logos can equal exactly one source — which is why Lesson 2's verification moves count *independent* origins, never raw repetitions.
</Checkpoint>

## The synthetic layer

Since roughly 2023, a new stratum has settled over the whole stack: media that no human witnessed because no event occurred. Two days before Slovakia's September 2023 election — during the legal pre-election media blackout, when corrections couldn't circulate — an audio [[term:deepfake]] of candidate Michal Šimečka apparently discussing vote-rigging with a journalist went viral; researchers still caution against claims that it swung the result, but it demonstrated the playbook. In January 2024, an AI clone of President Biden's voice robocalled New Hampshire Democrats urging them to skip the primary; the FCC proposed a $6 million forfeiture against the consultant who commissioned it (a New Hampshire jury later acquitted him on state charges — the law here is still being built). During Hurricane Helene that fall, an AI image of a crying girl clutching a puppy in floodwaters became a political cudgel and kept circulating even after sharers acknowledged it was fake. By late 2025, consumer video tools like OpenAI's Sora app could render photorealistic people on demand; watermark-removal services appeared within days of its launch.

Two consequences matter more than any single fake. First, fabrication is now cheap in exactly the formats — voice, candid photo, shaky video — that our brains treat as eyewitness evidence. Second, and more corrosive, the **liar's dividend**: once everyone knows convincing fakes exist, anyone caught on a *real* recording can shout "deepfake," and the mere possibility of synthesis becomes a universal alibi. Notice that both [[term:misinformation]] (false content spread in error) and [[term:disinformation]] (false content spread on purpose) predate the technology; what AI changes is the unit cost — and what the liar's dividend attacks is not your belief in any one clip but your confidence that *anything* can be settled by evidence. Holding the line against that last move is, as you'll see in Lesson 2, most of the job.

## What this lesson doesn't settle

You now have the machine in view: an attention economy that sells audiences to advertisers; feeds that optimize engagement and reshape what circulates (though less totally than the bubble metaphor claims); a local accountability layer in collapse, with imposters moving into the ruins; a story pipeline in which most content is derivative of a thin reporting layer; and a synthetic stratum that makes "I saw the video" no longer self-certifying.

What it deliberately has *not* told you: how to evaluate any particular claim (Lesson 2 — the verification toolkit, with the research on which moves actually work), or who should be empowered to referee truth at scale — editors, fact-checkers, platforms, crowds, or no one (Lesson 3, where the institutionalist, the anti-establishment skeptic, and the civic-empowerment view each get their strongest case). Knowing how the machine works is necessary for both. It is sufficient for neither.
