# Who decides what's true?

On February 19, 2020, *The Lancet* published a statement from 27 scientists condemning "conspiracy theories suggesting that COVID-19 does not have a natural origin." Within a year that sentence had hardened into infrastructure: outlets called the lab-leak hypothesis "debunked," and Facebook was removing claims that the virus was "man-made" from a platform with billions of users. Five years later, in January 2025, the CIA assessed that a research-related origin was "more likely" than a natural one — at *low confidence*, from no new evidence, with both scenarios still plausible. As of mid-2026, the question remains genuinely unresolved.

Read that arc twice, because partisans of every camp in this lesson misread it once. The gatekeepers really did enforce a premature consensus and suppress a legitimate hypothesis — labeling something [[term:misinformation]] that was, at worst, unproven. And the skeptics' favorite ending — "so the lab leak was *true* all along" — is also not what happened: the honest status is *contested, low confidence, evidence incomplete*, and most published virological analyses still favor a market origin. The episode is this lesson's case study precisely because it indicts everyone's preferred referee at once. So: when citizens disagree about facts at scale, **whose call should count?** Three serious answers compete, and per this catalog's rules for contested ground, each gets its smartest advocate — not its dumbest critic.

## Three answers, each at full strength

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Institutionalist (professional-gatekeeping) view">
    Verification is skilled labor performed inside accountable systems, and no society has found a substitute. The reporter's claim passes through editors, fact-checkers, and libel lawyers; the published error triggers a correction owed in public, under a masthead with a reputation that takes decades to build and one scandal to destroy. That machinery — not anyone's virtue — is why retractions exist at all. The empirical record vindicates the product: professional [[term:fact-checking]] corrections measurably and durably improve belief accuracy, across countries and across the political aisle (Porter & Wood's four-country experiments, PNAS 2021; Walter et al.'s meta-analysis, 2020). The deeper argument is Schudson's: a common, professionally vetted baseline of fact is *civic infrastructure* — what makes disagreement about values possible without disagreement about reality. Yes, the gatekeepers blew the lab-leak call. Notice, though, *who produced the reversal*: scientists publishing in Science, investigative reporters at legacy outlets, an institutional intelligence review. The system's misses are real, loud, and self-documented — which is exactly the point. Name the alternative that even *has* a corrections page. A world that fires its referees does not get fewer errors; it gets errors with no mechanism for ending.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Anti-establishment skeptic">
    The institutionalist story stumbles on its own case study. The lab-leak hypothesis wasn't felled by evidence — it was strangled by *consensus enforcement*: a letter with undisclosed conflicts, laundered through prestige media into a "debunked" label, enforced by platforms at the scale of billions. The same machinery throttled a New York Post story about a candidate's son's laptop weeks before the 2020 election (Twitter blocked the URL; its CEO later called that a mistake, and other outlets eventually verified key contents). Add the unanimous, prestigious, catastrophically wrong WMD consensus of 2002–03. The novelist Michael Crichton named the everyday version **Gell-Mann amnesia**: you read the paper's coverage of your own field, see it's wrong, turn the page, and trust the next story. And the experts behind the gatekeepers? Psychology's flagship replication project (Science, 2015) found only 36% of 100 landmark findings replicated significantly, effect sizes halved — credentialed consensus, peer-reviewed, wrong at scale. The remedy isn't "trust no one"; it's *decentralized, adversarial* correction with skin in the game. X's Community Notes is the prototype: notes publish only when raters who usually disagree both find them helpful — a bridging algorithm, not a committee — and a 2025 PNAS study of 40,078 noted posts found attached notes cut reposts of flagged posts nearly in half in the moment. Gatekeeping concentrated is a single point of failure; ten thousand motivated checkers with transparent rules are not.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Civic-empowerment (literacy-first) view">
    Both rivals argue about who should hold the referee's whistle; this view says the contest itself is the mistake. Any central arbiter — editorial, corporate, or crowd-sourced — becomes a target to capture and a grievance to mobilize against, and labels do nothing for the citizen who has already stopped trusting labelers. The durable defense is *distributed competence*: a population that verifies the way Lesson 2 teaches. This is the most evidence-rich corner of the debate. Lateral-reading instruction improves source evaluation in randomized and classroom studies (Stanford's Civic Online Reasoning curriculum; McGrew & Breakstone, AERA Open 2023). Prebunking works at platform scale: ninety-second inoculation videos improved manipulation-technique recognition and sharing judgment in six RCTs and a 22,000-person YouTube field study (Roozenbeek, van der Linden et al., Science Advances 2022). Finland — decades of media education in its schools, top of the European rankings — treats this as civil defense, though honesty requires the caveat below. The deepest argument is civic: deciding what's true *is the citizen's job description* — outsource it, and the capacity atrophies exactly when you need it most, since the referee you trust today will someday be staffed by people you don't.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## What the evidence actually shows

Steel-manning is not scorekeeping-abstinence; the positions make empirical claims, and some have been tested.

**Professional fact-checking: real effects, real ceiling.** The experimental literature is unusually consistent: corrections improve factual beliefs, the improvement persists for weeks, and "backfire effects" (corrections entrenching the false belief) are rare — much rarer than early lore claimed. The ceiling is reach and trust: fact-checks are read disproportionately by people who least need them, and the label "fact-checker" itself polarizes — which is partly why Meta, on January 7, 2025, ended its eight-year U.S. third-party fact-checking program and announced a move to a Community Notes model across Facebook, Instagram, and Threads: a live experiment in replacing answer one with answer two.

**Community Notes: the crowd works — late.** Two findings must be held simultaneously. When a note reaches a post, it works: the PNAS synthetic-control study found roughly 46% fewer reposts immediately after a note attached (about 12% over the post's whole life, since notes arrive mid-flight), with effects across the political spectrum. But at the *platform* level, the CSCW study of the full rollout found no detectable reduction in engagement with misleading posts — the consensus mechanism takes hours and virality takes minutes, so most misleading impressions happen before any note exists. Two further honesty notes: the bridging requirement means many accurate notes on politically charged posts *never* publish (raters who usually disagree rarely agree there), and notes lean heavily on professional fact-checks and news organizations as their evidence — the crowd, in practice, aggregates the gatekeepers it was supposed to replace. Symbiosis, not substitution.

**Literacy and prebunking: effective, modest, decaying.** The inoculation RCTs and lateral-reading classroom studies show genuine, replicated gains in *skill* — recognizing manipulation, rating sources more accurately. The caveats: effect sizes are modest, fade in weeks to months without boosters, and demonstrating that a skills gain changes *real-world belief or sharing at population scale* remains an open research frontier. And the Finland story deserves its asterisk: the famous rankings come from the Open Society Institute's Media Literacy Index, which scores countries on *inputs* — education quality (largely PISA scores), press freedom, social trust, e-participation — not on measured citizen performance against misinformation. Finland's curriculum is real and seriously implemented; the headline "Finland defeats fake news" outruns what the index can show. (In the 2026 edition, Denmark, Finland, Ireland, and the Netherlands share first place.) A media-literacy pack that hyped its own product would be self-refuting. The honest claim: these are the best-validated *individual-level* tools we have, and nobody has yet shown they scale into herd immunity.

## The law lives next door

Whoever you'd crown as referee, note what this lesson has *not* argued about: what platforms may legally host, remove, or be sued over. In the United States that is the domain of the First Amendment's state-action line, of [[term:section-230]]'s liability shield for user content, and of the jawboning question — when does government "asking" a platform to act become coercion? (The Supreme Court's *Murthy v. Missouri*, 2024, resolved a major jawboning suit on standing grounds while leaving the core line blurry.) This catalog's **Free Speech and the First Amendment** pack owns that terrain, including the platform-moderation cases. Keep the two questions separate: *who decides what's true* is an epistemic question that would survive any legal regime, and conflating it with *what speech is legal* is how both debates go bad.

## Holding a position without joining a tribe

The three perspectives are positions about *institutional design* — and every one of them curdles into something dumber when held tribally. The institutionalist view curdles into "my outlets are the real ones," which is just [[term:bias]] wearing a press badge. The civic-empowerment view curdles into "everyone just needs my workshop," a non-answer at viral speed. The skeptic's view curdles fastest, because its failure mode has a body count: from "gatekeepers err" it is a short, well-greased slide to [[term:conspiracism]] — the style of thought in which every institutional error is *intentional*, every absence of evidence is suppression, and every correction is the cover-up. Recall where that ends: a man with a rifle inside a Washington pizzeria in December 2016, "self-investigating" [[term:pizzagate]] — a claim the entire apparatus had checked and refuted, for an audience trained not to care.

Two traps guard the exits. One is [[term:false-equivalence]] — "every referee has failed, so all claims are equal," the [[term:bothsidesism]] that treats a low-confidence intelligence assessment and a peer-reviewed literature as tied. The other is the cynic's surrender from Lesson 2 — "nothing is true," the propagandist's finish line. The discipline that avoids both is the one this entire pack has been teaching in pieces: **proportion belief to track record and corroboration; state what would change your mind; check whether your referee complaint survives the cases where your own side benefited from refereeing.** A useful self-test on that last point: if you cheered when platforms suppressed the claim you hated, and raged when they suppressed the one you liked, your actual position is not one of the three above — it is "referees for thee."

<Checkpoint question="Design a quick falsification test for YOUR current lean. What near-term, observable evidence would move you toward each of the other two positions?">
  Toward the **institutionalist**: decentralized systems persistently failing on contested political claims (notes never publishing, hoaxes outrunning crowds) while professional outlets' corrections demonstrably land. Toward the **skeptic**: another lab-leak-scale episode — gatekeepers enforcing a "consensus" that collapses under undisclosed conflicts — or bridging-based systems starting to beat newsrooms on speed *and* accuracy. Toward **civic-empowerment**: prebunking and lateral-reading programs showing durable, population-scale effects on real sharing behavior (not just lab tasks). If nothing on this list would move you, you are holding an identity, not a position — the failure mode every lesson in this pack has warned about.
</Checkpoint>

## What this pack doesn't settle

This lesson closes the pack, so the ledger should be explicit. Settled here, as well as anything in this curriculum is: how the attention economy shapes what reaches you; that local accountability journalism collapsed and what that costs; that lateral reading, SIFT, and tracing-to-origin outperform staring harder at the artifact; that corrections work but reach poorly; that crowd notes work but arrive late; that literacy training works but modestly and perishably; and that reflexive cynicism is not a sophisticated stance but a manufactured one.

Genuinely unsettled: who, if anyone, should referee truth at scale — because that question is *value-laden* (it trades error rates against capture risk against civic capacity) and the evidence, honestly read, refuses to crown a winner. Unsettled too: the virus's origin, which is precisely why it made a good case study. A citizen who can say "contested, low confidence, here's what would change my mind" about both questions has gotten everything this pack has to give. The argument continues — in the Free Speech pack for the law, and in your feed, tonight, where all three referees and their failures will be on display.
