## Fifty-four is not sixty

On April 17, 2013, four months after the Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting, the Senate voted on the Manchin-Toomey amendment, a background-check compromise. Fifty-four senators voted aye, forty-six no. It failed. No one stood and talked through the night. Sixty was the price of proceeding, and fifty-four is not sixty.

- Manchin-Toomey: 54 to 46
- The price of proceeding: 60

> Fifty-four senators voted aye, and it failed.

## A series of doors

Legal scholars call the chokepoints vetogates: points in the process where some actor can block a bill outright. The Constitution built the first three. The House, the Senate and the White House can each say no. Everything else, from committee chairs to the 60-vote cloture threshold, is the chambers' own construction.

- Vetogate: where one actor can block

A bill's supporters must hold a winning coalition at every gate. Opponents need to capture just one keeper, which is why lobbying to kill is so much cheaper than lobbying to pass.

- Supporters: every gate
- Opponents: one keeper

Roughly nine in ten bills die in committee, most without a hearing or a vote.

- Roughly 9 in 10 die in committee

## 51, 60, 67

The Senate has not one threshold but three. 51 votes pass a bill, once it gets a final vote. 60 votes invoke cloture, and get it that final vote. 67 override a veto.

- 51: pass
- 60: reach the final vote
- 67: override a veto

A bill backed by 51 to 59 senators commands a majority and, for ordinary legislation, has no path to a final vote.

> From 51 to 59: a majority that cannot legislate.

## The pivotal senator

The political scientist Keith Krehbiel turned this arithmetic into a theory. The Senate is driven not by the 51st senator but by the pivots: the 60th, whose vote ends a filibuster, and the 67th, whose vote overrides a veto. Between the pivots lies the gridlock interval, the range of existing policies that cannot be changed.

- Pivots: the 60th and 67th senators
- Gridlock interval: policy that cannot change

That is why unified government disappoints. Winning the House, the Senate and the White House moves the median. If the 60th senator barely moves, the gridlock interval barely shrinks.

## Wisdom or pathology

The lesson does not settle whether the gauntlet is wisdom or pathology. The case for the gates is that supermajority hurdles force broad, durable coalitions, and a status quo that is hard to change is also hard to wreck. The case against is that the gridlock interval has no opinion about whether the status quo is any good, and problems compound while the math holds.

- For the gates: broad, durable coalitions
- Against: problems compound

> Bills die at gates, and the gates have keepers.

The lesson names each keeper, and gives a checklist for finding where a stalled bill is stuck.
