## The cartoon and the odds

In the old Schoolhouse Rock cartoon, a rolled-up scrap of paper sits on the Capitol steps and sighs, "I'm just a bill." By the end of the song, he is a law. The cartoon is accurate about the steps and wildly optimistic about the odds.

In the 118th Congress, members introduced more than 19,000 bills and resolutions. Exactly 274 became public law.

- Introduced: more than 19,000
- Enacted: 274

> Fewer than two in every hundred became law.

## Five places a bill dies

So the honest question is not how a bill becomes a law. It is where bills die. There are five chokepoints, in order: committee, the House Rules Committee, the Senate's 60-vote cloture threshold, the identical-text requirement, and the President's desk.

- Committee, then House Rules
- Senate cloture: 60 votes
- Identical text in both chambers
- The President's desk

> A bill must clear all five. Its opponents need to win at one.

## The committee graveyard

At the first stop, a referred bill's default fate is nothing: no hearing, no markup, no vote, just a quiet death at session's end. This is the single deadliest point in the process.

- Default fate: no hearing, no vote

The House has one escape hatch, the discharge petition. If 218 members sign, a majority of the full House, a bill is pried out of committee and onto the floor whether leadership likes it or not. In November 2025, a petition forced a vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act over the Speaker's opposition. The House passed the bill 427 to 1.

- Discharge petition: 218 signatures

## A majority is not sixty

In the Senate, the refusal to stop debating is the filibuster. Ending debate takes cloture: three-fifths of the full Senate, 60 votes. Picture a bill with 55 solid supporters and all 100 senators present. Passage takes 51. Getting to the passage vote takes 60, and 55 is five short. The bill stalls.

- To pass: 51
- To reach the vote: 60

> Having a majority and having sixty are different currencies in the Senate.

There is one regular exception. A reconciliation bill, limited to fiscal matters, cannot be filibustered and passes with a simple majority.

- Reconciliation: a simple majority

## Feature or defect

Whether all this friction is a feature or a defect, the lesson does not settle. The Framers wanted lawmaking slow. But the filibuster and the Rules Committee are not in the Constitution. They are choices that could be unmade by the chambers that made them.

- Not in the Constitution: filibuster, Rules Committee
