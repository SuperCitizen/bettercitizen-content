## The act that never passed the House

Before dawn on December 20, 2022, Senate appropriators released the text of a spending bill: 4,155 pages, roughly 1.7 trillion dollars. The President signed it on December 29. Inside rode the Electoral Count Reform Act, the first major overhaul since 1887 of the statute governing presidential electoral counts.

- 4,155 pages, roughly $1.7 trillion

Search the record for the moment that act passed the House as a bill. It never did.

> It never passed the House as a bill. It rode.

## The textbook became the exception

The political scientist Barbara Sinclair tracked major legislation, Congress by Congress, from the 1960s onward. The textbook sequence, in which one committee writes the bill, the floor amends it, and a conference reconciles the chambers, described most major bills when she started counting. It no longer does. Her label for what replaced it is unorthodox lawmaking.

- Sinclair: unorthodox lawmaking

The common thread is that power moved up. For the bills that matter most, the Speaker's office and the Senate Majority Leader's office negotiate the actual text.

- Power moved up, to party leaders

> For major bills, the textbook path is now the exception.

## Three vehicles

An omnibus fuses many measures into a single package, and the December 2022 bill was one. What makes it more than a stapling exercise is the rider: a provision, often unrelated to spending, attached to a vehicle that must pass. Riders work because the President cannot sign the spending and veto the rider.

- Omnibus: many measures, one package
- Rider: attached to a must-pass vehicle

A continuing resolution is a stopgap. It funds agencies at roughly last year's levels for a set number of weeks or months.

- Continuing resolution: last year's levels

And reconciliation passes the Senate with 51 votes, but the Byrd rule confines it to budgetary matters.

- Reconciliation: 51 votes, budget only

> The choice of vehicle often decides whether an idea becomes law.

## Adaptation or decay?

Reasonable institutionalists divide over what to make of this.

The pragmatic adaptationist argues that packaging is coalition-building made tractable. Hundreds of separately impossible agreements pass as one possible one, and every member still casts a recorded vote on every package.

- Adaptationist: packaging makes agreement possible

The regular-order restorationist argues that what is being lost is deliberation itself. A text released before dawn and passed within days has not been read by the people voting on it, and when everything passes in one vote, no one can be held responsible for anything in particular.

- Restorationist: deliberation is being lost

The lesson does not settle it. It gives both cases at full strength, traces two fiscal years funded at the cliff's edge, and sets the vehicles side by side.
