Picture a cartoon. A rolled-up scrap of paper sits on the steps of the Capitol, and sighs: "I'm just a bill."

That is the old Schoolhouse Rock song. By the end of it, the gavels bang, and the scrap of paper is a law.

[beat]

The cartoon is accurate about the steps. It is wildly optimistic about the odds.

Take one recent Congress, the 118th, which sat through 2023 and 2024. Its members introduced more than 19,000 bills and resolutions. [beat] Exactly 274 became public law.

[pause]

That is fewer than two in every hundred. And the count includes the laws that name post offices. Even in productive decades, the share of introduced bills that become law hovers in the low single digits.

So the honest question is not how a bill becomes a law. The honest question is where bills die. And what does it take to survive every one of those places, in sequence?

[pause]

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to trace a bill from an idea to an enacted law, and name each point along the way where it can be killed.

There are five of those points. Call them kill points. Here they are, in order.

Committee. The House Rules Committee. The sixty-vote threshold in the Senate. The requirement that both chambers pass identical text. And the President's desk.

A bill has to clear all five. Its opponents need to win at only one.

> Supporters must win at every stage. Opponents need to win only once.

That is why the status quo wins almost every tie. And none of these kill points is an accident. Each was added deliberately, at a different moment in history, by someone who wanted lawmaking to be harder.

We will walk the five in four steps. First, committee, the deadliest of them. Second, the two floors, the House and the Senate, which ration their time in opposite ways. Third, the last two gates: one text, and then the President. And last, a warning about the map itself, because the real Congress increasingly ignores it.

## Committee: the quiet death

Start at the very beginning. Who can introduce a bill? [beat] Only a sitting member of Congress.

Anyone can write one. The executive branch, advocacy groups and lobbyists draft bills constantly. But a member has to put a name on it. In the House, that means dropping the bill into the hopper, a wooden box beside the rostrum.

After a bill is introduced, the parliamentarian refers it to the committee with jurisdiction over its subject, and sometimes to several.

And there, at the very first stop, the textbook and reality part ways.

Each chamber divides its work among standing committees: about twenty in the House, sixteen in the Senate, and scores of subcommittees beneath them. So what happens to a bill once it lands in one of them?

[beat]

Usually, nothing. No hearing. No markup. No vote. Just a quiet death when the session ends.

> In committee, a bill's default fate is nothing.

This is kill point one, and it is the single deadliest point in the whole process. The handful of bills that advance do so because a chair chooses to move them. The chair schedules hearings. The committee amends the text line by line, in a session called a markup. And then a majority votes to report the bill to the floor.

Put that another way. A bill does not have to be defeated to die. It only has to be left alone.

Is there any way around a chair who will not move? In the House, there is one escape hatch. It is called a discharge petition.

If 218 members sign the petition, the bill is pried out of committee and onto the floor, whether the leadership likes it or not. Why 218? Because that is a majority of the full House.

Successful discharges are rare, and the reason matters. Signing is public. It is an open act of defiance against your own party's leaders.

But they happen. In November 2025, a petition forced a floor vote on a bill called the Epstein Files Transparency Act, over the Speaker's opposition. The petition reached its 218th signature on November 12. The House then passed the bill, 427 to 1. The Senate cleared it by unanimous consent, immediately afterward. And the President signed it on November 19.

[beat]

Seven days, from the 218th signature to public law.

That episode shows what a discharge petition is for: revealing a majority that the committee gate had been suppressing.

Let's test that. A bill has 230 declared supporters in the House. But the committee chair refuses to schedule it, and the leadership will not bring it up. Is the bill dead? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Not necessarily. 230 is more than the 218 signatures a discharge petition needs. The real question is whether all 230 will sign in public. A signature does not just oppose the chair and the Speaker in spirit. It defies them.

That gap, between quiet support and public commitment, is why most discharge petitions stall short of 218. Even when everyone says the votes are there.

## Two floors, and a side door

A bill that survives committee still needs time on the floor. And the two chambers ration floor time in opposite ways.

Take the House first. The House runs on majority control of the calendar. For any significant bill, a body called the Rules Committee writes a special rule. That rule sets how long the debate lasts, and which amendments are in order, if any.

The Rules Committee is stacked in the majority's favor. In effect, it is an arm of the Speaker. So a bill that the Rules Committee never schedules just waits. And usually, it dies waiting. That is kill point two.

The House, in short, is a machine that a majority can drive: ruthless, fast, and centralized.

The Senate runs on the opposite premise. Any senator may speak. And most business needs unanimous consent to go forward.

So what happens when some senators refuse to stop debating? That refusal is the filibuster. The vote that ends it, and forces a decision, is called cloture. And for legislation, cloture takes 60 votes. That is kill point three.

The number has a history. The Senate adopted its cloture rule in 1917, and set the bar at two-thirds of the senators voting. In 1975 it lowered the bar to three-fifths of the full Senate. [beat] Sixty votes. For legislation, that is where it stands today.

Nominations are a different story. In two steps, in 2013 and 2017, the Senate cut the threshold for nominations to a simple majority. It did so by majority-vote precedent, a move known as the nuclear option. So far, it has been used only for nominations.

Two more facts make the sixty-vote bar bite. First, the modern filibuster needs no marathon speech. A signaled objection is enough, and the majority schedules around the blocked bill.

Second, the filibuster is no longer rare. In the Congress that began in 1919, two cloture motions were filed. [beat] Two. By mid-2026, the Congress that began in 2025 had already filed 258.

Within living memory, filibustering went from a rarity to the Senate's default operating condition.

The fall of 2025 showed the mechanics in their purest form. A funding bill had the backing of a House majority and a Senate majority. It still sat for six weeks, failing one cloture vote after another, because its supporters could not assemble 60. The shutdown that resulted ran for 43 days, the longest in American history.

Here is a scene to test all that. A bill has 55 solid supporters in the Senate, and all 100 senators are present. The minority will not agree to end debate. The Majority Leader insists: "we have the votes to pass it." Does the bill pass? Think it through.

[long pause]

No. Passing the bill takes 51. But getting to the vote on passage takes 60, and 55 is five short. The bill stalls, indefinitely, unless one of three things happens. Five more senators cross over on cloture. Or the Senate sets a new majority precedent, this time for legislation. Or the bill qualifies for a side door, which we will come to in a moment.

> In the Senate, a majority and sixty votes are different currencies.

Let me say that once more, in other words. Fifty-one votes will pass a bill. But it takes sixty to reach the vote at all.

Now the side door. There is one regular exception to the sixty-vote Senate, and it is called reconciliation.

A reconciliation bill is a budget bill. It must be authorized by the annual budget resolution, and it is limited to fiscal matters. In return, its debate is capped by statute, so it cannot be filibustered. A reconciliation bill passes with a simple majority.

Most of the last decade's biggest domestic laws took this door. One of them was the sprawling package of July 2025, which its sponsors titled the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. It cleared the Senate 51 to 50, on the Vice President's tiebreaker.

But the door has a bouncer, and it is called the Byrd rule. It lets any senator strike a provision that is extraneous. Among other things, that covers a provision with no effect on the budget, and one whose budget effect is "merely incidental" to a policy aim.

Keeping such a provision takes 60 votes to waive the objection, which defeats the point of the side door. So every reconciliation cycle brings news stories about provisions quietly carved out of a bill that no filibuster could touch.

## One text, and the President's desk

Two kill points remain. Kill point four sounds like a formality, and it is not. Nothing goes to the President until the House and the Senate have passed the same text. [beat] Identical. Word for word.

The textbook mechanism is the conference committee. Negotiators from both chambers blend the two versions into one conference report. Then each chamber must approve that report, without amendment.

The modern reality is different. The congressional scholar Barbara Sinclair documented it, across editions of a book called Unorthodox Lawmaking. Formal conferences have grown rare. More often, leaders bounce amendments back and forth between the chambers, which is known as ping-pong. Or they negotiate a single text behind closed doors, before either floor votes.

Either way, the requirement bites.

> A bill that passes both chambers in different forms has not passed Congress.

If the two versions are never reconciled, the bill dies as surely as one that never left committee.

And so to the last gate: the President's desk. Article One, section seven gives the President ten days to act on a bill, not counting Sundays. Four things can happen.

One. The President signs it, and it is law.

Two. The President does nothing while Congress remains in session. The bill becomes law anyway, without a signature.

Three. The President vetoes it, which means returning it to Congress with written objections. Congress can then override the veto. That takes two-thirds of those present and voting, in each chamber.

Four. The President does nothing after Congress has adjourned, so the bill cannot be returned. That is the pocket veto. It kills the bill with no possibility of an override, because there is nothing to override.

> A regular veto can be overridden. A pocket veto cannot.

How often does an override succeed? The historical odds favor the President, overwhelmingly. From 1789 through mid-2026, presidents cast 2,599 vetoes. Congress has overridden 112.

That is about 7 percent of the regular vetoes. And the pocket vetoes, more than a thousand of them, were untouchable by design.

The most recent override was completed on New Year's Day, 2021, when Congress enacted a defense bill over a veto. Of the 25 vetoes cast from 2017 to mid-2026, that is the only one Congress has overridden.

But the veto's deepest effect never shows up in those counts. Congress knows the override math. So it routinely reshapes bills before passage, to keep the veto holstered.

One more scene. Congress passes two bills. Bill A is vetoed, and returned to Congress in February, in the middle of the session. Bill B is presented on the session's final day. Congress adjourns, and the President never signs it. Which bill has a future, and why? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Only Bill A. A returned veto can be overridden, by two-thirds in both chambers. That is steep, but it is possible. Bill B suffered a pocket veto. Adjournment prevented its return, so it never became law, and there is no veto message to override. Its sponsors must start from scratch in the next session.

So even the timing of a bill's arrival on the President's desk is part of legislative strategy.

## The map and the traffic

Before the warning I promised, pull the map back out. At the start I gave you five kill points, in order. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Committee. The House Rules Committee. Cloture in the Senate. Identical text. The President's desk.

Hold on to that order, because the order is the strategy. Each stage presumes that the bill survived every earlier one. So opponents aim for the earliest gate they control.

Now the warning. Everything so far describes the official map.

> The textbook gives the lane markings. The traffic increasingly ignores them.

The clearest case is spending. The law contemplates twelve separate spending bills, called appropriations bills, passed each year by October 1. How often has Congress hit that mark? In only four fiscal years since 1977, when the current budget process took effect. And none more recently than 1997.

Congress governs in two other ways instead. One is the continuing resolution: a stopgap that freezes funding at last year's levels. The other is the omnibus: a giant package that bundles thousands of pages into one vote that must pass.

Leadership-drafted mega-bills, reconciliation packages and continuing resolutions are now how most consequential lawmaking happens. Political scientists call the drift "the decline of regular order." Sinclair's blunter label, unorthodox lawmaking, has become the standard description, not the exception.

One more corrective, from scholarship. It is tempting to blame divided party control for everything that dies in this gauntlet.

The scholar David Mayhew took that on, in a book called Divided We Govern. He tracked landmark laws from 1946 onward. And he found that unified governments and divided governments produced major laws at strikingly similar rates.

Gridlock, in other words, has structural causes: the kill points you have just walked. They operate whichever party holds what. The deadliest stretches of the gauntlet were built into the rules long before today's parties showed up to run it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The odds. Fewer than two bills in a hundred become law, because supporters must win at every stage, and opponents need to win only once.

[beat]

Two. The five kill points, in order: committee, the House Rules Committee, cloture in the Senate, identical text, the President's desk. Committee is the deadliest, because a bill's default fate there is nothing. The escape hatch in the House is a discharge petition, with 218 signatures.

[beat]

Three. In the Senate, a majority and sixty votes are different currencies. Reconciliation is the one side door, open only to budget bills, and the Byrd rule is its bouncer.

[beat]

Four. At the President's desk, a regular veto can be overridden, by two-thirds of both chambers. That has worked about 7 percent of the time. A pocket veto cannot be overridden at all.

[beat]

And five. The textbook gives the lane markings, and the traffic increasingly ignores them. Spending now runs on stopgaps and giant packages. And gridlock has structural causes, whichever party holds what.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Is all this friction a feature, or a defect?

The Framers wanted lawmaking to be slow. Two chambers, and the trip to the President's desk, are brakes put there on purpose. But the filibuster, the Rules Committee and government by continuing resolution are not in the Constitution. They are accumulated institutional choices. And the chambers that made them could unmake them.

Does the sixty-vote Senate protect deliberation, or does it entrench a minority's veto? Is governing by omnibus a pragmatic adaptation, or a way to dodge accountability? Those arguments are still open.

The next lecture takes them up in earnest. It starts from the map you now have, and asks how major laws actually cross it, and who is really driving.

For now, go back to that scrap of paper on the Capitol steps. The song had the steps right. [beat] What it left out is the five places a bill can be killed. And that somebody put each one there on purpose.
