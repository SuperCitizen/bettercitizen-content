It is before dawn on December 20, 2022. Senate appropriators release the text of a giant spending bill. [beat] It runs to 4,155 pages. It spends roughly 1.7 trillion dollars.

Two days later, the Senate passes it. The next day, the House clears it. On December 29, the President signs it.

Inside are all twelve of the annual spending bills. And riding alongside them is something else entirely: the Electoral Count Reform Act. It is the first major overhaul, since 1887, of the law that governs how presidential electoral votes are counted.

Also inside: a sweeping rewrite of retirement-savings law. And a ban on TikTok on federal devices.

[pause]

Now imagine a student with the Schoolhouse Rock diagram, hunting for the moment the Electoral Count Reform Act passed the House as a bill. That student would search the record in vain. [beat] It never did. It rode.

[pause]

Last time, we drew the official map. This lecture is about how consequential lawmaking actually moves across that map. And about who is really driving.

By the end, you will be able to name the vehicles that carry most major legislation today, say what job each one does, and explain why leaders reach for them. We will take it in four steps. First, how the textbook path became the exception, and where the power went. Second, the must-pass vehicle and the riders it carries. Third, government by deadline, and the door that needs only 51 votes. And last, the argument over what it all means: adaptation, or decay.

## The textbook became the exception

The person who documented the shift most carefully was the political scientist Barbara Sinclair. Her book is called Unorthodox Lawmaking. In it she tracked major legislation, Congress by Congress, from the 1960s onward.

You know the textbook sequence. One committee writes the bill. The floor amends it. A conference reconciles the two chambers. When Sinclair started counting, that sequence described most major bills. [beat] It no longer does.

In its place she catalogued a toolkit. Bills referred to several committees at once, or to none. Leadership task forces drafting the text outside committee entirely. Leaders rewriting a bill after the committee has reported it, and before the floor sees it. Special rules that bar amendments. Omnibus packaging. And summit negotiations, between the leaders of Congress and the White House.

By the 2000s, a major bill that followed the once-standard path from end to end had become the exception.

> For major legislation, the textbook path is now the exception.

Sinclair's label stuck because it names the reality without sentimentality. Unorthodox lawmaking, in her telling, is not cheating. It is what the institution evolved under four pressures: polarization, narrow majorities, the sixty-vote threshold in the Senate, and unforgiving fiscal deadlines.

So what is the common thread in that toolkit? [beat] Power moved up.

The committee chairs who dominated lawmaking in the middle of the last century owed their gavels to seniority. They could defy their own parties for decades. Today's chairs owe their gavels to the party. Reforms after the 1970s put the gavels to a caucus vote. And House Republicans have term-limited their chairs since 1995.

So for the bills that matter most, the actual text is negotiated in two offices: the Speaker's, and the Senate Majority Leader's. Rank-and-file members first see the final language when the public does. Sometimes that includes the very committee whose subject it is.

> Power moved up: from committee chairs to party leaders.

The Speaker's instrument is the Rules Committee. It writes the terms of every major debate. And it now routinely reports rules that bar all amendments except the leadership's own.

An informal norm reinforces the centralization. It is called the Hastert rule, after a former Speaker. Under it, House leaders generally refuse to schedule a bill that a majority of their own majority opposes. [beat] Even a bill the full chamber would pass.

Let's test that. A House committee reports a bill favorably, by a lopsided vote: 32 to 8. Who most directly decides whether the full House ever votes on it: the committee chair, the President, or someone else? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Someone else. The Speaker, acting through the Rules Committee and the floor schedule.

Reporting a bill ends the committee's control, but it buys only a place in line. A special rule from the Rules Committee sets whether the House debates the bill, when, and under what terms for amendments. The President has no formal role in House scheduling, and neither does the Senate.

That is why a committee vote of 32 to 8 can still be followed by silence.

## Must-pass vehicles and their riders

That is who drives. Now for what they drive.

Start with the omnibus. An omnibus bill fuses many measures into a single package. Often it holds all or most of the twelve annual spending bills, plus whatever else the leaders fold in.

The practice is newer than it feels. For nearly two centuries, the spending bills moved as standalone laws. Then deadline politics made packaging routine. The Congressional Research Service counted fiscal years 1986 through 2016. In that span, almost 44 percent of the regular spending acts became law inside an omnibus, not on their own.

What makes an omnibus more than a stapling exercise is the rider. A rider is a provision, often unrelated to spending, attached to a vehicle that must pass. In plainer words: the must-pass bill is the train, and the rider is a passenger.

Riders are old. The Hyde Amendment, which restricts federal funding of abortion, has ridden a spending bill since 1976. It has never been a standalone statute.

But the modern omnibus turned the practice industrial. Go back to December 2022. The electoral-count reform, the retirement overhaul, the TikTok ban: all three were riders. Each was serious legislation that lacked a realistic path to floor time alone. So the leadership attached them to the one train guaranteed to leave the station.

Why do riders work? Because of a constitutional fact from last time. A bill goes to the President whole. It is all or nothing. The President cannot sign the spending and veto the rider.

Congress briefly tried to change that. The Line Item Veto Act of 1996 let the President cancel individual spending items. In 1998, in a case called Clinton v. City of New York, the Supreme Court struck it down. Its reasoning: a President who could cut pieces out of a bill would be signing a law that Congress never passed.

> The package is indivisible at the President's desk. Every negotiator knows it.

Now put yourself in the chamber. You are a House member, and a year-end omnibus arrives, 4,000 pages long. You support about 90 percent of it. But buried inside is one rider you strongly oppose. What are your real options? Think it through.

[long pause]

Take it, or leave it. Voting to strip the rider is probably not one of your options. Year-end omnibus bills come to the floor under closed rules, which put no amendments in order. And there is a reason. The package is a balance that was negotiated in advance. Pull out one brick, and other members' bricks come loose too.

So you swallow the rider. Or you vote against the whole bill and accept the consequences, possibly a shutdown.

That take-it-or-leave-it structure is not a bug. It is the leverage that makes packaging work for the leadership.

## Government by deadline

The second vehicle runs on the calendar. Every fiscal year begins on October 1, and the law contemplates twelve spending bills passed on time. Congress has managed that in only four fiscal years since 1977.

The fallback is the continuing resolution. A continuing resolution is a stopgap. It funds the agencies at roughly last year's levels, for a set number of weeks or months.

> Continuing resolutions were designed as a bridge. They became the road.

To feel the rhythm, follow the money through two recent fiscal years.

September 2024. Fiscal 2025 is about to open, and none of the twelve bills has passed. So a continuing resolution carries the government to December 20, at frozen funding levels.

December. A negotiated year-end deal implodes under outside political pressure, days before the deadline. A slimmer stopgap passes just before Christmas, and pushes the cliff to the middle of March.

March 2025. Rather than finish the twelve bills, Congress enacts a continuing resolution for the full year. Fiscal 2025 ends with zero regular spending bills passed.

October 1. Fiscal 2026 opens with no deal. Funding lapses. Cloture vote after cloture vote fails. And the government stays closed for 43 days, the longest shutdown in the country's history.

November 12. The law that ends the shutdown carries three of the twelve bills to full-year passage. For the rest, it is yet another stopgap, with a new deadline in early 2026.

[pause]

Take in the pattern, not just the dates. At no point in those two years did the spending process run through regular order, committee by committee. Every funding decision was made at a cliff's edge, in a package negotiated by the leadership, under threat of a shutdown.

And that points to the deeper cost of government by deadline. It is not only the freezes and the brinkmanship. It is where the leverage goes. When the only bills that must pass are giant and time-locked, the handful of leaders who control their text control nearly everything. And a shutdown threat becomes an all-purpose bargaining chip, for whoever is willing to wield it.

## The 51-vote workhorse, and the menu

The third vehicle you already know. Last time, reconciliation was the exception to the sixty-vote Senate: a budget bill, with its debate capped by statute, that passes the Senate with 51 votes.

Notice what it has become: the default vehicle for a unified government's entire domestic agenda. The tax act of 2017. The American Rescue Plan of 2021. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025. For a decade, each party's signature domestic legislation has taken the 51-vote door.

And the door shapes what passes through it. The Byrd rule, the bouncer from last time, requires provisions to have real budgetary effects, inside the budget window. So drafters convert policy goals into tax-and-spending form. And they attach expiration dates to make the arithmetic work. That is why the individual tax cuts in the 2017 act were written to lapse after 2025.

> Major policy now routinely arrives shaped like budget math.

Why? Because budget math is what fits through the door.

Now set the vehicles side by side, the way a leader would. There are four.

First, the standalone bill, in regular order. One subject, one committee, its own floor debate. It offers a clean mandate and accountability. But it needs scarce floor time, and 60 votes in the Senate.

Second, the omnibus. One must-pass vote carries everything, riders included. Its limit: all or nothing, and little scrutiny of the parts.

Third, the continuing resolution. It buys time, and it avoids or ends a shutdown. Its limits: stale priorities, agencies that cannot plan, and a cliff that returns.

And fourth, reconciliation. It passes the Senate with 51 votes. Its limits: the Byrd rule confines it to budgetary matters, and it is available only when a budget resolution unlocks it.

That list works as a menu, and that is exactly how modern leaders use it. They pick the vehicle whose limits they can live with, given their vote count and their deadline.

> The choice of vehicle often decides whether an idea becomes law.

Let's see whether the menu has stuck. At the very start, I told you about the Electoral Count Reform Act, which never passed the House as a bill. Which of these vehicles carried it? And what do we call a provision that travels that way? Take a moment.

[long pause]

An omnibus carried it: the spending package of December 2022. And a provision attached to a must-pass vehicle like that is a rider.

## Adaptation or decay?

So what should we make of all this? Reasonable people who care about Congress as an institution divide over it. I will give you both cases at full strength, and build them the same way: what each side wants to protect, its strongest argument, and the evidence it points to.

First, the pragmatic adaptationist.

This side wants to protect a Congress that can function under modern conditions.

The adaptationist argues from the constraints. The Constitution demands agreement from two chambers, elected differently, and from a President. The Senate adds a sixty-vote threshold. The parties are polarized, and majorities are narrow. Given all that, on this view, packaging is not a trick. It is coalition-building made workable. An omnibus lets hundreds of agreements that are impossible separately pass as one that is possible. And reconciliation lets the party that won the election actually govern.

On accountability, the adaptationist argues that it holds. Every member still casts a recorded vote on every package. And voters can judge the results by party label, which is easier to follow than 535 individual records.

And the adaptationist points to results. The new process demonstrably produces budgets, tax law and landmark statutes that a regular-order Congress was failing to produce. And this side cites Sinclair herself, who resisted the story of decline: the process changed because the old one stopped working.

> The adaptationist case: packaging is coalition-building made workable.

Now the regular-order restorationist.

This side wants to protect deliberation itself, and with it the institutional muscle of Congress.

The restorationist argues from the text. A bill of 4,155 pages, released before dawn and passed within days, goes unread by the people voting on it, let alone debated. Committees once stress-tested legislation with their expertise. That expertise atrophies when committees become bystanders to leadership deals.

On accountability, the restorationist argues that it blurs. When everything passes in one vote, no one can be held responsible for anything in particular. Members learn to blame the package for whatever their constituents dislike.

And the restorationist points to the cliff. Cliff-edge governance hands an extortionate weapon to whoever is most willing to risk a shutdown. It turns must-pass moments into auctions for riders that could never survive sunlight. On this view, a Congress that can act only by emergency bundle has not adapted. It has ceded the deliberative function the Framers assigned it. And the 43-day shutdown of 2025 is what that looks like in practice.

> The restorationist case: what is being lost is deliberation itself.

[pause]

Adaptation, or decay. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. For major legislation, the textbook path is now the exception. The normal path is a package negotiated by the leadership. Sinclair's name for it is unorthodox lawmaking.

[beat]

Two. Power moved up, from committee chairs to party leaders. The Speaker's instrument is the Rules Committee.

[beat]

Three. Three vehicles do most of the carrying. The omnibus fuses many measures into one must-pass vote. The continuing resolution freezes funding and buys time. Reconciliation passes the Senate with 51 votes, as long as it is budget math.

[beat]

Four. A rider is a provision attached to a vehicle that must pass. Riders work because the package is indivisible at the President's desk.

[beat]

And five. The choice of vehicle often decides whether an idea becomes law. Whether that is adaptation or decay is argued by serious people on both sides.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has settled, and what it has not. The description is settled. For major legislation, the leadership-negotiated package is now the normal path, and the Schoolhouse Rock diagram is the special case.

The verdict is not settled. Both cases are held by serious scholars and serious members of Congress.

And the reform ideas on offer do not end the argument. Budgeting every two years. Continuing resolutions that start automatically, and take shutdowns off the table. Restoring the committees to primacy. Each one fixes one side's complaint, at a price the other side names.

There is a deeper question underneath. Why are the workarounds necessary at all? Why do so many bills with majority support die in the regular process, so that leaders must smuggle their contents aboard must-pass trains?

That is the next lecture: the arithmetic of where bills die.
