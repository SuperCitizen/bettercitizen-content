# Why bills die

On April 17, 2013 — four months after the Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting — the Senate voted on the Manchin–Toomey amendment, a background-check compromise negotiated by two senators with strong gun-rights records, one from each party, with the President's backing and broad public support in polling. Fifty-four senators voted aye, forty-six no. It failed. No one stood and talked through the night; under the agreement governing the vote, sixty was the price of proceeding, and fifty-four is not sixty. The amendment now sits in the same graveyard as thousands of measures that never even got that far — bills that died without a hearing, without a floor slot, without a fingerprint on them. Lesson 1 mapped the kill points and Lesson 2 toured the workarounds; this lesson is the autopsy manual. Who guards each gate, what the guards want, and — the part political science can actually predict — the arithmetic that tells you in advance where a given bill will die.

## A bill is a series of doors

Legal scholars call the chokepoints **vetogates**: points in the process where some actor can block a bill outright, coined in the positive-political-theory literature and elaborated by William Eskridge. The Constitution built the first three — a [[term:bicameral]] legislature plus presentment to the President means the House, the Senate, and the White House can each say no. Everything else is the chambers' own construction: subcommittees, committee chairs, the Speaker's scheduling monopoly, the Rules Committee, the Senate's unanimous-consent practice and its 60-vote cloture threshold, the identical-text requirement, and behind it all the veto and the two-thirds override. Each gate has a keeper, and each keeper has interests of their own:

| Gate | Who holds the key | What gets a bill through anyway |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Committee | The chair (and a committee majority) | Discharge petition (218 signatures); leadership bypass |
| House floor agenda | The Speaker, via the Rules Committee | Discharge petition; suspension of the rules (two-thirds vote, no amendments) |
| Senate floor | Any 41 senators, via the [[term:filibuster]] | 60 votes for [[term:cloture]]; [[term:reconciliation]]; unanimous consent |
| The second chamber | All of the above, again | — |
| The President | The [[term:veto]] (or pocket veto) | Two-thirds of those present and voting, in both chambers |

Two things to read off the table. First, the asymmetry you met in Lesson 1, now in its sharpest form: a bill's supporters must hold a winning coalition at *every* gate, while opponents need to capture just *one* keeper — which is why lobbying to kill is so much cheaper than lobbying to pass, and why the status quo wins ties. Second, notice "suspension of the rules": the House can pass an uncontroversial bill by a two-thirds vote with no amendments and forty minutes of debate, and most of the bills that actually become law — the post-office namings, the technical fixes — take this low door. The gauntlet is reserved for bills somebody cares enough to fight about.

## The graveyard with no gravestones

Roughly nine in ten bills introduced in a modern Congress die in committee, most without a hearing or a vote. That is not laziness; it is the system's deliberate first filter, and the political scientists Gary Cox and Mathew McCubbins gave the mechanism a name in *Setting the Agenda* (2005): **negative agenda control**. Their argument: the House majority party operates as a cartel whose first commandment is never to let the floor vote on anything that would pass *against* the wishes of most of the majority party. Positive power — passing what you want — is hard and depends on votes you may not have. Negative power — ensuring the vote never happens — is nearly absolute, because the party controls the chairs and the calendar. The empirical signature is striking: the House majority party is almost never "rolled" (defeated on a bill it opposed), not because it wins every fight but because fights it would lose are never scheduled.

Inaction has a second virtue defeat lacks: it leaves no record. A member forced to vote no on a popular bill makes an enemy of its supporters; a bill that quietly expires in committee makes an enemy of no one in particular. This is why the discharge petition — the crowbar from Lesson 1 — was redesigned in 1993 so that signatures are public as they accumulate: secrecy had let members tell constituents they supported a bill while declining to sign the only instrument that could free it. Publicity cuts both ways, though. A public signature is open defiance of the Speaker, which is precisely why petitions usually stall short of 218 even for bills that would pass the floor overwhelmingly — the Epstein-files episode of 2025, which did reach 218 and then passed 427–1, is famous because it is rare.

<Checkpoint question="A bill would pass the House floor with 200 minority-party votes plus 40 majority-party defectors. Leadership refuses to schedule it. Under the Cox–McCubbins logic, why — even though the bill has 240 supporters?">
  Because passing it would *roll* the majority party: a law would be made against the wishes of most of the members who control the chamber, spending the majority's agenda power against itself and blurring its brand. Negative agenda control means the test for floor time isn't "would it pass?" but "would most of *us* want it to pass?" A 240-vote coalition built mostly from the other party fails that test — which is why such bills move only by discharge petition, where the 40 defectors must put their names on the defiance one by one.
</Checkpoint>

## 51, 60, 67: the Senate's real arithmetic

Now to the gate where Manchin–Toomey died. The Senate has not one threshold but three, and confusing them is the most common error in reading congressional news. Fifty-one votes (or fifty plus the Vice President) pass a bill — once it gets a final vote. Sixty votes invoke cloture and *get* it that final vote. Sixty-seven — two-thirds, if all hundred are voting — override a veto. The figure below puts the three on one line:

The gold zone is where modern legislation goes to die. A proposal with 54 or 57 supporters reads as a winner in every poll and every press release, yet for ordinary legislation it has no more path to enactment than a proposal with 20 — unless it can be recast as budget math and ride reconciliation, which is precisely the Lesson 2 story run in reverse. And the zone's history cuts the other way too: when the Senate invoked cloture on the Civil Rights Act on June 10, 1964 — 71 votes to 29, the first cloture ever achieved on a civil rights bill — the supermajority requirement meant the law arrived with a coalition broad enough that no later Congress seriously tried to repeal it. Both facts are the same mechanism.

## The pivotal senator

The political scientist Keith Krehbiel turned this arithmetic into a full theory in *Pivotal Politics: A Theory of U.S. Lawmaking* (1998). Line the hundred senators up by policy preference and the institution's behavior is driven not by the median senator — the 51st, who would rule in a pure majority body — but by the **pivots**: the 60th senator, whose vote ends a filibuster, and the 67th, whose vote overrides a veto. Between the pivots lies what Krehbiel called the **gridlock interval**: the range of existing policies that *cannot be changed*, because any coalition large enough to want change is too small to clear the supermajority gates protecting the status quo. Most policy, most of the time, sits inside the interval. That single move explains three things the naive diagram cannot:

First, why unified government disappoints. Winning the House, the Senate, and the White House moves the median, but if the 60th senator's preferences barely move, the gridlock interval barely shrinks — which is Krehbiel's formal vindication of Mayhew's *Divided We Govern* finding from Lesson 1 that divided control changes lawmaking output far less than everyone assumes. Second, why the laws that do pass tend to pass big: a bill that can clear the 60th senator has, by construction, assembled an oversized, usually bipartisan coalition, so lopsided final votes are not evidence the bill was easy — they are the entry fee. Third, why lawmaking comes in bursts: only an electoral wave large enough to drag the *pivots* — not just the median — across the policy spectrum (1933, 1965, briefly 2009) opens the interval, and Congress legislates furiously until equilibrium closes it again.

> **Think it through:** If the legislative filibuster vanished tomorrow, the binding pivot for most bills would jump from the 60th senator to the 51st — or, with an opposition President, the 67th. Would Congress pass *more* laws, or mostly *different* laws, with each new majority repealing the last one's work? What does your answer assume about how durable a 51-vote law can be?

## Reading a death certificate

You can now diagnose the cases that began this pack. The FY2021 defense bill became law over a veto because its coalition — 322 in the House, 81 in the Senate — sat above the override pivot; the veto gate was real but unguardable. Manchin–Toomey, at 54, died between passage and cloture. The Epstein Files Transparency Act was never short of votes anywhere; it was short of a *scheduler*, and died (until the discharge petition) at a gate held by one actor. And the 2025 reconciliation act passed at 51–50 — below the cloture pivot — only because it took the one door the filibuster does not guard.

When a bill you care about stalls, run the checklist in gate order before accepting anyone's spin: Has the committee scheduled anything (if not, the chair or leadership is the gate)? Has the Rules Committee reported a rule? Has a cloture motion been filed, and what did it get (congress.gov posts every count)? The answer usually identifies the keeper — and tells you whether the bill needs five more senators, 218 signatures, a budget-resolution vehicle, or a different Speaker.

## What this lesson did not settle

Whether the gauntlet is wisdom or pathology. The steel-manned case for the gates is Madisonian: supermajority hurdles force the broad, durable coalitions that made the 1964 Act permanent, and a status quo that is hard to change is also hard to *wreck*. The steel-manned case against is that the gridlock interval has no opinion about whether the status quo is any good: problems compound while the math holds, minorities rule by veto, and the workarounds of Lesson 2 — governing by cliff and by budget math — are themselves the pathology's symptoms. That argument is live, loudest in the perennial fight over the [[issue:filibuster]], and this pack has given you both sides' best evidence. What is settled is the method: bills do not die of bad luck. They die at gates, the gates have keepers, and the arithmetic — 218, 51, 60, 67 — tells you which keeper to watch.
