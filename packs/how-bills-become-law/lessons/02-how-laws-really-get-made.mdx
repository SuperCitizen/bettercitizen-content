# How laws really get made

Before dawn on December 20, 2022, Senate appropriators released the text of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023: 4,155 pages, roughly $1.7 trillion. Two days later the Senate passed it 68–29; the House cleared it the next day, 225–201; the President signed it on December 29. Inside, alongside all twelve annual spending bills, rode the Electoral Count Reform Act — the first major overhaul of the statute governing presidential electoral counts since 1887 — plus a sweeping rewrite of retirement-savings law (SECURE 2.0) and a ban on TikTok on federal devices. A student tracing the Schoolhouse Rock diagram would search the record in vain for the moment the Electoral Count Reform Act passed the House as a bill. It never did. It rode. Lesson 1 gave you the official map; this lesson is about how consequential lawmaking actually moves across it — and who is really driving.

## The textbook became the exception

The political scientist who documented the shift most carefully was Barbara Sinclair, whose *Unorthodox Lawmaking* (first edition 1997, fifth edition 2016) tracked major legislation Congress by Congress from the 1960s onward. The textbook sequence — one committee writes the bill, the floor amends it, a conference reconciles the chambers — described most major bills when she started counting. It no longer does. In its place she catalogued a toolkit: bills referred to several committees at once or to none, leadership task forces drafting text outside committee entirely, "post-committee adjustments" in which leaders rewrite a reported bill before the floor sees it, special rules that bar amendments, omnibus packaging, and summit negotiations between congressional leaders and the White House. By the 2000s, a major bill that followed the once-standard path end to end had become the exception. Her label stuck because it names the reality without sentimentality: *unorthodox lawmaking* is not cheating; it is what the institution evolved under polarization, narrow majorities, the 60-vote [[term:filibuster]] threshold, and unforgiving fiscal deadlines.

The common thread in the toolkit is that power moved up. The committee chairs who dominated mid-century lawmaking owed their gavels to seniority and could defy their parties for decades; today's chairs owe them to the party — the post-1970s reforms put gavels to a caucus vote, and House Republicans have term-limited their chairs since 1995. For the bills that matter most, the Speaker's office and the Senate Majority Leader's office negotiate the actual text, and rank-and-file members — sometimes the relevant committee itself — first read final language when the public does. The Speaker's instrument is the Rules Committee, which writes the terms of every major debate and now routinely reports rules barring all amendments but leadership's own. An informal norm reinforces the centralization: under the "Hastert rule," named for a former Speaker, House leaders generally refuse to schedule bills opposed by a majority of their own majority, even bills the full chamber would pass.

## Must-pass vehicles and the riders they carry

An **[[term:omnibus-bill]]** fuses many measures — often all or most of the twelve annual [[term:appropriations]] bills, plus whatever else leaders fold in — into a single package. The practice is newer than it feels: for nearly two centuries the spending bills moved as standalone laws (Congress tried a single consolidated appropriations bill once, for fiscal 1951, and abandoned the experiment). Then deadline politics made packaging routine. The Congressional Research Service counts 22 omnibus measures enacted across fiscal years 1986–2016; in that span, almost 44 percent of regular appropriations acts became law inside an omnibus rather than on their own.

What makes an omnibus more than a stapling exercise is the **rider**: a provision, often unrelated to spending, attached to a vehicle that must pass. Riders are old — the Hyde Amendment, restricting federal funding of abortion, has ridden the Labor-HHS appropriations bill since 1976 and has never been a standalone statute — but the modern omnibus turned the practice industrial. The December 2022 package's electoral-count reform, retirement overhaul, and TikTok ban were all riders in this sense: serious legislation that lacked a realistic standalone path to floor time, attached by leadership to the one train guaranteed to leave the station.

Riders work because of a constitutional fact from Lesson 1: presentment is all-or-nothing. The President cannot sign the spending and veto the rider. Congress briefly tried to change that — the Line Item Veto Act of 1996 let the President cancel individual spending items — but the Supreme Court struck it down in *Clinton v. City of New York* (1998): a President who could excise pieces of a bill would be signing a law Congress never passed. The package is indivisible at the desk, and every negotiator knows it. The same logic explains the return of the **[[term:earmark]]** — targeted spending items for individual members' districts, banned in 2011 and revived in 2021 under the tidier name "community project funding." Leaders assemble majorities for giant vehicles partly out of small, named things members can take home; [[term:pork-barrel]] is the pejorative, but vote-buying glue is the function.

<Checkpoint question="You are a House member who supports about 90 percent of a 4,000-page year-end omnibus but strongly opposes one rider buried in it. What are your real options — and why is 'vote to strip the rider' probably not one of them?">
  Your options are take it or leave it. Year-end omnibuses come to the floor under closed rules that put no amendments in order, because the package is a pre-negotiated equilibrium — pull one brick and other members' bricks come loose too. So you either swallow the rider or vote against the whole bill and accept the consequences (possibly a shutdown). That take-it-or-leave-it structure is not a bug; it is the leverage that makes packaging work for leadership.

## Government by deadline

The purest expression of unorthodox lawmaking is what happens every October 1, when the fiscal year begins. The law contemplates twelve appropriations bills passed on time; Congress has managed that in only four fiscal years since the current budget process took effect in 1977, most recently for fiscal 1997. The fallback is the **[[term:continuing-resolution]]** — a stopgap that funds agencies at (roughly) last year's levels for a set number of weeks or months, with limited "anomalies" patched in. Read the pattern, not just the dates: at no point in those two years did the spending process run through committee-by-committee regular order. Every funding decision was made at a cliff's edge, in a leadership-negotiated package, under threat of shutdown. That is the deeper cost of government by deadline — not only the freezes and the brinkmanship, but *where it puts the leverage*. When the only bills that must pass are giant and time-locked, the people who control their text — a handful of leaders — control nearly everything, and a shutdown threat becomes an all-purpose bargaining chip for whoever is willing to wield it.

## The 51-vote workhorse

Lesson 1 introduced **[[term:reconciliation]]** as the exception to the 60-vote Senate; here, note what it has become: the default vehicle for a unified government's entire domestic agenda. The 2017 tax act, the 2021 American Rescue Plan, the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act, and the July 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act — each party's signature domestic legislation for a decade — all took the 51-vote door. The Byrd rule's bouncer duty shapes the laws themselves: because provisions must have real budgetary effects inside the budget window, drafters convert policy goals into tax-and-spending form and attach expiration dates to make the arithmetic work, which is why the 2017 act's individual tax cuts were written to lapse after 2025 and why each reconciliation cycle ends with the parliamentarian carving provisions out. Major policy now routinely arrives shaped like budget math because budget math is what fits through the door.

The vehicles, side by side:

| Vehicle | What it is | Why leaders reach for it | Built-in limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standalone bill (regular order) | One subject, one committee, its own floor debate | Clean mandate and accountability | Needs scarce floor time — and 60 in the Senate |
| Omnibus | Many measures fused into one package | One must-pass vote carries everything, riders included | All-or-nothing; little scrutiny of the parts |
| Continuing resolution | Freezes existing funding for a set period | Buys time; avoids or ends a shutdown | Stale priorities; agencies can't plan; the cliff returns |
| Reconciliation | Budget bill with debate capped by statute | Passes the Senate with 51 votes | Byrd rule confines it to budgetary matters; available only when a budget resolution unlocks it |

The table reads as a menu, and that is exactly how modern leadership uses it: pick the vehicle whose limits you can live with, given your vote count and your deadline. The choice of vehicle, more than the content of the idea, often decides whether an idea becomes law.

## Adaptation or decay?

Reasonable institutionalists divide over what to make of all this. Take both cases at full strength:

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Pragmatic adaptationist">
    Unorthodox lawmaking is what a Madisonian system *has* to do to function under modern conditions. The Constitution demands agreement from two differently elected chambers and a President; the Senate adds a 60-vote threshold; the parties are polarized and majorities are narrow. Given those constraints, packaging is not a trick — it is coalition-building made tractable: omnibus vehicles let hundreds of separately impossible agreements pass as one possible one, and reconciliation lets the party that won the election actually govern. Every member still casts a recorded vote on every package; voters can still judge the results by party label, which is more legible accountability than judging 535 individual records. Sinclair herself resisted the decline narrative — the process changed because the old one stopped working, and the new one demonstrably produces budgets, tax law, and landmark statutes a "regular order" Congress was failing to produce.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Regular-order restorationist">
    What is being lost is deliberation itself — and with it, Congress's institutional muscle. A 4,155-page text released before dawn and passed within days has not been read by the people voting on it, let alone debated; the committee expertise that once stress-tested legislation atrophies when committees become bystanders to leadership deals. Accountability blurs, not sharpens: when everything passes in one vote, no one can be held responsible for anything in particular, and members learn to blame "the package" for whatever constituents dislike. Cliff-edge governance hands an extortionate weapon to whoever is most willing to risk a shutdown, and turns must-pass moments into auctions for riders that could never survive sunlight. A Congress that can act only by emergency bundle has not adapted — it has ceded the deliberative function the Framers assigned it, and the 43-day shutdown of 2025 is what that cession looks like in practice.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

## What this lesson did not settle

Whether unorthodox lawmaking is the rational adaptation or the institutional decay — both tabs above are held by serious scholars and serious members, and reform ideas (biennial budgeting, automatic continuing resolutions that take shutdowns off the table, restoring committee primacy) each fix one side's complaint at a price the other side names. What the lesson did settle is the descriptive question: for major legislation, the leadership-negotiated package — omnibus, CR, or reconciliation — is now the normal path, and the Schoolhouse Rock diagram is the special case. The deeper question is *why* the workarounds are necessary at all: why so many bills with majority support die in the regular process that leaders must smuggle their contents aboard must-pass trains. That is the next lesson — the arithmetic of where bills die.
