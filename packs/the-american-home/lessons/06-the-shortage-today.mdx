# Is there really a housing shortage?

In December 2025 two analysts at the [[org:congressional-research-service]], Katie Jones and Lida Weinstock, sent Congress a short memo with scare quotes in its title: *Estimates of a "Housing Shortage."* Its table listed five respected estimates of how many homes the country lacks, from 3.7 million to 5.5 million, and then added a caution: "there is not universal agreement that the United States has a housing shortage at all." Four months later the White House Council of Economic Advisers put the figure at 10 million or more single-family homes; the previous July, Moody's Analytics had put it at about 2 million. Same country, same homes, a fivefold spread.

## Why do careful counts of the shortage differ by millions?

Start on neutral ground. Picture a town with 10,000 homes and 9,000 households. Is it short? That depends on three answers. How many households live there now? How many more would form if housing cost less, such as grown children who would rather not live with their parents? Say 400. And what share of homes should stand empty, so that people can move without bidding wars? At 12 percent, the town needs about 10,700 homes and is short about 700; at 8 percent, it needs about 10,200 and is short about 200. Nothing was built or torn down. Only an assumption changed.

National estimates make the same choices. Freddie Mac's 3.7 million, as of the third quarter of 2024, adds about 1.0 million households that would have formed at 2000's rents to 2.7 million empty homes needed for an 11.7 percent vacancy rate. Moody's Analytics counts only homes actually on the market and finds about 2 million. Others ask different questions. The National Association of Realtors measured how far building from 2001 to 2020 fell below the pace of 1968 to 2000, and the White House did the same for single-family homes since 2008. Zillow counts 8.2 million families doubled up with people outside the family and subtracts the 3.4 million homes for sale or rent. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies asks only how many more vacant homes would restore the vacancy rates of the 1990s: 600,000 in early 2026, down from 1.5 million in 2022, after the most apartment completions since 1986.

The vacancy choice alone is huge. Elena Patel, a co-author of a Brookings estimate, shows in a November 2026 conference paper that gross vacancy averaged 10.7 percent from 1980 to 2000 and 13.2 percent from 2010 to 2019; on about 148 million homes, choosing one period over the other moves the target by about 3.7 million, more than the whole gap between Moody's and the Realtors. And "missing" households depend on prices, so the shortage helps set the very count used to measure it.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why can careful estimates of the housing shortage differ by millions of homes?">
  Because a shortage is estimated, not counted. Each estimate chooses how many households would form if housing cost less and how many empty homes a healthy market needs, judged from which years, or asks a different question, such as how far building fell behind an old pace. Change one assumption and the answer moves by millions.
</Checkpoint>

The same logic predicts how a change in method moves the answer.

## Why does a home cost so many years of income?

This series prices homes the same way in every lesson: the Census value of the median owner-occupied home, divided by median family income. It was 2.4 years in 1950, 1.8 in 1970, 2.4 again in 1980 and 2000, and about 3.5 in 2025. (The 2025 figure covers all owned homes and the earlier ones single-family homes on less than ten acres, so the comparison is approximate.) Harvard's Joint Center divides what existing single-family homes actually sold for by median household income, which runs lower than family income because it counts people living alone. By that yardstick the median home cost a record 5.0 times income in 2022 and 4.7 in 2025, against 4.1 in 2019 and an average of 3.2 in the 1990s. One ratio prices the homes people own; the other prices the homes people must buy.

Interest rates did the rest: the monthly payment on the median-priced home was $1,240 at the end of 2020, with mortgage rates near record lows, and $2,420 in late 2025, at 6.23 percent. With taxes and insurance added, the income needed to afford that home rose from $68,700 to $120,800 in five years, a bar that only 32 percent of households clear. And because half of all mortgages carry rates below 4 percent, owners stay put: economists at the Federal Housing Finance Agency found that each percentage point between a borrower's rate and the market rate cut the chance of a sale by 18.1 percent.

Builders have not rushed in, because every input got dearer. The Bureau of Labor Statistics index of what goes into a new home, leaving out labor, rose 40 percent between January 2020 and early 2026, and lot values rose 52 percent from 2020 to 2024. In a study published in June 2026 and built on surveys of its members, the National Association of Home Builders put regulation at 26.4 percent of a new home's price, up from 23.8 percent in 2021. That is the builders' own count, and it includes rules most people want, such as fire codes. But minimum lot sizes, bans on apartments and slow approvals are local choices. [[term:federalism]] leaves land-use power with the states, which hand most of it to city councils and county boards.

The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko trace the result. The housing stock grew 4 percent a year in the 1950s and 0.64 percent a year in the 2010s. In the suburbs of Atlanta, Dallas, Miami and Phoenix, once the nation's building champions, new supply answered rising prices less than a quarter as strongly in the 2010s as in the 1970s. This series began on a frontier of open land, and their 2025 paper ends on another: "America's suburban frontier appears to be closing."

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: why does a home cost more years of income now than in 1970?">
  Prices outran incomes: by the Census measure, from 1.8 years of family income in 1970 to about 3.5 in 2025, and by Harvard's sale-price measure to 4.7. Higher rates roughly doubled the monthly payment after 2020, and builders could not answer with enough homes, because land, materials and local rules made building dearer and slower.
</Checkpoint>

Reporters often quote both ratios side by side.

## How do today's households compare with every earlier era?

A shortage shows up less as people on the street than as households that never form. The country added 2.0 million households a year in 2020 and 2021, when borrowing was cheapest, and only 1.1 million in 2025. The share of adults 18 to 34 heading their own household rose from 30.3 percent in 2019 to 33.0 percent in 2022, and young adults have formed households more slowly since 2024. In the Realtors' 2026 survey the median first-time buyer was 40, a record.

Marriage and children come later and less often. In 1950 the median man first married at 22.8 and the median woman at 20.3; in 2025, at 30.8 and 28.4. In 2024 the total fertility rate, the births a woman would have at that year's rates, fell to 1.60, the lowest ever recorded; 2025 has no final figure yet. Childcare stacks on top of the rent or the mortgage. The [[org:us-department-of-labor]] found that in 2022 full-day care for one child cost $6,552 to $15,600 a year, depending on the county and the kind of care: 8.9 to 16 percent of median family income.

Do housing costs cause the later marriages and the fewer babies? That is contested. The economists Lisa Dettling and Melissa Kearney (2014) found that when local house prices rise, births fall among renters and rise among owners, whose home equity grows. But Kearney, Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue (2022) found that the states where rents and childcare spending rose most after 2007 saw no bigger drop in births; they conjecture that the "shifting priorities" of younger generations matter more.

Two measures moved mostly one way: households shrank, from 5.04 people in 1880 to 2.50, and married women's paid work rose, from 5.6 percent in 1900 to 58.2 percent. The other three bent. Marriage age fell to its low in the 1950s and has since climbed past every earlier figure. Births rose in the baby boom, then fell to the lowest on record. And a home's price held between about 1.8 and 2.4 years of family income from 1950 to 2000 before climbing to about 3.5.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: how does the typical household of 2025 differ from the typical household of 1950?">
  It is smaller (2.50 people against 3.37), it forms later (first marriage about eight years later), it has fewer children (1.60 births per woman in 2024 against 3.09), and the wife is far more likely to work for pay. Its home costs about a year more of family income.
</Checkpoint>

## Why are homes so expensive? Three serious answers

Some facts are not in dispute. Prices rose more than 54 percent from January 2020 to early 2026, almost 25 percent after inflation. Monthly payments roughly doubled. Building has answered prices less since 2008. And rentals under $600 a month, after inflation, fell from 8.3 million in 2014 to 5.8 million in 2024. What is contested is which cause matters most, and so which lever would help most; the [[issue:nimby-and-yimby]] fight is one front of that argument. Here is each answer as its strongest advocates put it.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Land-use rules hold back supply">
    Economists such as Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko argue that prices rose most where building fell most. They point out that if the housing stock had grown as fast from 2000 to 2020 as from 1980 to 2000, the country would have 15 million more homes. Both parties' White Houses have agreed: the Obama administration blamed "zoning, other land use regulations, and lengthy development approval processes" (2016), and the Trump administration blamed "fees, mandates, regulations, and red tape" (2026). This view protects newcomers, renters and the young, who are priced out of the places with jobs. It would change its mind if places that legalize more building got the homes but no slower rise in rents and prices than their neighbors.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Interest rates, investors and finance">
    Economists who study credit and demand argue that money, not rules, set the timing. They point out that rules barely changed in 2020, yet prices jumped: John Mondragon and Johannes Wieland find that the move to remote work explains over half of the 18.9 percent rise in real house prices from 2019 to 2023, and record-low rates followed by lock-in cut the supply of homes for sale. Geographers such as Manuel Aalbers argue that housing has become a financial asset as much as a home. Large investors, nearly absent before the 2008 crash, bought heavily after it: by June 2022, 32 of them owned nearly 450,000 homes, about 3 percent of single-family rentals nationally and about 25 percent in metro Atlanta, and the Trump administration's 2026 report also blamed institutional investors. This view protects ordinary buyers bidding against cheap money and corporate landlords. It concedes that investors' national share is small and their buying has slowed, and it would change its mind if prices stayed near record multiples of income after rates fell back.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Incomes and inequality">
    The housing scholars Kirk McClure and Alex Schwartz argue that the country has enough homes but not enough income at the bottom. They point out that from 2000 to 2020 housing production exceeded the growth of households by 3.3 million units, and that only four of 381 metropolitan areas showed a shortage; the problem, they write, is "the mismatch between the distribution of incomes and the distribution of housing prices." The Joint Center's numbers fit: a newly built apartment asked a median $1,900 a month in 2025, while the median renter could afford about $1,400. This view protects the poorest renters, whom market-rate building may never reach. It would change its mind if new building were shown to lower rents for the poorest too; critics answer that counting today's households understates need, because high prices keep households from forming.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

All three accept the same price and payment figures. They split over cause, and each cause points to a different lever. Some of today's conditions cannot be repeated or undone by policy: the collapse of mortgage rates in 2020 and 2021 and the lock-in it left, the sudden move to remote work, and the using up of cheap land near job centers. Others are levers that still exist. Zoning, permitting and building codes can be rewritten. [[term:monetary-policy]] and the rules of mortgage finance still shape what borrowing costs. [[term:subsidy]] programs for low-rent homes, rental aid and help with childcare are choices governments make every year.

<Checkpoint question="In your own words: what do the three explanations agree on, and where do they split?">
  They agree on the facts: prices and payments are near records relative to incomes, building slowed, and the cheapest rentals are disappearing. They split over the main cause (local rules, the cost and flow of money, or low incomes at the bottom) and so over which lever to pull first.
</Checkpoint>

## What the evidence settles, and what it leaves open

Some things are settled. By either ratio, a home costs more years of income than it did in the second half of the twentieth century, and payments roughly doubled after 2020. Building answers prices less than it once did. Americans marry later and have fewer children than in any earlier era of this series. The costs fall unevenly: owners who bought before 2022 hold cheap loans and more equity, while renters and the young pay today's prices.

What remains open is how large the shortage is, which cause dominates, and how much housing costs, rather than changing priorities, explain later marriages and fewer births.
