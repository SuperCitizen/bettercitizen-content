# Was the frontier's free land really free?

On January 1, 1863, the first day the Homestead Act was in force, a Union soldier named Daniel Freeman filed a claim at the land office in Brownville, Nebraska, for 160 acres along Cub Creek, about five miles west of Beatrice. In 1865 Agnes Suiter joined him on the claim; they had eight children, and he received title to the land in 1869. Near Beatrice, on a reservation along the Big Blue River, lived the Otoe and Missouria, who in 1854 had signed a treaty giving up "all their country west of the Missouri River" except that strip. In 1880 and 1881 Congress sold the strip too and moved them to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma.

Both halves of that story belong to this series, which asks what made a home and a family affordable in each era, what made the postwar "American dream" possible, and which parts of it policy could realistically recreate today. Every lesson tracks five measures: what a home cost in years of income, how many people lived in a household, who worked outside the home, age at first marriage, and the birth rate. Every lesson also sorts what it finds into one-time conditions and policy levers that still exist. Freeman's claim belongs in both piles.

## What did a homestead actually cost?

No reliable figure exists for a home's price in years of income before 1950: the Census Bureau's national home-value series begins in 1940, and its national median family income in 1947. What can be shown is what land cost the people who took it, in money, time and work.

Before 1862 the federal government mostly sold its land: after 1820, at a minimum of $1.25 an acre, in cash, for at least 80 acres. The Homestead Act, signed on May 20, 1862, offered a quarter section, 160 acres, to anyone who headed a family or was at least 21, was a citizen or had declared the intention to become one, and had never borne arms against the United States. The money was small: by the National Archives' account, $10 to file and $6 for the patent, the deed. The time was not: five years living on the claim, building a dwelling and cultivating the land. Or, after six months, the settler could buy the claim at $1.25 an acre, $200 in all.

The largest cost was making a farm. By one textbook's estimate, although the land was nearly free, "it cost close to $1000 for the necessary supplies to start up a farm," meaning horses, livestock, wagons, wells, fencing and seed. In 1941 the economist Clarence Danhof argued that cheap western land was not cheap once those costs were counted. Railroads held alternate sections along their routes, so homesteaders were often forced to claim land 30 to 60 miles from transportation, while railroad land beside the tracks averaged $4.76 an acre in 1880.

Think of a homestead as a scholarship that pays tuition but not room, board or books, and must be kept for five years. In modern terms it was a [[term:subsidy]]: the government gave away an asset it could have sold. The analogy breaks in one place. A scholarship is paid from money someone gave; this land had belonged to someone who did not give it.

## Why were frontier families so large?

In 1880, 44 percent of Americans lived on farms, and on a farm the household was the firm: its members were the workforce, and much of what they made was eaten or worn at home. In 1751 Benjamin Franklin argued that land in America was "so cheap as that a labouring Man, that understands Husbandry, can in a short Time save Money enough to purchase a Piece of new Land sufficient for a Plantation." His conclusion: "Hence Marriages in America are more general, and more generally early, than in Europe."

By the demographer Michael Haines's estimates, around 1800 a white woman bore about seven children, and at mid-century enslaved women, a largely rural population, bore between seven and eight. Many children died: around 1850 an estimated 217 of every 1,000 white infants died before their first birthday. In 1790 the average household held 5.79 people, a figure that divides only the free population by the number of households.

Then families began to shrink, in rural areas before about 1870, too early for the usual explanations of cities and schooling. By 1890 births per white woman had fallen to an estimated 3.87. Haines notes that the rising scarcity and cost of good farmland "has been proposed as a prime factor, although this is controversial," and that recent work credits women themselves with much of the decline.

## Who paid for the free land, and who was shut out?

"Free land" was free only to the settler. The land of North America, the economists Ann Carlos, Donna Feir and Angela Redish write, "was already made productive and was the recognized territory of sovereign Indigenous Nations." [[term:sovereignty]], a people's authority over its own territory, had to be extinguished before land could enter the public domain, and the United States extinguished it in three ways.

The first was the [[term:treaty]]. The Otoe and Missouria gave up their country in 1854 for annual payments that began at $20,000 and shrank over forty years, about $405,000 in all. The second looked like purchase: in buying Louisiana from France in 1803, the same economists argue, the United States acquired "not land but rather an exclusive right to treat with" the nations whose land it was. The third was force: the removals of the 1830s, remembered for the [[term:trail-of-tears]], and wars on the Plains.

In 1862 the Pacific Railroad Act, whose route crossed land not yet ceded, promised that "the United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian Titles" to it. That same year the Morrill Act funded colleges with land that the historian Robert Lee and the journalist Tristan Ahtone traced to about 10.7 million acres taken from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities. In 1887 the Dawes Act turned the homestead's template on the reservations, dividing them into allotments and selling what was left to settlers in tracts of up to 160 acres. Reservation lands fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million in 1934.

The rules also shut people out. Immigrants qualified only by declaring the intention to become citizens, which the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese immigrants from doing. Married women generally could not file unless they headed the household, and before 1900 women were fewer than 10 percent of homesteaders.

For people freed from slavery, land was promised and taken back. General William T. Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15, in January 1865, set aside coastal land so that "each family shall have a plot of not more than (40) forty acres of tillable ground." Roughly 40,000 freedpeople settled on about 400,000 acres before President Andrew Johnson overturned the order that fall and returned most of the land to planters. The Southern Homestead Act of 1866 reserved public land in five Southern states for homesteaders, but much of it was swamp or heavy forest, land offices were few, and labor contracts held freedpeople in place. By the historian Keri Leigh Merritt's count, only 4,000 to 5,500 Black claimants ever received final patents under it. On the Plains, Black homesteaders gained title to 650,000 acres between 1877 and 1920, founding towns such as Nicodemus, Kansas, after [[term:reconstruction]] ended.

## Was homesteading a leveler or a captured subsidy?

Some of the story is counting. About 4 million claims were filed, and about 1.6 million homesteaders obtained title between 1863 and 1961; the act distributed some 270 million acres in all, about 10 percent of the country's land. Most of that came after this lesson's era: more claims were patented after 1900 than before, and claims peaked in 1913. Railroads received grants of some 181 million acres between 1850 and 1871. And by the National Archives' tally, of some 500 million acres the General Land Office gave out from 1862 to 1904, only 80 million went to homesteaders.

A bulletin of the 1890 census announced that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner built a theory on it: "The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development." Even the homesteaders' success rate is disputed: one count divided by the other gives 40 percent, while the Park Service now says more than half proved up.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Free land as opportunity">
    This view argues that free land made owners of people who could never have bought land, and that ownership made citizens. In Turner's words, "So long as free land exists, the opportunity for a competency exists, and economic power secures political power," a competency being a modest independent living. It points to 1.6 million titles and an estimated 93 million living descendants; to Richard Edwards, Jacob Friefeld and Rebecca Wingo, whose *Homesteading the Plains* (2017) uses newly digitized claim files to argue that earlier historians underrated homesteading's success; and to the economist Joseph Ferrie, whose linked 1850 to 1870 census records show many urban unskilled workers moving west and gaining wealth. What would change it: evidence that most patents went to dummy claimants or the already well-off.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="A subsidy the well-placed captured">
    This view, built by the historian Paul Gates in "The Homestead Law in an Incongruous Land System" (1936), argues that a generous law sat on a land system that worked against it, so railroads, states, cash buyers and speculators got the best land first. It points to 80 million homestead acres out of 500 million, to railroad land at $4.76 an acre, and to Danhof's farm-making costs; to Fred Shannon's 1945 "post-mortem" on the idea that free land drained off poor eastern workers; and to William Sparks, head of the General Land Office, who declared in 1885 that "the public domain was being made the prey of unscrupulous speculation." What would change it: new counts showing that most land and patents went to genuine small farmers.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Dispossession at the foundation">
    This view argues that the free land was, first of all, taken land. The scholar Patrick Wolfe (2006) argued that settler colonialism is a continuing structure, not a single event, and the political scientist Paul Frymer (2017) argues that federal land policy, settlement and removal alike, promoted the formation of a white settler nation. It points to Carlos, Feir and Redish, who find settlers' property rights growing stronger as Native rights were disregarded; to the economist Douglas Allen (1991), who argues that the government steered homesteaders toward nations that had not yet ceded land, to pressure them; and to the Dawes Act's loss of 90 million acres in 47 years. What would change it: evidence that the cessions were freely bargained at something like market value.
  </Perspective>

## What this era settles, and what it leaves open

Some things are settled. The land came from Native nations, by treaty, by trades of sovereignty between empires, and by force. About 1.6 million homesteaders gained title, while railroads, states and buyers got far more land. Farm households were large, worked mostly at home, and were shrinking by 1890. And no one can honestly say what a frontier home cost in years of income.

Some things are not: how many homesteaders succeeded; whether free land leveled American society or mostly enriched the well-placed; and, as Carlos, Feir and Redish ask, how essential taking Native land was to later American prosperity.

Three conditions of this era were one-time: a continent taken from its inhabitants and given away; an economy in which nearly half of Americans lived on farms and children were workers; and, in Turner's words, "never again will such gifts of free land offer themselves." The lever that remains is land policy. Governments still own land, as the debate over [[issue:public-lands-protection]] shows; still decide how land may be divided and what may be built on it, the subject of [[issue:zoning-reform]]; and still subsidize ownership, through tools such as the [[issue:mortgage-interest-deduction]]. The U.S. Housing Policy pack takes up today's version of that argument.

The next lesson follows Americans into the industrial cities, where households lived on wages and many made room for boarders and lodgers.
