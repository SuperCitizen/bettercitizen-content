# How did the New Deal remake the American mortgage?

Between August 1933 and June 1935, nearly 1.9 million American homeowners applied for help to a brand-new federal agency. At the worst, in 1933, some 1,000 home loans were being foreclosed every day. The agency, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, made just over a million loans, each rewritten as a 15-year loan repaid in monthly installments. Today it is best known for something else: city maps with some neighborhoods shaded red. Both came out of the same [[term:new-deal|New Deal]] effort to rebuild the American mortgage.

## Why was a 1920s mortgage so easy to lose?

Start on invented ground: a family buying a $5,000 house in 1926. A typical home loan of the decade, the economic historian Kenneth Snowden writes, covered no more than half the property's value and ran three to five years. It was a balloon loan: the borrower paid interest, perhaps a little principal, and owed the rest in one lump at the end. So the family puts down $2,500 and borrows $2,500 at 6 percent, a common rate. It pays $12.50 a month, and after five years it still owes all $2,500. Nobody planned to pay that lump from savings; lenders were expected to renew, and usually did. Building and loan associations were the exception, lending up to two-thirds of a home's value, repaid over eleven or twelve years.

A loan paid down in regular installments, so that the last scheduled payment clears it, is called fully amortized. A balloon loan was closer to renting money: you pay rent on the sum and hand it all back when the lease ends, unless the lender renews. The comparison breaks at the end: a tenant who cannot renew moves out, but a borrower who cannot renew or repay loses the house.

From 1929 to 1932, personal disposable income fell 41.0 percent and the value of nonfarm homes 25.7 percent, while mortgage debt fell only 6.8 percent, reports the Federal Reserve economist David Wheelock. Thousands of banks and other lenders failed, cutting the ties that renewal depended on. A Commerce Department study of 22 cities found that on January 1, 1934, 43.8 percent of urban owner-occupied homes with a first mortgage were in default.

Two different things broke. The collapse itself, a [[term:depression|depression]] that cut incomes by two-fifths and took thousands of banks with it, was a one-time catastrophe. The contract that turned it into mass foreclosure was a design choice, and design can change.

## How did three agencies build a new kind of loan?

The first response was a rescue. The HOLC, created by Congress in 1933, bought mortgages in default from lenders and rewrote each as a 15-year loan at 5 percent interest, later 4.5 percent, for at most 80 percent of the home's appraised value. By June 1936 it had made just over a million loans worth $3.1 billion, about one in five of the nation's mortgaged owner-occupied nonfarm homes. Nearly half of all applications were rejected or withdrawn, one in five of its loans still ended in foreclosure, and its operating income of $352 million slightly exceeded its losses of $338 million.

The second piece was meant to last. The National Housing Act of 1934 created the Federal Housing Administration, which did not lend. It insured private lenders' loans against default and, in exchange, set their terms. Under its 1938 rules an insured loan had to be repaid in monthly installments that cleared it completely, at no more than 5 percent interest, over no more than 20 years, for up to 80 percent of the appraised value. A small new house bought with 10 percent down could get 90 percent over as long as 25 years. Borrowers paid the premium, half a percent to one percent a year.

The difference that mattered is the shape of the debt: a smaller stake, and a balance that reaches zero on schedule.

The third piece answered a lender's other worry, tying up money for 20 years. In 1938 a federal agency created the Federal National Mortgage Association, later Fannie Mae, to buy FHA-insured loans from the lenders that made them. A market in existing loans is called a secondary market: a lender sells a loan and lends the money again. Fannie Mae started small, holding about 1 percent of the nation's home mortgage debt at the end of 1941, and lenders came slowly to the new loan: in 1938 FHA-insured loans were under 20 percent of new mortgages.

Here the one-time and the lasting part company. The HOLC, wound up in 1951, lent just after the bottom and collected during a recovery no law can order up. The FHA, the long amortized loan and the secondary market are still here: the FHA joined the [[org:us-department-of-housing-and-urban-development|Department of Housing and Urban Development]] in 1965 and says it has insured over 50 million mortgages since 1934, and Congress rechartered Fannie Mae in 1968 as a government-sponsored private corporation.

## What did the maps and the manual say?

Redlining, in the definition used by the University of Richmond's Mapping Inequality project, is refusing mortgage credit to whole neighborhoods, not just individuals. From September 1935 the HOLC's City Survey sent agents to cities of 40,000 people or more, 239 in all by 1940. With local lenders, developers and appraisers, they graded neighborhoods from A, colored green and judged best, to D, colored red and judged "hazardous," and wrote an area description of each that recorded residents' race, ethnicity and class. Black residents, and to a lesser degree immigrants and Jews, were logged as an "infiltration." Most red areas were white, often immigrant, neighborhoods, but Black neighborhoods were graded D almost without exception, whatever their residents' incomes.

In Tacoma, Washington, a small area otherwise like the B-graded neighborhood around it got a D. Its description explained: "Three highly respected Negro families own homes and live in the middle block of the area.... While very much above the average of their race, it is quite generally recognized by Realtors that their presence seriously detracts from the desirability of their immediate neighborhood."

The FHA wrote the same judgment into its rules. Its 1938 Underwriting Manual told the staff who decided which loans to insure to investigate whether "incompatible racial and social groups" lived nearby, and explained: "If a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes." It counted "natural or artificially established barriers" as protection against "inharmonious racial groups." And among the deed restrictions it recommended, called restrictive covenants, was "Prohibition of the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended."

The FHA acted on those rules, with neighborhood maps of its own from 1935. In loan records for three cities from 1935 to 1940, the economic historian Price Fishback and his coauthors found one FHA-insured loan to a Black borrower in Greensboro, North Carolina, a city with about 1,300 Black homeowners, and none in Peoria, Illinois. The HOLC's lending looked different, because its maps came late: it had made 90 percent of its loans before the survey began, and it refinanced nearly 24,000 African American homeowners. Yet it asked every applicant's race, and when it resold foreclosed homes it barred Black buyers from white-majority neighborhoods until at least 1943.

## How much did the HOLC maps themselves cause?

All of that is well documented. The open question is narrower: did the HOLC maps cause the later decline of the neighborhoods they marked, or record judgments that lenders and the FHA already acted on? The historian Kenneth Jackson brought the maps to wide attention in *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985) and treated the HOLC as the pioneer of federal redlining. Three readings have emerged since, each given here at its strongest.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="The maps as a cause">
    Held by the economists Daniel Aaronson, Daniel Hartley and Bhashkar Mazumder of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago (2021), this reading protects a finding: an official grade on a place can itself steer credit for decades. They argue that the maps had "meaningful and lasting effects on the development of urban neighborhoods." They point to blocks on either side of HOLC lines, checked against comparison lines with matching earlier trends: the lower-graded side later had less homeownership, lower values and rents, and a rising Black share, a gap that grew until about 1970 or 1980. They also point to similar effects on C-to-B lines where almost no Black residents lived, and to cities just above the 40,000-person cutoff for a map, which fell behind cities just below it. Its hardest question: how did the effect travel if few lenders saw the maps?
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The maps as a record">
    Held by the economic historians Price Fishback and his coauthors (2023, 2024) and by Amy Hillier (2003), this reading protects an accurate account of which agency did what, so that blame lands where it belongs. They argue that the maps recorded a segregated market and that the FHA did the excluding. They point to timing: the HOLC made 90 percent of its loans before the maps and lent to Black homeowners roughly in proportion to their numbers, while the FHA's exclusion began before the maps and changed little after. They also point to maps kept largely inside government, to Philadelphia lenders already avoiding the later-red areas, and to an estimate that racial bias in drawing the maps explains at most 4 to 20 percent of the concentration of Black households in the lowest-rated zones. Its hardest question: why do outcomes split so sharply at the lines?
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="The maps as one part of a system">
    Held by historians such as Todd Michney, who with LaDale Winling has traced the HOLC's place in a wider lending regime, this reading protects the record of the HOLC's own responsibility, which its late maps could seem to excuse. They argue that the maps are the best-preserved artifact of a regime that made racial risk-rating an industry norm. They point to an HOLC that asked applicants' race, tracked whether a neighborhood's Black population was growing, and barred Black buyers from white-majority neighborhoods when reselling foreclosed homes. They also point to the HOLC and the FHA teaching racial appraisal to the industry through workshops, trade journals and a 1937 forum of more than 1,000 people where HOLC maps were displayed. Its hardest question: how much does a whole system explain beyond earlier segregation?
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

All three accept that the FHA excluded Black neighborhoods by rule and that the HOLC's graders treated Black residents as a hazard. They disagree about the weight of one document against FHA practice and older segregation. Loan timing and boundary comparisons are real evidence on different sides; whether a map or a regime is the right unit of blame is partly judgment, and it remains contested.

## What is settled, what is not, and what comes next

Much here is settled: the fragile 1920s loan, its collapse, and the HOLC's rescue of about a million homeowners. The FHA and Fannie Mae made the long, fully amortized, insured and resellable loan the American standard: 15 years at the HOLC, 20 and then 25 at the FHA, longer later. The FHA's manual wrote racial exclusion into the rules for who got that loan, and open racial discrimination in mortgage lending stayed legal until the Fair Housing Act of 1968, a [[term:civil-rights|civil rights]] landmark.

Not settled: how much the maps themselves did, and whether insuring long, high-leverage loans was wise. The savings associations' trade group, the U.S. Building and Loan League, saw FHA insurance as a government [[term:subsidy]] for the same risky kind of loan, Snowden writes, that its own members had promoted in the 1920s. Today's version of that argument is in this site's U.S. Housing Policy pack.

For the series' central question, sort the decade. The banking collapse and the fall in incomes were one-time, and so was the HOLC's timing. The levers remain: federal mortgage insurance, the long amortized loan, a secondary market, and the model of a temporary agency that rewrites failing mortgages. Anyone who credits Washington with affordable homes in the 1930s should add that homeownership hit its census low in 1940, and that the new rules decided who would be let in.

The next lesson follows the machine into the postwar boom, when the GI Bill, Levittown and the highways ran it at full speed, and these rules traveled with it.
