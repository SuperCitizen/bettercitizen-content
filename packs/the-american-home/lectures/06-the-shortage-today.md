December 2025. Two analysts at the Congressional Research Service send Congress a short memo. Its title puts two words in quotation marks: Estimates of a "Housing Shortage."

[beat]

The memo lists five respected estimates of how many homes the country lacks, running from 3.7 million to 5.5 million.

Then it adds a caution. In the memo's words: "there is not universal agreement that the United States has a housing shortage at all."

[pause]

Four months later, the White House put the figure at 10 million or more single-family homes. The previous July, Moody's Analytics had put it at about 2 million.

Same country. Same homes. [beat] A fivefold spread.

This is the last era in our series: the present. By the end, you will be able to say why honest counts of the shortage disagree. And you will be able to set today's five measures beside every era before it.

We will take it in four steps. First, why the counts differ. Second, what a home costs now, in years of income and in monthly payments. Third, what has happened to households, to marriage and to children. And last, three serious explanations for why homes cost so much.

## Why the counts differ

Start somewhere nobody owns. Picture a town with 10,000 homes and 9,000 households. Is it short of homes?

You cannot say yet. You need three answers first.

[beat]

How many households live there now. How many more would form if housing cost less, like grown children who would rather not live with their parents. And what share of homes should stand empty, so that people can move without a bidding war.

Say 400 more households would form. Suppose a healthy town keeps 12 percent of its homes empty. Then it needs about 10,700 homes, and it is short about 700.

Now suppose 8 percent is enough. Then it needs about 10,200 homes, and it is short only about 200.

[pause]

Nothing was built. Nothing was torn down. Only an assumption changed.

> A shortage is estimated, not counted.

Every national estimate makes those same choices, and Freddie Mac's shows how. As of the third quarter of 2024, it comes to 3.7 million homes.

About 1 million of that is households that would have formed at the rents of 2000. The rest is empty homes, enough to reach a vacancy rate of 11.7 percent.

Moody's Analytics counts only the homes actually on the market, and it finds about 2 million.

Other estimates ask different questions. The Realtors asked how far building since 2001 fell behind its old pace, and their answer was 5.5 million.

Zillow counts families who are doubled up with people outside the family, then subtracts the homes for sale or rent. [beat] That leaves 4.7 million.

And Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies asks a narrower question. How many more empty homes would bring back the vacancy rates of the 1990s? In early 2026, its answer was 600,000. That was down from 1.5 million in 2022, after the most apartment completions since 1986.

[pause]

Put another way, before you trust a shortage number, ask which question it answers.

The vacancy choice alone is huge. Elena Patel, a co-author of a Brookings estimate, compares two periods. Vacancy in the 2010s ran about 2.5 points above vacancy from 1980 to 2000.

On today's stock of homes, choosing one period over the other moves the target by about 3.7 million homes. [beat] That is more than the whole gap between Moody's and the Realtors.

Let's test that. An analyst redoes one of these estimates. She takes the healthy vacancy rate from the 2010s, when vacancy ran higher, instead of from 1980 to 2000. Does the shortage grow, or shrink? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It grows. The target is every household, plus the empty homes a healthy market is assumed to need. Raise the assumed vacancy rate, and the target rises, while the real stock of homes stays put. So the gap gets bigger.

[beat]

Each percentage point of vacancy is about 1.5 million homes.

## What a home costs

That is why the counts differ. Now the price.

This series has measured a home's price the same way in every lecture. Take what the median owner says her home is worth. Divide by median family income.

[beat]

In 1950, that came to 2.4 years of income. In 1970, it came to 1.8. In 1980 and 2000, it was 2.4 again. And in 2025, it was about 3.5.

> A home now costs about a year more of income than in 1950.

The comparison is approximate, because the Census now counts all owned homes, not only single-family houses on less than ten acres.

Harvard's Joint Center measures something different. It takes what homes actually sold for, and divides by household income, which runs lower because it counts people living alone.

By that yardstick, the median home cost a record 5.0 times income in 2022. In 2025 it cost 4.7 times income. In the 1990s, the average was 3.2.

So you will hear both numbers. One prices the homes people own. The other prices the homes people must buy. [beat] Both are honest.

Then come interest rates. At the end of 2020, with mortgage rates near record lows, the monthly payment on the median-priced home was 1,240 dollars. In late 2025, it was 2,420 dollars.

[beat]

Roughly double.

Add taxes and insurance, and the income needed to afford that home rose to 120,800 dollars. Only 32 percent of households clear that bar.

And half of all mortgages carry rates below 4 percent, so owners stay put. Economists at the Federal Housing Finance Agency measured it. Each percentage point between a borrower's rate and the market rate cut the chance of a sale by 18.1 percent.

[pause]

So why don't builders rush in? Because every input got dearer.

The government's index of what goes into a new home, leaving out labor, rose 40 percent between January 2020 and early 2026. Lot values rose 52 percent from 2020 to 2024.

Home builders, surveying their own members, put regulation at 26.4 percent of a new home's price. That is the builders' own count. It includes rules most people want, like fire codes.

But some rules are local choices. Minimum lot sizes. Bans on apartments. Slow approvals. [beat] City councils and county boards make those choices.

The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko trace the result. In the 1950s, the housing stock grew 4 percent a year. In the 2010s, it grew 0.64 percent a year.

Atlanta, Dallas, Miami and Phoenix were once the nation's building champions. In their suburbs in the 2010s, new building answered rising prices less than a quarter as strongly as in the 1970s.

This series began on a frontier of open land. Their paper ends on another one: "America's suburban frontier appears to be closing."

[pause]

The suburban frontier. Closing.

## Households, marriage and children

That is the price. Now the people.

A shortage shows up less as people on the street than as households that never form.

In 2020 and 2021, when borrowing was cheapest, the country added 2.0 million households a year. In 2025, it added only 1.1 million. Young adults have formed households more slowly since 2024.

And in the Realtors' 2026 survey, the median first-time buyer was 40. [beat] A record.

Marriage and children come later, and less often. In 1950, the median man first married at 22.8, and the median woman at 20.3.

In 2025, the man was 30.8, and the woman was 28.4. [beat] About eight years later, for both.

In 2024, the total fertility rate fell to 1.60, the lowest ever recorded. That is the number of births a woman would have at that year's rates.

And childcare stacks on top of the rent or the mortgage. The Department of Labor found that in 2022, full-day care for one child took between 8.9 and 16 percent of median family income.

> Households are smaller, marriages later, and births the fewest on record.

Now set today beside every era in this series.

Two measures moved mostly one way. Households shrank, from about 5 people in 1880 to 2.50 today. And married women's paid work rose, from 5.6 percent in 1900 to 58.2 percent.

The other three bent. Marriage age fell to its low in the 1950s, then climbed past every earlier figure. Births rose in the baby boom, then fell to the lowest on record. And a home's price held between about 1.8 and 2.4 years of income from 1950 to 2000. Then it climbed to about 3.5.

[pause]

Do housing costs cause the later marriages and the fewer babies? Here is a finding to test.

The economists Lisa Dettling and Melissa Kearney found that when local house prices rise, births fall in one group and rise in another. Which group's births fall? Think it through.

[long pause]

Renters'. Rising prices are a cost to people who still have to buy, and a gain to people who already own. So owners' births rose, as their home equity grew.

But hold that loosely. Kearney, working with Phillip Levine and Luke Pardue, also found something else. The states where rents and childcare spending rose most after 2007 saw no bigger drop in births.

They conjecture that the "shifting priorities" of younger generations matter more. [beat] That question is still open.

## Three answers to why

So far, facts. Now the argument.

Start with what nobody disputes. Prices rose more than 54 percent from January 2020 to early 2026. Monthly payments roughly doubled. Building has answered prices less since 2008.

And the cheapest rentals are vanishing. Homes renting for under 600 dollars a month, after inflation, fell from 8.3 million in 2014 to 5.8 million in 2024.

What is contested is the main cause. And the cause matters, because each one points to a different lever. Here are three serious answers, each as its strongest advocates would put it.

First, the land-use view. Economists such as Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko argue that prices rose most where building fell most.

They point to the pace of building. Had the housing stock grown as fast from 2000 to 2020 as it did from 1980 to 2000, the country would have 15 million more homes.

And both parties' White Houses have agreed. The Obama administration, in 2016, blamed zoning and lengthy approvals. The Trump administration, in 2026, blamed "fees, mandates, regulations, and red tape."

This view protects the newcomer, the renter and the young, who are priced out of the places with jobs.

> The land-use view: prices rose most where building fell most.

Second, the finance view. Economists who study credit and demand argue that money, not rules, set the timing.

They point out that rules barely changed in 2020, yet prices jumped. John Mondragon and Johannes Wieland find that the move to remote work explains over half of the rise in real house prices from 2019 to 2023. Record-low rates, followed by lock-in, cut the supply of homes for sale.

And large investors, nearly absent before the 2008 crash, bought heavily after it. By June 2022, 32 of them owned nearly 450,000 homes. That is about 3 percent of single-family rentals nationally. In metro Atlanta, it is about 25 percent.

This view protects the ordinary buyer, bidding against cheap money and corporate landlords. It concedes that investors' national share is small, and that their buying has slowed.

> The finance view: cheap money and investors set the timing.

Third, the income view. The housing scholars Kirk McClure and Alex Schwartz argue that the country has enough homes, but not enough income at the bottom.

They point out that from 2000 to 2020, housing production exceeded the growth of households by 3.3 million units. The problem, they write, is "the mismatch between the distribution of incomes and the distribution of housing prices."

Harvard's numbers fit that view. A newly built apartment asked a median of 1,900 dollars a month in 2025. The median renter could afford about 1,400.

This view protects the poorest renters, whom new building at market prices may never reach.

> The income view: the homes exist, but the poorest cannot afford them.

[pause]

Notice what kind of disagreement this is. All three accept the same price and payment figures. They split over the cause.

And each side faces evidence it did not choose. Prices jumped in loosely and tightly zoned places alike, which fits the finance view. The land-use side answers that rules decide how much of any surge becomes price instead of homes. And critics of the income view answer that counting today's households understates need, because high prices keep households from forming.

That is a contested question, not a settled one.

You may already lean toward one of these three. So take the one you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

One more distinction. Some of today's conditions cannot be repeated or undone by policy. The collapse of mortgage rates in 2020 and 2021, and the lock-in it left. The sudden move to remote work. The using up of cheap land near job centers.

Others are levers that still exist. Zoning, permitting and building codes can be rewritten. Monetary policy and mortgage rules still shape what borrowing costs. And subsidies, rental aid and help with childcare are choices governments make every year.

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull something back. At the start, I gave you three questions that every shortage estimate has to answer. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

How many households there are now. How many more would form if housing cost less. And how many homes should stand empty.

[beat]

Change any one, and the shortage moves by millions.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A shortage is estimated, not counted. Published estimates run from about 2 million to 10 million, because they ask different questions.

[beat]

Two. A home now costs about a year more of income than in 1950, by the Census measure. By what homes sold for, it costs 4.7 times household income. And payments roughly doubled after 2020.

[beat]

Three. Households are smaller, marriages later, and births the fewest on record.

[beat]

Four. Three explanations share the facts and split over the cause. Land-use rules. Money and investors. Incomes at the bottom.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. How large the shortage really is. Which cause dominates. And how much housing costs, rather than changing priorities, explain later marriages and fewer births.

The next lecture asks the question this whole series set out to answer. Which of the postwar era's conditions were one-time gifts that cannot be repeated? And which levers could policy realistically pull today?

For now, go back to that memo from December 2025, with two words of its title in quotation marks. You know now why they were there.

> The size of the shortage depends on the question you ask.
