In the week of October 9, 1981, Freddie Mac's weekly survey of lenders came back with a number. [beat] 18.63 percent.

That was the average rate on a 30-year fixed mortgage. It is the highest the survey has ever recorded.

At that rate, a loan of 40,000 dollars cost about 623 dollars a month. When the survey began, in April 1971, the rate was 7.33 percent. The same loan cost about 275 dollars.

[pause]

The same loan. More than double the payment.

And buyers were not the only ones in trouble. The savings and loan associations that had made the old loans, at the old rates, were going broke.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to explain what changed for the American home between 1970 and 2008. And you will be able to sort those changes into two piles. The things that happened once. And the choices that policy can still make.

We will take it in four steps.

First, inflation, and the first crash: the savings and loans.

Second, why two paychecks became normal.

Third, why prices climbed so much faster in some places than in others.

And last, the second crash, in 2008.

## Eighteen percent

Start with the 1970s. The decade brought something called stagflation. Prices rose fast, while growth stalled and unemployment climbed. Stagflation is the worst of both worlds: inflation and a weak economy, at the same time.

The Federal Reserve's own history calls these years the Great Inflation. Inflation peaked at nearly 15 percent, in March 1980.

In 1979, Paul Volcker became chairman of the Federal Reserve. The Fed squeezed money until the rate banks charge one another overnight approached 20 percent. A deep recession followed, and unemployment climbed near 11 percent. But by October 1982, inflation was down to 5 percent.

[pause]

Now watch what this did to housing. It did two opposite things.

Take a family that already owned a home, with a fixed-rate mortgage. For them, inflation was a gift. The price of the house rose along with everything else. But the monthly payment stayed the same. So every year, the real weight of the debt got lighter.

Now take a family trying to buy in 1981. For them, the cure for inflation meant borrowing at 18 percent.

> Inflation made owners richer and made entry harder.

Put another way, the people already inside gained, and the people at the door paid. Against family income, the median owned home went from 1.8 years to 2.4 in that one decade.

So who paid for the owners' gift? [beat] The lenders.

Savings and loan associations, often called thrifts, worked in a plain way. They took in people's savings, which could leave at any time. And they made 30-year home loans, at fixed rates.

Picture a bakery. It has promised to sell bread at a fixed price for thirty years. But it buys its flour at whatever flour costs that week. When flour soars, every loaf loses money.

When rates soared, thrifts had to pay savers more than their old mortgages earned. By the end of 1982, 415 insured thrifts were insolvent.

> Thrifts borrowed short and lent long, and the rate shock broke them.

Washington chose to wait, and to loosen the rules, rather than shut them down. A 1980 law raised federal deposit insurance to 100,000 dollars an account. Insolvent thrifts stayed open and poured insured money into speculative real estate. Much of it failed.

About 1,300 savings institutions failed between 1980 and 1994. The cleanup cost taxpayers an estimated 132 billion dollars.

The crisis, the FDIC's own history concludes, "reflected a massive public policy failure."

[pause]

A massive public policy failure.

Let's test the bakery on a new case. A credit union, in 1972, lends a member money for 30 years, at a fixed 7.5 percent. It pays its savers 5 percent. By 1981, it must pay them 15 percent to keep them. What has happened to the credit union? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is losing money on that loan every year. The loan still earns 7.5 percent, and the savers now cost twice that. [beat] It is the bakery again.

Before we move on, sort what we have so far. The Great Inflation and its cure happened once, and so did the long slide in mortgage rates that followed. That slide helped every later buyer, and it cannot repeat unless rates first climb that high again.

What remains are levers. Deposit insurance. [beat] Capital rules. [beat] And how fast regulators shut down a failing lender.

## Two paychecks

That was the money. Now the household.

In 1970, 40.5 percent of married women living with their husbands were in the labor force. By 2000, it was 61.1 percent. And there it stayed, through 2007.

Count by couples. Both spouses had earnings in 45.7 percent of married couples in 1970. By 2000, it was 59.8 percent.

Americans married later, too. In 1970, the median woman first married at 20.8. By 2007, she married at 25.6. Families had fewer children, and households shrank.

[pause]

Two more facts frame the debate. Among men who worked full time all year, median income barely moved. In 2025 dollars, it was about 69,500 in 1973, and about 68,800 in 2007. Among women, it rose, from about 39,300 to about 53,900.

[beat]

And homes? Against family income, the price of a home barely moved after 1980. It sat near 2.4 years. But by then, family income usually included a wife's paycheck.

Nobody disputes these numbers. What caused what is the argument. So here is the question, on ground nobody owns. Were wives pulled into paid work by opportunity, or pushed into it by cost?

The first answer is the opportunity view. It comes from the economic historian Claudia Goldin, who won the 2023 Nobel Prize in economics. What it protects is the idea that women's work was a choice, not a squeeze.

Goldin argues that wives went to work because work changed, and because women's expectations changed. She points to a rise that began early in the twentieth century, as service jobs grew and women's schooling climbed. She points to the pill, which let women plan careers. And in a paper called "The Quiet Revolution," she dates a sharp break to the late 1970s. Young women began to expect long working lives. They began to count a career as part of who they were.

> The opportunity view: wives went to work because work and expectations changed.

The second answer is the trap view. It comes from the bankruptcy scholar Elizabeth Warren and the consultant Amelia Warren Tyagi, in a 2003 book called The Two-Income Trap. What it protects is the family's safety net.

Warren and Tyagi argue that much of the second paycheck never reached the family's pocket. They point to a bidding war. Families competed for homes in a shrinking set of neighborhoods with good public schools. And two incomes let them push those prices up.

The result, in their account, was a family that earned far more than its one-earner parents, yet had less left after fixed costs. And it had lost its safety net: a parent at home, who could take a job if the other lost one.

> The trap view: much of the second paycheck was bid into house prices.

What does the evidence settle? [beat] The timing. Married women's work had been rising for decades before 1970. And from 1950 to 1970, it climbed while homes were getting cheaper, from 2.4 years of income down to 1.8. That fits Goldin's long story.

But Warren and Tyagi's comparison starts in the 1970s. And national figures cannot show how much of the later paycheck went into bidding for houses, one school district at a time. That is a contested question, not a settled one.

Either way, the shift could happen only once. A household goes from one earner to two just one time. And by around 2000, the share of wives at work had stopped rising.

Here is a way to test the trap. A county decides that any child can attend its best high school, by lottery, whatever the family's address. If Warren and Tyagi are right, what should happen to home prices inside that school's old zone? Think it through.

[long pause]

They should fall, compared with similar homes nearby. Part of what buyers were paying for was a seat in that school. And if prices barely moved, the bidding war explained less than the authors argue.

## Rules about building

So far, the money and the household. Now the land.

In 1971, the town of Petaluma, California, completed 891 new homes. The next year, it adopted a five-year plan. No more than 500 new homes a year.

The purpose, as the appeals court put it, was "to protect its small town character and surrounding open space." Builders sued, and won in a federal district court. But in 1975 the appeals court reversed.

[beat]

A town could ration its own growth.

What do rules like that do to prices? The economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko built a test. Compare what homes sell for with what it costs to build them.

Where homes sell for about what they cost to build, supply is answering demand. Where homes sell far above that cost, something is stopping builders. Glaeser and Gyourko attribute much of that gap to regulation. They call it a regulatory tax.

> Where prices sit far above building costs, something is stopping builders.

Put another way, where builders can answer demand, new demand becomes new homes. Where they cannot, new demand becomes higher prices.

Why would a town choose scarcity? The economist William Fischel offers an answer he calls the homevoter hypothesis. For most families, a home is their largest asset. And it cannot be insured against changes in the neighborhood. So homeowners vote, reasonably from where they stand, against development that might lower its value.

[pause]

Economists broadly accept that tight rules raise prices where demand is strong. How much of the national rise they explain is still argued. The next lecture, and the U.S. Housing Policy pack, take up today's version of that fight.

Of everything in this lecture, land-use rules are the most plainly still in policy's hands.

## The second crash

Earlier I gave you a picture of a bakery. Can you still say why it went broke?

[pause]

It sold bread at a fixed price and bought flour at that week's price. The second crash had a different shape.

From January 2000 to July 2006, a national index of home prices rose about 85 percent. Then it fell about 27 percent, to a low in February 2012.

On the way up, homeownership reached a record 69.2 percent in 2004. On the way down, in September 2008, the government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and Lehman Brothers failed. By early 2011, about four million families had lost their homes to foreclosure.

[pause]

Congress created a ten-member commission to explain why. Its members were appointed by leaders of both parties. And they split three ways.

Here is the ground nobody disputes. The boom. [beat] The bust. [beat] Looser lending. [beat] The foreclosures. What is disputed is which cause was essential.

First, the commission's majority. What its view protects is a lender's duty to check that a borrower can pay.

The majority argues that the crisis was avoidable. In its words, "collapsing mortgage-lending standards and the mortgage securitization pipeline lit and spread the flame of contagion and crisis."

[beat]

Lit and spread the flame. Securitization means pooling loans into securities and selling them to investors.

The majority points to lenders who lent with little regard for whether borrowers could pay. And it points to regulators who heard warnings and did not act.

The economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi point to neighborhood data. From 2002 to 2005, mortgage credit grew fastest in ZIP codes heavy with subprime borrowers, even as their incomes fell behind. Lenders who sold loans soon after making them, they conclude, had too little reason to care whether the loans were repaid.

> The majority view: lax lending and securitization lit and spread the crisis.

Second, Commissioner Peter Wallison, in his dissent. What his view protects is sound underwriting standards.

Wallison argues that government housing policy was the essential cause. It began with affordable-housing goals that Congress imposed on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac in 1992. The housing department pressed those goals under Presidents Clinton and George W. Bush. In his account, policy pushed lenders to lower their standards, to reach borrowers at or below their area's median income.

He points to his count of 27 million subprime and other risky loans. By his count, that was half of all American mortgages. And he argues they were ready to default once the bubble began to deflate.

> Wallison's view: government housing policy pushed lending standards down.

Third, the saving-glut view. What it protects is an explanation that fits every country the crisis hit.

Ben Bernanke, then a Federal Reserve governor, argued in 2005 that a global saving glut had emerged over the previous decade. It helped explain unusually low long-term interest rates.

Three commissioners, Keith Hennessey, Douglas Holtz-Eakin and Bill Thomas, built their dissent on that idea. They argue that China, other developing countries and big oil producers saved more than they spent, and sent the surplus to America and Europe.

They point to housing bubbles in Britain, Spain, Australia, France and Ireland. Some were larger than America's. And some were in countries that relied little on American-style securitization.

> The saving-glut view: global savings made credit cheap in many countries at once.

What does the evidence settle? The commission's majority found that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac "contributed to the crisis, but were not a primary cause." Wallison disputes that finding.

And two studies complicate every side. Glaeser, Gyourko and Joshua Gottlieb estimated that lower real interest rates explain only about a fifth of the price rise from 1996 to 2006.

And Manuel Adelino, Antoinette Schoar and Felipe Severino found that middle- and high-income borrowers made up most new mortgages, even at the peak. They describe the boom as a shared bet that prices would keep rising.

So which cause was essential? That is a contested question, not a settled one.

Try one more case. Suppose a country with no Fannie Mae, no Freddie Mac, and little American-style securitization had a boom and bust as large as America's, in the same years. Which explanation does that case help most? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The saving glut. Neither American cause can explain a boom with no American housing goals and no American-style securitization. That was the dissenters' point about Spain and Ireland.

It does not prove the glut caused America's bubble. It shows that some cause common to many countries was at work.

The flood of global savings was a condition of the world economy. Lending standards, the rules for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and the supervision of lenders are levers that remain.

Three explanations, then. Lax private lending. Government housing policy. A global saving glut. You may already lean toward one of them. So take the one you find least convincing, and state the best case for it, the way its smartest advocate would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. Inflation made owners richer and made entry harder. And the thrifts, which borrowed short and lent long, broke.

[beat]

Two. Two paychecks became normal. The opportunity view says work and expectations changed. The trap view says much of the second paycheck was bid into house prices.

[beat]

Three. Where prices sit far above building costs, something is stopping builders. And land-use rules are the lever most plainly still in policy's hands.

[beat]

And four. The crash of 2008 has three explanations. Lax lending and securitization. Government housing policy. A global saving glut.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Why two paychecks became normal. How much land-use rules explain. And which cause of 2008 was essential.

Sort the whole era, and you get two piles. The first pile happened once. The Great Inflation and its cure. The long slide in rates. The move of wives into paid work. The flood of global savings.

The second pile is levers that remain. Land-use rules. Lending standards. Deposit insurance. The rules for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. And monetary policy, which broke an inflation at the price of 18 percent mortgages.

The next lecture picks up where the crash left off: today's housing shortage, why the estimates of it differ, and what a home costs in years of income now.

For now, go back to that survey in October 1981. 18.63 percent. [beat] You know now who gained from that number, and who paid for it. [beat] And you know which parts of that era happened once, and which levers are still in reach.
