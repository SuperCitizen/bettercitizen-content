January 1, 1863, the first day of the Homestead Act. At a land office in Brownville, Nebraska, a Union soldier named Daniel Freeman files a claim for 160 acres along Cub Creek, near the town of Beatrice.

Agnes Suiter joins him there in 1865. They will have eight children. And in 1869, the land becomes his.

[pause]

Near Beatrice, on a strip along the Big Blue River, the Otoe and Missouria have their reservation. In 1854 they signed a treaty giving up "all their country west of the Missouri River", all except that strip.

In 1880 and 1881, Congress sold the strip too. [beat] And it moved them to Indian Territory, in what is now Oklahoma.

A family and two nations. One stretch of country. [beat] Both belong in this story.

This lecture opens a series about the American home. The series asks what made a home and a family affordable in each era. It asks what made the postwar American dream possible, and which parts of it policy could realistically recreate today.

Each lecture keeps track of five measures. What a home cost in years of income. How many people lived in a household. Who worked outside the home. How old people were when they first married. And how many children were born.

Each lecture also sorts what it finds into two piles: conditions that cannot be repeated, and policy levers that still exist.

By the end of this one, you will be able to say what a free homestead really cost, and who paid for the land. We will take it in four steps.

First, what a homestead cost the family that took it.

Second, why frontier families were so large.

Third, who paid for the free land, and who was shut out.

And last, the argument over what homesteading meant.

## What a free farm cost

Start with the first measure, and with a gap. For this era, there is no reliable figure for a home's price in years of income.

The Census Bureau's national series of home values begins in 1940, and its national median family income begins in 1947. So for any year before 1950, there is no national ratio to give you.

What can be shown instead is what land cost the people who took it. In money. In time. And in work.

The Homestead Act was signed on May 20, 1862. It offered a quarter section, which is 160 acres, to anyone who headed a family or was at least 21. They had to be a citizen, or to have declared the intention to become one, and they could never have borne arms against the United States.

The money was small. By the National Archives' account, it was 10 dollars to file, and 6 dollars for the patent, which is the deed.

The time was not small. A homesteader had to stay on the claim for five years, build a dwelling, and cultivate the land. Or, after six months, the settler could buy the claim outright, for 200 dollars.

But the largest cost was making a farm. One textbook puts it at nearly 1,000 dollars in supplies: horses, livestock, wagons, wells, fencing and seed.

In 1941 the economist Clarence Danhof made the case that cheap western land was not cheap at all, once those costs were counted.

Location cost money too. Railroads held alternate sections along their routes, so homesteaders were often forced to claim land 30 to 60 miles from transportation. Railroad land beside the tracks cost more. In 1880 it averaged four dollars and seventy-six cents an acre.

> The land was free. The farm was not.

Here is a way to hold it. Think of a homestead as a scholarship that pays the tuition, but not room, board or books. And you must keep it for five years.

In modern terms, that makes it a subsidy: the government gave away an asset it could have sold.

But the image breaks in one place, and we will come back to it. A scholarship is paid from money someone gave. [beat] This land had belonged to someone who did not give it.

Let's test the costs. It is 1880. A family has about 800 dollars saved, and wants to farm in Kansas. Should they buy 160 acres of railroad land beside the tracks, or homestead 160 acres far from the railroad? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Look at the railroad land first. At the average price, 160 acres by the tracks would cost about 762 dollars. That leaves almost nothing of the nearly 1,000 dollars it took to start a farm.

The homestead saves the price of the land. Instead, it costs five years, and a long haul to market. [beat] The family trades cash for time and distance.

## Why families were so large

That is what the land cost. Now the people on it.

In 1880, 44 percent of Americans lived on farms. And on a farm, the household was the firm. Its members were the workforce, and much of what they made was eaten or worn at home.

> On a farm, the family was the workforce.

Benjamin Franklin saw what cheap land did to families. In 1751 he argued that land in America was so cheap that a laboring man who knew farming could soon save enough to buy some. Listen for what he thought followed.

"Hence Marriages in America are more general, and more generally early, than in Europe."

[pause]

More general, and more early. Cheap land, on his account, meant early marriage.

For his century there are only estimates. The demographer Michael Haines estimates that around 1800, a white woman bore about seven children, and that at mid-century, enslaved women bore between seven and eight.

Many of those children died. Around 1850, an estimated 217 of every 1,000 white infants died before their first birthday.

Households were large too. In 1790 the average household held 5.79 people, and that figure counts free people only.

Then families began to shrink. In the countryside the decline was under way before about 1870, too early for the usual explanations, such as cities and schooling.

By 1890, births per white woman had fallen to an estimated 3.87. Why so early? Haines notes that the rising cost of good farmland has been proposed as a prime factor, "although this is controversial." And recent work credits women themselves with much of the decline.

Now the other measures, as the record has them. The first national figures for marriage age come in 1890. That year, the median man first married at 26.1, and the median woman at 22.0.

That 26.1 matches the figure for 1990. [beat] The early marriages Franklin described do not show up in the first national count. And even with free land on offer, only 47.8 percent of households owned their home in 1890.

One measure has a blind spot. In 1890, only 4.6 percent of married women were counted in the labor force. That measure counts work for pay or profit, so the farm household barely registers in it.

Let's test that blind spot. In 1890, a Kansas farm wife churns butter and sells it in town. She keeps the hens, and she does the farm's accounts. Would the labor-force measure most likely count her? Think it through.

[long pause]

Most likely not. The census counted people by a usual occupation they reported, and the work of running a farm household rarely got reported that way.

So a low figure for 1890 means less paid work was recorded. [beat] It does not mean less work was done.

## Who paid, and who was shut out

Now back to the place where the scholarship image broke.

"Free land" was free only to the settler. The economists Ann Carlos, Donna Feir and Angela Redish put it plainly. The land of North America, they write, "was already made productive and was the recognized territory of sovereign Indigenous Nations."

Sovereign means self-governing. Before land could become public land of the United States, that sovereignty had to be extinguished. It was extinguished in three ways.

The first way was the treaty. The Otoe and Missouria gave up their country in 1854 for annual payments that started at 20,000 dollars and shrank over forty years. In all, they came to about 405,000 dollars.

The second way looked like a purchase. In 1803 the United States bought Louisiana from France. But Carlos, Feir and Redish argue that what it acquired was "not land but rather an exclusive right to treat with" the nations whose land it was.

[beat]

In plainer words, it bought the right to bargain with the people who held the land. Not the land itself.

The third way was force: the removals of the 1830s, remembered for the Trail of Tears, and wars on the Plains.

The year 1862 shows how the pieces fit. The Pacific Railroad Act laid a route across land not yet ceded, and promised that "the United States shall extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian Titles" to it.

That same year, the Morrill Act funded colleges with land. The historian Robert Lee and the journalist Tristan Ahtone traced it to about 10.7 million acres, taken from nearly 250 tribes, bands and communities.

Then, in 1887, the Dawes Act turned the homestead's own template on the reservations, and sold what was left to settlers. Reservation lands fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million in 1934.

> The free land was free only to the settler.

The rules also shut people out. Immigrants qualified only by declaring the intention to become citizens, and after 1882 the Chinese Exclusion Act barred Chinese immigrants from doing that. Married women generally could not file unless they headed the household.

For people freed from slavery, land was promised and taken back. In January 1865, General Sherman's Special Field Orders Number 15 set aside coastal land, up to forty acres for each family, and roughly 40,000 freedpeople settled there. That fall, President Andrew Johnson overturned the order, and most of the land went back to the planters.

The Southern Homestead Act of 1866 reserved public land in five Southern states. But much of it was swamp or heavy forest, land offices were few, and labor contracts held freedpeople in place. By the historian Keri Leigh Merritt's count, only 4,000 to 5,500 Black claimants ever received final patents under it.

On the Plains, Black homesteaders did better. Between 1877 and 1920 they gained title to 650,000 acres, and founded towns such as Nicodemus, Kansas.

## Leveler, captured subsidy, or dispossession

So far, the costs and the people. Now the argument. And it starts on ground nobody owns: the counts.

About 4 million claims were filed. About 1.6 million homesteaders obtained title, between 1863 and 1961. In all, the act gave out some 270 million acres, about 10 percent of the country's land, and most of it after this lecture's era.

Railroads, meanwhile, received grants of some 181 million acres. And by the National Archives' tally, the General Land Office gave out some 500 million acres from 1862 to 1904. Only 80 million went to homesteaders.

A bulletin of the 1890 census announced that "there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." In 1893 the historian Frederick Jackson Turner built a theory on that sentence. Listen for the phrase that carries it.

"The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development."

[pause]

Free land. On Turner's account, it explains the country.

Even a simple fact is disputed here: how many homesteaders succeeded. Divide one count by the other and you get 40 percent. The Park Service now says more than half proved up.

Three readings of the era stand out. Each gets the same four things: who holds it, what it protects, its argument, and its evidence.

The first is the opportunity view, held by Turner and by today's homestead historians. It protects the link between owning land and being a citizen.

It argues that free land made owners of people who could never have bought land, and that ownership made citizens. In Turner's words, "So long as free land exists, the opportunity for a competency exists, and economic power secures political power." A competency was a modest independent living.

It points to 1.6 million titles, and an estimated 93 million living descendants. It points to Richard Edwards, Jacob Friefeld and Rebecca Wingo, who used newly digitized claim files in 2017 to argue that earlier historians underrated homesteading's success. And it points to the economist Joseph Ferrie, whose linked census records show many unskilled workers from the cities moving west and gaining wealth.

> The opportunity view: free land made owners, and ownership made citizens.

The second is the captured-subsidy view, built by the historian Paul Gates in 1936. It protects the promise of the law against the people who gamed it.

It argues that a generous law sat on a land system that worked against it, so railroads, states, cash buyers and speculators got the best land first.

It points to 80 million homestead acres out of 500 million, and to railroad land priced above government land. It points to Danhof's farm-making costs. And it points to William Sparks, head of the General Land Office, who declared in 1885 that "the public domain was being made the prey of unscrupulous speculation."

> The captured-subsidy view: speculators and railroads got the best land first.

The third is the dispossession view, held by scholars of settler colonialism and by economic historians of Native land. It protects the record of whose land it was.

It argues that the free land was, first of all, taken land. The scholar Patrick Wolfe argued that settler colonialism is a continuing structure, not a single event. And the political scientist Paul Frymer argues that federal land policy, settlement and removal alike, promoted the formation of a white settler nation.

It points to Carlos, Feir and Redish, who find settlers' property rights growing stronger as Native rights were disregarded. It points to the economist Douglas Allen, who argues that the government steered homesteaders toward nations that had not yet ceded land, to pressure them. And it points to the Dawes Act's loss of 90 million acres in 47 years.

> The dispossession view: the free land was, first of all, taken land.

[pause]

The evidence settles the counts. It cannot settle which counts matter most. You may already lean toward one of these three views. So take the one you find least convincing, and state its best case, the way its own advocates would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Before we gather this up, pull something back. Near the start, I gave you an image for what a homestead was. Can you still name it? And where did it break?

[long pause]

A scholarship that paid tuition, but not room, board or books. It broke on one point. A scholarship is paid from money someone gave, and this land had belonged to someone who did not give it.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The land was free. The farm was not. Sixteen dollars in fees, five years on the claim, and nearly 1,000 dollars to start farming.

[beat]

Two. On a farm, the family was the workforce. Families were large, and already shrinking by 1890, and the measure of who worked outside the home barely sees them.

[beat]

Three. The free land was free only to the settler. It came from Native nations, by treaty, by purchase between empires, and by force. And the rules shut many others out.

[beat]

Four. The counts are settled: about 1.6 million homesteaders gained title. What homesteading meant is not. Opportunity, captured subsidy, or dispossession.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture leaves open. How many homesteaders succeeded. Whether free land leveled American society, or mostly enriched the well-placed. And, as Carlos, Feir and Redish ask, how essential taking Native land was to later American prosperity.

Some of this era cannot come back: a continent taken from its inhabitants and given away, and an economy where nearly half of Americans lived on farms. In Turner's words, "never again will such gifts of free land offer themselves."

But one thing remains. Governments still own land, still decide what may be built on it, and still subsidize ownership.

> The continent was one-time. Land policy is a lever that still exists.

The next lecture follows Americans into the industrial cities, where households lived on wages, and many made room for boarders and lodgers.

For now, go back to Cub Creek in 1863. A soldier filing for 160 acres. And near Beatrice, the Otoe and Missouria, on the strip they had kept. [beat] You know now who paid for the free land, and why the argument over it is still going.
