New York. Just past midnight. A policeman pounds on a tenement door on Bayard Street. He is gathering evidence of illegal overcrowding.

Behind him comes a man with a flash camera. His name is Jacob Riis.

[beat]

The flash goes off, and Riis writes down what it caught. "In a room not thirteen feet either way slept twelve men and women, two or three in bunks set in a sort of alcove, the rest on the floor."

[pause]

Most of the men, he explains, "were lodgers, who slept there for five cents a spot."

Riis put that scene in a book called How the Other Half Lives, in 1890. Later in the book, he meets a cloak-maker on Hester Street. The man earns seven dollars a week. His rent is nine and a half dollars a month. And then Riis adds one short line. "A boarder pays about a third of it."

[pause]

So here is the puzzle. The crowded room was a horror. [beat] It was also how a working family kept a roof overhead.

By the end, you will be able to explain how a working family paid for a home around 1900. And you will know why people still argue about the law that set out to fix the tenements.

We will take it in four steps.

First, how the move from the farm to the city changed what a family was for.

Second, who earned the money, and what the census failed to see.

Third, how a boarder helped pay the rent, and what New York's law of 1901 changed.

And last, the argument over that law, which is still going.

## From the farm to the wage

For most of the nineteenth century, the typical household was a farm. And a farm was a workplace.

That world was ending. In 1900, 41 percent of American workers were in agriculture. By 1930, the share was 21.5 percent. And the 1920 census was the first in which most Americans lived in urban areas.

Many of the newcomers came from abroad. In 1890, 14.8 percent of Americans had been born in another country.

In the city, a household's job changed. It stopped making things. It earned wages, and it spent them.

The sociologist Steven Ruggles has traced what that did to the home where generations lived together. On the farm, land held the family together. A son who expected to inherit had a reason to stay. So in the middle of the nineteenth century, almost 70 percent of Americans aged 65 or older lived with an adult child.

Wage work broke that bargain. Here is how Ruggles puts it. "Millions of young people left their parents' farms, attracted by the high wages, independence, and excitement of town life."

[beat]

The excitement of town life. The norm faded slowly, and almost half of elders still lived with an adult child in 1920. But the home of many generations was shrinking, along with the farm economy that had held it together.

So how did a wage household pay its way? [beat] With several paychecks.

In 1901, federal investigators surveyed the budgets of workers' families. Nearly every family had earnings from the husband. But listen to the rest.

More than one family in five had earnings from children. [beat] Nearly a quarter had income from boarders or lodgers. [beat] And fewer than one in ten had earnings from the wife.

> In the city, the home itself earned money.

Put another way, the family worked like a small firm with several lines of income, and the rooms it rented out were one of them.

Here is where that leaves the five measures this series tracks. Around 1900, the average household held 4.76 people. Fewer than half of households owned their home. The figure was 46.7 percent.

The census counted 5.6 percent of married women as workers. [beat] And for the price of a home in years of income, there is no reliable figure at all. Hold on to those last two. Both of them turn out to be traps.

Let's test the idea. Picture two widowers, each 70 years old, each with a married son. One is an Ohio farmer in 1880, who owns 80 acres. The other is a Pittsburgh steelworker in 1915, who rents four rooms.

Which father is likelier to be living in his son's household? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The farmer. His son works land he expects to inherit, so both generations gain by staying together. The steelworker has nothing to pass on, and his son's wages do not depend on him.

That is the shift Ruggles found. When the farm went, the reason to stay went with it.

## What the census saw

That is how families earned. Now, who did the counting see?

Before 1940, the census did not measure work the way we do now. It counted what it called gainful workers. A gainful worker was anyone aged 10 or older who reported a usual occupation.

Notice that age. [beat] Ten. The census assumed a ten-year-old might have a job.

And many children did. The 1870 census found 1 in 8 children employed. Between 1890 and 1910, the share was higher still. No less than 18 percent of children aged 10 to 15 were working.

Their pay mattered. In one study of Philadelphia families, children supplied 28 to 33 percent of household income. That was in native-born families with two parents. In Irish families, it was as much as 46 percent.

The sociologist Viviana Zelizer traces what came next. Over a few decades, she argues, the child became economically "useless" and emotionally "priceless." Federal law came late. Only the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 banned oppressive child labor in the industries it covered.

Now the wives. The census counted 4.6 percent of married women as gainful workers in 1890. That sounds like almost none. [beat] It is badly wrong.

The economist Claudia Goldin found the gap. Married women were most of the people keeping boarders. They cooked for them and cleaned for them, for pay. And the census mostly missed them.

Here is her evidence. In Philadelphia in 1880, 14 percent of white households had boarders. But fewer than 1 percent of dwellings were recorded with a woman keeping a boardinghouse.

[pause]

Goldin estimated what a fuller count would show. For married white women in cities in 1890, the census found 4.0 percent. Counting the women who kept boarders would add another 5.9 to 7.4 points. [beat] That more than doubles it.

> Keeping boarders was paid work, and the census mostly missed it.

In plainer words, the wife's work was there. It was in the kitchen, not on the census form.

One figure misleads in the other direction. In 1910, the census recorded 10.7 percent of married women as workers, and in 1920, 9.0 percent. That looks like a surge and a retreat. [beat] It was neither.

That year, census takers had special instructions to record the occupations of women and children. So they likely counted many women, especially farm laborers, who would not have been counted in other years. The 1910 figure is an artifact, not a trend.

## The rent and the rule

So families had several earners, and the home was one of them. Now the rent itself.

First, a warning about the yardstick. This series compares eras by the price of a home in years of income. For 1900, that number cannot be computed. The Census Bureau's series on home values begins in 1940. Its national median family income begins in 1947.

> No one can honestly put a price-to-income number on 1900.

What we have instead are family budgets. In 1901, the federal Bureau of Labor recorded the spending of 2,567 families. They lived in the leading industrial centers of 33 states. Each was headed by a wage worker or a salaried man, earning no more than 1,200 dollars a year.

The biggest line was food. It took 42.5 percent of their spending. Rent took about 13 percent. Add payments on homes being bought, and fuel and lighting, and shelter came to about a fifth.

[beat]

Food, not housing, was the heavy line.

How could shelter take so modest a share? Because families bought very little of it, and they shared what they bought.

Riis reported that three-fourths of New York's people lived in tenements. And the housing scholar Richard Harris estimates that in 1900, about a quarter of American households had a lodger. Almost as many more took one in at some point.

Think back to the cloak-maker on Hester Street. A boarder paid about a third of his rent. That was ordinary arithmetic.

[pause]

But the buildings were shaped by law, and the law was failing. New York's tenement law of 1879, later called the old law, required a window in every room. The window could open onto the street, the yard, or an air shaft.

The air shafts gave the buildings a shape like a dumbbell. And they failed. They were often too narrow to light the lower floors. They collected garbage. And they carried fire.

The death records showed the cost. Riis printed the 1888 figures for one block, the Mulberry Bend. Deaths there ran at 35.75 per 1,000 residents. For the whole city, the rate was 26.27. And for children under five on that block, it was 139.83 per 1,000. [beat] About one young child in seven, every year.

Across the street stood a well-run model tenement. And there, Riis wrote, "the hearse called that year only twice."

[pause]

Then came the new law, the Tenement House Act of 1901. Its main framer was a reformer named Lawrence Veiller.

The law raised the requirements for light and air so far that a tenement on New York's standard lot, 25 feet wide, no longer paid. That effectively ended the dumbbell. The law also required one toilet in every apartment. New tenements typically rose six or seven stories around light courts, on lots 35 feet wide or more.

> The 1901 law priced the old dumbbell off the standard lot.

Other cities copied it, Chicago, Philadelphia and Boston among them. And in 1936, the planning authority James Ford called it "the most significant regulatory act in America's history of housing."

So rules on crowding reached the building. Did they also reach the budget? Let's test it.

A family pays nine and a half dollars a month for two rooms. A boarder pays about a third. Now the city starts enforcing a rule against lodgers in small apartments, and the boarder has to go.

What can the family do? And what does each choice cost? Think it through.

[long pause]

Each road costs something. The family can pay the full rent and cut food, already the biggest line. It can send a child to work. It can move to a worse room. Or it can move in with relatives, which is doubling up by another name.

The rule buys privacy only if one of those costs can be borne.

## The argument

That brings us to the argument. Start on ground that nobody owns.

Both sides accept the facts so far. Crowding was deadly in the worst blocks. Lodging was common. And the 1901 law raised the floor for new buildings.

Both also accept that lodging faded. In 1910, 20.6 percent of central-city households had a lodger. By 1940, 8.5 percent did, as war and quotas cut off immigration and rising incomes let more people keep a household of their own.

The question is what the rules did for the families they meant to protect. There are two serious answers.

The first is the reform case. It belongs to the reformers themselves: Jacob Riis, Lawrence Veiller, and the Chicago investigators Sophonisba Breckinridge and Edith Abbott. What they protect is life, health and privacy.

They argue that the tenement was a market failure that killed. Their strongest point is about the building. One family cannot buy light and air when its windows face a shaft the landlord will never widen. Only a rule binding every builder can do that.

They point to the death records: one young child in seven on the Mulberry Bend block, and two deaths in the model tenement across the street. And they point to what lodging meant. Breckinridge and Abbott found men lodgers sleeping in the same room as a household's young daughters.

> The reform case: one family cannot buy light and air alone.

Put another way, standards are how a society says that no one may be housed below a floor.

The second is the family-strategy case. Social historians such as John Modell and Tamara Hareven argue that taking in boarders evened out a family's income over its life cycle. The historians David and Linda Royster Beito argue that lodging was a strategy for getting ahead. What they protect is the bottom rung: the cheapest home a poor family could legally find.

They argue that every rule that raised quality also raised the price of that cheapest legal home. Their strongest point is about price.

They point to Detroit, where the historian Olivier Zunz found that 35 percent of unskilled workers owned homes in 1900, largely thanks to doubling up. And they point to Milwaukee, where new owners rented out part of the house to meet the mortgage, then took back the whole building. The Beitos conclude that building codes, zoning and density limits "sawed off the bottom rungs of the housing ladder."

> The family-strategy case: every rule raised the price of the cheapest home.

[pause]

So what does the evidence settle? It settles that crowding killed. And the studies of Detroit and Milwaukee show that lodging was often a temporary path to owning a home.

What it cannot settle is the road not taken. How many cheaper homes would have been built without the rules? And how would the people in them have fared?

No data can settle the value question underneath, either. How high should the floor be set, and who should pay to raise it? That is a contested question, not a settled one.

You may already lean one way. So take the side you find less convincing, and state the best case for it, the way its smartest defender would.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Before we gather it up, pull something back. Earlier I gave you three sources of a working family's income in 1901, beyond the husband's pay. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Children's earnings. Boarders and lodgers. And, least often, the wife's earnings. [beat] Remember the twist, though. The boarders were very often the wife's work, and the census mostly missed it.

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. When families left the farm, the household stopped producing and started earning. In the city, the home itself earned money.

[beat]

Two. Keeping boarders was paid work, and the census mostly missed it. The 4.6 percent for married women in 1890 is far too low, and the jump in 1910 is an artifact.

[beat]

Three. No one can honestly put a price-to-income number on 1900. Food was the heavy line in the budget, and shelter took a modest share because families shared it.

[beat]

Four. The 1901 law priced the old dumbbell off the standard lot. The reform case says one family cannot buy light and air alone. The family-strategy case says every rule raised the price of the cheapest home.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Did those rules help poor families on balance? Or did they raise the floor so high that the poorest were pushed off it?

And here is what carries forward. Some of this era will not come back. The move off the farm happened once. Immigration on that scale was cut off by war and quotas. And no serious side wants children's wages back.

But two levers still exist. Building codes still decide what the cheapest legal home must include. And the law still decides which kinds of homes are allowed.

In 1974, in a case called Belle Terre versus Boraas, the Supreme Court upheld a village rule. It limited a house to a family, or to no more than two unrelated people. Renting out a room, or adding a small unit in the backyard, is today's version of taking a boarder. It is legal in some places, and banned in others.

The next lecture follows the money. In 1920, only 40 percent of homes off the farm were mortgaged, and in the 1920s that debt tripled. So the next lecture asks how those mortgages worked, why so many failed in the Depression, and how the New Deal rebuilt the way Americans buy a home.

For now, go back to Bayard Street, just past midnight. Twelve people in a room not thirteen feet across. [beat] You know now who paid the rent in rooms like that, and why people still argue about the laws aimed at them.
