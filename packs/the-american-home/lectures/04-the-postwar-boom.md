Long Island, 1949. A line of former soldiers and their families, about 3,000 of them, waits to buy houses.

Jim and Virginia Tolley are in that line. They live in a three-room apartment in Queens. [beat] They leave having signed for a house that has not been built yet.

[pause]

That same year, another veteran sees the same newspaper advertisements. His name is Gene Burnett, and he drives out with his fiancée.

The salesman will not give him an application form. The agent tells him why. "The builders have not at this time decided to sell to Negroes."

[pause]

Two veterans. One war. One subdivision. [beat] Two answers.

Between 1940 and 1960, the share of American households that owned their home rose from 43.6 percent to 61.9 percent. It was the largest rise of the twentieth century.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to say what made that boom possible, and who was kept out of it. And you will be able to sort its causes into two piles: the ones that cannot happen again, and the ones policy still controls.

We will take it in four steps. First, the machine: the loans, the houses and the roads. Second, the families, and why they formed so young. Third, who was kept out, and how. And last, the argument over why it all happened.

## The machine

Start with the money. The last lecture built the first engine: the long, amortized mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration under the New Deal.

The war added a second engine. On June 22, 1944, days after D-Day, President Franklin Roosevelt signed the GI Bill.

One part of the law was about homes. The Veterans Administration would guarantee up to half of a veteran's home loan, to a limit of 2,000 dollars.

There were conditions. The loan could charge no more than 4 percent interest, and it had to be repaid within twenty years.

So what does a guarantee do? It is a promise that the government will cover part of the lender's loss if the borrower stops paying.

> A guarantee is a promise to the lender, not a gift to the borrower.

Think of a parent co-signing a first lease. The landlord accepts a tenant he would otherwise refuse, because someone solvent stands behind the debt.

[beat]

The picture breaks at scale. A parent co-signs for one child. Washington stood behind millions of loans, and it wrote their terms.

With the government carrying part of the risk, lenders made these loans with little down payment, and at one time with none. Taxpayers paid only when a loan failed, plus the first year's interest on the guaranteed share.

By 1955 there were 4.3 million of these home loans. And veterans had bought 20 percent of all the new homes built after the war.

[pause]

Cheap credit needs houses to buy. So builders learned to make houses the way factories made cars.

On potato farms about 30 miles east of New York City, Levitt and Sons built more than 17,000 houses between 1947 and 1951. The house stood still. Specialized crews moved through it, 27 steps in all.

William Levitt liked to call his firm the General Motors of housing. In 1949, his houses sold for 7,990 dollars. That was about two and a half times the median family income that year.

Then came the roads. In June 1956, President Dwight Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act. It expanded the interstate system to 41,000 miles.

Levittown came first, so the interstates did not start the suburbs. [beat] They carried them farther.

The economist Nathaniel Baum-Snow found that each new highway through a central city cut its population by about 18 percent. And inside the cities, in the words of the National Archives, the highways "cut through neighborhoods and destroyed housing, much of it in poorer sections of the city."

[pause]

Let's test the first idea. A state today wants to help first-time buyers, but it has little cash to spend up front. Which tool works most like that 1944 guarantee? A grant toward each buyer's down payment, or a promise to cover part of the lender's loss? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The promise. [beat] A guarantee costs nothing up front, and it pays only when a loan fails. It works by changing what lenders will accept. A grant hands money to buyers, which is a different tool.

## The families

That is the machine. Now the people who moved in.

The family side of the dream shows up in three of the measures this series tracks. Ownership, which we have heard already. Age at first marriage. And births.

In 1950, the median man first married at 22.8, and the median woman at 20.3. The low came in 1956, when the median man married at 22.5.

Births per woman climbed too. They stood at 2.30 in 1940. At the peak, in 1957, they reached 3.77.

The Census Bureau dates the baby boom from 1946 to 1964. In those years, births averaged just under 4 million a year. In the nineteen years before, they averaged 2.6 million.

[pause]

Now here is the turn. It is tempting to hear all that as the traditional American family. The longer record says otherwise.

In 1890, the median man first married at 26.1. In 1990, he married at 26.1 again. [beat] The 1950s were a dip, and the trend came back.

> The 1950s family was a swing away from the trend, not the old normal.

Put another way, the young marriages and big families of the 1950s were the exception. The decades on either side were the rule.

The same goes for the single paycheck. In 1950, fewer than one married woman in four was in the labor force. By 1960, almost one in three was. [beat] The single-earner household was common, and it was already giving way.

The historian Stephanie Coontz argues that the breadwinner and homemaker family of the 1950s was a new and unusual arrangement. Not the traditional one.

So why did so many couples marry and buy at once? Partly because of conditions that came together only once. Here are three.

One. Americans had saved heavily during the war. In 1944 they saved 27.9 percent of their after-tax income. In 1939, it had been 5.4 percent.

[beat]

Two. American factories came through the war untouched by the fighting. Western Europe, by contrast, needed more than 12 billion dollars in Marshall Plan aid to rebuild.

[beat]

Three. Pay gaps between workers narrowed sharply during the 1940s, and stayed narrow until about 1970. The economists Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo call it the Great Compression. They give the war and its wage controls some of the credit, and rising demand for less-skilled workers much of it.

[pause]

One more cause belongs on the list, but it is a different kind. Unions were at their height. In 1954, about one worker in three belonged to one. By 2022, about one in ten did. And law can strengthen or weaken unions.

Let's test that. A candidate says: "In 1955 a family could live on one income, so the economy was better for families then." What would you want to know before agreeing? Think it through.

[long pause]

Three things, at least. Whether that one income rested on conditions no law can recreate, like wartime savings and the Great Compression. Whether married women's paid work was already rising, and it was. [beat] And who was shut out of the era's loans and houses altogether.

That last question is our next stop.

## Who was kept out

Gene Burnett did not meet one prejudiced salesman. He met a system with four layers. Some were public, and some were private.

The first layer was the deed. A racial covenant is a clause that binds owners not to sell or rent to people of a named race.

In 1911, owners on one street in St. Louis signed a covenant. It barred occupancy "by any person not of the Caucasian race," for fifty years.

In 1945, a Black family, the Shelleys, bought a house on that street. The neighbors went to court to force them out.

In 1948, in a case called Shelley versus Kraemer, the Supreme Court ruled for the Shelleys. It held that "in granting judicial enforcement of the restrictive agreements in these cases, the States have denied petitioners the equal protection of the laws."

[pause]

Judicial enforcement. [beat] A court that enforces a covenant is the state acting, and the promise of equal protection binds the states.

But the Court drew a line. The covenants themselves, standing alone, broke no right under the Fourteenth Amendment.

> After Shelley, covenants were unenforceable, but not illegal to write.

In other words, a judge could no longer evict the Shelleys. But nothing stopped a developer from writing the clause into a deed. In 1953, in a case called Barrows versus Jackson, the Court closed one more door: no suits for damages either.

The second layer was federal practice. The FHA's own Underwriting Manual of 1938 held that "if a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial groups." And it recommended covenants.

From February 15, 1950, the FHA would not insure homes with newly recorded covenants. The VA adopted similar rules.

Did that change much? In 1961, the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights found that the change "has not had a great effect in securing equal housing opportunity."

Here is one comparison it reported. In 1956, 2.9 percent of homes with VA loans had a nonwhite head of household. [beat] Yet about 7.5 percent of the veterans of World War II and Korea were nonwhite.

[pause]

The third layer was the builder's own rule. Levitt's rental contracts of 1947 barred anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race." After Shelley, the exclusion went on as sales policy. That is what Gene Burnett met.

The fourth layer was the neighbors. In August 1957, William and Daisy Myers, a Black couple, bought a house in Levittown, Pennsylvania.

About 600 residents petitioned against them. Crowds gathered. Stones broke their picture window, and three crosses burned on the lawn, before the governor sent in the state police.

[pause]

Washington changed course late, and in part. In 1962, President John F. Kennedy signed an executive order on housing. It barred discrimination in homes financed with federally backed loans made from then on. For homes already financed, it asked agencies only for their "good offices."

Let's test the line the Court drew. It is 1952. A subdivision's deeds still carry a covenant from 1940 that bars Black owners. A white owner sells to a Black family, and a neighbor sues to undo the sale. What should the court do? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Refuse. [beat] The date of the covenant does not matter. A court enforcing it would be the state denying equal protection. The covenant could still sit in the deed, but no court would enforce it.

## Policy, circumstance or exclusion

So far, the facts. Now the argument.

Start with what nobody disputes. Ownership soared. Families formed young. And Black families were largely shut out of the new suburbs. [beat] The fight is over weights.

The best evidence cuts more than one way. The economist Daniel Fetter estimates that GI Bill loans explain 7.4 percent of the rise in ownership from 1940 to 1960. Mostly, he finds, they moved purchases earlier in life.

But by a rough extrapolation, he finds that the broader change in mortgage terms could explain 40 percent. And in other work, he finds that ownership rose about 10 percentage points during the war itself, while wartime rules severely restricted building.

[pause]

Three readings of the boom take that evidence in different directions. Each has its holders, a thing it protects, its evidence, and what would change its mind.

The first reading is built by policy. The historian Kenneth Jackson argues that federal mortgage and highway policy did much to make the suburbs. On this view, the boom was designed.

Its holders point to Fetter's 40 percent for mortgage terms, to Baum-Snow's finding on highways, and to veterans buying a fifth of new postwar homes. It protects the claim that policy can make ownership broad again. It would be moved by evidence that ownership rose as fast among people the programs did not reach.

> The policy view: the boom was designed, from the mortgage to the interstates.

The second reading is built by circumstance. Economic historians who stress conditions over programs argue that circumstances that could not last did most of the work. They draw on Goldin and Margo, and on Robert Gordon's argument that mid-century productivity came from a one-time wave of great inventions.

Its holders point to ownership rising during the war, before the postwar programs, to Fetter's 7.4 percent, and to wartime savings and ruined rivals. It protects against nostalgia, because copying 1950s policy would not bring back 1950s conditions. It would be moved by evidence that similar programs produced similar booms where those conditions were absent.

> The circumstance view: conditions that could not last did most of the work.

The third reading is built on exclusion. Richard Rothstein, in his book The Color of Law, argues that segregation was imposed by explicit government policy at every level. So, he argues, it was a constitutional wrong that calls for a remedy. Ira Katznelson argues that the GI Bill's reliance on local banks and offices let discrimination through.

Its holders point to the FHA manual, to VA loans reaching nonwhite households well under their share of veterans, and to a federal order that came only in 1962. It protects an honest account of who got to build wealth through the era's houses. It would be moved by evidence that private builders and neighbors alone would have excluded just as completely.

> The exclusion view: segregation was government policy, and so a constitutional wrong.

What can the evidence settle? The measures, the laws, the court holdings and the Commission's findings. What it cannot settle is how to weigh the causes. [beat] That is a contested question, not a settled one.

Three readings. You may already lean toward one of them. So take the one you find least convincing, and state the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

Whichever weights you give them, the causes can be sorted. Earlier I gave you three conditions that came together only once. Can you still name them?

[pause]

Wartime savings. Factories untouched by the fighting. And the Great Compression in pay. Add the baby boom, and open farmland close to big cities, much of it since built over. None of those can be summoned by a law.

Now the levers that still exist. Federal guarantees and insurance on mortgages, which the VA and FHA still offer. Spending on roads and transit that opens land to homes. Land-use rules that decide how many homes may go up on a tract. Labor law, which shapes how strong unions can be. And fair housing law, and its enforcement.

> Some causes were one-time conditions. Others are levers policy still holds.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A guarantee is a promise to the lender, not a gift to the borrower. The GI Bill used it to back millions of veterans' loans, while mass builders and new roads supplied the houses and the land.

[beat]

Two. The 1950s family was a swing away from the trend, not the old normal. Young marriages, big families and the single paycheck were the exception.

[beat]

Three. After Shelley, covenants were unenforceable, but not illegal to write. Exclusion went on through federal practice, builders' rules and neighbors.

[beat]

And four. Three readings compete: built by policy, built by circumstance, and built on exclusion. And some causes were one-time conditions, while others are levers policy still holds.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. How to weigh the causes. And how much of the exclusion came from government, and how much from private hands. Rothstein puts policy at the center. Thomas Sugrue, in his book The Origins of the Urban Crisis, gives great weight to white homeowners' own organized resistance, the force the Myers family met.

By 1960 the ground was already shifting. Married women's paid work was climbing, and the baby boom would end in 1964. The next lecture follows what came after, from 1970 to 2008: two earners, and two crashes.

For now, go back to that line on Long Island in 1949. The Tolleys, signing for a house that did not exist yet. And Gene Burnett, driving back without an application form. [beat] You know now what put the Tolleys in that line, and what kept Gene Burnett out of it.
