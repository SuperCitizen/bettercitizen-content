1973. A special committee of the Senate sets out to answer a question that nobody in government can answer. How many states of emergency is the United States under? And what do they permit?

[beat]

Its report opens with one of the great deadpan sentences in congressional history. "Since March 9, 1933, the United States has been in a state of declared national emergency."

[pause]

Not one emergency. Four, stacked and forgotten. Roosevelt's banking emergency of 1933. Truman's Korea emergency of 1950. And two declared by Nixon. Each was still technically alive.

The committee counted some 470 provisions of law that switched on whenever a president uttered the magic words. Powers to seize property, to organize industry, to restrict travel. No court had policed any of it. And Congress had no inventory of what it had given away.

Last time, the subject was the Constitution's silence on emergencies. That silence, it turned out, had been filled after all. Not by an emergency article. By four decades of accumulated statute, switched on and never off.

This lecture is about the machine Congress built to fix that, how its safety mechanism died within seven years, and what the wreckage looked like under stress, in 2025 and 2026. By the end, you should be able to walk an emergency through that machine, and to sort a president's emergency action into one of three categories.

Four stops. First, the machine, and how it broke. Second, a sanctions statute that became a tariff machine. Third, soldiers in the streets. And last, the framework inside which every one of these fights was argued.

## The machine, and how it broke

The machine is the National Emergencies Act of 1976. Start with what it did not do. It did not define the word "emergency." Hold on to that choice.

What it imposed was procedure. The old emergencies were terminated, and every future one would pass through four steps.

Declare. The president declares the emergency formally, and publishes it in the Federal Register.

Specify. The president names the statutory powers that the emergency switches on.

Renew. The emergency lapses after a year, unless the president renews it by notice.

Terminate. Congress can end it.

> Declare. Specify. Renew. Terminate.

The keystone was that last step. As written in 1976, Congress could end an emergency by a concurrent resolution: a vote of both chambers that never reaches the president's desk. So a majority could recall an emergency, and no president could veto the recall. That device is called a legislative veto.

And that was the bargain. Broad, undefined power to declare, up front, because a cheap recall waited at the back.

[pause]

The bargain lasted seven years. In 1983, in a case called INS v. Chadha, about one deportable foreign student, the Supreme Court held legislative vetoes unconstitutional across the board. When Congress alters the legal rights of people outside the legislature, the Court reasoned, that is legislation. And legislation takes both chambers, and then the president's desk.

At a stroke, the termination provisions of the National Emergencies Act were void, along with those of some two hundred other statutes.

Justice White, in dissent, predicted the consequence. The veto was the price of broad delegation. Strike the veto and leave the delegations standing, and the executive collects the winnings from both halves of a broken bargain.

Congress patched the Act in 1985. The recall became a joint resolution, which the president signs, or vetoes.

Let's run the arithmetic. A senator proposes to end a ten-year-old national emergency, and the sitting president supports that emergency. As the law has stood since 1985, what must happen? And how many votes does it realistically take? Think it through.

[long pause]

A joint resolution of termination must pass both chambers. Then it goes to the president, and he will veto it. Overriding that veto takes two-thirds of each chamber. That is 67 senators. And 290 representatives.

> A recall designed for a simple majority now takes two-thirds of both chambers.

In plainer words, ending an emergency over a president's objection now takes the margin it takes to convict on impeachment. Nobody voted for that allocation. It is the residue of a deportation case.

Now the data, because here the data are the argument. The Brennan Center for Justice keeps a running tally. As of mid-2026, it counts forty-eight national emergencies in effect.

The oldest was declared by Jimmy Carter in November 1979, to block Iranian assets during the hostage crisis. Every president of both parties has renewed it, for forty-six years.

> As of mid-2026: forty-eight emergencies in effect, the oldest from 1979.

The Act also commands each house to meet every six months to consider a vote on ending each emergency. That command has been honored almost entirely in the breach. And when Congress did vote, twice in 2019, to end an emergency at the southern border, the president vetoed both resolutions, and the overrides failed.

The drafters imagined emergencies as temporary by nature. Renewal by notice made them permanent by default.

## The sanctions statute and the tariffs

That is the machine. Now its first stress test. It begins with a statute passed a year after the National Emergencies Act: the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Everyone calls it IEEPA.

IEEPA holds the president's economic emergency powers toward the outside world. Its trigger is "any unusual and extraordinary threat" whose source lies, in whole or in substantial part, outside the United States. The president must declare a national emergency over that threat.

Its powers come as a string of verbs. The president may investigate, regulate, nullify, prevent or prohibit transactions. And, in two words that would one day carry a trade war, the president may regulate importation.

For five decades, IEEPA was the workhorse of sanctions. The freeze on Iranian assets. Libya. Terrorism designations after September 11. Russia after 2022. The overwhelming majority of those forty-eight emergencies exist to anchor sanctions programs under IEEPA. That is the statute working as intended: economic coercion, aimed outward, at named threats.

Then came 2025. In February, the administration used IEEPA emergencies to impose tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China. In April, it declared an emergency over trade deficits themselves, and imposed a global schedule of tariffs. No president had ever used IEEPA to impose a tariff.

Importers sued within days. A trade court ruled against the tariffs. An appeals court affirmed. And on February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed as well, six to three, in a case called Learning Resources v. Trump.

The majority opinion, by Chief Justice Roberts, is a holding about a statute, and its language repays exact quotation. "IEEPA contains no reference to tariffs or duties." The government had argued that those two words, regulate importation, include taxing imports at any rate, indefinitely. Roberts answered: "Those words cannot bear such weight."

Justice Kavanaugh, in dissent, argued that tariffs are a traditional instrument of import regulation. And the lineup, once again, scrambled the usual coalitions.

Now be precise about the edges, because the edges are the point. The Court did not hold tariffs unconstitutional. It did not touch tariffs that rest on express delegations from Congress, the kind that require an investigation and findings first. And it did not end the underlying emergencies. It held one thing: this statute never granted this power.

The force of the ruling showed within days, when collections stopped. A refund program opened in April 2026, to return on the order of 166 billion dollars, with interest.

Let's test those edges. It is the morning after the ruling. What remains lawful? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Two things. Tariffs under Congress's express delegations were untouched. And the emergency declarations themselves stayed formally in effect, because under the National Emergencies Act only the president, or a veto-proof Congress, can end an emergency.

> A court can strike the action. It cannot end the emergency.

That asymmetry is the architecture since Chadha, in one sentence.

So a sanctions statute had quietly become the broadest economic power any president ever claimed. And the correction did not come from the recall machinery, broken since 1983. It came from courts, reading two words.

## Soldiers in the streets

The second stress test was domestic, and it sits on a different shelf of statutes. There are three of them, and the task is to keep them apart.

The baseline is the Posse Comitatus Act of 1878. It is one sentence of criminal law, and its rule is this: federal military forces do not enforce civilian law, unless a statute expressly says otherwise.

The oldest and broadest statute that says otherwise is the Insurrection Act of 1807. Listen to its verbs of discretion. "Whenever the President considers" that obstruction or rebellion makes it impracticable to enforce the laws in the ordinary way, he may use the armed forces "as he considers necessary."

No definition of rebellion. No time limit. No congressional check. No required consent from the state. Read literally, the Act lets a president decide for himself when ordinary law has failed, and answer his own question with troops. It has been invoked roughly thirty times, most recently in 1992.

And here is what made 2025 interesting. The administration did not invoke it.

To federalize National Guard units over governors' objections, in Los Angeles, then in Portland and Chicago, it relied on a third statute: section 12406 of Title 10. Call it the Guard statute. It allows federalization on a list of conditions, and one of them is when "the President is unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States."

The litigation that followed is the most significant law on domestic deployment since Reconstruction. Take it in order.

First, an appeals court, the Ninth Circuit, held that judges may review the president's determination under the Guard statute, but only deferentially. It found the Los Angeles federalization likely lawful.

Then trial judges ruled against the government in three states. In California, a judge held that troops had been used for civilian law enforcement, in violation of the Posse Comitatus Act. In Oregon and in Illinois, judges blocked the deployments.

So the government went to the Supreme Court over Chicago. The answer came on December 23, 2025, in Trump v. Illinois: a brief order, six to three, denying a stay. "At this preliminary stage, the Government has failed to identify a source of authority that would allow the military to execute the laws in Illinois."

The majority took "the regular forces" to mean the regular federal military. So a president must actually need the Guard, because the regulars are insufficient for some lawful function. Preferring the Guard is not enough. Justice Alito, joined by Justice Thomas, dissented at length.

Within days, the administration announced its withdrawal from all three cities. The remaining federalized Guard demobilized in early January 2026.

Where does that leave the three statutes, as of mid-2026? Deployments under the Guard statute are reviewable: deferentially in the Ninth Circuit's formulation, with more bite in the Supreme Court's. The Posse Comitatus question from California remains unresolved on appeal. And the broadest and vaguest of the three?

> In 2025, the Insurrection Act was never invoked. So it was never tested.

The loaded statute is still on the shelf.

## Three categories

Every dispute you have just heard was argued inside one framework, and it was built in 1952.

President Truman, facing a steel strike during the Korean War, seized the steel mills by executive order, on inherent authority. The Supreme Court struck the seizure down, in a case called Youngstown. The opinion that lasted is a concurrence by Justice Jackson, the author of the Korematsu dissent. It sorts presidential action into three categories.

One. The president acts with the authorization of Congress, express or implied. Then, in Jackson's words, "his authority is at its maximum."

Two. Congress has neither granted the authority nor denied it. Then the president can rely only on his own independent powers, in what Jackson called "a zone of twilight."

Three. The president acts against the expressed or implied will of Congress. Then "his power is at its lowest ebb."

> With Congress: maximum. Congress silent: twilight. Against Congress: lowest ebb.

Now run this lecture's statutes through it. The IEEPA tariffs were defended as category one: express delegation, maximum power. The Court held that the delegation did not exist. And because Congress has occupied the tariff field with express, conditioned delegations, a tariff outside them slides toward category three.

The Guard deployments were category one claims as well, and the Court found the statutory condition unmet, at that preliminary stage. Even Lincoln's suspension of 1861 fits this grammar. It was a category two act, with Congress absent and the clause silent, promoted to category one when Congress ratified it in 1863.

So notice what the framework does. It does not decide cases. It allocates the burden of justification, which is why every advocate fights over the category before the merits.

Let's try one. Congress debates a bill that would authorize the seizure of refineries during a declared energy emergency, and declines to pass it. The president, citing the emergency and his inherent power, orders the seizure anyway. Which category? And does the declared emergency change it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Category three. The lowest ebb. Congress's considered refusal is an expressed will against the measure. These are nearly the Youngstown facts themselves, because Truman, too, acted after Congress had rejected seizure authority.

And the declaration changes nothing by itself. A declared emergency unlocks only the statutory powers that Congress attached to it.

> "Emergency" is not a category. Authorization is.

Jackson had an answer for the argument that emergencies imply their own powers. His answer was a suspicion: that "emergency powers would tend to kindle emergencies."

[pause]

One more test before we gather this up. Near the start of this lecture, I gave you four verbs: the machine of 1976. Can you still name them?

[long pause]

Declare. Specify. Renew. Terminate. And it is the fourth that broke.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The National Emergencies Act: declare, specify, renew, terminate. It never defined "emergency."

[beat]

Two. In 1983, Chadha struck down the legislative veto. A recall designed for a simple majority now takes two-thirds of both chambers. And the count stands at forty-eight emergencies, the oldest from 1979.

[beat]

Three. IEEPA is a sanctions statute. In 2026, the Supreme Court held that its power to regulate importation does not authorize tariffs. A court can strike the action. It cannot end the emergency.

[beat]

Four. Three statutes for troops at home. Posse Comitatus is the ban. The Insurrection Act is the broad exception, never invoked in 2025, and so never tested. Section 12406, the Guard statute, is the one that was used, and the courts gave its conditions an enforceable meaning.

[beat]

And five. Jackson's three categories. With Congress: maximum. Congress silent: twilight. Against Congress: lowest ebb. Emergency is not a category. Authorization is.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not settled whether the Ninth Circuit's deferential review, or the Supreme Court's harder-edged reading, will govern the next deployment. The Court resolved a stay, not the merits.

The Insurrection Act is not settled at all. And the three perspectives this series carries each read its absence their own way: as prudence, as deterrence by litigation, or as a near miss.

Then there is the largest question the data raise. Forty-eight standing emergencies. The oldest in its fifth decade. A review clause that is a dead letter. A recall that takes impeachment-scale majorities.

> The open question: a system that is broken, or one every branch finds convenient?

Choose the answer you find less convincing, and make its best case. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

That question belongs to the next lecture: the proposals for reform, the scholars who think reform is the wrong frame entirely, and the evidence from 2025 and 2026 that each side claims.

For now, go back to 1973, and that deadpan sentence. A committee found four forgotten emergencies, and Congress built a machine to end them. [beat] The count, as of mid-2026, is forty-eight.
