Two o'clock in the morning. May 25, 1861. Union soldiers walk into a farm estate north of Baltimore, and arrest its owner.

His name is John Merryman. He is a gentleman farmer. He is also a lieutenant in a secessionist cavalry company, one that has burned the railroad bridges linking Washington to the North.

The soldiers take him to Fort McHenry. [beat] No warrant. No charge.

Within a day, his lawyers reach the Chief Justice of the United States, Roger Taney. Taney issues a writ of habeas corpus: the ancient order that commands a jailer to produce his prisoner, and to justify the detention.

The fort's commander refuses, citing the president's authority. The marshal sent to hold him in contempt is turned away at the gate.

[pause]

Within the week, the Chief Justice has ruled the president's conduct unconstitutional. And the president has declined to obey.

Every American argument about emergency power since is, in some register, a continuation of that week.

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to argue that week from both sides, as law, and to say what the courts made of it in the wars that followed.

Four steps. First, the one sentence the Constitution gives to emergencies. Second, Lincoln against Taney. Third, four wartime cases. And last, three theories of what an emergency does to a constitution.

## One sentence

Begin with what the Constitution does not contain. There is no emergency article. No clause postpones elections, and none authorizes rule by decree. The word "emergency" does not appear at all.

The document has exactly one explicit emergency provision, known as the Suspension Clause. Here it is. Listen for what the sentence leaves out.

"The Privilege of the Writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, [beat] unless when in Cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it."

[pause]

Notice the form. It hands nobody a power.

> The Constitution has one explicit emergency provision. It is a limit, not a grant.

The whole fight lives in that sentence's grammar, and there are two fights in it.

The first is about who. "Shall not be suspended." [beat] By whom? The clause never says.

The strongest clue is where the sentence sits: in Article One, section nine, which is a list of restraints on Congress. So the power being limited looks like a power of Congress. But an inference is not a sentence that reads "Congress alone." Keep that gap in mind, because Lincoln's lawyers would live in it.

The second fight is about when. "Rebellion or Invasion." Those are words with edges: a pandemic is neither, and an economic collapse is neither. Yet the clause is silent on who decides whether a rebellion or an invasion exists.

Let's test those two fights. Suppose a devastating cyberattack cripples the power grid in six states, and the president suspends habeas corpus. Before any court has spoken, the clause itself supplies two objections. What are they? Take a moment.

[long pause]

First, the condition fails. A cyberattack, however catastrophic, is not clearly a rebellion or an invasion. The clause names two triggers, not a standard of severity.

Second, the actor is wrong. The clause sits among the limits on Congress, which implies that suspension is a power of Congress.

But hear the word "implies." The passive voice keeps that second objection an inference, and that is exactly why it has never been finally settled.

## Lincoln against Taney

That inference was Taney's, in 1861. So now, the clash itself.

Start with the ground nobody disputes. Virginia had seceded. Maryland was torn by riots, it was the only land route into Washington, and its bridges were ash. Congress stood adjourned, and had deliberately not yet been recalled. On April 27, Lincoln authorized General Winfield Scott to suspend the writ along the military line from Philadelphia to Washington.

A month later, Merryman was inside Fort McHenry, and two men gave opposite answers about the same clause. We will build each answer the same way: what he was protecting, what he argued, and what he pointed to.

Taney first. What Taney was protecting was a government of laws.

His argument was short and unhedged. The president, he wrote, "cannot suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, nor authorize a military officer to do it." The power belongs to the legislature. He pointed to English practice, where Parliament suspends and never the Crown, and to the clause's place among the limits on Congress.

Then Taney raised the stakes. Suppose military officers may arrest at their discretion, on a president's authority alone. Then, he wrote, "every citizen holds life, liberty and property at the will and pleasure of the army officer in whose military district he may happen to be found."

> Taney's case: the power to suspend belongs to Congress, not to the president.

Now Lincoln, built the same way. What Lincoln was protecting was the government itself.

His answer came on July 4, 1861, in a message to Congress. It holds two arguments, and it pays to keep them apart.

The first is the famous one: necessity. Suppose the suspension was illegal. Then, Lincoln asked, "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?"

[pause]

All the laws, but one. On this view, necessity is not a reading of the Constitution at all. It is a higher law, which now and then overrides it.

Then Lincoln stepped back from that ledge. "It was not believed that any law was violated."

That is his second argument, the legal one, and he pointed to the clause itself. The clause is silent about who suspends. It was plainly made for dangerous emergencies. And an emergency that arises while Congress stands adjourned cannot have been meant to wait for Congress.

> Lincoln's case: the clause never says who suspends, and the danger could not wait.

Now see what his two arguments do to each other. If the legal one works, the necessity one is surplus. Lincoln made both. That tells you he knew the legal argument might fail, and that he refused to rest on naked necessity alone.

Then he asked Congress to ratify what he had done, and he stood for reelection in the middle of the war. Congress delivered. The Habeas Corpus Act of 1863 authorized suspension for as long as the rebellion lasted, with strings attached.

So who won? [beat] Formally, nobody.

Lincoln never complied. Merryman was indicted for treason, bailed, and never tried. Taney's opinion was never appealed, never overruled, and never obeyed. The 1863 Act made the question moot without answering it, and the Supreme Court never has answered it.

Stay with those two men. Choose the one you find less convincing, and state his best case, the way he would have stated it. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## Four wartime cases

Now the wars that followed, in four cases. For each one: the name, what it held, and what later law did to it.

The first is Ex parte Milligan, from 1866. Lambdin Milligan, of Indiana, was tried by a military commission in 1864 and sentenced to hang. The Supreme Court freed him, and announced a sweeping rule. "Martial rule can never exist where the courts are open." Indiana's courts had been open the whole time.

Critics have long argued with that opinion, and they start with the year. 1866. The shooting had stopped. To announce that a crisis cannot suspend the Constitution, once the crisis has ended, is cheap in a way the same sentence would not have been in 1863.

The second case is Ex parte Quirin, from 1942. Eight Nazi saboteurs landed by U-boat, and one of them claimed American citizenship. Roosevelt ordered a secret military commission, and the Court, unanimously, let it proceed.

How, after Milligan? By drawing a line of status. Milligan had been no part of the enemy's armed forces. These men were enemy belligerents out of uniform, and so, the Court said, "subject to trial and punishment by military tribunals." Citizenship does not change the category. The line is not whether the courthouse doors are open. The line is who the prisoner is.

As doctrine, that is defensible. As process, it is a cautionary tale. The Court blessed the commission in one paragraph, after a rushed sitting in July, and delivered its reasoning in late October. By then, six of the eight had been electrocuted.

[pause]

The result first. The reasons after the executions.

The third case is the low point. Korematsu v. United States, 1944. By six votes to three, the Court upheld the exclusion of Japanese Americans from the West Coast, as a military necessity. That was the machinery that sent roughly 120,000 people to camps. Two-thirds of them were citizens.

The dissent to carry forward is Justice Jackson's, because it is about emergency power as such. A military order, he wrote, "is not apt to last longer than the military emergency." The lasting danger is the court that ratifies it. Once a judicial opinion rationalizes such an order, here is what follows, in his words.

"The principle then lies about like a loaded weapon, [beat] ready for the hand of any authority that can bring forward a plausible claim of an urgent need."

[pause]

A loaded weapon.

> Jackson's warning: a court that ratifies an emergency order leaves a loaded weapon behind.

Repudiation took decades. In 2018, in a case called Trump v. Hawaii, Chief Justice Roberts wrote that Korematsu "was gravely wrong the day it was decided."

But there is a complication. The same opinion upheld a different national-security order, the third travel ban, under deferential review.

The majority argued that the two cases were nothing alike: one a neutral entry policy, the other the forcible relocation of citizens to concentration camps, solely and explicitly on the basis of race.

Critics across the spectrum argued that the Court unloaded Jackson's weapon by name while demonstrating, in the same breath, the deference that loads it.

So did the Court retire the Korematsu principle, or only the citation? That is an open dispute, not a settled one.

The fourth case is Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, from 2004. Yaser Hamdi was an American citizen, captured in Afghanistan in 2001, who spent over two years in a Navy brig without charge.

The Court produced no majority opinion. Justice O'Connor, writing for four, held that the authorization of force that followed September 11 implied a power to detain battlefield captures, citizens included. But due process entitled a citizen detainee to notice, and to a hearing before a neutral decisionmaker. One sentence of hers became the era's epigraph: "a state of war is not a blank check for the President when it comes to the rights of the Nation's citizens."

Now the dissent, and the strangest alignment in these cases. Justice Scalia, the Court's arch-originalist, was joined by Justice Stevens, its senior liberal. Together they took the position most protective of the citizen. Try him for treason in an ordinary court, or persuade Congress to suspend the writ. There is no third box. If civil rights are to be curtailed in wartime, Scalia wrote, "it must be done openly and democratically, as the Constitution requires."

So both opinions reject a blank check, and yet in doctrine they are opposites. You have heard this exact disagreement once already in this lecture. Where? Think back.

[long pause]

Baltimore, 1861.

Scalia's dissent is Taney's position, surviving into the twenty-first century: detention like this is unlawful, unless Congress suspends the writ. O'Connor's plurality is Lincoln's second argument, domesticated into procedure: detention is lawful, if it is authorized and procedurally fair.

Put another way, the plurality manages emergency power, and the dissent insists the Constitution already allocated it.

That lineup tells you the question underneath is not left against right. It is who decides the exception.

And one pattern runs through these cases. Milligan came after the shooting stopped. Quirin's reasons came after the executions. Korematsu's repudiation came in 2018.

> The rhythm of the wartime cases: deference during the storm, doctrine after.

## Three theories of the exception

Step back from the cases. Three theories compete to explain them, and each gets the same treatment: who, the claim, and what the theory rests on.

First, Carl Schmitt, and his challenge. In 1922, he opened a book called Political Theology with the most quoted sentence in the field.

> Schmitt's challenge: "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception."

The claim is cold: no legal order can specify in advance the conditions of its own suspension. So whoever effectively decides that the emergency exists holds sovereignty, whatever the parchment says.

There is a standard cautionary footnote: Schmitt joined the Nazi Party. But biography does not refute analysis, and the American record gives his theory ammunition. Who actually decided, in May of 1861? [beat] Not the clause. A man.

Second, Clinton Rossiter, and his wager. In 1948, in a book called Constitutional Dictatorship, he accepted Schmitt's premise in order to defeat his conclusion. Crisis government is inevitable. So the task is to constitutionalize it, the way Rome did with its dictators: never begun by the person who wields it, and never outlasting the crisis.

His most quoted sentence shades into paradox. "No sacrifice is too great for our democracy, least of all the temporary sacrifice of democracy itself."

[pause]

Rossiter's theory rests on Lincoln, his star exhibit: the leader who seized dictatorial powers, and then submitted them to a sitting Congress, to hostile courts, and to a wartime election he expected to lose.

> Rossiter's wager: crisis government will come, so constitutionalize it, as Rome did.

Third, Bruce Ackerman, and his design. In 2004, in an article called "The Emergency Constitution," he accepted that both men are half right. Emergencies will be declared. And parchment alone will not hold.

His answer rests on mechanism. The legislature must reauthorize an emergency at short intervals, each time by a larger supermajority, so that continuing it becomes progressively harder instead of progressively normal. Where Rossiter trusts the republican character of the crisis leader, Ackerman trusts nobody, and rigs the incentives.

> Ackerman's design: each vote to continue an emergency must be harder than the last.

So which theory does the American record support? This much can be said.

Schmitt's story fits the opening days of American emergencies better than what came after: Congress ratifying with strings, elections going ahead, courts eventually biting. The pure legalist story, with law in command throughout, fits the ends better than the beginnings: Milligan, and the repudiation of Korematsu, are magnificent, and they are late.

Rossiter's uncomfortable middle is, as description, the most honest of the three. That is not the same as a safe prescription, as his own paradox shows.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The Constitution has one explicit emergency provision, the Suspension Clause, and it is a limit, not a grant. It leaves two questions open: who may suspend, and who decides that there is a rebellion or an invasion.

[beat]

Two. Taney's case: the power to suspend belongs to Congress, not to the president. Lincoln's case: the clause never says who suspends, and the danger could not wait. Formally, nobody won.

[beat]

Three. Four cases, four holdings. Milligan: no military trial of civilians where the courts are open. Quirin: enemy belligerents, citizens included, may be tried by military commission. Korematsu: a wartime exclusion by race, upheld six to three, and formally repudiated in 2018. Hamdi: citizen detainees get due process, and war is not a blank check.

[beat]

Four. Jackson's warning: a court that ratifies an emergency order leaves a loaded weapon behind. And the rhythm of the wartime cases: deference during the storm, doctrine after.

[beat]

And five. Three theories. Schmitt: sovereign is he who decides on the exception. Rossiter: crisis government will come, so constitutionalize it. Ackerman: make each vote to continue an emergency harder than the last.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. The Supreme Court has never held, on the merits, whether a president may suspend the writ when Congress cannot act. Nor is it settled whether Trump v. Hawaii unloaded Jackson's weapon.

And then there is that rhythm. Is it a failure of checks and balances? Or is it the only realistic form that judicial review of an emergency can take? That question splits the three perspectives this series carries: executive flexibility, legalist reform, and judicial engagement. The third lecture forces it.

The Constitution's silence on emergencies was filled, mostly in the 1970s, by an architecture of statutes. What that architecture is, and what happened to it in 2025 and 2026, is the next lecture.

For now, go back to the gate of Fort McHenry. A prisoner inside. A marshal turned away. A Chief Justice who has ruled, and a president who has declined to obey. [beat] The question at that gate was who decides. Nobody has closed it since.
