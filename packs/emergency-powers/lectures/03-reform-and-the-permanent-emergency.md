Hold two numbers side by side.

The first comes from Rome. Cincinnatus was handed the most absolute power Rome could confer, with a lawful term of six months. He resigned it, Livy says, on the sixteenth day. The emergency was over.

The second is American. The oldest national emergency of the United States was declared on November 14, 1979, over the seizure of an embassy in Tehran. It has outlived the Soviet Union, and every hostage it was declared about. In November 2025 it turned forty-six. And it was renewed again, on schedule, by a notice in the Federal Register.

[pause]

Sixteen days. Forty-six years.

Somewhere between those two numbers, "emergency" stopped meaning a state of exception, and started meaning a filing system.

Is that drift a civilizational warning? A fixable design defect? Or a harmless accounting convention? Those are three positions, and this lecture has to hold each one at full strength before it weighs them.

By the end, you should be able to state three programs the way their smartest advocates would. And you should be able to say what evidence, over the next decade, would count for each one and against it.

Four stops. First, the warning, in its strongest form. Second, the three programs. Third, one history run through all three lenses. And last, the evidence that would tell you who is right.

## The warning

Start with the warning. Its strongest statement comes from the Italian philosopher Giorgio Agamben, in a book from 2005 called State of Exception, written in the shadow of Guantánamo. Here is his claim.

"The state of exception tends increasingly to appear as the dominant paradigm of government in contemporary politics."

[pause]

In plainer words, the exception does not interrupt the legal order from outside. The legal order produces it, normalizes it, and makes it permanent, until emergency government quietly becomes government as such.

> Agamben's thesis: the exception is becoming the ordinary way of governing.

Set that beside what the last lecture counted, and the thesis has real purchase. Forty-eight standing emergencies. A review clause ignored for five decades. An "emergency" tariff regime that taxed most of world trade. And a military authorization from 2001, still alive a quarter-century later.

But the honest reading of 2025 and 2026 is hard on Agamben. His framework predicts that the exception, once invoked, escapes legal control, and that courts and legislatures become decoration.

What the record shows is litigation. The tariffs were struck down by three levels of courts, and the money was refunded. The troop deployments were halted by judges, up to a six to three order of the Supreme Court, and the troops went home.

A theory in which the sovereign decides the exception struggles to explain a sovereign who loses the exception in court, and complies.

His defenders have two answers. Compliance in low-stakes cases proves nothing about the cases that matter. And the declarations all survived their lost lawsuits, exactly as the thesis predicts.

Let's weigh it. From what you have just heard, what is the single strongest piece of evidence for Agamben's thesis? And the single strongest against it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

For: even after the Supreme Court held the tariffs unlawful, the underlying emergency declarations remained formally in effect. Ending them is left to the president, or to a veto-proof Congress. The exception survived its own legal defeat.

Against: the same season produced court-ordered refunds on the order of 166 billion dollars, and the federalized Guard demobilized within weeks of losing at the Supreme Court. The exception was litigated, lost, and obeyed.

> The thesis explains the declarations that never die. It strains to explain the compliance.

One caution about the tool itself. In 2020, Agamben turned this framework on pandemic health measures, to widespread criticism, even from sympathizers. A concept that explains everything explains nothing.

So keep the critique as a lens, not as a verdict.

## Three programs

That is the warning. Now three programs for answering it. Each is a real research program with serious people behind it, and each gets the same treatment: who holds it, what it protects, its argument in its own terms, and the evidence it points to.

First, executive flexibility. Its manifesto is a book from 2011, The Executive Unbound, by Eric Posner and Adrian Vermeule.

What this school protects is the capacity to act when a crisis comes.

Its argument is a description before it is a prescription. In a crisis, the Madisonian machinery of separated powers does not govern, and cannot. Congress is slow, plural, and poorly informed. Courts get the facts after the fact. Only the executive has the speed, the secrecy, and the organizational capacity that an emergency demands.

So what does discipline a modern executive? Not law, the school argues, but politics: elections, opinion, markets, a professional bureaucracy that leaks and resists, and the credibility a president must hoard to govern at all. It has a word, tyrannophobia, for the reflex that treats every energetic executive as a Caesar-in-waiting. On this view, that fear is itself a cost. It deters useful action, and it fails to stop abuse.

The evidence it points to begins with the wartime cases, which on this view are mostly theater after the fact. Milligan arrived after Appomattox. Quirin and Korematsu show what courts do when the pressure is real.

And it points to 2025 and 2026, without embarrassment. The tariffs were abandoned because markets, refund liabilities and electoral arithmetic made them unsustainable, so the court rulings ratified a political verdict already rendered. The administration folded on deployments after one adverse order, because the political price of defiance exceeded the value of the policy.

> The flexibility case: politics binds the executive. The statute never did.

If that is the mechanism, then sunset clauses and escalators are parchment.

Second, legalist reform. Its flagship is a bill called the Article One Act, led in each chamber of Congress by a Republican and a Democrat together.

What this school protects is Congress's say over whether an emergency continues.

Its argument begins by accepting the diagnosis. The National Emergencies Act is broken, the school argues, so fix the default. As the law has stood since 1985, it takes two-thirds of both chambers to stop an emergency. The Article One Act inverts that.

Every declared emergency would expire after 30 days, unless Congress affirmatively approves it, under expedited procedures that bar the filibuster and guarantee a vote. An approved emergency would last one year, and need fresh approval to renew. And a rejected emergency could not be declared again on the same facts, for the rest of that president's term.

So it would take simple majorities to continue an emergency, where it now takes two-thirds to stop one. Congressional inaction, the most reliable force in American politics, switches sides.

> The reform case: change the default, so that inaction ends an emergency.

Bruce Ackerman pushes the same logic further, with an escalating supermajority at each renewal. A majority first, then 60 percent, then 70, then 80. A permanent emergency becomes arithmetically impossible without a permanent consensus.

The evidence it points to is the flexibility school's own. The executive complied with adverse court orders in 2025 and 2026. That means legal form is doing real work, and a statute that changes the default changes outcomes. Rules that merely re-channel politics are still rules.

Third, judicial engagement. Its charter is a Supreme Court case from 1932, Sterling v. Constantin.

What this school protects is a ruling from outside the declaring branch, while it still matters.

Its argument is that the decisive variable is neither statutes nor politics. It is judicial nerve. Courts can police emergencies in real time, the school argues, when they choose to.

In Sterling, the governor of Texas had declared martial law to shut down oil wells. The Court, through Chief Justice Hughes, refused to step aside. "What are the allowable limits of military discretion, and whether or not they have been overstepped in a particular case, are judicial questions."

[pause]

Judicial questions. An emergency declaration does not validate itself.

> The engagement case: someone outside the declaring branch must rule while it matters.

The modern restatement came in 2008, in Boumediene v. Bush, when judicial review reached even an offshore wartime prison. "The laws and Constitution are designed to survive, and remain in force, in extraordinary times."

The evidence it points to is the school's best season in living memory. Three levels of courts struck down the tariff program, and the money came back. District judges in three states enjoined Guard deployments, the Supreme Court declined to rescue the one in Chicago, and the troops demobilized.

From that, the school draws a lesson in design. Do not bet on Congress finding majorities in a panic. Treat the conditions written into emergency statutes as real conditions, which a judge can enforce. Let states and injured parties sue fast. And let deference track the emergency's age: broad in week one, and narrowing as the executive's advantage in information decays.

[pause]

Now notice the structure of the disagreement.

Flexibility says constraint comes from politics, so legal design is froth. Reform says defaults decide what politics can do, so design is everything. Engagement says both depend on whether anyone can get a ruling while the emergency is alive, so jurisdiction is everything.

Those are not three answers to one question.

> Three schools hold three theories of where the binding constraint lives.

And that is why each school reads the same record, of 2025 and 2026, as vindication.

Now choose the school you find least convincing. State its case, the way its smartest advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## One history, three readings

Run one history through all three lenses.

On September 14, 2001, Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force: a single sentence, aimed at the attackers of September 11. A national emergency was declared as well. As of mid-2026 it remains in effect, renewed every year.

At home, that authorization meant military detention of citizens, military commissions, and an architecture of surveillance. It also met corrections. The Supreme Court held that the commission system violated the law, in the middle of the conflict, against a sitting commander in chief. And Congress recalibrated the surveillance in 2015, after a court of appeals held that one program exceeded its statute.

Now the three readings.

The flexibility school reads this as the system working as described. No statute forced the corrections. Political and institutional pressure recalibrated policy: elections, leaks, professional resistance, the opinion of allies. And the executive absorbed the courts' interventions, retooled, and continued.

The reform school reads it as the ratchet in motion. The authorization had no sunset, so no second vote was ever required, and a single sentence governed for twenty-five years. No design with a sunset would have permitted that.

The engagement school reads it as proof of concept. The Court's rulings came in the middle of a war, against executive protest, and they were obeyed. The judiciary policed an emergency in progress, and the sky did not fall.

> All three readings are accurate. They disagree about which fact bears the load.

## What would tell you who is right

So how would you ever know? A position you cannot falsify is a mood. Each school makes predictions, from mid-2026 forward, and each can be wounded.

First, pull something back. Each school put the binding constraint in a different place. Can you name the three places?

[long pause]

Politics. Defaults. And a ruling in time.

Now the tests, one school at a time.

For flexibility, watch whether compliance tracks political cost, or court orders. Retreats that come before the rulings, driven by markets and polls, would vindicate it. Compliance keyed tightly to court mandates would wound it.

For reform, watch the fate of the Article One Act, and the count of emergencies. Passage, followed by a falling count and real votes on renewal, would vindicate it. Passage followed by workarounds would wound it. So would a bill that never passes.

For engagement, watch the first genuine crisis to reach a court in its opening weeks. Review that holds under real panic would vindicate it. A second Quirin would wound it: rushed ratification first, and the reasoning later.

And where does the scoreboard stand, as of mid-2026? Three honest observations, one hard fact for each school.

For flexibility: withdrawal followed the Supreme Court's order, and refunds followed the mandate. That pattern is the most law-shaped data point in the modern emergency record. For now, it cuts against the strong form of the thesis.

For reform: the tariff episode handed Congress the most favorable conditions for reform in fifty years. And as of mid-2026, the Article One Act has still not reached the Senate floor. A remedy that is always available, and never enacted, must eventually explain why. And the explanation on offer, that members prefer presidential responsibility to recorded votes, is itself a point for the flexibility school.

For engagement: the record, however good, was compiled entirely in fair weather. No attack. No body count. No panic. And the school's own cases show courts performing worst when the danger is greatest. The storm test is still ahead.

Let's put those tests to work. It is 2029. A massive cyberattack, attributed within days to a foreign state, disables payment systems. The president declares an emergency, freezes assets broadly, and federalizes the Guard in twelve states. Three weeks pass. Courts have enjoined nothing. Markets have forced two policy reversals. Congress has scheduled no vote. Which school does that evidence support? Take a moment.

[long pause]

So far, executive flexibility. Politics is constraining, and law is dormant.

But the duration is the tell. The engagement school itself predicts deference in week one, and narrowing after that. So the verdict turns on what courts do in month two and beyond. Does the first court to reach the merits treat the conditions in the statutes as enforceable by a judge? Or as the president's to declare? One ruling, either way, would move more weight than everything before it.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Agamben's thesis: the exception is becoming the ordinary way of governing. It explains the declarations that never die. It strains to explain the compliance.

[beat]

Two. The flexibility case: politics binds the executive. The statute never did.

[beat]

Three. The reform case: change the default, so that inaction ends an emergency. Under the Article One Act, an emergency would expire after 30 days unless Congress approves it.

[beat]

Four. The engagement case: someone outside the declaring branch must rule while it matters.

[beat]

And five. Three schools hold three theories of where the binding constraint lives: politics, defaults, or a ruling in time. Each one can say what would count against it.

[pause]

Here is what this series has not settled.

It has not settled where the binding constraint lives. In 2025 and 2026, law, politics and courts all bound together, and there is no controlled experiment to separate them.

It has not settled whether Congress's half-century of leaving the Act unrepaired is dysfunction, or revealed preference.

It has not settled who may suspend the writ of habeas corpus when the catastrophe is real, and Congress cannot or will not act. That is the question of 1861, still unassigned.

And it has not settled Schmitt's challenge, that the sovereign is whoever decides the exception. The recent record shows exceptions litigated, narrowed, refunded and obeyed. But the sample is one administration, one configuration of courts, and one set of emergencies that never included a smoking city.

Jackson's loaded weapon has not been fired in this generation. It also has not been melted down.

So where does the argument go from here? To the docket. A defensible position in this debate names its school, states what the record would have to show to refute it, and then watches.

Sixteen days. Forty-six years. [beat] The liberties at stake between those two numbers are not abstractions. John Merryman could tell you what two o'clock in the morning sounds like.
