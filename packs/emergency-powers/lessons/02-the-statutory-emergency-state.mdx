# The statutory emergency state

In 1973, a special Senate committee chaired by Frank Church and Charles Mathias set out to answer a question nobody in government could: how many states of emergency was the United States under, and what did they permit? The answer, Senate Report 93-549, opens with one of the great deadpan sentences in congressional history: "Since March 9, 1933, the United States has been in a state of declared national emergency." Not one emergency — four, stacked and forgotten: Roosevelt's 1933 banking emergency, Truman's 1950 Korea emergency, and two Nixon declarations (the 1970 postal strike, the 1971 monetary crisis), each still technically alive. The committee counted some 470 statutory provisions that switched on whenever a President uttered the magic words — powers to seize property, organize industry, restrict travel, impose [[term:martial-law]]-adjacent controls. No court had policed any of it; Congress had no inventory of what it had given away. The Constitution's emergency silence, it turned out, had been filled after all — not by an emergency article but by four decades of accumulated statute, switched on and never off. This lesson is about the machine Congress built to fix that, how its safety mechanism died within seven years, and what the wreckage looked like when it was stress-tested in 2025 and 2026.

## The machine: declare, specify, renew, terminate

The National Emergencies Act of 1976 did not define "emergency" — hold onto that choice. It imposed *procedure* instead. Existing emergencies were terminated. Future ones must be declared formally and published in the Federal Register; the President must specify which statutory powers the emergency activates; the emergency lapses after a year unless renewed by notice; and Congress can end it. The promise: with the standing emergencies cleared and a sunset-plus-recall installed, emergency power would become what Rome's had been — exceptional, bounded, supervised.

The keystone was that last step: the **legislative veto**. A concurrent resolution never reaches the President's desk, so Congress could recall an emergency by majority vote, full stop. That was the bargain — broad, undefined declaration authority up front *because* a cheap recall waited at the back.

## Chadha breaks the machine

In *INS v. Chadha* (1983), a case about one deportable foreign student, the Supreme Court held legislative vetoes unconstitutional across the board: congressional action that alters the legal rights of people outside the legislature *is* legislation, and legislation requires bicameralism *and presentment* — both chambers, then the President's desk. At a stroke, the termination provisions of the NEA and some two hundred other statutes were void. Justice White's dissent predicted the consequence: the veto was the price of broad delegation, and striking the veto while leaving the delegations intact would simply hand the executive the winnings from both halves of a broken bargain.

Congress patched the NEA in 1985 by converting the concurrent resolution to a joint resolution — which the President signs or vetoes. Run the arithmetic. To end an emergency the President wants to keep, Congress must pass a termination resolution *and override a veto*: two-thirds of both chambers, against a President defending his own declaration. A recall designed as a majority-vote safety valve now demands the same [[term:supermajority]] as conviction on impeachment. Nobody voted for that allocation; it is the residue of a deportation case.

The data are the argument. As of the Brennan Center's running tally — last updated May 26, 2026 — **forty-eight** national emergencies are in effect. The oldest was declared by Jimmy Carter on November 14, 1979, blocking Iranian assets during the hostage crisis; every president of both parties has renewed it, for forty-six years. The six-month reviews essentially never occur. When termination resolutions have passed — Congress voted twice in 2019 to end the southern-border emergency funding wall construction — the President vetoed them and the overrides failed. In 2025 the Senate passed resolutions against tariff-bearing emergencies; House leadership kept them off the floor by procedural device. And the completing detail: even after the Supreme Court held the tariffs themselves unlawful in February 2026, the underlying *declarations* remained formally in effect — courts can strike an action taken under an emergency, but under the NEA only the President or a veto-proof Congress can end the emergency itself.

  A **joint resolution** of termination must pass both chambers and then survive the President's desk — and he will veto it. Overriding requires **two-thirds of each chamber**: 67 senators, 290 representatives. Congress granted the declaration power in 1976 on the understanding it could be recalled by simple majorities; the recall now requires impeachment-scale margins. That gap between the bargain as struck and as it operates is Chadha's orphan.

## IEEPA: the sanctions statute that became a tariff machine

A year after the NEA, Congress carved the President's international economic emergency powers out of the cavernous Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917 and re-housed them in the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — narrower on paper, gated by NEA procedures. The trigger, 50 U.S.C. §1701: the powers may be used "to deal with any unusual and extraordinary threat, which has its source in whole or substantial part outside the United States, to the national security, foreign policy, or economy of the United States, if the President declares a national emergency with respect to such threat." The powers, §1702: the President may "investigate," "regulate," "direct and compel," "nullify," "prevent or prohibit" transactions — and, in the two words that would carry a trade war, "regulate … importation."

For five decades IEEPA was the workhorse of [[term:sanctions]]: the Iranian asset freeze (upheld in *Dames & Moore v. Regan* (1981), which leaned on congressional acquiescence — Youngstown's first category, as we will see), Libya, terrorism designations after [[term:september-11]], Russia after 2022. The Brennan data show the consequence: the overwhelming majority of the forty-eight active emergencies exist to anchor IEEPA sanctions programs. That is the statute working as intended — economic coercion aimed outward at named threats.

Then 2025. In February, the administration imposed tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China under fentanyl- and border-related IEEPA emergencies; on April 2 it declared an emergency over trade deficits themselves and imposed a global "reciprocal" [[term:tariff]] schedule. No president had ever used IEEPA to impose tariffs — the closest precedent was Nixon's 1971 import surcharge under the *old* 1917 Act. Importers sued within days, and the litigation became the separation-of-powers test of the era. Verified and in order: the Court of International Trade granted summary judgment against the tariffs on May 28, 2025 (*V.O.S. Selections*); the en banc Federal Circuit affirmed on August 29, 2025; and the Supreme Court, taking the consolidated cases, affirmed 6–3 on February 20, 2026.

Chief Justice Roberts's majority opinion in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* — joined, in another coalition-scrambling lineup, by Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett, and Jackson — is a statutory holding whose language repays exact quotation. "IEEPA contains no reference to tariffs or duties." "When Congress has delegated its tariff powers, it has done so in explicit terms, and subject to strict limits." And of the government's reading — that "regulate … importation" includes taxing imports at any rate, indefinitely — "Those words cannot bear such weight." Three of the six (Roberts, Gorsuch, Barrett) added that a claim of this magnitude triggers the major-questions doctrine and that the President holds no inherent peacetime tariff power; Kagan, for three, thought ordinary statutory reading sufficed. Kavanaugh's dissent, joined by Thomas and Alito, argued tariffs are a traditional instrument of import regulation.

Be precise about the edges, because they are the lesson. The Court did *not* hold tariffs unconstitutional; did not touch tariffs resting on express delegations — Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, with their investigation-and-findings prerequisites, survived; and did not end the underlying emergencies. It held that *this statute never granted this power*. The aftermath shows the ruling's force and its limits: collections stopped within days; the government's refund program opened in April 2026 to return on the order of $166 billion to more than 330,000 importers, with interest; and the administration promptly re-imposed a smaller global tariff under Section 122 — the 150-day balance-of-payments provision — which the Court of International Trade ruled unlawful on May 7, 2026, for want of the statutory predicate. As of mid-2026 the replacement litigation continues, and trade policy is migrating back to the narrow statutes. The foreign-policy pack tracks the trade consequences; what matters *here* is structural: a sanctions statute had quietly become the broadest economic power any president ever claimed, and the correction came not from the NEA's recall machinery — broken since 1983 — but from courts reading two words.

## Soldiers in the streets: the Insurrection Act, Posse Comitatus, and §12406

The second stress test of 2025–26 was domestic, on a different shelf of statutes. The baseline rule is the Posse Comitatus Act of 1878 — Reconstruction's procedural residue — one sentence of criminal law: "Whoever, except in cases and under circumstances expressly authorized by the Constitution or Act of Congress, willfully uses any part of the Army, the Navy, the Marine Corps, the Air Force, or the Space Force as a posse comitatus or otherwise to execute the laws shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than two years, or both." Federal military forces do not enforce civilian law — *unless a statute expressly says otherwise*.

The oldest and broadest such statute is the Insurrection Act of 1807, now 10 U.S.C. §§251–255. Its central provision, §252, deserves full quotation precisely for how much it leaves to one person's judgment: "Whenever the President considers that unlawful obstructions, combinations, or assemblages, or rebellion against the authority of the United States, make it impracticable to enforce the laws of the United States in any State by the ordinary course of judicial proceedings, he may call into Federal service such of the militia of any State, and use such of the armed forces, as he considers necessary to enforce those laws or to suppress the rebellion." Read the verbs of discretion: *whenever the President considers* … *as he considers necessary*. No definition of rebellion, no time limit, no congressional check, no required state consent (§253 reaches further still, to violence that obstructs federal law where a state fails or refuses to protect rights). Invoked roughly thirty times — Eisenhower at Little Rock over a governor's objection in 1957; last used at California's request in the 1992 Los Angeles riots — the Act is the field's standing puzzle: a statute that, read literally, lets a President decide for himself when ordinary law has failed, and answer his own question with troops.

What made 2025 doctrinally interesting is that the administration *didn't* invoke it. To federalize National Guard units over governors' objections — Los Angeles in June 2025, then Portland and Chicago that fall — it relied on 10 U.S.C. §12406, which allows federalization upon invasion or its danger, "a rebellion or danger of a rebellion," or when "the President is unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States," with orders issued "through the governors." The resulting litigation is the most significant domestic-deployment law since Reconstruction. Its verified course: in *Newsom v. Trump*, a Ninth Circuit panel held on June 19, 2025 that the §12406 determination is judicially *reviewable* — rejecting the government's unreviewability claim — but only deferentially, and that the Los Angeles federalization was likely lawful; en banc rehearing was denied October 22. After a bench trial, Judge Charles Breyer held on September 2, 2025 that troops in Los Angeles had been used for civilian law enforcement in violation of the Posse Comitatus Act, and enjoined such use across California — an injunction the Ninth Circuit administratively stayed pending appeal. In Oregon, Judge Karin Immergut blocked the Portland deployment in October (the en banc Ninth Circuit vacating a panel stay that had briefly cleared it) and entered a permanent injunction after trial on November 7, 2025. In Illinois, Judge April Perry blocked the Chicago deployment, and the government went to the Supreme Court.

The answer came December 23, 2025 — a brief per curiam in *Trump v. Illinois*, 6–3, denying a stay: "At this preliminary stage, the Government has failed to identify a source of authority that would allow the military to execute the laws in Illinois." The majority read §12406(3)'s "unable with the regular forces" to refer to the regular *federal military* — the President must actually need Guard forces because regulars are insufficient for some lawful function, not merely prefer them. Justice Alito, joined by Thomas, dissented at length; Justice Gorsuch — in the tariff majority two months later — would also have granted the stay. Within days the administration announced withdrawal from Chicago, Los Angeles, and Portland; the remaining federalized Guard demobilized in early January 2026; the government dropped its Oregon appeal on February 6, 2026, leaving the permanent injunction standing; and on April 20, 2026 Judge Perry dismissed the Illinois case as moot. The mid-2026 scoreboard, then: §12406 deployments are reviewable (deferentially in the Ninth Circuit's formulation, with more bite in the Supreme Court's); the "unable … to execute the laws" predicate has a judicially enforced meaning; the California Posse Comitatus question remains unresolved on appeal; and the Insurrection Act itself — broader and vaguer — was never invoked, so never tested. The loaded statute is still on the shelf.

One adjacent shelf, briefly: the Stafford Act of 1988 governs *disaster* declarations — the routine, governor-requested kind that unlock FEMA assistance dozens of times a year. It is emergency law's benign, consensual face, and a reminder that most emergency governance is unremarkable. The fights in this pack are about the other shelves.

## Youngstown: the operating system

Every dispute above was argued inside a framework built in 1952. Truman, facing a steel strike during the Korean War, seized the mills by [[term:executive-order]] on inherent authority; the Court struck the seizure down in *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*. Justice Black's majority was rigidly formalist, but Justice Jackson's concurrence — by the author of the *Korematsu* dissent, himself a former Attorney General who had written expansive opinions for FDR — became the canonical framework. Quote it in full, because everything in this pack runs on it:

> **In the primary sources:** "1. When the President acts pursuant to an express or implied authorization of Congress, his authority is at its maximum, for it includes all that he possesses in his own right plus all that Congress can delegate. … 2. When the President acts in absence of either a congressional grant or denial of authority, he can only rely upon his own independent powers, but there is a zone of twilight in which he and Congress may have concurrent authority, or in which its distribution is uncertain. … 3. When the President takes measures incompatible with the expressed or implied will of Congress, his power is at its lowest ebb, for then he can rely only upon his own constitutional powers minus any constitutional powers of Congress over the matter." — Jackson, J., concurring, *Youngstown* (1952)

Now run the lesson's statutes through it. The IEEPA tariffs were defended as **category one** — express delegation, maximum power. *Learning Resources* held the delegation didn't exist: "regulate … importation" never included taxing. And because Congress has occupied the tariff field with express, conditioned delegations (232, 301, 122), a tariff outside them isn't stranded in twilight; it slides toward **category three**, where a plurality added there is no inherent peacetime tariff power to subtract from. The §12406 deployments were likewise category-one claims — and *Trump v. Illinois* found the statutory predicate unmet "at this preliminary stage," while the Posse Comitatus Act stands as an express congressional *prohibition* pushing domestic law-enforcement use of troops, absent the Insurrection Act, to the lowest ebb. Lincoln's 1861 suspension, in this grammar, was a **category two** act — Congress absent, the clause silent — promoted to category one by the 1863 ratification. The framework does not decide cases; it *allocates the burden of justification*, which is why every advocate fights over the category before the merits.

Jackson's concurrence also contains the passage that makes him this pack's presiding spirit — his answer to the argument that emergencies imply their own powers: "They knew what emergencies were, knew the pressures they engender for authoritative action, knew, too, how they afford a ready pretext for usurpation. We may also suspect that they suspected that emergency powers would tend to kindle emergencies." And his credo: "With all its defects, delays and inconveniences, men have discovered no technique for long preserving free government except that the Executive be under the law, and that the law be made by parliamentary deliberations."

<Checkpoint question="Congress debates and declines to pass a bill authorizing seizure of refineries during a declared energy emergency.   **Category three — lowest ebb.** Congress's considered refusal is an "expressed or implied will" against the measure, so the President relies on Article II *minus* Congress's powers over commerce and property — nearly the Youngstown facts themselves (Truman, too, acted after Congress had rejected seizure authority). The NEA declaration changes nothing by itself: a declared emergency unlocks only the statutory powers Congress attached to it. Jackson's whole point is that "emergency" is not a category; authorization is.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson does not settle

It does not settle whether the Ninth Circuit's deferential review of §12406 or the harder-edged reading in the *Trump v. Illinois* per curiam will govern the next deployment — the Court resolved a stay posture, not the merits, and the California Posse Comitatus appeal was still open when the troops went home. It does not settle the Insurrection Act at all: its self-judging predicates have never received a modern construction, because the administration best positioned to invoke it chose adjacent authority instead — a fact each of this pack's perspectives reads its own way (prudence, deterrence-by-litigation, or a near miss). It does not settle whether IEEPA's sanctions core — untouched by *Learning Resources* — could survive the nondelegation scrutiny three justices hinted at. And it leaves open the largest question the data raise: whether forty-eight standing emergencies, an oldest entry in its fifth decade, a dead-letter review clause, and a recall requiring impeachment-scale majorities describe a system that is *broken* — or one that every branch, Congress included, finds convenient to leave exactly as it is. The reform proposals, the scholars who think reform is the wrong frame entirely, and the 2025–26 evidence each side claims — that is Lesson 3.
