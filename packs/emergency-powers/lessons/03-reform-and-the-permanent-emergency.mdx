# Reform and the permanent emergency

Hold two numbers side by side. Cincinnatus, handed the most absolute power Rome could confer, with a lawful term of six months, resigned it — Livy says — on the sixteenth day, the emergency being over. The United States' oldest national emergency, declared on November 14, 1979 over the seizure of an embassy in Tehran, turned forty-six in November 2025; it has outlived the Soviet Union, ten presidencies, and every hostage it was declared about, and it was renewed again, on schedule, by notice in the Federal Register. Sixteen days; forty-six years. Somewhere between those two numbers, "emergency" stopped meaning a state of exception and started meaning a filing system. Whether that drift is a civilizational warning, a fixable design defect, or a harmless accounting convention — those are, respectively, the three positions this lesson must hold at full strength before weighing them.

## The permanent-emergency critique

The strongest statement of the warning reading comes from the Italian philosopher Giorgio Agamben, whose *State of Exception* (2005) — written in the shadow of Guantánamo — generalized Carl Schmitt into a theory of modern government: "the state of exception tends increasingly to appear as the dominant paradigm of government in contemporary politics." On Agamben's account, the exception does not interrupt the legal order from outside; it is *produced* by the legal order, normalized, and made permanent, until emergency governance quietly becomes governance as such. Detention without trial at Guantánamo was his exhibit; the camps of the twentieth century were his horizon.

Read against Lesson 2's data, the thesis has real purchase. Forty-eight standing emergencies. A review clause ignored for five decades. An "emergency" tariff regime taxing most of world trade. A post-[[term:september-11]] military authorization — the 2001 AUMF — still alive a quarter-century later. The ratchet Madison predicted in Federalist 41 ("every precedent of which is a germ") is observable in the Federal Register.

But scholar's duty cuts both ways, and the honest reading of 2025–26 is hard on Agamben. His framework predicts that the exception, once invoked, escapes legal control — that courts and legislatures become decoration. What the record shows is litigation: the tariffs were struck down by three levels of courts and the money refunded; the troop deployments were halted by district judges, an appellate court, and a 6–3 Supreme Court order, and the troops went home; the government *dropped its appeal* in Oregon rather than press the claim. A theory in which the sovereign decides the exception struggles to explain a sovereign who loses the exception in court and complies. Agamben's defenders answer that compliance in low-stakes cases proves nothing about the cases that matter — and that the *declarations* all survived their lost lawsuits, exactly as the permanent-exception thesis predicts. Note, too, that Agamben himself showed the framework's brittleness when he deployed it against pandemic health measures in 2020, to widespread criticism even from sympathizers: a theory that classifies every inconvenient emergency as creeping totalitarianism has stopped discriminating, and a concept that explains everything explains nothing. Keep the critique as a lens, not a verdict.

## Three programs, at full strength

Each tab below is a real research program with serious people behind it. Read each as its smartest advocate would write it — the contested-pack discipline — and notice that each one *concedes* something the others build on.

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Executive flexibility">
    Eric Posner and Adrian Vermeule's *The Executive Unbound: After the Madisonian Republic* (2011) is the position's manifesto, and its core claim is descriptive before it is normative: in crises, the Madisonian machinery of [[term:separation-of-powers]] does not and cannot govern. Congress is slow, plural, and poorly informed; courts get the facts after the fact; only the executive has the speed, secrecy, and organizational capacity that emergencies demand. The legalist's cherished frameworks, on this view, are mostly *ex post* theater — Lesson 1's own canon is the evidence: *Milligan* arrived after Appomattox, *Youngstown* was the rare wartime defeat of a weak president, *Quirin* and *Korematsu* show what courts do when the pressure is real. What actually disciplines the modern executive is not law but politics: elections, opinion, markets, a professionalized bureaucracy that leaks and resists, credibility the president must hoard to govern at all. Posner and Vermeule coined "tyrannophobia" for the reflex that treats every energetic executive as a Caesar-in-waiting — a fear, they argue, that is itself a cost, deterring useful action while failing to stop abuse. Their reading of 2025–26 is not embarrassed: the tariffs were abandoned because markets, refund liabilities, and electoral arithmetic made them unsustainable — the court rulings ratified a political verdict already rendered; and the administration folded on deployments after one adverse stay order because the political price of defiance exceeded the policy's value, which is precisely how political constraint works. William Howell's *Power Without Persuasion* (2003) supplies the political-science foundation: presidents act unilaterally where the other branches' collective-action problems leave space, and retreat where they don't. If that is the mechanism, then sunset clauses and escalators are parchment — the binding constraint was never the statute.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Legalist reform">
    The reform school's answer begins by accepting the diagnosis: of course the NEA is broken — *so fix the default*. The ARTICLE ONE Act, the flagship proposal, is bipartisan in a way almost nothing else is: led in the Senate by Mike Lee (R-UT) and Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) and in the House by Chip Roy (R-TX) and Steve Cohen (D-TN), approved by the Senate Homeland Security Committee on a lopsided bipartisan vote in 2019, and reintroduced in successive Congresses since — most recently in the 119th (2025) — without ever receiving a Senate floor vote as of mid-2026. Its mechanism is a clean inversion of Chadha's orphan: every declared emergency **expires after 30 days unless Congress affirmatively approves it** by joint resolution, under expedited procedures that bar the [[term:filibuster]] and guarantee a vote; approved emergencies last one year and require fresh approval to renew; and a rejected emergency cannot be redeclared on the same facts for the rest of the president's term. Where the post-1985 NEA requires two-thirds of both chambers to *stop* an emergency, ARTICLE ONE requires simple majorities to *continue* one — congressional inaction, the most reliable force in American politics, switches sides. Bruce Ackerman's "The Emergency Constitution" (2004) pushes the same logic further: reauthorization at short intervals by an escalating [[term:supermajority]] — a majority first, then 60 percent, then 70, then 80 — so that keeping an emergency becomes progressively harder and a permanent one becomes arithmetically impossible without a permanent consensus. The broader Brennan Center agenda adds repairs Lesson 2 showed to be live: defining the Insurrection Act's predicates and adding time limits and review, giving the Posse Comitatus Act enforcement teeth, and ending the anomaly by which a court can void an emergency's actions while the declaration itself survives. To the flexibility school's taunt — parchment — the reformers answer with the school's own evidence: the executive *complied* with adverse court orders in 2025–26, which means legal form is doing real work, and a statute that changes the default changes outcomes. Rules that merely re-channel politics are still rules; that is what the 1863 Habeas Corpus Act was.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Judicial engagement">
    The third school holds that the decisive variable is neither statutes nor politics but judicial nerve — and that the cases prove courts *can* police emergencies in real time when they choose to. The tradition's charter is *Sterling v. Constantin* (1932), where the governor of Texas declared martial law to shut down oil wells and the Supreme Court, through Chief Justice Hughes, refused the political-question escape: "What are the allowable limits of military discretion, and whether or not they have been overstepped in a particular case, are judicial questions." If the executive's say-so could end the inquiry, Hughes wrote, "it is manifest that the fiat of a state Governor, and not the Constitution of the United States, would be the supreme law of the land." That sentence is the whole program: emergency declarations are not self-validating, and someone outside the declaring branch must be able to say so *while it matters*. Its modern restatement is *Boumediene v. Bush* (2008) — [[term:judicial-review]] reaching even an offshore wartime prison: "The laws and Constitution are designed to survive, and remain in force, in extraordinary times." And 2025–26 is the school's best season in living memory, every item verified in Lesson 2: a specialized trade court, an en banc circuit, and the Supreme Court struck down the tariff program (Feb. 20, 2026) and the money came back; district judges in three states enjoined Guard deployments, the Supreme Court declined to rescue the Chicago deployment ("the Government has failed to identify a source of authority"), and the troops demobilized; the Oregon permanent injunction stands because the government abandoned the appeal. Engagement's advocates draw the design lesson: don't bet on Congress finding majorities in a panic — harden *justiciability*. Statutory predicates ("unable," "impracticable," "unusual and extraordinary threat") should be construed as real, judicially enforceable conditions, standing doctrines should let states and injured parties sue fast, and deference should track the emergency's age — broad in week one, narrowing as the executive's informational advantage decays. The school's honest concession: its own canon shows courts performing worst precisely when the danger is greatest (*Quirin*, *Korematsu*), and 2025–26 involved no attack, no body count, no panic. Engagement has now been proven in fair weather. The storm test is still ahead.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Notice the structure of the disagreement. Flexibility says constraint comes from politics, so legal design is froth. Reform says defaults decide what politics can do, so design is everything. Engagement says both depend on whether anyone can get a ruling while the emergency is alive, so jurisdiction is everything. These are not three answers to one question; they are three theories of *where the binding constraint lives* — which is why each school reads the same 2025–26 record as vindication.

## The case study: September 11 and the quarter-century authorization

Run one arc through all three lenses. On September 14, 2001, Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force — a single sentence aimed at the September 11 attackers — and a national emergency was declared that remains in effect, renewed annually, as of mid-2026. The AUMF's career abroad — stretched across organizations and continents, surviving the repeal of the separate Iraq authorizations in December 2025 — belongs to the foreign-policy pack, along with the [[term:war-powers-act]] compliance record. Its domestic face belongs here: military detention of citizens (*Hamdi*), military commissions (*Hamdan v. Rumsfeld* (2006), where the Court held the commission system violated the Uniform Code of Military Justice and the Geneva Conventions' Common Article 3 — mid-conflict, against a sitting commander-in-chief), offshore habeas (*Boumediene*), and a surveillance architecture recalibrated by Congress in 2015 after a court of appeals held the bulk telephony program exceeded its statute.

The flexibility school reads the arc as the system working as described: no statute forced the corrections; political and institutional pressure — elections, leaks, professional resistance, allied opinion — recalibrated policy, and the courts' interventions (*Hamdan*, *Boumediene*) were absorbed by an executive that retooled and continued. The reform school reads the same arc as the ratchet in motion: because the AUMF had no sunset, no re-vote was ever required, and a one-week-old Congress's single sentence governed for twenty-five years — a permanent delegation no sunset-equipped design would have permitted; the December 2025 Iraq-AUMF repeals took *two decades* after their war ended, which is what "Congress can always act later" means in practice. The engagement school reads it as proof of concept: *Hamdi*, *Hamdan*, and *Boumediene* were decided mid-war, against executive protest, and were obeyed — the judiciary policed a live emergency without the sky falling. All three readings are factually accurate. They disagree about which fact is load-bearing.

## What evidence would tell you who's right

A seminar position you cannot falsify is a mood. Here is what each school predicts, observably, from mid-2026 forward — and what would count against it. This table is the lesson's real assessment:

| School | Watch for | Would vindicate | Would falsify or wound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Executive flexibility | Whether compliance tracks political cost rather than court orders | Retreats *before* rulings (markets, polls); defiance where politics permits, regardless of doctrine | Compliance keyed tightly to mandates — as in Dec. 2025–Apr. 2026, when withdrawal followed the stay denial and refunds followed the mandate |
| Legalist reform | The ARTICLE ONE Act's fate; the emergency count | Passage followed by a falling count and real renewal votes; emergencies actually ending | Passage followed by workarounds (new statutes, creative readings) — or perpetual non-passage proving Congress prefers the broken default |
| Judicial engagement | The first *genuine* first-weeks crisis to reach a court | Sterling-style review holding under real panic — predicates enforced mid-storm | A Quirin redux: rushed ratification first, reasoning later; or doctrines (standing, mootness, deference) deployed to avoid the merits |

Three honest observations about the scoreboard as of mid-2026. First, the compliance pattern of 2025–26 — withdrawal after the per curiam, refunds after the mandate, an appeal dropped — is the single most law-shaped data point in the modern emergency record, and it cuts against the strong flexibility thesis *for now*. Second, the legalist program's own best argument is also its embarrassment: the tariff episode handed Congress the most politically favorable conditions for NEA reform in fifty years — a cross-party coalition of injured importers, a Supreme Court opinion doing the doctrinal work, public salience — and as of mid-2026 the ARTICLE ONE Act still has not reached the Senate floor. A theory whose remedy is perpetually available and perpetually unenacted must eventually explain the non-enactment, and the explanation (members prefer presidential responsibility to recorded votes — the incentive structure Howell describes) is itself a point for the flexibility school. Third, the engagement record, however good, was compiled entirely in fair weather; its advocates should want, and dread, the storm case that would settle the question.

## What this pack does not settle

This lesson hands you three live research programs, each with verified evidence on its side, and adjudicates only what the record permits. It does not settle where the binding constraint on emergency power lives — 2025–26 showed law, politics, and courts all binding *together*, and offers no controlled experiment separating them. It does not settle whether Congress's half-century refusal to repair the NEA is dysfunction or revealed preference — whether the broken machine persists because it cannot be fixed or because every faction, in its presidential years, likes it broken. It does not settle the question Lesson 1 opened and no court has closed: who may suspend the writ when the catastrophe is real and Congress cannot or will not act — the allocation question of 1861, still on the books, still unassigned. And it does not settle Schmitt. The 2025–26 record shows exceptions litigated, narrowed, refunded, and obeyed — a sovereign, if there is one, who lost in court and complied. But the sample is one administration, one configuration of courts, one set of emergencies that never included a smoking city. Jackson's loaded weapon has not been fired in this generation; it also has not been melted down. A defensible position in this debate names its school, states what the 1979→2026 record would have to show to refute it, and watches the docket. The [[term:civil-liberties]] at stake are not abstractions; John Merryman could tell you what two o'clock in the morning sounds like.
