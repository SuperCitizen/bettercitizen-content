## Two in the morning, 1861

At two o'clock in the morning on May 25, 1861, Union soldiers arrested John Merryman at his farm north of Baltimore and took him to Fort McHenry. No warrant, no charge.

- No warrant, no charge

Chief Justice Roger Taney issued a writ of habeas corpus, the order commanding a jailer to produce his prisoner and justify the detention. The fort's commander refused.

- Habeas corpus: justify the detention

> The Chief Justice ruled. The President declined to obey.

## One sentence

The Constitution has no emergency article. It has exactly one explicit emergency provision, the Suspension Clause, and it is written as a limit: the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it.

- One emergency provision: the Suspension Clause

Shall not be suspended, by whom? The clause sits in Article One, section nine, a list of restraints on Congress, which implies the power is congressional. But that is an inference.

> Shall not be suspended. By whom? The clause never says.

## Lincoln against Taney

Taney held that the President cannot suspend the writ, nor authorize a military officer to do it. The power, he wrote, is legislative.

- Taney: the power is legislative

Lincoln answered on July 4, 1861, with two arguments. The first is necessity: "are all the laws, but one, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" The second is legal: "It was not believed that any law was violated." The clause is silent about who suspends, and the emergency arose while Congress stood adjourned.

- Lincoln, first: necessity
- Lincoln, second: no law was violated

> Taney's opinion was never appealed, never overruled, never obeyed.

## Deference in the storm, doctrine after

The cases that followed have a rhythm. Ex parte Milligan held that there can be no military trial of civilians where the courts are open. It came in 1866, after the shooting stopped. Korematsu, in 1944, upheld the exclusion of Japanese Americans from the West Coast, six to three. The Court formally repudiated it in 2018.

- Milligan, 1866: after the war
- Korematsu, 1944: repudiated in 2018

Justice Jackson's dissent in Korematsu warned what a ruling like that leaves behind.

> Jackson: the principle "lies about like a loaded weapon."

## What stays open

Is that rhythm a failure of checks and balances, or the only realistic form judicial review of emergencies can take? The lesson leaves it open. And the Supreme Court has never held whether a President may suspend the writ when Congress cannot act.

- Who may suspend: never settled

The lesson quotes each opinion and argues with it, adds Quirin and Hamdi, and sets three theories of the exception against each other: Schmitt's, Rossiter's and Ackerman's.
