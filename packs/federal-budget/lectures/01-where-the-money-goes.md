Before anything else, a question. Out of every dollar the federal government spends, how much goes to foreign aid? Pick a number, and hold on to it.

[long pause]

In early 2025, pollsters at KFF put that question to the American public. The average answer was about a quarter: 26 percent. More than half of the public said it was over 10 percent.

About one person in ten got it right. [beat] The real share is roughly one percent.

In fiscal year 2025, the entire international affairs budget came to 45 billion dollars. That is every embassy, every development program, every dollar of humanitarian relief. And it came out of total spending of just over 7 trillion. [beat] About six-tenths of a cent on the dollar.

[pause]

This is not a trivia miss. People who believe that a quarter of the budget is foreign aid will support a certain budget strategy: cut the aid, balance the books. And that strategy is arithmetically impossible.

The same poll found something else. When people were told the real figure, the share saying America spends "too much" on aid dropped by more than 20 points.

So the single most useful thing a citizen can carry into any budget argument is the actual map of where 7 trillion dollars goes. This lecture is that map.

By the end, you should be able to sketch it from memory, within a few percentage points. And you should be able to hold any plan to balance the budget up against it.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the plumbing: the three channels that all federal spending flows through.

Second, the shape of the 7 trillion: where the money lands.

Third, the other side of the ledger: what comes in, and the gap.

And last, two popular stories about closing that gap, and what the arithmetic does to each of them.

## Three channels

Start with the plumbing, not the programs, because the deepest structural fact about the federal budget is this.

> Most of the federal budget is never voted on.

Federal spending flows through three channels, and the law treats each one differently.

The first channel is spending written into permanent law. Social Security. Medicare. Medicaid. Veterans' benefits. Federal retirement. In each case the statute itself defines who qualifies and what they get, and the checks go out to everyone who qualifies. No annual vote takes place.

This channel is called mandatory spending, and a program built this way is called an entitlement program. The Congressional Budget Office, known as CBO, puts mandatory spending at about 4.2 trillion dollars in fiscal 2025.

Congress can change the formula. But that takes a new law, through the full gauntlet of House, Senate and president. And if Congress does nothing, the money flows. [beat] That is what autopilot means.

The second channel is the part Congress must approve afresh, every single year. It is called discretionary spending, and it came to roughly 1.8 trillion dollars. It moves through twelve yearly bills, called appropriations bills.

About half of it is defense. The rest is everything people picture when they imagine the government: the FBI, the national parks, air traffic control, research at the NIH, border security, the State Department. If the appropriations lapse, those activities shut down. How that yearly process works, and how often it breaks, is the subject of the third lecture in this series.

The third channel is the least optional of all. It is net interest: the price of past borrowing. On the Treasury's accounting, it came to 970 billion dollars in fiscal 2025. No committee sets that figure. The bond market sets it, along with the size of the debt.

[pause]

So, three channels. Mandatory spending, which runs on autopilot. Discretionary spending, which is fought over every year. And interest, which is owed.

Now put the three together, because the arithmetic is the insight.

Mandatory spending plus interest came to about 5.2 trillion dollars. And that is almost exactly what the government collected in total revenue.

Think about what that means. The spending that nobody voted on that year used up nearly every dollar that came in.

So take everything Congress actually debated, amended and voted on: the entire discretionary budget, defense included. In an accounting sense, all of it was financed by borrowing.

Put another way: a budget fight that never touches autopilot spending or revenue is a fight over the minority of the budget. [beat] And it is conducted entirely on credit.

Let's test that. A newly elected Congress promises to shrink the budget dramatically. But it will do so only by writing leaner versions of the twelve annual appropriations bills. It will touch nothing else.

What share of federal spending can that effort even reach? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Roughly a quarter. The twelve bills govern only discretionary spending: about 1.8 trillion dollars, out of 7 trillion. Mandatory programs flow from permanent law that no appropriations bill touches. And interest is owed regardless.

So serious deficit reduction has to change mandatory law, or raise revenue, or both. The annual bills cannot do it alone.

## The shape of 7 trillion

Those are the channels. Now, where does the money actually land?

Here are the shares, from the Treasury's final accounting for fiscal 2025. Listen for whatever surprises you, because that is the thing to remember.

Social Security: 23 percent.

Medicare: 14 percent.

Other health programs, mostly Medicaid: another 14 percent.

National defense: 13 percent.

And foreign aid, together with all of diplomacy: about 1 percent.

[pause]

Hear that list the way an auditor would. Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid, just those three, were about 46 percent of everything the government spent.

Medicare and Medicaid together now outweigh Social Security itself. And Social Security alone cost more than defense, veterans' programs and all of international affairs combined.

> In dollar terms, the federal government is an insurance company with an army.

That is why two documents matter to every budget debate: the 2025 reports of the Social Security and Medicare trustees. In the trustees' projection, the reserves of the Social Security retirement trust fund run out in 2033. Medicare's hospital fund runs out the same year.

Those dates sit inside the career of every politician now promising never to touch either program.

[pause]

Now, I left one large item off that list, on purpose. You already hold the numbers you need to place it.

In fiscal 2025, which did the federal government spend more on: national defense, or interest on its debt? Think back to the three channels.

[long pause]

Interest. A few minutes ago I said that net interest came to 970 billion dollars. I also said that defense was about half of a discretionary budget of 1.8 trillion. Half of that is about 900 billion. The exact figure for defense was 917 billion.

So interest joins the list at 14 percent, just ahead of defense. It is now the fourth-largest claim on the budget.

A decade ago, that would have been unthinkable. The crossover came in fiscal 2024: for the first time, the federal government spent more on interest than on national defense. In 2025, the gap widened.

> The insurance company with an army now pays its creditors more than its soldiers.

No Congress ever voted for that. It is the compound consequence of two decades of deficits, meeting the rise in interest rates that came after 2022.

And consider what a dollar of interest buys. No aircraft carrier. No clinic. No road. It buys the continued willingness of bondholders to hold 31.6 trillion dollars of publicly held federal debt.

## What comes in, and the gap

That is the spending side. Now the other side of the ledger, and only briefly. Who pays which federal taxes is a subject of its own, with a series of its own, called Federal Taxes. Here we need only the totals.

In fiscal 2025, just over 7 trillion dollars went out. About 5.2 trillion came in.

About half of that revenue came from individual income taxes. About a third came from payroll taxes. The corporate income tax brought in 9 percent. Tariffs brought in 4 percent, after an extraordinary one-year jump.

For the budget, the headline is the subtraction. Revenue minus spending left a deficit of about 1.8 trillion dollars. CBO puts that at 5.9 percent of GDP.

Now notice the conditions. This was the third year in a row with a deficit above 6 percent of GDP, or just under it. And it came at full employment, with no war and no recession.

Deficits near that size used to appear only in deep recessions or world wars. Outside an emergency, at this scale, they are historically new.

In plainer words: this gap is not the mark of a bad year. It is built into an ordinary one. The word for that is structural.

Each year's deficit then rolls into the national debt. As of June 2026, the total stood at 39.2 trillion dollars. Of that, 31.6 trillion is held by the public. The rest is IOUs between government accounts.

The publicly held share is about 101 percent of GDP. In CBO's projection, it reaches 120 percent by 2036. The all-time record is 106 percent, set at the end of World War II.

Is that path an emergency? A manageable cost? Or even, under some conditions, no problem at all? That is precisely the argument the next lecture stages. [beat] What it is not is small.

Now put the map to work. Suppose Congress had eliminated the entire Department of Defense in fiscal 2025. Every soldier, every ship, every base. 917 billion dollars, down to zero.

Would the budget have balanced? Think it through.

[long pause]

Nowhere near. The deficit was about 1.8 trillion dollars. Remove all 917 billion of defense, and a hole of roughly 860 billion remains. In nominal terms, that is still a larger deficit than the United States ran in any year before 2009.

> The deficit is bigger than the entire defense budget.

Defense is the largest item that most cut-the-spending rhetoric targets. It is 13 percent of the budget. The deficit is bigger than all of it.

That is the discipline the map forces on every balancing plan. Any honest path to balance runs through the big four: Social Security, the health programs, interest, by way of the debt itself, and revenue.

## Two stories, and the arithmetic

With the map in hand, we can test two popular stories about how to balance the budget. Both are comforting. Neither survives the arithmetic.

Story one: cut foreign aid, and balance the budget.

The poll explains the appeal. A budget line that the average voter believes is 26 percent of spending feels decisive. But the actual line is 45 billion dollars. Zero it out entirely, and it covers about 2.5 percent of the 2025 deficit.

And the country, in effect, ran the experiment. Between fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025, international affairs spending fell from 72 billion dollars to 45 billion. That is a cut of more than a third, amid the dismantling of USAID.

And the total deficit? [beat] It declined by just 41 billion dollars, and stayed within sight of 1.8 trillion.

You can think foreign aid is wasteful. You can think it is the best money America spends. Either way, it cannot move the fiscal needle, because there is almost no needle for it to move.

And the misperception does real damage. It lets the public believe that painless balance is available. It lets them believe that politicians who decline to deliver it are merely corrupt, rather than arithmetically constrained.

[pause]

Story two: just eliminate waste, fraud and abuse.

This one deserves more respect, because the underlying problem is real, and it is big. In April 2024, the Government Accountability Office, the GAO, published its first government-wide estimate of fraud, meaning intentional deception. It put direct losses at somewhere between 233 billion and 521 billion dollars a year.

And agencies reported about 186 billion dollars in improper payments for fiscal 2025 alone.

Anti-fraud investment pays for itself many times over, and both parties are right to pursue it.

But watch the arithmetic. Take the GAO's upper bound. Then assume a perfect government, one that recovers every fraudulent dollar. No auditor believes that is possible, because most fraud is identified statistically, not traced to accounts that can be recovered.

Even then, fiscal 2025 still runs a deficit above 1.25 trillion dollars. Use the midpoint of the estimate instead, and you have covered about a fifth of the gap.

So waste, fraud and abuse is a worthy target, and a real agenda for program integrity. As a balancing plan, it is something else: a way of promising deficit reduction while naming no program, no tax, and no voter who will feel it. That is exactly why the phrase polls so well and delivers so little.

The pattern in both stories is the same, and it is the skill to take from this lecture.

> When anyone proposes a path to fiscal balance, find the denominator.

Ask: out of how much? The mass of the budget sits in Social Security, the health programs, defense and interest. Its revenue sits in income taxes and payroll taxes.

A plan that touches none of those is not a plan. [beat] It is an applause line.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. Three channels: mandatory spending on autopilot, discretionary spending in twelve annual bills, and interest. Most of the federal budget is never voted on. The twelve annual bills reach about a quarter of federal spending.

[beat]

Two. The shape of the money. Social Security, 23 percent. Medicare, 14. Other health programs, mostly Medicaid, 14. Interest, 14. Defense, 13. Foreign aid, about 1. In dollar terms, the federal government is an insurance company with an army.

[beat]

Three. Since fiscal 2024, the insurance company with an army has paid its creditors more than its soldiers. No Congress ever voted for that.

[beat]

Four. The gap. About 7 trillion dollars out, about 5.2 trillion in, and a deficit of about 1.8 trillion, at full employment, with no war and no recession. The deficit is bigger than the entire defense budget.

[beat]

And five. When anyone proposes a path to fiscal balance, find the denominator. Ending all foreign aid would cover about 2.5 percent of the gap. Recovering every dollar of fraud, at the highest estimate, would still leave a deficit above 1.25 trillion.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It gave you composition, not judgment. It established that social insurance and interest dominate the budget, that the deficit is structural, and that the popular painless fixes fail the arithmetic. It did not establish that the deficit is a crisis. [beat] Or that it isn't.

That takes arguments, and there are at least three serious ones. Each one looks at the same map, and each one reads it differently.

The fiscal consolidation hawk says to look at the totals: 39 trillion dollars of debt, and an interest bill above the defense budget.

> The hawk's reading: a slow-motion emergency.

The full-employment dove, in a tradition called functional finance, says to look at who is borrowing: a government that issues its own currency.

> The dove's reading: judge the budget by inflation and employment, not by the gap.

The public-investment advocate says that both of those camps are scoring the wrong thing.

> The investment advocate's reading: what matters is what the borrowing buys.

One of those three probably sounded wrong to you the moment you heard it. Take that one. Using only the map you now carry, what is the best case you could make for it?

[pause 5s]

The next lecture gives each of the three its strongest case, so you can set yours beside it. The lecture after that turns to the machinery: appropriations, shutdowns, reconciliation, the debt ceiling. That is where every one of these arguments either becomes law or dies.

For now, go back to the number you picked at the start. The country's average answer was a quarter. The real one is about six-tenths of a cent on the dollar. [beat] You carry the map now. It will not tell you what to want. It will tell you what any plan has to touch.
