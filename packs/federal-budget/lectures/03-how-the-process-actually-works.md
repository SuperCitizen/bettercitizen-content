April 30, 2026. The president signs a funding bill, and the Department of Homeland Security reopens.

It has gone 76 days without funding: the longest partial shutdown in American history. And it is the last unfunded department of a fiscal year that is, by now, seven months old.

[beat]

It is also the third funding collapse of that single fiscal year. First came a 43-day shutdown of the full government, in October and November 2025: the longest full shutdown ever. Then a brief lapse at the turn of February. Then Homeland Security, from Valentine's Day to the end of April.

[pause]

Here is the uncomfortable joke of American civics. Every textbook prints the same diagram. The president proposes in February. Congress passes twelve bills by October 1.

> The textbook budget process has run on schedule four times since 1977.

So there are two machines to learn: the one on paper, and the one that actually runs. By the end, you should be able to hear a budget fight in the news and say which lever is being pulled, and what that lever can and cannot do.

Last time, the arguments over what the deficit means. This time, the machinery those arguments must pass through. Four steps.

First, the machine on paper.

Second, the machine in practice: stopgaps, giant bundles and shutdowns.

Third, two special levers: an express lane called reconciliation, and the debt ceiling.

And last, the newest fight over the oldest question. Once Congress appropriates money, must the president spend it?

## The machine on paper

The modern process dates to 1974, and to a law with a long name: the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act. It was Congress's counterattack against Richard Nixon, who had claimed the power to simply not spend appropriated money he disliked.

Keep that claim in mind. It returns at the end.

The act built Congress its own budget machinery. Budget committees. The Congressional Budget Office, known as CBO, as a counterweight to the president's numbers. A fiscal year that starts on October 1. And a formal calendar, in five steps.

First, the president's budget request, due on the first Monday in February. It is a request. Congress can ignore it wholesale, and does.

Second, a budget resolution, which sets the overall totals. It is not a law, and the president never signs it. But it binds the two chambers procedurally.

Third, twelve bills, each drafted by a subcommittee to fund its slice of the government for the year.

Fourth, passage in both chambers, in identical form. In the Senate, that usually means 60 votes, to clear a filibuster. [beat] This is where the schedule dies.

Fifth, the president's signature on all twelve, by October 1. Any agency left unfunded at midnight must cease its non-excepted operations. [beat] That is a shutdown.

[pause]

One distinction makes everything after this easier to follow: the difference between authorizing and appropriating.

An authorizing law creates a program and sets its legal ceiling. An appropriations act actually provides the money.

> Authorization is permission. Only an appropriation is money.

So a program can be authorized and never funded. And vast stretches of the government have been funded for decades on authorizations that expired.

Let's test that. Congress authorizes a new rural broadband program, at 5 billion dollars a year, and the press celebrates. The next year, the program spends nothing.

What happened? And was anything illegal? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Nothing illegal. Nothing happened at all. Authorization is permission, not money. Unless an appropriations act, or a mandatory spending provision, actually provides the funds, an authorized program is an empty vessel.

So when you hear that Congress passed a 5 billion dollar program, ask which of the two acts actually occurred. The headline usually doesn't say.

One reminder. This whole pipeline governs only discretionary spending, about a quarter of the budget. Social Security checks went out on time through every shutdown of fiscal 2026, because entitlement spending never passes through these twelve bills.

## The machine in practice

That is the paper. Now the practice.

Pew Research keeps the on-time record. All twelve bills were enacted by October 1 in just four fiscal years out of nearly fifty. The most recent was fiscal 1997.

What fills the gap is a technology of improvisation, and it has two main tools.

The first tool is a stopgap. It extends last year's funding levels for weeks or months, and it is called a continuing resolution, or CR.

A continuing resolution keeps the lights on, at a price. It freezes every agency in last year's priorities. It bars new programs from starting, no matter how far along the real bills are. And it turns budgeting into a recurring hostage negotiation, because the day each one expires is a new shutdown deadline.

In short, a continuing resolution is a photocopy of last year's budget, not an early draft of this year's.

And notice what it is not. It is not a shutdown. [beat] It is what prevents one.

The second tool is the bundle. When the bills finally move, they rarely move alone. Leadership packs several of them, or all twelve, into one package called an omnibus. It runs to thousands of pages, it is negotiated by a handful of principals, and it is voted on in days.

Members denounce it, and reliably pass it. By that point, the alternative is a shutdown that nobody scheduled.

Fiscal 2026 compressed the whole pathology into one year.

October 1, 2025. The fiscal year opens with no bills enacted, and no continuing resolution. Senate Democrats withhold the votes needed to reach 60, demanding an extension of expiring enhanced ACA premium subsidies. Roughly the entire discretionary government lapses.

Day 43, in mid-November. A package ends the shutdown. It funds three of the twelve bills for the full year, and puts the other nine on a continuing resolution.

Late January. Three more bills become law. Six done, six left.

Then the deadline passes, and there is a second lapse, the length of a long weekend. A new law funds five more bills, and gives Homeland Security a short stopgap of its own.

February 14. That stopgap expires, amid an impasse over immigration-enforcement policy. One department, with roughly a quarter-million employees, enters a lapse. Most of them are "excepted," and work unpaid.

April 30. Day 76. The last bill is signed. [beat] Twelve bills, four laws, three lapses.

[pause]

Hear that timeline as a mechanism, not a melodrama. Funding requires affirmative action by three players, and each one holds a veto: the House, the Senate, and the president.

Any one of the three can convert the deadline into leverage for an unrelated policy demand. ACA subsidies in October. Immigration enforcement in February.

Then a law called the Antideficiency Act makes the failure physical. Furloughs. Unpaid work by air-traffic controllers and Border Patrol agents. Suspended statistics. Closed parks. By CBO's estimate, the 43-day shutdown alone permanently destroyed billions of dollars in output, even after back pay.

## The express lane

Everything so far requires 60 votes in the Senate. The 1974 act left one express lane. It is called budget reconciliation.

Here is how it works. Congress first adopts a budget resolution with instructions. Then it may pass one bill that cannot be filibustered, changing mandatory spending, revenues and the debt limit. That bill needs 51 votes.

Nearly every major one-party fiscal law of the modern era rode that lane.

The lane has a toll. It is called the Byrd rule. Under it, a senator can strike any provision that is "extraneous" to the budget.

The most important target is anything whose budgetary effect is "merely incidental" to a policy aim.

Before the floor debate, the parliamentarian goes through the bill, ruling provision by provision. That review has a nickname: the Byrd bath.

Let's run two provisions through the bath. A senator wants to add both to a reconciliation bill. The first raises the federal tobacco tax by one dollar a pack. The second requires all federal contractors to use E-Verify, and CBO scores it as having a tiny, indirect effect on the budget.

Which one survives? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The tobacco tax survives. It is a direct change in revenue, and that is reconciliation's core business. The E-Verify mandate almost certainly falls. Its budgetary effect is merely incidental to a regulatory goal, and that is the classic Byrd-rule kill.

> Reconciliation moves dollars, not policy wearing a dollar costume.

That is why majorities keep testing the line. And the freshest case is the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, of July 2025. It shows the lane's power, and its stretching.

The power first. This was a once-in-a-generation fiscal package, built around a permanent extension of the 2017 tax rates. It passed the Senate 51 to 50, with the vice president breaking the tie. It needed no votes from the minority.

And the bath did bite. The parliamentarian ruled more than a dozen provisions extraneous, among them a forced sale of millions of acres of public lands. They fell out.

Now the stretch. Senate Republicans scored the bill against a novel yardstick, a "current-policy baseline." Under it, extending the expiring 2017 tax cuts, the costliest element of the bill, counted as costing nothing, since those rates were current policy. The majority declined to submit that question to the parliamentarian, and turned back the challenge on the floor.

So that time, the majority did not go past the rule. It went around the referee.

Against CBO's standard baseline, the law adds about 3.4 trillion dollars to deficits over ten years. With interest, the total is about 4.1 trillion.

Whatever one thinks of the policy, process scholars in both parties noticed the precedent. If current policy is free, any expiring giveaway can be made permanent at an official price of zero. [beat] By either party. Forever.

## The debt ceiling

The second special lever is the most misunderstood in the budget: the debt limit, often called the debt ceiling.

It is a cap, set by statute, on the total debt the Treasury may have outstanding. And here is the part people get wrong. It does not authorize any spending, and it does not restrain any. Every dollar that requires borrowing was already enacted, by past Congresses, through the processes we have just walked through.

> The debt ceiling is a limit on paying, not on spending.

Put another way: refusing to raise the ceiling is not refusing a new credit card. [beat] It is refusing to pay the bill for dinner already eaten.

When the limit binds, the Treasury buys time with "extraordinary measures": accounting maneuvers, such as suspending investments in federal retirement funds. They last until a day known as the X-date. On the X-date, the cash and the maneuvers run out, and the government cannot meet all its obligations in full and on time.

What happens then is unknown territory. Treasury securities are the bonds that anchor global finance, as the "risk-free" asset. Missed payments on them would be a sovereign default. And every camp from the last lecture agrees the consequences would be severe.

In 2011, a deal came two days before the Treasury's estimated exhaustion. Standard and Poor's downgraded the United States from AAA anyway. It was the first downgrade in history, and what it cited was not arithmetic. [beat] It was governance.

In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act raised the limit to 41.1 trillion dollars. As of June 2026, the debt stands at 39.2 trillion. And in the Bipartisan Policy Center's projection, the new limit binds again between late winter and mid-summer 2027. [beat] The cycle, in other words, is already scheduled.

Remember what the ceiling covers: past decisions, already on the books. That is why economists across the spectrum, hawks included, treat threats of default as a category error. A Congress that wants smaller deficits has two real levers. Spend less. Tax more.

What to do with the ceiling itself is argued both ways. Some reformers argue for abolishing the limit, or for deeming it raised whenever a budget passes. Defenders argue that it is the one moment that forces Congress to look at the debt it has already created.

## Who controls the purse?

The newest front is the oldest question. Once Congress appropriates, must the president spend?

You have met this question already. At the start, I said the 1974 act was Congress's counterattack against one president. Who was he, and what power had he claimed?

[long pause]

Richard Nixon. He had claimed the power to simply not spend appropriated money he disliked. The name for that is impoundment.

The 1974 act was supposed to be the final answer. It requires the president to spend what is appropriated, unless Congress approves a proposed cancellation within 45 days. A cancellation of that kind is called a rescission.

In July 2025, that route worked as designed, for the first time in more than three decades. Congress enacted a rescissions package of 9 billion dollars.

What happened next is the constitutional story of 2025 and 2026.

The administration asserted broad authority to withhold spending it opposed. By September 2025, the Government Accountability Office, the GAO, had found six violations of the Impoundment Control Act, and had opened dozens more inquiries.

Then came the escalation. On August 28, 2025, the administration sent a "pocket rescission." That is a rescission message sent so close to the end of September that the money expires before the 45-day clock runs out. The spending is canceled without Congress's approval. This one targeted 4.9 billion dollars in foreign-aid funds.

Two positions now stand against each other.

The GAO's position is that pocket rescissions are flatly illegal: a unilateral repeal of duly enacted law. On this view, it is the same defect that doomed the line-item veto in 1998, in Clinton v. City of New York.

The administration's position is that the Impoundment Control Act is itself unconstitutional: an invasion of inherent executive authority.

The courts now hold the question. In September 2025, the Supreme Court let the administration keep withholding roughly 4 billion dollars of the disputed funds while the litigation proceeded. The money lapsed on schedule. And as of mid-2026, the broader fight remains unresolved on the merits.

> Must a president spend what Congress appropriates? The courts have not yet decided.

The outcome decides whether Congress's power of the purse describes the Constitution, [beat] or merely a custom.

## Three readings of one year

Process is never neutral. Three lapses, the baseline maneuver, the impoundment campaign: is that a system in crisis, or a system absorbing hardball? How you answer depends partly on the camps from the last lecture.

> The hawk sees institutions doing exactly what unconstrained incentives predict.

So the hawk wants harder rules: automatic continuing resolutions, debt-limit reform, enforced baselines.

> The dove sees a fetish for procedure, distracting from outcomes.

> The investment advocate sees a process structurally unable to tell what borrowing buys.

Take the reading you find least convincing. Using what you have heard in this lecture, make the best case for it. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The paper machine: a request in February, twelve bills, all signed by October 1. That process has run on schedule four times since 1977. And authorization is permission. Only an appropriation is money.

[beat]

Two. The machine in practice. A continuing resolution is a photocopy of last year's budget. And a shutdown is what happens when one of three veto players turns the deadline into leverage. Fiscal 2026 had three of them.

[beat]

Three. Reconciliation is the express lane: one bill, 51 votes, no filibuster. The Byrd rule holds it to dollars, not policy wearing a dollar costume. And in 2025, a current-policy baseline counted the extension of the tax cuts as costing nothing.

[beat]

Four. The debt ceiling is a limit on paying, not on spending. It is the bill for dinner already eaten.

[beat]

And five. Impoundment. Must a president spend what Congress appropriates? The courts have not yet decided.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. Three questions are worth carrying out the door.

Would an automatic continuing resolution end shutdowns, or merely make minority leverage permanent?

Does the current-policy precedent now bind both parties, and can it be undone?

And if the courts bless even part of the impoundment theory, what exactly does an appropriations law mean?

[pause]

This series ends here. The argument does not. The composition of the budget is arithmetic. The meaning of deficits is argument. The process is power.

So go back, one last time, to April 30, 2026, and a president signing the final bill of a fiscal year already seven months old. You know now which machine produced that day. And you know that all three, the arithmetic, the argument and the power, are on the ballot every cycle. [beat] Whether the ballot says so or not.
