January 4, 2019. A ballroom in Atlanta. At the lectern stands Olivier Blanchard, former chief economist of the IMF, and about as establishment as the economics profession gets.

He is giving the presidential address of the American Economic Association, the most prestigious lectern in the discipline. And he uses it to say something close to heresy.

When the interest rate on government debt is below the economy's growth rate, he says, "public debt may have no fiscal cost."

[pause]

No fiscal cost. A government in that position can roll its debt over forever. It never has to raise taxes to pay the debt down. And it can watch the debt shrink, as a share of the economy, all by itself.

[beat]

Seven years later, the United States owes 39.2 trillion dollars. It pays more in interest than it spends on defense. And in the projection of the Congressional Budget Office, known as CBO, it breaks its World War II debt record within a decade.

So was Blanchard wrong? Or early? Or was he right in a way that makes today's numbers more alarming, not less, because the cheap money his argument required has lapsed?

Every serious position on the federal deficit is an answer to that question. There are three of them. By the end, you should be able to state each one the way its own best advocate would, and to say what each one still has to answer for.

Last time, we drew the map of where the money goes. This time, the argument over what the map means. Four steps.

First, the numbers that every camp accepts, and the one race that governs them.

Second, the three cases, each in its turn: the fiscal consolidation hawk, the full-employment dove, and the public-investment advocate.

Third, the cross-examination: the weakness each camp must answer for.

And last, the one point on which all three agree.

## The numbers everyone accepts

Stating a position at its strongest is called steel-manning, and it only works on shared facts. So fix the facts first. None of them is contested by any of the three camps. The entire argument is about what they mean.

The total federal debt, as of June 2026: 39.2 trillion dollars.

The part of it held by the public: about 101 percent of GDP.

The path: in CBO's projection, 120 percent of GDP by 2036. The record, set in 1946, is 106.

Net interest in fiscal 2025: 970 billion dollars, more than national defense.

And one date, twice over. In the trustees' projection, the reserves of the Social Security retirement fund are depleted in 2033. So are those of Medicare's hospital fund.

[pause]

Two ideas, and then the argument.

The first is a pair of words. The deficit is a flow: this year's borrowing. The debt is a stock: all the past borrowing not yet repaid.

The second idea carries the weight of everything that follows. Whether the debt grows or shrinks, measured against the economy, depends on a race between two rates.

One is the average interest rate the government pays. The other is the growth rate of the economy. Economists label them r and g, so you will hear this race called r versus g.

When growth outruns interest, the economy grows faster than the interest compounds. The debt ratio can fall, even while the government runs modest deficits. The ratio melts.

When interest outruns growth, the compounding wins. The ratio snowballs, unless taxes rise or spending falls.

> The debt's path depends on a race between the interest rate and growth.

For most of the 2010s, interest sat clearly below growth. That is what gave Blanchard's address its force. Since 2022, the gap has narrowed or closed. The Treasury now refinances cheap debt, as it matures, at materially higher rates. And that is exactly why the interest bill crossed the defense budget.

Let's test that with a scenario. The year is 2031. The average interest rate on the federal debt is 3 percent. The economy is growing at 4.5 percent. And Congress is running a small deficit.

What happens to the debt, as a share of the economy? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It can fall, even with the deficit. Growth is outrunning interest, so the economy outgrows the compounding, and the ratio melts. That is Blanchard's condition, and it held through most of the 2010s.

But notice the catch, the one the hawks emphasize. Neither rate is a policy choice. A strategy that depends on interest staying below growth is a bet on a variable that no Congress controls.

## The fiscal consolidation hawk

Each camp now gets the same treatment, in the same order: what it is protecting, what it argues, the evidence it points to, and what it would do.

Begin with the fiscal consolidation hawk. What the hawk is protecting is room to act: the country's capacity to borrow in a crisis, and the generation that inherits the bill.

The hawk argues from compound interest, and from political economy. Take the compounding first.

For evidence, the hawk points to the Penn Wharton Budget Model. It estimates that financial markets cannot absorb more than roughly two more decades of deficits under current policy. Markets price Treasury debt as they do today only because they assume Washington corrects course in time. If that assumption ever breaks, the correction arrives suddenly, through interest rates, not gradually, through warnings.

The hawk also argues that fiscal space is a national-security asset. The United States could deploy trillions of dollars in 2008 and in 2020 because it entered those crises with capacity. Enter the next one at 120 percent of GDP, and the credit card is already half drawn.

And on this view, every dollar borrowed today, at full employment, is a tax invoice mailed to people too young to vote.

Now the political economy. The 2025 reconciliation act added an estimated 4.7 trillion dollars to projected debt, and it was passed by majorities that once called themselves fiscally conservative. Both parties, the hawk argues, have stopped pretending.

So what would the hawk do? Enact a consolidation package now: changes to the entitlement formulas, plus revenue. Now, while it can still be gradual. Because every sovereign-debt episode, the hawk argues, shares one thing.

> The hawk's case: the adjustment comes on your schedule, or on the market's.

## The full-employment dove

Now the full-employment dove, in the same order. What the dove is protecting is full employment, and the real people who are made poorer when a government cuts too soon.

The dove argues from a tradition that descends from Abba Lerner, who in 1943 called it functional finance. Judge fiscal policy by its functions: full employment and stable prices. Never judge it by whether a bookkeeping line called the deficit is big or small.

A government that issues its own floating currency, the dove argues, is not a household. It can always meet obligations denominated in dollars. So the binding constraint is not solvency. It is real resources, and inflation.

The tradition's modern edge is Modern Monetary Theory, and its accessible statement is Stephanie Kelton's book, The Deficit Myth. It changes the question to ask of any spending. Not "how will we pay for it?" Instead: "what real resources does it claim, and are they idle?"

For evidence, the dove points to the 2010s: the natural experiment that, on this view, the hawks lost.

After 2010, deficit alarm produced premature consolidation across the West: sequestration in the United States, austerity in the United Kingdom and the eurozone. The result, the dove argues, was a lost decade. In 2013, the IMF's own Blanchard and Leigh found that the damage done by austerity had been systematically underestimated.

And the debt crisis so often prophesied for the United States never came. Trillion-dollar deficits met some of the lowest borrowing costs in history.

So the dove would watch different dials. Inflation is the signal of overspending. Unemployment is the signal of underspending. And the trust-fund dates of 2033 are accounting conventions that Congress can amend. The real question of 2033, on this view, is whether the economy can produce what retirees will consume.

So what would the dove do? Keep supporting full employment. To cut that support in order to soothe a ratio, the dove argues, makes real people poorer now, to prevent a crisis that exists only in projections.

> The dove's case: the deficit is the wrong dial. Watch inflation and employment.

## The public-investment advocate

And the third camp, in the same order: the public-investment advocate. What this advocate is protecting is borrowing that builds: the bridge, the basic research, the investment in early childhood.

The advocate argues that both rivals are fighting over the wrong column. The budget counts the borrowing. It does not count what the borrowing bought.

Picture two families. One borrows 400,000 dollars for a house. The other borrows 400,000 dollars for lottery tickets. On paper, they show identical debt.

No bank keeps its books that way. No firm does, and almost no state government. They keep capital budgets, which set investment, the spending that creates an asset, apart from operating expense.

The federal budget draws no such line. So a dollar spent on a bridge scores exactly like a dollar of any transfer or tax break.

The advocate's standard was formalized in 2020, by Jason Furman and Lawrence Summers. Judge whether the debt is sustainable by real interest costs, as a share of GDP. And judge any borrowing by whether the social return on what it buys exceeds the government's real cost of borrowing.

For evidence, the advocate points to history. The interstate highways. The GI Bill. DARPA's seeding of the internet and GPS. The NIH's role in the biotech sector. In each case, the advocate argues, the returns dwarfed the carrying cost.

And the advocate looks at today's borrowing with alarm at what it is made of, not just at its size. A deficit of 6 percent of GDP, at full employment, goes overwhelmingly to current consumption: transfers, interest and tax cuts. Obligations accumulate, and no assets accumulate beside them.

So what would the advocate do? Give the federal government a capital budget, or at least have CBO's scoring credit the returns on investment. Then fight about the operating deficit, on the terms of the hawk and the dove.

> The investment advocate's case: judge the borrowing by what it buys.

## The tests each camp has to pass

Notice what just happened. Three camps, one set of numbers. And the disagreement is not about the facts. It is about which variable is sovereign.

The hawk crowns the path of the debt. The dove crowns inflation and employment. The investment advocate crowns the return on what the borrowing buys.

> The three camps are not answering one question differently. They are asking different questions.

But a case at its strongest must also face cross-examination. Each camp has a weakness to answer for, from the last fifteen years. Same order, same questions: what goes against it, what it answers, and what would change its mind.

First, the hawk. What goes against the hawk is the decade from 2010 to 2021. Hawks warned of imminent punishment by the bond market, from 2009 onward. What came was a decade of record-low rates.

A prediction of crisis with no date attached cannot be proved false. So the hawk must say why this decade's warning differs from the last one's.

The hawk answers that the interest rate has actually risen, and that the trust-fund dates now sit inside the ten-year window. That is a stronger answer than the 2010 version was. [beat] But hawks said that then, too.

And what would change the hawk's mind? A durable return of interest below growth.

[pause]

Second, the dove. What goes against the dove is 2021 to 2023. The one constraint that functional finance names as real is inflation. And inflation arrived. In mid-2022 it reached a four-decade high in the United States, after the largest peacetime fiscal expansion ever.

When the signal flashed, no mechanism of functional finance throttled spending. The Federal Reserve's rate hikes did. And they pushed interest up toward growth, which weakens the dove's arithmetic everywhere else.

The dove answers that supply shocks did much of the work, and that employment recovered in two years instead of ten. That is a fair answer. [beat] But this is now part of the dove's record: the constraint bound, and the institutions ignored it until the central bank acted.

And what would change the dove's mind? Persistent inflation, or a failed Treasury auction.

[pause]

Third, the investment advocate. What goes against this camp is the word "investment" itself. Every interest group calls its appropriation an investment. A capital budget without ruthless gatekeeping becomes a hole in the budget, and everything migrates through it. That is the reason CBO and OMB have historically resisted one.

So the advocate owes an answer to a test. Can the camp name, in advance and in public, the spending it would decline to call capital? And can it accept hawk-grade discipline on the rest?

And what would change the advocate's mind? A decade of capital spending that underperforms its scoring.

[pause]

Now put all three camps to work on one case. A recession hits in 2027. Unemployment jumps to 7 percent. The deficit is already near 6 percent of GDP. As revenues fall, it heads toward 9 percent by itself.

What does each camp prescribe? Begin by recalling the variable each one crowned. And ask whether there is any point on which all three converge. Think it through.

[long pause]

The dove crowned inflation and employment. So the dove prescribes aggressive stimulus. Idle resources are the green light, and the automatic rise in the deficit is the system working.

The investment advocate crowned the return on what the borrowing buys. So this camp prescribes stimulus aimed at capital. A recession is when labor and borrowing are cheapest, so build then.

The hawk crowned the path of the debt. Even so, the hawk accepts deficits that run against the cycle. Most hawks are not absolutists who want a balanced budget every year. But the hawk demands that the package be temporary, and paired with credible long-run consolidation, so that markets fund the surge calmly.

And the convergence.

> All three camps agree: a recession is no time for sudden spending cuts.

Their fight is over what happens in the recovery.

[pause]

One last exercise, and this one is yours alone. Take the camp you find least convincing. State its best case, the way its own advocate would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The shared facts. 39.2 trillion dollars of debt. Debt held by the public at about 101 percent of GDP, heading for 120. Interest above defense. Trust-fund dates in 2033. No camp disputes any of it.

[beat]

Two. The debt's path depends on a race between the interest rate and growth. When growth is ahead, the ratio melts. When interest is ahead, it snowballs. Since 2022, the gap has narrowed or closed.

[beat]

Three. The cases. The hawk's: the adjustment comes on your schedule, or on the market's. The dove's: the deficit is the wrong dial, so watch inflation and employment. The investment advocate's: judge the borrowing by what it buys. They are asking different questions.

[beat]

Four. The weakness each must answer for. The hawk: the crisis that did not come after 2010. The dove: the inflation that did come after 2021. The investment advocate: the word "investment."

[beat]

And five. What would change each mind. The hawk: a durable return of interest below growth. The dove: persistent inflation, or a failed Treasury auction. The investment advocate: a decade of capital spending that underperforms its scoring. And on one point, all three camps agree: a recession is no time for sudden spending cuts.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It has not told you which camp is right. The honest answer is that each is right about a different way of failing.

A defensible position states which risks it is choosing to run, and what evidence would change its mind. [beat] That goes for the camps. It goes for you.

And no camp controls the machine that converts these philosophies into law. Twelve appropriations bills. A reconciliation process with arcane rules. A statutory debt ceiling. And a president testing how much of the spending power can be reclaimed from Congress. That machine broke three times in fiscal 2026 alone, and it is the subject of the next lecture.

For now, go back to that ballroom in Atlanta. Was Blanchard wrong, or early, or right in a way that should alarm us more? [beat] You now hold three answers, each at its strongest. And you know which risk each one has chosen to run.
