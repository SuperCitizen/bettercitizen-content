# The strongest cases: what the deficit means

On January 4, 2019, in an Atlanta ballroom, Olivier Blanchard — former chief economist of the IMF, about as establishment as the economics profession gets — used the most prestigious lectern in the discipline, the American Economic Association presidential address, to say something close to heresy: when the interest rate on government debt is below the economy's growth rate, "public debt may have no fiscal cost." A government in that position can roll its debt over forever, never raising taxes to pay it down, and watch the debt *ratio* shrink on its own. After a decade of deficit alarm and [[term:austerity]] politics, the discipline's high priesthood was saying the alarm had been miscalibrated. Seven years later the United States owes $39.2 trillion, pays more interest than it spends on defense, and is projected by CBO to break its World War II debt record within a decade. So was Blanchard wrong? Or early? Or right in a way that makes today's numbers *more* alarming, not less — because the cheap-money condition his argument required has lapsed? Every serious position on the federal deficit is an answer to that question. This lesson gives you the three strongest, each as its own best advocate would make it.

## The numbers everyone accepts

Steel-manning only works on shared facts, so fix the table first. None of the figures below is contested by any of the three camps; the entire argument is about what they mean.

| Fact (as of mid-2026) | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total federal debt | $39.2 trillion (June 2026) | Treasury, Debt to the Penny |
| Held by the public | $31.6 trillion — about 101% of GDP | Treasury; CBO |
| Deficit, FY2025 actual | $1.8 trillion — 5.9% of GDP | CBO |
| Deficit path | 5.8% of GDP in 2026, rising to 6.7% by 2036 | CBO, February 2026 outlook |
| Debt path | 120% of GDP by 2036 — above the 1946 record of 106% | CBO |
| Net interest, FY2025 | $970 billion — more than national defense | Treasury |
| Social Security retirement fund | Reserves depleted 2033; combined funds 2034, then ~81% of benefits payable | 2025 Trustees Report |
| Medicare hospital fund | Reserves depleted 2033 | 2025 Trustees Report |

Two background concepts, and then the argument. First, the deficit is a *flow* (this year's borrowing); the debt is a *stock* (all past borrowing not yet repaid). Second — the load-bearing variable — the debt's trajectory depends on the race between **r**, the average interest rate the government pays, and **g**, the economy's growth rate. When g outruns r, the economy grows faster than the interest compounds, and the debt-to-GDP ratio can fall even while the government runs (modest) deficits — the ratio "melts." When r outruns g, the interest compounds faster than the economy grows, and stabilizing the ratio requires primary surpluses — the ratio "snowballs" unless taxes rise or spending falls. For most of the 2010s, r sat clearly below g, which is what gave Blanchard's address its force. Since 2022, the gap has narrowed or closed: the Treasury now refinances maturing cheap debt at materially higher rates, which is exactly why the interest line in Lesson 1 crossed the defense budget.

<Checkpoint question="Debt held by the public is about 101% of GDP — still below the 106% record of 1946, which the country outgrew without ever 'paying it off.' Why does CBO's 101% worry many economists more than 1946's 106% did?">
  Direction and driver. The 1946 peak was the end point of a war: spending collapsed the next year, the economy boomed, and with g above r the ratio melted for three decades. The 2026 figure is a *waypoint on a rising path* — CBO projects 120% by 2036 — driven not by an emergency that ends but by permanent structure: an aging population, health costs, interest compounding, and revenue persistently below spending at full employment. A stock you are growing into is a different object from the same stock you are growing out of. (Whether that path is catastrophic, manageable, or fixable by what the debt buys is precisely the three-way argument below.)
</Checkpoint>

## Three readings of the same table

<PerspectiveTabs>
  <Perspective name="Fiscal consolidation hawk">
    The hawk's case is compound interest plus political economy. Start with the arithmetic: the Penn Wharton Budget Model estimates that financial markets cannot absorb more than roughly two more decades of current-policy deficits — that U.S. debt cannot rationally exceed an outer bound around 210 percent of GDP even under favorable assumptions, with a one-in-four chance the window closes within 14 years — and that markets price Treasuries today only because they assume Washington corrects course in time. If that assumption ever breaks, the correction arrives suddenly, through rates, not gradually through warnings. Second, the crowd-out is no longer hypothetical: interest already exceeds the defense budget, and every refinancing at post-2022 rates converts old cheap debt into new expensive debt — money that buys nothing. Third, fiscal space is a national-security asset: the United States could deploy trillions in 2008 and 2020 *because* it entered those crises with capacity; entering the next one at 120 percent of GDP means facing it with the credit card already half-drawn. Fourth, generational equity — every dollar of today's full-employment borrowing is a tax invoice mailed to people too young to vote, who will also inherit trust funds that the 2025 trustees say run dry in 2033–2034. And both parties, the hawk notes bitterly, have stopped pretending — the 2025 reconciliation act added an estimated $4.7 trillion to projected debt (CRFB's dynamic tally through 2035), passed by majorities that once called themselves fiscally conservative. The prescription: a consolidation package — entitlement-formula changes plus revenue — enacted *now*, while it can still be gradual, because the one thing every [[term:sovereign-debt]] episode shares is that the adjustment happens either on your schedule or on the market's.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Full-employment (functional finance) dove">
    The dove descends from Abba Lerner's 1943 "functional finance": judge fiscal policy by its *functions* — full employment and stable prices — never by whether a bookkeeping line called the deficit is big or small. A government that issues its own floating currency is not a household; it can always meet obligations denominated in dollars, so the binding constraint is real resources and inflation, not solvency. Modern Monetary Theory, the tradition's contemporary edge (Stephanie Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* is the accessible statement), sharpens this: the question to ask of any spending is "what real resources does it claim, and are they idle?" — not "how will we pay for it?" The dove's evidence is the 2010s, the natural experiment the hawks lost. After 2010, deficit alarm produced premature consolidation across the West — U.S. sequestration, U.K. and eurozone austerity — and the result was a lost decade: the IMF's own Blanchard and Leigh showed in 2013 that austerity's damage had been systematically underestimated, the eurozone double-dipped, and the much-prophesied U.S. debt crisis never came; instead a decade of r below g meant the debt the doves said not to panic over was, in fact, nothing to panic over. [[term:bond]] markets confirmed it: trillion-dollar deficits met some of the lowest borrowing costs in history. The dove reads today's table accordingly: the deficit is the wrong dial. Watch inflation (the overspending signal), watch unemployment (the underspending signal), and treat the 2033 trust-fund dates as accounting conventions Congress can amend — the real question of 2033 is whether the economy can produce what retirees will consume, not whether a ledger shows a positive balance. Cutting support for full employment to soothe a ratio makes real people poorer now to prevent a crisis that exists only in projections.
  </Perspective>
  <Perspective name="Public-investment (capital budgeting) advocate">
    The third camp says both rivals are arguing over the wrong column: the budget's great defect is that it records borrowing without recording what the borrowing *bought*. A family that borrows $400,000 for a house and one that borrows $400,000 for lottery tickets show identical debt; no bank, no firm, and almost no state government accounts this way — they keep capital budgets, separating investment (which creates an asset and future returns) from operating expense (which doesn't). The federal budget does not, so a dollar of bridge, basic research, or early-childhood investment scores exactly like a dollar of any transfer or tax break. The advocate's standard, formalized by Jason Furman and Lawrence Summers in 2020: judge fiscal sustainability by *real interest costs as a share of GDP* and judge borrowing by whether the social return on what it buys exceeds the government's real borrowing cost. History's verdict on that test is generous — the interstate highways, the GI Bill, DARPA's seeding of the internet and GPS, NIH's role in the biotech sector — all debt-financed or tax-financed outlays whose returns dwarfed their carrying cost. The advocate reads today's table with alarm at the *composition*, not just the size, of borrowing: a 6-percent-of-GDP deficit at full employment, devoted overwhelmingly to current consumption (transfers, interest, and tax cuts — the 2025 reconciliation act being the latest), is the worst of both worlds, accumulating obligations without accumulating assets. Borrowing for a national R&D surge or grid modernization at 2 percent real could be self-financing; borrowing the same sum for current consumption is exactly what the hawks say it is. The prescription: a federal capital budget, or at least CBO scoring that credits investment returns — then fight about the operating deficit on hawk-dove terms.
  </Perspective>
</PerspectiveTabs>

Notice what just happened: three camps, one table, and the disagreement is not about facts but about *which variable is sovereign*. The hawk crowns the debt trajectory; the dove crowns inflation and employment; the investor crowns the return on what borrowing buys. That is why budget debates feel like ships passing — the camps are not giving different answers to the same question; they are asking different questions.

## The scholar's lens: Buchanan against Blanchard

<Callout type="scholar">
  The deepest version of this fight predates everyone above. James Buchanan and Richard Wagner's *Democracy in Deficit* (1977) argued that the [[term:keynesian-economics]] revolution loosed something its architects never priced in: once the old balanced-budget *norm* died, democratic politics acquired a permanent asymmetry. Deficits deliver visible benefits to current voters and defer invisible costs to future ones — so politicians of both parties learn to cut taxes in downturns and never raise them in booms, run deficits in recessions and never surpluses in recoveries. Voters, suffering "fiscal illusion," never feel debt-financed government's true price, and demand more of it than they would if fully billed. On the Buchanan view the post-1970s record — deficits in roughly nine of every ten years, booms and busts alike, culminating in 6 percent of GDP at full employment — is not a policy error awaiting better arguments; it is the predictable *equilibrium* of unconstrained democratic budgeting, fixable only by constitutional-level rules. Blanchard's 2019 address is the precise counterpoint: arithmetic, not incentives — if r stays below g, the feared bill never arrives, and Buchanan-style alarm priced debt wrongly for decades. Hold both in mind and you can map almost any budget pundit: those who locate the problem in the *incentive structure* descend from Buchanan; those who locate it (or dissolve it) in the *arithmetic* descend from Blanchard. The 2020s have been unkind to each in turn.
</Callout>

## The tests each camp has to pass

Steel-manning includes the cross-examination, and each camp carries a wound from the last fifteen years.

**The hawks' wound is 2010–2021.** They warned of imminent bond-market punishment from 2009 onward; instead came a decade of record-low rates, and the intellectual centerpiece of the austerity era — Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's claim that growth collapses past 90 percent debt-to-GDP — was found in 2013 (by Herndon, Ash, and Pollin's replication) to rest partly on a spreadsheet error and fragile weighting. Crisis-prediction without a date is unfalsifiable, so hawks must say why this decade's warning differs from the last one's. Their honest answer — r has actually risen, the trust-fund dates now sit inside the ten-year window, and Penn Wharton's bound is about *forward-looking* market tolerance, not a magic threshold — is stronger than the 2010 version was. But they said that then, too.

**The doves' wound is 2021–2023.** The one constraint functional finance names as real — [[term:inflation]] — arrived, hitting a four-decade U.S. high in mid-2022 after the largest peacetime fiscal expansion ever, and the camp's critics (Mankiw's "A Skeptic's Guide to Modern Monetary Theory" is the canonical mainstream audit) had warned precisely that the inflation dial is easy to read in a seminar and brutal to obey in a Congress. When the signal flashed, no functional-finance mechanism throttled spending; the [[term:federal-reserve]]'s rate hikes did the throttling — at the cost of pushing r up toward g and weakening the dove arithmetic everywhere else. Doves fairly reply that supply shocks did much of the work and employment recovered in two years instead of ten — but "the constraint binds, and our institutions ignored it until the central bank acted" is now part of their record. And the 2022 U.K. gilt crisis — a bond-market revolt against unfunded tax cuts that unseated a government in seven weeks — showed even currency-issuing governments where market patience ends.

**The investors' wound is the word "investment."** Every interest group calls its appropriation an investment, and a capital budget without ruthless gatekeeping becomes a hole in the budget through which everything migrates — the reason CBO and OMB have historically resisted one. The camp's test is whether it can name, in advance and in public, the spending it would *refuse* to call capital — and accept hawk-grade discipline on the rest.

<Checkpoint question="A recession hits in 2027: unemployment jumps to 7% and the deficit, already near 6% of GDP, heads toward 9% on its own as revenues fall. What does each camp prescribe — and on what single point do all three converge?">
  The dove prescribes aggressive stimulus — idle resources are the green light, and the automatic deficit increase is the system working. The investment advocate prescribes stimulus *aimed at capital* — recessions are when labor and borrowing are cheapest, so build then. The hawk accepts counter-cyclical deficits (most hawks are not balanced-budget-every-year absolutists) but demands the package be temporary and paired with credible long-run consolidation, precisely so markets fund the surge calmly. The convergence: all three agree the moment to fight a recession is not the moment for sudden spending cuts — their fight is over what happens in the *recovery*, which is where, on the Buchanan reading, democracies always flinch.
</Checkpoint>

## What this lesson doesn't settle

It does not tell you which camp is right, because the honest answer is that each is right about a different failure mode: the hawk about compounding and the political costs of waiting, the dove about the human and economic price of premature consolidation, the investor about the difference between borrowing that builds and borrowing that consumes. A defensible position states which risks it is choosing to run and what evidence would change its mind — for a hawk, a durable return of r below g; for a dove, persistent inflation or a failed Treasury auction; for an investor, a decade of capital spending that underperforms its scoring. What none of the camps controls is the machine that converts any of these philosophies into law: twelve appropriations bills, a reconciliation process with arcane rules, a statutory debt ceiling, and a President testing how much of the spending power can be reclaimed from Congress. That machine — which broke three times in fiscal 2026 alone — is Lesson 3.
