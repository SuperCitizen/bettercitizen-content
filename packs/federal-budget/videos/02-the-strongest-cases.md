## A heresy in Atlanta

On January 4, 2019, Olivier Blanchard, former chief economist of the IMF, used the most prestigious lectern in economics to say something close to heresy: when the interest rate on government debt is below the economy's growth rate, "public debt may have no fiscal cost."

- Blanchard, 2019

Seven years later the United States owes 39.2 trillion dollars, and pays more interest than it spends on defense.

- Total federal debt: $39.2 trillion

## r against g

Call the average interest rate the government pays r, and the economy's growth rate g. When g outruns r, the debt ratio can fall even while the government runs modest deficits. When r outruns g, stabilizing the ratio requires primary surpluses. For most of the 2010s, r sat below g. Since 2022, the gap has narrowed or closed.

- g above r: the ratio melts
- r above g: the ratio snowballs

> The debt's path is a race between r and g.

## The fiscal consolidation hawk

The hawk argues from compound interest: every refinancing converts old cheap debt into new expensive debt. The hawk points to the Penn Wharton Budget Model, which estimates that markets cannot absorb more than roughly two more decades of current-policy deficits. The prescription is to consolidate now, while it can still be gradual.

- Penn Wharton: roughly two more decades

> The hawk: adjust on your schedule, or on the market's.

## The full-employment dove

The dove argues that fiscal policy should be judged by its functions, full employment and stable prices, never by the size of the deficit. The dove points to the 2010s: austerity brought a lost decade, the prophesied U.S. debt crisis never came, and trillion-dollar deficits met some of the lowest borrowing costs in history. The prescription is to watch inflation and unemployment.

- The 2010s: no debt crisis, low rates

> The dove: the deficit is the wrong dial.

## The public-investment advocate

The third camp argues that the budget records borrowing without recording what the borrowing bought. A family that borrows for a house and one that borrows for lottery tickets show identical debt. The advocate points to the interstate highways, the GI Bill and DARPA, whose returns dwarfed their carrying cost. The prescription is a federal capital budget, which separates investment from operating expense.

- A house, or lottery tickets?
- Highways, the GI Bill, DARPA

> The investor: judge borrowing by what it buys.

## Three different questions

The lesson does not say which camp is right. Each crowns a different variable: the debt trajectory, inflation and employment, or the return on what borrowing buys.

- Three camps, three different questions
