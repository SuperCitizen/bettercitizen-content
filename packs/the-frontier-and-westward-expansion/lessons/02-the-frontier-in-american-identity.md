# The frontier in American identity

If manifest destiny describes how the United States acquired a continent, the idea of "the frontier" describes how Americans made sense of what that meant. For more than a century, the frontier served as a master story about national character — and that story has been both celebrated and sharply revised.

## Turner's Frontier Thesis

In 1893, at a meeting of the American Historical Association in Chicago, the young historian Frederick Jackson Turner delivered a paper that would shape American history for generations: "The Significance of the Frontier in American History." He opened with a striking claim:

> "The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development."

Turner argued that the frontier — the meeting point between settlement and unsettled land — had repeatedly forced Americans to begin again from primitive conditions, and that this process forged a distinctive national character. The frontier, he wrote, "promoted the formation of a composite nationality for the American people." He credited it with fostering democracy, individualism, practicality, and restless energy, and he treated the availability of "free land" as a safety valve that gave ordinary people opportunity and independence.

## "The closing of the frontier"

Turner's argument was prompted by an observation in the report of the **1890 census**, which noted that the country no longer had a clear, continuous frontier line of settlement. For Turner this marked the end of an era. "And now," he concluded, "four centuries from the discovery of America, at the end of a hundred years of life under the Constitution, the frontier has gone, and with its going has closed the first period of American history."

The thesis was enormously influential. It offered a tidy explanation for why American democracy and culture seemed different from Europe's, and it framed the West not as a place but as a process of self-renewal. For decades it shaped how the West was taught, written about, and imagined in popular culture.

## Myth and reality

Alongside Turner's scholarship grew a romantic popular image — the rugged cowboy, the self-made pioneer, the lone homesteader taming the wilderness. Dime novels, Wild West shows, and later films cemented this myth.

The documented reality was harsher and more crowded. Homesteading was often grueling and frequently failed: drought, isolation, debt, and poor land defeated many families, and large speculators and railroads claimed much of the best terrain. The land was not vacant. Settlement depended on the removal and confinement of Native nations.

The **Dawes Severalty Act of 1887** illustrates the point. It broke up communally held tribal lands into individual allotments, with "surplus" land opened to non-Native buyers. The stated aim was to assimilate Native people into individual farming; the practical effect was a dramatic loss of Native landholdings — from roughly 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934. Removal, broken treaties, reservations, and allotment were not incidental to the frontier story; they were central to it.

## The New Western History

Beginning in the 1980s, a group of scholars sometimes called the **New Western History** challenged Turner's framework. Rather than treating the West as a process that ended in 1890, they treated it as a region with a continuous, contested past. They emphasized conquest, environmental change, and the experiences of Native peoples, Hispanic and Asian residents, women, and laborers — voices largely absent from the triumphal account.

The historian Patricia Nelson Limerick, in *The Legacy of Conquest* (1987), argued that the West is best understood through the ongoing consequences of conquest rather than a closed frontier. Conquest, she wrote, "left a substantial legacy" of unresolved conflicts over land, water, and belonging that persist into the present.

These revisions do not simply discard Turner. Historians still debate how much the frontier shaped American democracy, and Turner's thesis remains a landmark precisely because so much later work defines itself for or against it. The frontier endures as one of the most powerful — and most contested — ideas Americans have used to explain themselves.
