# Manifest destiny

In the first half of the nineteenth century, many Americans came to believe that the United States was destined to spread across the North American continent. This belief — a mix of nationalism, religious conviction, and economic ambition — acquired a name in 1845 and a momentum that reshaped the map of the nation.

## The phrase and the doctrine

The term "manifest destiny" was popularized by the journalist John L. O'Sullivan. Writing in 1845 in support of annexing Texas, he argued that other nations should not interfere with "the fulfillment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions."

> "Away, away with all these cobweb tissues of rights of discovery, exploration, settlement... The American claim is by the right of our manifest destiny to overspread and to possess the whole of the continent which Providence has given us."

The doctrine was never an official policy, but it captured a widely shared assumption: that American expansion was natural, inevitable, and morally justified. It bound together republican ideals, the promise of land for ordinary settlers, and a sense of national mission.

## Acquiring a continent

Expansion was accomplished through purchase, negotiation, annexation, and war.

The **Louisiana Purchase (1803)** doubled the size of the country. President Thomas Jefferson acquired roughly 828,000 square miles from France for about $15 million, extending the United States from the Mississippi River toward the Rocky Mountains.

The **annexation of Texas (1845)** brought the independent Republic of Texas into the Union and heightened tensions with Mexico, which had not recognized Texan independence.

The **Mexican–American War (1846–1848)** followed. Its end came with the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)**, in which Mexico ceded a vast territory — including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and parts of Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming — in exchange for $15 million. The war was militarily decisive but politically divisive; critics, among them a young Congressman Abraham Lincoln, questioned its justification.

The **Oregon Country** was settled by treaty with Britain in 1846, fixing the boundary at the 49th parallel and securing the Pacific Northwest. The **Gadsden Purchase (1853)** added a final strip of land in present-day southern Arizona and New Mexico, intended in part to facilitate a southern railroad route.

## Opening the West

Acquiring territory was one thing; settling it was another. Two policies and a discovery accelerated the process.

The **Homestead Act of 1862** offered 160 acres of public land to any adult citizen (or intended citizen) who would live on and improve the claim for five years. Over time it transferred some 270 million acres into private hands and drew hundreds of thousands of families westward.

The **Pacific Railway Act (1862)**, along with later legislation, authorized and subsidized a transcontinental railroad, completed in 1869 at Promontory Summit, Utah. Railroads collapsed travel times, opened markets, and made distant lands commercially viable. They were granted enormous tracts of public land along their routes.

The discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill touched off the **California Gold Rush (1848–1855)**, drawing roughly 300,000 people and hastening California's statehood in 1850.

## The costs of expansion

Manifest destiny was a powerful national ideology, and it was also a justification whose costs are part of the historical record. The lands opened to settlers were not empty. They were home to many Native nations, and expansion proceeded through their dispossession.

The **Indian Removal Act of 1830** authorized the federal government to negotiate the removal of southeastern tribes to lands west of the Mississippi. Its enforcement produced forced marches such as the Cherokee "Trail of Tears," on which thousands died. As settlement pushed further west, treaties were repeatedly made and broken, and Native peoples were confined to shrinking reservations.

The Mexican–American War, which secured much of the Southwest, also brought a large Spanish-speaking population under U.S. jurisdiction and intensified national conflict over whether slavery would expand into the new territories — a conflict that helped set the stage for the Civil War.

The historian Richard White has emphasized that the federal government was the central actor in conquering, surveying, distributing, and governing the West — and that the romance of self-reliant settlement obscures the violence and public power that made expansion possible. Holding both truths together — expansion as national achievement and as conquest — is essential to understanding what manifest destiny meant.
