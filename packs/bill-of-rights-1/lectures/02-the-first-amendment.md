Think of a time you wore something because of what it said. A shirt with a message, maybe. Now picture being sent home from school for wearing it.

[beat]

That happened to a group of students in Des Moines, Iowa.

In December of 1965, they made a plan. The United States was fighting a war in Vietnam, and they wanted to show that they hoped it would end. So they decided to wear black armbands to school. One of them was Mary Beth Tinker. She was 13 years old.

The principals heard about the plan, and they made a rule against armbands. The students wore them anyway. [beat] And they were sent home.

[pause]

Think about what those students had done. They had not shouted. They had not stopped any class. They had only worn a strip of black cloth.

Their families went to court, and the case went all the way to the Supreme Court. In 1969, the Court ruled, 7 to 2, that the schools had been wrong.

Students do not give up their freedom of speech, the Court said, "at the schoolhouse gate."

[pause]

Why did the students win? We will find out in four steps. First, the five freedoms that one sentence protects. Second, who has to follow that sentence. Third, the right not to speak. And last, the limits.

## Five freedoms in one sentence

The students won because of the First Amendment. It is one sentence long. [beat] And it protects five freedoms. Count them with me.

One. Religion. The government may not set up an official religion, and it may not stop you from following yours.

Two. Speech. You may say what you think, even when leaders do not like it.

Three. The press. That means newspapers, books and websites, and they may report what they find.

Four. Assembly, which means gathering. People may gather peacefully in groups.

Five. Petition, which means asking. People may ask the government to fix a problem.

[pause]

> Religion. Speech. The press. Assembly. Petition.

Now go back to the second freedom, because it is wider than it sounds. The armbands were speech, because they sent a message without a word being said. A sign is speech too, and so is a drawing or a T-shirt.

> Speech is more than talking.

## Who has to follow it?

So the First Amendment protects five freedoms. But who has to follow it?

Listen to how it begins: "Congress shall make no law."

[pause]

Those words tell you something important. The amendment is a limit on the government.

A public school is part of the government. That is why the Des Moines schools had to follow it.

Your parents are not the government. Neither is a company that runs a website or a game. So if a parent takes away your phone, or a website removes your post, the First Amendment has nothing to say about it.

> The First Amendment limits the government, not families or private companies.

Let's test that. Here are four things that might happen. Listen for the one that is a First Amendment problem.

A parent tells a child not to post a video.

A website removes a comment that broke its rules.

A city arrests someone for holding a sign about the mayor.

A friend stops talking to you after an argument.

Which one is it? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is the city. Only the city is part of the government, and arresting a person for a peaceful sign is the government punishing speech.

A parent, a website and a friend may all be unfair. But the First Amendment does not apply to them.

## The right not to speak

Here is the third step, and it may surprise you. Freedom of speech also protects staying silent.

> The government cannot make you say something you do not believe.

In the 1940s, West Virginia required every student to salute the flag and say the Pledge of Allegiance. Some families belonged to a religion that taught them not to salute any flag. Their children refused. And the schools expelled them, which means the schools put them out.

The Supreme Court took their side in 1943. No official, it said, can decide what people must believe, or force them to say it.

Now put the two cases together, and you can see how wide this freedom is. The students in Iowa wanted to say something that many adults disliked. The students in West Virginia wanted to stay silent when everyone else was speaking. [beat] The First Amendment protected both groups.

## Rights have limits

One last step. No freedom covers everything.

Go back to the armband case. The armbands were allowed because they were quiet. A student who shouted through every lesson would not win the same case. The Court gave a rule for that, too.

> A school may stop speech that seriously gets in the way of class.

Let's try that rule on the shirt we began with. A student wears a T-shirt to a public school. The shirt has a message about an election. The principal dislikes the message, but no class is disturbed. Can the school punish the student? What would you say?

[long pause]

Probably not. Start with two things you already know. A public school is part of the government. And the shirt is speech.

In the armband case, the Supreme Court said a school needs more than dislike of a message. It needs a real reason to expect that the speech will seriously disrupt school. To disrupt something is to get in its way.

And if the shirt did cause that kind of trouble? Then the answer could change.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. The First Amendment is one sentence long, and it protects five freedoms. Can you still name all five?

[long pause]

Religion. Speech. The press. Assembly. Petition.

[beat]

Two. Speech is more than talking. An armband is speech, and students do not give up their freedom of speech at the schoolhouse gate.

[beat]

Three. The First Amendment limits the government, not families or private companies. A public school is part of the government.

[beat]

Four. The government cannot make you say something you do not believe. Freedom of speech protects the students who speak, and the students who stay silent.

[beat]

Five. No freedom covers everything. A school may stop speech that seriously gets in the way of class.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. Where do the lines belong? Judges still argue about that, and new cases come up every year.

The First Amendment is only the first of ten. Nine more amendments follow it, and all ten together are called the Bill of Rights. The next lecture goes through the rest. It begins with a man who wrote to the Supreme Court from a prison cell.

For now, remember Mary Beth Tinker. She was 13 years old, and she wore a strip of black cloth to school. [beat] She did not give up her freedom of speech at the schoolhouse gate.
