You know this feeling. Everyone around you is ready to say yes, and it would be easy to go along. But something in you says: not yet.

[beat]

Now picture a room where that happened. It is September 17, 1787. The men who wrote the Constitution are lining up to sign it. One of them is George Mason of Virginia. [beat] And Mason will not pick up the pen.

[pause]

Mason had worked on the plan all summer, and he agreed with much of it. But he had found something missing.

The Constitution said a great deal about what the new government could do. It said almost nothing about what the government could not do to ordinary people.

So Mason wrote down what he thought was wrong with the plan. His words were printed and passed around the country. Many Americans read them, and agreed.

[pause]

What Mason wanted was a list of rights. Today we will find out how that list got into the Constitution. We will take three steps. First, what a right is. Second, the argument over writing the rights down. And third, how the list was added at last.

## What is a right?

Start with the word itself.

> A right is a freedom the government must respect.

It is a freedom that belongs to you. The right to say what you think is one example. The right to follow your own religion is another.

Mason and many others wanted a written list of rights added to the Constitution. A list like that has a name. It is called a bill of rights.

Here is a way to picture one. Imagine a row of signs that say "Keep Out." Now imagine that every sign faces the government. Behind the signs are the freedoms that belong to the people.

That is what a bill of rights is. It does not tell citizens what to do.

> A bill of rights tells the government where it has to stop.

One sign says the government may not punish you for what you say. Another says it may not search your home without a good reason.

The picture is not perfect, though. A sign cannot stop anyone by itself. [beat] Rights on paper only work when courts enforce them. To enforce a right is to make sure it is respected. And people have to insist on their rights, too.

## The argument

So Mason wanted a list. Not everyone thought a list was needed.

James Madison had done more than anyone to design the Constitution. And at first, Madison said no.

Here was his reason. The new government could only use the powers the Constitution gave it. And nothing in the Constitution gave it the power to take away anyone's freedom.

Put another way, the government had a list of powers, and taking away freedom was not on it.

The other side had a simple answer. If the government was never going to take away those freedoms, what was the harm in saying so in writing?

[pause]

The argument nearly sank the Constitution. Several states agreed to approve it only after a promise. The promise was that a list of rights would be added.

Now a question for you. Madison said a list of rights was not needed, because the government had only the powers on its list. Why did many people want the rights written down anyway? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Here is why. Leaders might stretch their powers further than the writers expected. With a list, a person can point to the exact words and say, "The government may not do this." And a court can point to the same words.

> A written promise is much harder to ignore.

One more worry, before we go on. It came from some supporters of the Constitution. Suppose ten rights were written down. People might start to think those were the only rights they had.

That worry did not go away. Hold on to it. The Bill of Rights has an answer, and you will hear it in the last of these lectures.

## Madison changes his mind

Now comes the turn. The promise was kept. [beat] And the man who kept it was Madison himself.

He had come to see how much the voters cared about it. So on June 8, 1789, in the very first Congress, he stood up and proposed a list of amendments.

An amendment is a change added to the Constitution. The man who had said no list was needed was now asking for one.

Congress argued over the list for months. Listen to the numbers as they fall. The House approved seventeen amendments. The Senate cut them down to twelve. Those twelve went to the states. [beat] And the states approved ten.

Seventeen. Twelve. Ten.

On December 15, 1791, those ten became part of the Constitution. They have been called the Bill of Rights ever since.

> The Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments.

## Who gets the orders?

One idea is left, and it matters most. Who does the Bill of Rights give orders to?

A few minutes ago, I asked you to picture a row of signs that say "Keep Out." Which way did those signs face? Think back.

[long pause]

Every sign faced the government.

So the Bill of Rights gives its orders to the government, about what it may not do. It does not tell citizens how they must behave. It does not tell businesses or other countries what to do.

You can hear it in the words. The First Amendment begins this way: "Congress shall make no law."

[pause]

Congress shall make no law. Those words are talking to the government, and to no one else.

> The Bill of Rights is a list of limits on the government.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. A right is a freedom that belongs to you, and that the government must respect. A bill of rights is a written list of rights.

[beat]

Two. The Constitution was signed without that list. George Mason would not sign, and several states approved it only after they were promised a list.

[beat]

Three. Madison said at first that no list was needed. Then he kept the promise himself, and proposed a list of amendments. An amendment is a change added to the Constitution. How many of them did the states approve?

[long pause]

Ten. Those first ten amendments are the Bill of Rights.

[beat]

Four. The Bill of Rights is a list of limits on the government. It tells the government where it has to stop.

[pause]

Here is what we have not settled. Remember the second worry. If ten rights are written down, are those the only rights people have? The answer comes in the last of these lectures.

And remember the flaw in our picture. Rights on paper only work when courts enforce them, and when people insist on them.

The next lecture takes up the first of the ten amendments, which is also the most famous. What does it protect? We will start with a group of students in Iowa.

For now, go back to that room in 1787. George Mason would not pick up the pen, because the Constitution had no list of rights. [beat] A little more than four years later, it had one.
