Think of a time you were blamed for something. Everyone else in the room knew the rules, and you did not. And you had to speak up for yourself, alone.

[beat]

Now picture that in a courtroom.

A man named Clarence Earl Gideon was arrested in Florida. He was charged with breaking into a building. He had left school after the eighth grade, and he had no money. So when his trial began, he asked the judge to give him a lawyer.

The judge said no.

[pause]

In Florida at that time, a poor person got a free lawyer only when the punishment could be death.

So Gideon had to be his own lawyer. He questioned the witnesses, and he spoke to the jury as well as he could. He lost. He was sent to prison for five years.

From his cell, Gideon wrote a letter by hand to the Supreme Court of the United States. He said the Constitution promised him a lawyer.

In 1963, all nine of its judges, who are called justices, agreed with him.

[pause]

Since then, any person accused of a serious crime, who cannot pay for a lawyer, must be given one.

And Gideon himself got a new trial, with a lawyer this time. [beat] The jury found him not guilty.

[pause]

The promise Gideon wrote about is in the Bill of Rights. Last time, we took the First Amendment. Now we take the other nine, in four steps. First, the rights of a person accused of a crime. Second, two amendments about weapons and soldiers. Third, the last two, which do a different kind of job. And last, a surprise about who has to follow these rules.

## The rights of the accused

Gideon's promise came from the Sixth Amendment. And the Sixth has company. Half of the Bill of Rights is about how the government must treat people it suspects of a crime. Those are the Fourth through the Eighth Amendments.

But why protect people who may have broken the law? Think about Gideon, and take a moment.

[long pause]

Here is the reason.

> Being accused is not the same as being guilty.

Gideon was accused, and in the end a jury found him not guilty. Anyone can be accused, including someone who did nothing wrong.

Now the five amendments, in order.

The Fourth. The police need a good reason, and often a judge's permission, to search you or your home.

The Fifth. You cannot be forced to speak against yourself, and you cannot be tried twice for the same crime.

The Sixth. You get a quick and public trial, a jury, and a lawyer.

The Seventh. A jury can also decide arguments over money or property.

The Eighth. Punishments may not be cruel, and fines may not be far too large.

[pause]

Let's put one of them to work. A police officer stops a person on the sidewalk. The officer wants to look through her backpack, for no reason at all. Which amendment protects her?

[long pause]

The Fourth Amendment. It protects people from searches that are not reasonable. The officer needs a real reason to search. And in many cases, the officer needs permission from a judge, which is called a warrant.

## The Second and Third

Now two amendments from earlier in the list.

The Second Amendment protects the right to keep and bear arms, which means to own and carry weapons. Americans disagree strongly about which gun laws it allows, and courts are still deciding cases about it.

The Third Amendment says the government cannot make you let soldiers live in your house.

That may sound strange now. But before the American Revolution, British soldiers had been allowed to take over people's homes. Americans did not want that to happen again.

## The Ninth and Tenth

The last two amendments do a different kind of job.

Two lectures ago, I asked you to hold on to a worry. Here it is again. A list of rights might make people think those were the only rights they had.

The Ninth Amendment is the answer. It says that people have other rights too, even if the list does not name them.

> The Ninth Amendment says people have other rights that are not on the list.

That leaves the Tenth.

> The Tenth Amendment is about powers, not rights.

It says that any power the Constitution does not give to the national government belongs to the states, or to the people.

## Who has to follow these rules?

Here is the surprise. When the Bill of Rights was new, a state could ignore most of it.

> At first, the Bill of Rights limited only the national government.

That changed after the Civil War. In 1868, the Fourteenth Amendment was added. It says that no state may take away a person's life, liberty or property unfairly. Liberty is another word for freedom.

Over the next hundred years and more, the Supreme Court used those words to make the states follow almost all of the Bill of Rights.

Now think back to the start of this lecture. Gideon was tried in Florida, and Florida is a state. Which amendment gave him the right to a lawyer? And why did Florida have to obey it?

[long pause]

It was the Sixth Amendment. The Sixth lists the rights of a person on trial, and one of them is the help of a lawyer.

And Florida had to obey it because of the change that came after the Civil War. Gideon's case was one step in that change.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Three things to keep, and then the whole list.

One. Half of the Bill of Rights protects people the government suspects of a crime. Being accused is not the same as being guilty.

[beat]

Two. The last two amendments do a different job. The Ninth is about rights that are not on the list. The Tenth is about powers, not rights.

[beat]

Three. At first, the Bill of Rights limited only the national government. After the Fourteenth Amendment, the Supreme Court made the states follow almost all of it.

[pause]

Now the whole list, in order. See how many you can call to mind before I get there.

The First. [beat] Religion, speech, the press, assembly and petition.

The Second. [beat] The right to keep and bear arms.

The Third. [beat] No soldiers living in your home without your say.

The Fourth. [beat] No unreasonable searches.

The Fifth. [beat] You need not speak against yourself. No second trial for the same crime.

The Sixth. [beat] A quick and public trial, a jury, and a lawyer.

The Seventh. [beat] A jury in arguments over money or property.

The Eighth. [beat] No cruel punishments and no huge fines.

The Ninth. [beat] People have other rights that are not on the list.

The Tenth. [beat] Powers not given to the national government belong to the states or the people.

[pause]

Here is what is not settled. The Bill of Rights is more than two hundred years old, and people still argue about what its words mean. What makes a search unreasonable? What makes a punishment cruel?

Each time a new question comes up, a court has to decide. And ordinary people are often the ones who ask.

Clarence Gideon was one of them. He had no money and no lawyer. [beat] He wrote a letter by hand, from a prison cell, and all nine justices agreed with him.
