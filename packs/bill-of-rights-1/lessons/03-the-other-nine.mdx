# What do the other nine amendments protect?

Clarence Earl Gideon was arrested in Florida and charged with breaking into a building. He had left school after the eighth grade, and he had no money. When his trial began, he asked the judge to give him a lawyer.

The judge said no. In Florida at that time, a poor person got a free lawyer only when the punishment could be death.

So Gideon had to be his own lawyer. He questioned the witnesses and spoke to the jury as well as he could. He lost, and he was sent to prison for five years.

From his cell, Gideon wrote a letter by hand to the Supreme Court of the United States. He said the Constitution promised him a lawyer. In 1963, all nine justices agreed with him. Since then, any person accused of a serious crime who cannot pay for a lawyer must be given one.

Gideon got a new trial, with a lawyer this time, and the jury found him not guilty.

## The rights of the accused

Gideon's promise came from the Sixth Amendment. Half of the Bill of Rights, the Fourth through the Eighth Amendments, is about how the government must treat people it suspects of a crime.

Why protect people who may have broken the law? Because being accused is not the same as being guilty, and anyone can be accused, including someone who did nothing wrong.

<Figure caption="The five amendments that protect a person the government suspects or accuses." source="Constitution of the United States, Amendments IV through VIII">

- **Fourth.** The police need a good reason, and often a judge's permission, to search you or your home.
- **Fifth.** You cannot be forced to speak against yourself, and you cannot be tried twice for the same crime.
- **Sixth.** You get a quick and public trial, a jury, and a lawyer.
- **Seventh.** A jury can also decide arguments over money or property.
- **Eighth.** Punishments may not be cruel, and fines may not be far too large.

</Figure>

## The Second and Third

The **Second Amendment** protects the right to keep and bear arms, which means to own and carry weapons. Americans disagree strongly about which gun laws it allows, and courts are still deciding cases about it.

The **Third Amendment** says the government cannot make you let soldiers live in your house. That may sound strange now. Before the American Revolution, British soldiers had been allowed to take over people's homes, and Americans did not want that to happen again.

## The Ninth and Tenth

The last two amendments do a different kind of job.

Remember the worry from the first lesson, that a list of rights might make people think those were the only rights they had? The **Ninth Amendment** is the answer. It says that people have other rights too, even if the list does not name them.

The **Tenth Amendment** is about powers, not rights. It says that any power the Constitution does not give to the national government belongs to the states or to the people.

## Who has to follow these rules?

Here is a surprise. When the Bill of Rights was new, it limited only the national government. A state could ignore most of it.

That changed after the Civil War. In 1868 the Fourteenth Amendment was added, and it says that no *state* may take away a person's life, liberty or property unfairly. Over the next hundred years and more, the Supreme Court used those words to make the states follow almost all of the Bill of Rights.

Gideon's case was one step in that change. Florida, a state, had to obey the Sixth Amendment.

## Still being argued

The Bill of Rights is more than two hundred years old, and people still argue about what its words mean. What makes a search "unreasonable"? What makes a punishment "cruel"? Each time a new question comes up, a court has to decide, and ordinary people such as Clarence Gideon are often the ones who ask.
