# What does the First Amendment protect?

In December of 1965, a group of students in Des Moines, Iowa, made a plan. The United States was fighting a war in Vietnam, and they wanted to show that they hoped it would end. They decided to wear black armbands to school. One of them, Mary Beth Tinker, was 13 years old.

The principals heard about the plan and made a rule against armbands. The students wore them anyway, and they were sent home.

They had not shouted or stopped any class. They had only worn a strip of black cloth. Their families went to court, and the case went all the way to the Supreme Court. In 1969 the Court ruled 7 to 2 that the schools had been wrong.

Students do not give up their freedom of speech, the Court said, "at the schoolhouse gate."

## Five freedoms in one sentence

The students won because of the First Amendment. It is one sentence long, and it protects five freedoms.

<Figure caption="The five freedoms the First Amendment protects." source="Constitution of the United States, Amendment I">

- **Religion.** The government may not set up an official religion, and it may not stop you from following yours.
- **Speech.** You may say what you think, even when leaders do not like it.
- **The press.** Newspapers, books and websites may report what they find.
- **Assembly.** People may gather peacefully in groups.
- **Petition.** People may ask the government to fix a problem.

</Figure>

Speech is more than talking. The armbands were speech, because they sent a message without a word being said. So is a sign, a drawing or a T-shirt.

## Who has to follow it?

The First Amendment begins with the words "Congress shall make no law." Those words tell you something important. The amendment is a limit on the *government*.

A public school is part of the government, which is why the Des Moines schools had to follow it. Your parents are not the government, and neither is a company that runs a website or a game. If a parent takes away your phone, or a website removes your post, the First Amendment has nothing to say about it.

## The right not to speak

Freedom of speech also means the government cannot make you say something you do not believe.

In the 1940s, West Virginia required every student to salute the flag and say the Pledge of Allegiance. Some families belonged to a religion that taught them not to salute any flag. Their children refused, and the schools expelled them.

The Supreme Court took their side in 1943. No official, it said, can decide what people must believe or force them to say it.

Put the two cases together and you can see how wide this freedom is. The students in Iowa wanted to say something that many adults disliked. The students in West Virginia wanted to stay silent when everyone else was speaking. The First Amendment protected both groups.

## Rights have limits

No freedom covers everything. In the armband case, the Court also said that a school may stop speech that seriously gets in the way of class. The armbands were allowed because they were quiet. A student who shouted through every lesson would not win the same case.

Judges still argue about where the lines belong, and new cases come up every year.

<Checkpoint question="A student wears a T-shirt with a message about an election to a public school. The principal dislikes the message, but no class is disturbed. Can the school punish the student?">

Probably not. A public school is part of the government, and the shirt is speech. In the armband case the Supreme Court said a school needs more than dislike of a message. It needs a real reason to expect that the speech will seriously disrupt school. If the shirt did cause that kind of trouble, the answer could change.

</Checkpoint>
