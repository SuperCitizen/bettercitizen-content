# Why does the Constitution have a Bill of Rights?

On September 17, 1787, the men who had written the Constitution lined up to sign it. George Mason of Virginia would not pick up the pen.

Mason had worked on the plan all summer, and he agreed with much of it. But he had found something missing. The Constitution said a great deal about what the new government could do. It said almost nothing about what the government could *not* do to ordinary people.

Mason wrote down his objections, and they were printed and passed around the country. A lot of Americans read them and agreed with him.

## What is a right?

A *right* is a freedom that belongs to you, and that the government has to respect. The right to say what you think is one example. The right to follow your own religion is another.

Mason and many others wanted a written list of rights added to the Constitution. A list like that is called a *bill of rights*.

Here is a way to picture it. Imagine a row of signs that say "Keep Out," and imagine that every sign faces the government. Behind the signs are the freedoms that belong to the people. That is what a bill of rights is. It does not tell citizens what to do. It tells the government where it has to stop. One sign says the government may not punish you for what you say. Another says it may not search your home without a good reason.

The picture is not perfect, though. A sign cannot stop anyone by itself. Rights on paper only work when courts enforce them and when people insist on them.

## The argument

Not everyone thought a list was needed. James Madison, who had done more than anyone to design the Constitution, said at first that it was not. The new government could only use the powers the Constitution gave it, he argued, and nothing in the Constitution gave it the power to take away anyone's freedom.

The other side had a simple answer. If the government was never going to take away those freedoms, what was the harm in saying so in writing?

Some supporters of the Constitution had a second worry. If ten rights were written down, people might start to think those were the only rights they had. That worry did not go away, and you will see in the last lesson how the Bill of Rights answers it.

The argument nearly sank the Constitution. Several states agreed to approve it only after they were promised that a list of rights would be added.

<Checkpoint question="Madison said a list of rights was not needed, because the government had only the powers on its list. Why did many people want the rights written down anyway?">

Because a written promise is much harder to ignore. Leaders might stretch their powers further than the writers expected. With a list, a person can point to the exact words and say, "The government may not do this." A court can point to the same words.

</Checkpoint>

## Madison changes his mind

Madison kept the promise himself. He had come to see how much the voters cared about it. On June 8, 1789, in the very first Congress, he stood up and proposed a list of amendments.

Congress argued over the list for months. The House approved seventeen amendments, the Senate cut them down to twelve, and those twelve went to the states. The states approved ten of them. On December 15, 1791, those ten became part of the Constitution, and they have been called the Bill of Rights ever since.
