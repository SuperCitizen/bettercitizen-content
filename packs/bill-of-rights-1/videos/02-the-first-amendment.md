## A strip of black cloth

In December of 1965, a group of students in Des Moines, Iowa, made a plan. The United States was fighting a war in Vietnam, and they hoped it would end. They decided to wear black armbands to school. One of them, Mary Beth Tinker, was 13 years old.

The principals made a rule against armbands. The students wore them anyway, and they were sent home.

- Des Moines, Iowa, 1965
- Black armbands, worn quietly

## The Court's answer

They had not shouted or stopped any class. Their families went to court, and the case went all the way to the Supreme Court. In 1969 the Court ruled 7 to 2 that the schools had been wrong.

- 1969: the students win, 7 to 2

Students do not give up their freedom of speech, the Court said, "at the schoolhouse gate."

> Students keep their freedom of speech at school.

## Five freedoms, one sentence

The students won because of the First Amendment. It is one sentence long, and it protects five freedoms: religion, speech, the press, assembly and petition. Assembly means gathering peacefully in groups. Petition means asking the government to fix a problem.

- Religion
- Speech
- The press
- Assembly and petition

Speech is more than talking. The armbands were speech, because they sent a message without a word being said.

## Who has to follow it?

The First Amendment begins with the words "Congress shall make no law." So it is a limit on the government.

A public school is part of the government. Your parents are not, and neither is a company that runs a website or a game. If a website removes your post, the First Amendment has nothing to say about it.

- A public school: part of the government
- A parent or a company: not the government

> The First Amendment limits the government, not parents or companies.

## Rights have limits

No freedom covers everything. The Court also said a school may stop speech that seriously gets in the way of class. The armbands were allowed because they were quiet.

> The armbands were speech, and they did not disrupt school.
