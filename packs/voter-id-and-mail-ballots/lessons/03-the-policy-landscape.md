# The current policy landscape

Election-administration policy is overwhelmingly state-level, with federal floor and federal litigation in the background. Recent developments have moved both directions — some states tightening, others loosening — without convergence.

## Post-2020 state-level changes

After the 2020 election, multiple states passed major voting laws:

- **Restrictive direction**: Georgia (SB 202, 2021), Texas (SB 1, 2021), Florida (SB 90, 2021), Iowa, Arizona, others. Common features: stricter ID requirements, reduced mail-ballot drop-box availability, shorter mail-ballot windows, restrictions on third-party ballot collection.
- **Expansive direction**: Vermont (universal mail), New York (early voting expansion, automatic registration), California (expanded mail), Michigan (constitutional amendment expanding access), Massachusetts (no-excuse mail).

The split tracks partisan control of state legislatures. Restrictive measures generally pass in Republican-controlled states; expansive measures in Democratic-controlled states.

## Federal litigation post-Shelby

*Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) invalidated VRA Section 5's preclearance formula — the requirement that historically-discriminatory jurisdictions get pre-clearance from the DOJ before changing voting laws. After Shelby, voting laws can still be challenged under VRA Section 2 (which prohibits discriminatory voting practices) but not preemptively prevented.

*Brnovich v. DNC* (2021) narrowed Section 2's reach for vote-denial cases, requiring plaintiffs to show that disparate impact is "substantial" — a higher bar than the previous "results test." Combined with Shelby, the federal litigation landscape has shifted toward fewer successful pre-emptive challenges.

The result: federal courts can still strike down voting laws, but the bar is higher and the timing is post-implementation.

## Federal legislative proposals

- **Freedom to Vote Act (S. 2747).** Comprehensive voting-rights reform: automatic voter registration, election-day registration, mandatory minimum early-voting periods, no-excuse mail-ballot access, redistricting reforms, campaign-finance disclosure. Passed House; fell short on Senate cloture. Will return.
- **John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act (H.R. 4).** Restores VRA preclearance with an updated formula. Passed House; fell short on Senate cloture. Will return.
- **Election Reform Act variants.** Smaller-bore federal reforms — uniform mail-ballot standards, federal funding for election security, etc. Periodic and partisan-aligned.

The Senate filibuster has been the binding constraint. Senate Democrats considered carve-outs for voting-rights legislation but did not have the votes.

## Voter ID specifically

Roughly 35 states + D.C. have some form of voter-ID requirement; 8 states are strict-photo-ID. The number has been climbing slowly but the changes are at the margin. Major shifts have been few:

- Texas, Wisconsin, North Carolina passed strict ID laws in the 2010s.
- Several other states have tightened or modified existing requirements.
- A few states (Pennsylvania, Missouri) have struck down ID laws under state constitutional grounds.

National polling shows voter ID has substantial public support across partisan lines (often 70%+). The political fight is about implementation details (which IDs qualify, how easy is it to get a free ID, what alternatives exist) more than about ID-or-no-ID.

## What's likely next

- **Continued state-level divergence.** Restrictive states will pass further restrictions; expansive states will continue expanding access. The patchwork will deepen.
- **Federal RCV / voting-rights legislation.** Will return periodically but face filibuster constraints.
- **Risk-limiting audits.** Bipartisan support for post-election audit standards may produce smaller-bore legislation.
- **Election-administration standards.** Bipartisan concerns about election infrastructure (cybersecurity, backup paper ballots, post-election audits) may produce smaller-scope legislation.
- **Restoration of VRA preclearance.** Politically difficult but consistently on the agenda when the Senate dynamics shift.

## What a learner should take away

A defensible position on voter-ID and mail-ballot policy requires:

1. A view on **how to weigh fraud-prevention vs. access** (the central trade-off).
2. A view on **what role federal vs. state authority** should play.
3. A view on **what specific reforms** would improve the system regardless of partisan alignment.
4. An honest accounting of what evidence would change the position.
