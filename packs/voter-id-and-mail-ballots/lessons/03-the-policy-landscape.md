# The current policy landscape

On March 25, 2021, Georgia's legislature passed Senate Bill 202 — ninety-eight pages rewriting the state's election code — and Governor Brian Kemp signed it the same evening, behind closed doors, in front of a painting of a plantation, flanked by six white legislators. A Black state representative, Park Cannon, was arrested for knocking on the office door during the signing; within days the president had called the law "Jim Crow in the twenty-first century," Major League Baseball had moved the All-Star Game out of Atlanta, and the state's largest corporations were issuing dueling statements. Eighteen months later, Georgia's 2022 midterm set state records for early-voting turnout — and both camps declared total vindication, one because turnout proved the law suppressed no one, the other because turnout-despite-the-law proves nothing about the burden, only about the countermobilization. That single episode is the post-2020 policy era in miniature: legislation moving at maximum speed, rhetoric outrunning the provisions, and an empirical scoreboard everyone reads through their own lens. This lesson maps what actually changed — in the states, in the courts, and in Congress — through mid-2026.

## The state wave: both directions at once

The 2021 sessions produced the largest burst of state election lawmaking since the immediate post-*Shelby* years — in *both* directions. The Brennan Center's tracking, the standard count, recorded **34 laws restricting voting access enacted in 19 states in 2021 — and 62 expansive laws in 25 states** the same year, a fact each side's narrative tends to omit half of. The pattern tracks partisan control almost perfectly, deepening rather than converging since. The marquee statutes reward precise reading, because what they actually do is routinely misstated by friend and foe alike:

| State, law | What it actually does (selected provisions) | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Georgia SB 202 (2021) | Replaces signature match with ID numbers on absentee applications and ballots; shortens the absentee request window; caps drop boxes (indoors, early-voting hours only); **adds** a second mandatory Saturday of early voting; cuts runoffs from 9 to 4 weeks; bars non-poll-workers from giving food or water within 150 feet of voters in line; lets the State Election Board take over county election boards | Mostly tightening, with an access expansion inside |
| Texas SB 1 (2021) | Bans the 24-hour and drive-thru voting Harris County pioneered in 2020; requires a driver's-license or Social Security number on mail-ballot applications and envelopes; monthly citizenship checks on the rolls; new protections for partisan poll watchers; bars officials from sending unsolicited mail-ballot applications | Tightening |
| Florida SB 90 (2021) | Mail-ballot requests expire every cycle; drop-box limits; ID number for absentee requests | Tightening |
| Vermont Act 60 (2021) | Universal mail ballots for all general elections | Expanding |
| Nevada AB 321 (2021) | Makes pandemic-era universal mail delivery permanent, with opt-out | Expanding |
| California AB 37 (2021) | Permanent mail ballots to every active registered voter | Expanding |
| Michigan Proposal 2 (2022, constitutional) | Nine days of early voting, state-funded drop boxes and postage, ID affidavit option enshrined | Expanding |
| New York Early Mail Voter Act (2023) | Any voter may request an early mail ballot — functionally ending the excuse requirement | Expanding |
| Wisconsin Question 1 (April 2025, constitutional) | Writes the existing photo-ID requirement into the state constitution (passed with about 63%) | Entrenching |

The first thing the table teaches is that "voter suppression law" and "election integrity law" are each, as descriptions, doing rhetorical work the texts don't fully support: SB 202 *expanded* mandatory weekend early voting even as it constrained the mail channel; the expansive states were simultaneously making permanent exactly the mail practices the tightening states were banning. The second thing it teaches is where the action is: almost every contested provision, in both directions, concerns the **mail-ballot pipeline** from Lesson 1's figure — who gets a ballot, how envelopes are verified, where they can be returned. The polling-place photo-ID fight of the 2010s is largely settled ground; 36 states have ID laws and the public approves of them by huge margins. The 2020s fight is about the kitchen-table ballot.

## The courts: a high bar, getting higher

Three Supreme Court decisions structure all the litigation. ***Crawford v. Marion County* (2008)** upheld Indiana's pioneering photo-ID law, 6–3, against a facial challenge: Justice Stevens's controlling opinion balanced the state's integrity and confidence interests against a burden the record left abstract, and the resulting posture — challengers must show concrete, weighty burdens, not hypothetical ones — has governed ID litigation since. ***Shelby County v. Holder* (2013)** retired the VRA's preclearance formula, and with it the requirement that covered states get federal sign-off *before* changing voting rules; Texas announced its strict-ID law would take effect within hours of the ruling, and North Carolina enacted an omnibus bill weeks later that the Fourth Circuit ultimately struck down in 2016 with the era's most quoted line — its provisions "target African Americans with almost surgical precision." The structural point survives the anecdote: after *Shelby*, restrictive laws operate immediately and are challenged afterward, flipping the burden of inertia. ***Brnovich v. DNC* (2021)** then raised the bar for those after-the-fact challenges, reading VRA §2's "results test" through five state-friendly "guideposts" — among them the size of the disparity, the rules' commonness in 1982, and the state's legitimate interest in fraud prevention *without* needing to prove in-state fraud. Lower courts got the message: the Fifth Circuit's 2024–25 rulings in the *LUPE v. Abbott* litigation reversed the one major trial-court loss for Texas's SB 1, holding that ID numbers on mail-ballot envelopes are "plainly material" to voter qualification (so requiring them doesn't violate the Civil Rights Act's materiality provision) and upholding the law's paid ballot-collection ban. As of mid-2026, SB 1 stands essentially intact.

State constitutions cut both ways, and increasingly decide outcomes. Missouri's supreme court (2006) and a Pennsylvania state court (2014) struck photo-ID laws under their state constitutions; North Carolina's ID requirement survived its state-court odyssey and has applied statewide since 2023; and Wisconsin's electorate took the question away from courts entirely in April 2025 by writing photo ID into the constitution. The post-*Rucho*, post-*Brnovich* pattern from the [[term:gerrymandering]] pack repeats here: as federal doctrine narrows, the fight migrates to state constitutional law — and to the ballot measure, where, as Wisconsin shows, the security side usually wins on ID questions.

## The federal front: two stalled agendas

Each party has a signature federal bill, and each has failed the same way — in the Senate, short of sixty.

The Democratic package, the **Freedom to Vote Act** (automatic and same-day registration nationwide, two weeks' early voting, no-excuse mail voting, ID flexibility, redistricting standards) and the **John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act** (a new preclearance formula to replace what *Shelby* struck down), passed the House in 2021. In January 2022 the Senate majority tried to carve the filibuster for them and failed, 52–48, two Democrats joining all Republicans. Both bills return each Congress as messaging votes; neither has moved since.

The Republican package is the **SAVE Act** — documentary proof of citizenship (a passport, birth certificate, or equivalent) presented *in person* to register or re-register for federal elections, replacing the NVRA's sworn-attestation system. It passed the House in July 2024 (221–198) and again in April 2025 (220–208, four Democrats joining), and a broader successor, the **SAVE America Act** — adding a federal photo-ID mandate and required DHS database checks of the rolls — passed the House on February 12, 2026 with all 217 Republicans and a single Democrat. In the Senate it has met the same wall as the Democratic bills: a cloture attempt failed to advance on March 27, 2026, with Republicans (holding 53 seats) at least seven votes short and Senator Murkowski voting against proceeding; as of mid-2026 it is **not law**, and leadership talk of moving pieces through reconciliation remains talk. Running alongside: a March 2025 executive order directing documentary-citizenship requirements onto the federal registration form was preliminarily enjoined in key parts by federal courts in 2025, with appeals pending as of mid-2026 — the constitutional objection being that the Elections Clause gives election-regulation power to states and Congress, not the presidency.

The empirical stakes underneath the citizenship fight deserve the Lesson 2 treatment: registration by noncitizens is illegal everywhere, documented instances are rare — Georgia's 2024 citizenship audit of its rolls found 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million registrants, nine of whom had voted — and the burden question is real on the other side, since several million eligible citizens (disproportionately women whose legal names changed at marriage, the elderly, and the poor) lack easy access to the qualifying documents. Arizona has run a natural experiment since 2004: its state-form proof-of-citizenship requirement produced a parallel class of "federal-only" voters — registrants who attested but didn't document citizenship, numbering in the tens of thousands — whose existence is either a security gap or a federalism artifact, depending on which lesson-2 lens you hold. With the federal bill stalled, the action has moved to the states: New Hampshire's documentary-proof law took effect for 2024–25 registrations, Wyoming followed in 2025, and others — Florida prominently — were advancing their own versions in 2026 sessions.

## What to watch from here

Four live wires, each checkable against this lesson rather than against headlines. **The SAVE/SAVE America endgame:** any Senate rules change, reconciliation maneuver, or 2026-election shift would convert a House messaging bill into the largest federal change to registration since the NVRA. **The executive-order litigation:** if appellate courts uphold presidential authority over the federal form, the Elections Clause settlement described in Lesson 1 moves. **The state proof-of-citizenship wave:** watch whether new laws replicate Arizona's federal-only bifurcation and Kansas's experience (its 2013 law suspended about 30,000 registrations before federal courts struck it down in 2018). **The quiet bipartisan layer:** post-election audits, ERIC-style list hygiene, and cure standardization keep advancing without cameras — the standards-based reformer's evidence that the system improves fastest where nobody is watching.

## What this lesson is not

It is not a forecast, and it is not a verdict on the three perspectives — it is the board state they now argue over. A security-first advocate reads mid-2026 as overdue consolidation: ID consensus locked in constitutionally, mail channels finally getting the verification Carter–Baker urged two decades ago, citizenship documentation the unfinished item. An access-first advocate reads the same record as burden-shifting after *Shelby* and *Brnovich*: rules that operate first and answer questions later, aimed at the channels disadvantaged voters use most. A standards-based reformer reads two parties entrenching opposite regimes state by state and concludes the patchwork itself is now the problem. You have the statutes, the cases, the bill statuses, and the evidence base from Lesson 2 — enough to declare a position with `slug: voter-id-and-access` and defend it. Before you do, run the steel-man drill against the perspective you found least persuasive; if you cannot state what evidence would change your mind, you have a team, not a position.
