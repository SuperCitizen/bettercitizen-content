# How U.S. voter ID and mail balloting actually work

Stand on the bridge over the Snake River between Ontario, Oregon and Fruitland, Idaho, and you are standing between two election systems. The Oregonians behind you will not go to a polling place this year: their ballots arrive automatically in the mail two to three weeks before Election Day, no request needed, no ID shown at any counter, signature on the envelope checked against the registration file. The Idahoans ahead of you will mostly vote the way their grandparents did — in person, at a neighborhood polling place, after showing a photo ID. Neither state is an outlier, neither system is new, and each state's voters express high confidence in their own arrangements. Five minutes apart, two complete answers to the same question. That is the single most important fact about American election administration: there is no such thing as "how the U.S. votes." There are fifty-one systems — and the differences among them are precisely what this pack's argument is about.

## Fifty-one systems, one thin federal floor

The Constitution's Elections Clause (Article I, §4) hands the "Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections to state legislatures, subject to congressional override — the same clause doing the work in this pack's sibling on [[term:gerrymandering]]. Congress has used its override power sparingly. The floor it has built has three main planks.

The **National Voter Registration Act of 1993** ("Motor Voter") requires states to offer [[term:voter-registration]] at DMVs and public-assistance offices, to accept a federal mail registration form, and — less famously but just as consequentially — regulates how voters may be *removed* from the rolls: list maintenance must be uniform and nondiscriminatory, and a voter may not be purged simply for not voting without first failing to respond to a confirmation notice and then sitting out two more federal elections. The **Help America Vote Act of 2002**, passed after the Florida 2000 meltdown, paid to retire punch-card machines, required every state to build a single statewide registration database, created provisional ballots (you vote, the eligibility question gets resolved afterward), established the Election Assistance Commission — and imposed the only *federal* ID rule in American law: a first-time voter who registered by mail without verifiable identification must show some form of ID, photo or not, the first time they vote. The **Voting Rights Act of 1965** overlays both: its Section 2 ban on racially discriminatory voting practices applies to every rule below, though *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) ended the requirement that states with discriminatory histories preclear changes with Washington before making them — which is why the post-2013 and post-2020 waves of state lawmaking, tightening and loosening alike, took effect first and got litigated later.

Everything else — whether you need an excuse to vote by mail, what ID counts, how early voting runs, whether a misdelivered ballot can be fixed — is state law, administered in practice by roughly 8,000 county and municipal election offices. One state, North Dakota, has no voter registration at all and never has since abolishing it in 1951; you show up with proof of who you are and where you live, and you vote.

<Checkpoint question="A state election director wants to delete from the rolls everyone who skipped the last election. A federal lawyer objects. What rule is the lawyer invoking, and what process would make the purge legal?">
  The **NVRA's list-maintenance rules**: nonvoting alone can't trigger removal. The state must first send a confirmation notice; only if the voter fails to respond *and* then sits out two further federal general elections may the registration be canceled. The Supreme Court held in *Husted v. A. Philip Randolph Institute* (2018) that Ohio's version of exactly this notice-then-wait process complies. The lesson: "purges" are legal, regulated, and routine — the fights are over how aggressively the triggers are pulled.
</Checkpoint>

## Getting on the rolls

Above the NVRA floor, registration policy has been moving — mostly toward automation — for a decade. **Automatic voter registration**, which registers eligible citizens from DMV records unless they decline, began with Oregon in 2015 and has spread to about half the states plus D.C. (NCSL's count as of 2025). **Same-day registration**, which lets a citizen register and vote in one trip, exists in about two dozen states plus D.C.; the political-science literature consistently credits it with the largest [[term:voter-turnout]] effect of any registration reform, on the order of three to five percentage points. Forty-two states plus D.C. take registrations online. About twenty allow 16- and 17-year-olds to pre-register.

The unglamorous counterpart is keeping the rolls accurate as tens of millions of Americans move every year. States check death records and change-of-address data; about two dozen share data through ERIC, the Electronic Registration Information Center, an interstate compact that flags duplicate registrations and voters who have moved — and which lost nine member states in a politicized 2022–23 exodus, leaving the cross-state matching problem it was built to solve partially unsolved. Registration errors — the deceased voter still listed, the student registered in two states — are common, and they are the kernel of truth inside most viral "dirty rolls" claims. What they are not, by themselves, is fraud: an obsolete entry only matters if someone votes on it, and the documented instances of that are the subject of Lesson 2.

## Showing ID at the polls

Every state verifies identity somehow; the argument is over *documents*. The analytically useful grid, maintained by the National Conference of State Legislatures, has two axes: does the state ask for a **photo** ID specifically, and is the rule **strict** — meaning a voter without the document casts only a provisional ballot that counts *only if* they return with ID within a deadline — or **non-strict**, meaning some fallback at the polls (an affidavit, a poll worker vouching, a signature match) lets the ballot count without a return trip. As of NCSL's April 2026 update:

| Category | How it works | States | Count |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strict photo ID | No qualifying photo ID → provisional ballot, counted only if ID shown later | Ark., Ga., Ind., Kan., Miss., N.H., N.C., Ohio, Tenn., Wis. | 10 |
| Non-strict photo ID | Photo ID requested; affidavit or other fallback lets the ballot count | Ala., Fla., Idaho, Ky., La., Mich., Mo., Mont., Neb., R.I., S.C., S.D., Texas, W.Va. | 14 |
| Strict non-photo ID | Document required but need not bear a photo | Ariz., N.D., Wyo. | 3 |
| Non-strict non-photo ID | Non-photo document requested, with fallback | Alaska, Colo., Conn., Del., Iowa, Okla., Utah, Va., Wash. | 9 |
| No document required | Identity verified by signature, attestation, or poll book | 14 states + D.C. | 14 + D.C. |

Read the table's totals before its labels: **36 states** now request or require some document at the polls, **23** of them a photo ID, **10** of those on strict terms — numbers that have ratcheted steadily upward since the Supreme Court upheld Indiana's pioneering photo-ID law in *Crawford v. Marion County Election Board* (2008), a case Lesson 3 treats in full. The category boundaries move almost every year: Ohio went strict-photo in 2023; Nebraska's voters added an ID requirement to their constitution in 2022 and Wisconsin's did the same in April 2025, by about 63 percent; West Virginia adopted a photo requirement in 2025. Note also what the table quietly shows: "photo ID state" does not mean "no ID, no vote." In the fourteen non-strict photo states, including Texas since 2017, a voter without ID can sign a sworn statement (a "reasonable impediment declaration," in Texas's phrase) and cast a countable ballot. Every strict state offers a free state ID to voters who lack one — the contested question, taken up next lesson, is how many people that paper guarantee actually reaches.

<Checkpoint question="State A requests photo ID but lets a voter without one sign an affidavit and cast a regular ballot. State B requires any of six listed documents, none necessarily with a photo, and a voter with none of them casts a provisional ballot that counts only if she brings a document to the county office by Friday. Classify both.">
  State A is **non-strict photo ID** — the affidavit fallback means no return trip is required, so it sits with Florida, Michigan, and Texas. State B is **strict non-photo ID** — the document list is flexible but the consequence is strict, like Arizona. The strict/non-strict axis (what happens to the voter *without* the document) matters more for turnout analysis than the photo/non-photo axis, which is why careful studies code laws this way rather than counting "ID states."
</Checkpoint>

## Voting by mail: one ballot, three regimes

Mail voting is older than the argument about it — Civil War soldiers voted absentee from the field, and every state has run absentee ballots for well over a century. What states disagree about is *who may use it*. The spectrum, as of mid-2026:

| Regime | How it works | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All-mail ("universal") elections | Every active registered voter is automatically mailed a ballot; in-person voting remains available | Calif., Colo., Hawaii, Nev., Ore., Utah, Vt. (general elections), Wash. — 8 states + D.C. |
| No-excuse absentee | Any voter may request a mail ballot, no reason needed | About 27 additional states — with the all-mail states, roughly two-thirds of the country (NCSL) |
| Excuse-required absentee | Mail ballot only for listed reasons (illness, disability, absence, age) | About 15 states, concentrated in the South and Northeast — e.g., Ala., Ind., Miss., Mo., N.H., S.C., Texas, W.Va. |

Oregon went first, by 1998 ballot initiative, and has run every election by mail since 2000; Washington (2011), Colorado (2013), and the rest followed, with California making pandemic-era universal mail delivery permanent in 2021. Utah is the spectrum's most interesting datapoint — a deep-red all-mail state since 2019, though 2025 legislation will move it to an opt-in system by 2029, with ID numbers required on return envelopes in the interim. Nationally, mail's share of all ballots ran about one in four in 2016, spiked to 43 percent in pandemic-year 2020, and settled back to about 30 percent in 2024 (EAC survey data) — a politically scrambled pattern, since the pre-2020 heaviest users included Republican strongholds like Utah and Arizona's retiree precincts.

What happens to a mail ballot after the voter signs the envelope is where security design actually lives, and it is the part of the system almost nobody can describe. The figure traces it.

Three details from that pipeline carry most of the policy weight. *Verification:* most states compare the envelope signature against registration records; some instead or additionally require an ID number (Georgia since 2021, Texas since 2021) or a witness signature. *Deadlines:* more than half of states require ballots to be *received* by Election Day; the rest count ballots *postmarked* by Election Day and arriving within a grace window — the difference decides tens of thousands of ballots in every close state. *Cure:* in roughly two-thirds of states, officials must notify a voter whose envelope fails verification and give them a window to fix it; in the rest, a flagged ballot is simply rejected. Nationally about 1.2 percent of returned mail ballots — roughly 584,000 — were rejected in 2024, with first-time mail voters rejected at multiples of that rate — and studies in Georgia and Florida have found rejection falling disproportionately on younger and nonwhite voters, a fact every perspective in Lesson 2 has to account for.

<Checkpoint question="Two states both require mail ballots 'by Election Day.' In State A, a ballot postmarked Monday and delivered Thursday counts; in State B it doesn't. A campaign lawyer says one of these states is 'counting late votes.' Is that right?">
  No — both ballots were *cast* on time; the states differ on **receipt vs. postmark deadlines**. State A counts ballots postmarked by Election Day arriving within its grace period; State B requires physical receipt by poll close. Neither involves voting after Election Day. The rhetorical frame "counting late votes" trades on conflating when a ballot is cast with when the Postal Service delivers it — and the choice between the two rules is pure state law, which is why post-election counting takes longer in some states than others.
</Checkpoint>

## Who controls what — and why it stays this way

Stack the layers and the division of labor is clear. Federal law guarantees registration access (NVRA), minimum infrastructure and provisional ballots (HAVA), and nondiscrimination (VRA §2). State law decides ID rules, mail eligibility, deadlines, cure processes, early-voting calendars, drop boxes, and audits. Counties run the machinery. This is [[term:federalism]] doing exactly what its defenders claim — fifty laboratories, no single point of failure for an attacker to compromise, rules adapted to a state spread across six time zones or one you can drive across in an hour — and exactly what its critics claim: your practical ability to vote by mail, or to vote without a driver's license, depends on which side of the Snake River you live on. Both descriptions are true. Whether the variation is a feature, a bug, or a problem requiring national standards is not a factual question, and the next lesson takes it up as the genuine three-way argument it is.

## What this lesson is not

It is not yet an argument. You now hold the machinery: the thin federal floor, the registration pipeline, the ID grid and its mid-2026 head-counts, the three mail regimes, and the verify-and-cure stage where mail-ballot security actually lives. What this lesson deliberately did not settle: whether documented fraud is large enough to justify documentary ID; whether ID rules and mail restrictions measurably suppress turnout, and whose; and who should bear the cost when security and access trade off. Those are the contested questions — and the empirical literature on them is better, and stranger, than either party's talking points admit. Lesson 2 steel-mans all three positions against that evidence.
