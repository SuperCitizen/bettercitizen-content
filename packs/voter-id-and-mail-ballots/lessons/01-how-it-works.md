# How U.S. voter ID and mail balloting actually work

Election administration is overwhelmingly a state-and-local function in the U.S. The federal government sets a floor (mostly through the National Voter Registration Act and the Help America Vote Act), and states fill in the details. The result is enormous variation, which makes "How does U.S. voting work?" almost impossible to answer without specifying which state.

## Voter ID: the categories

Voter-ID requirements vary along several dimensions:

- **Strict photo ID**: voters must present a current government-issued photo ID. If they lack one, they typically can vote provisionally and provide ID later. Examples: Indiana, Georgia, Wisconsin, Tennessee.
- **Non-strict photo ID**: photo ID is requested but voters without one can sign an affidavit or use other identification methods. Examples: Florida, Idaho, Michigan.
- **Strict non-photo ID**: ID required but doesn't have to include a photo (utility bill, bank statement, etc., may qualify). Examples: Ohio, Arizona.
- **Non-strict non-photo ID**: ID requested but not strictly required. Examples: Connecticut, Delaware.
- **No document required**: voter signs the poll book or signs an affidavit. Examples: California, Oregon, Washington, Vermont.

NCSL maintains the current state-by-state classification. The number of strict-photo-ID states has grown significantly since the 2008 *Crawford v. Marion County* decision upheld Indiana's law.

## Voter registration

Federal law (NVRA, 1993) sets a floor for voter registration:
- States must offer registration at DMV, public-assistance offices.
- States must process mail-in registration applications.
- States must maintain accurate rolls.

Above this floor, states vary:
- **Election Day Registration (EDR)**: 22 states + D.C. allow same-day registration. Increases turnout by ~3-5 percentage points (multiple studies).
- **Automatic Voter Registration (AVR)**: 23 states + D.C. automatically register people who interact with the DMV unless they opt out. Started with Oregon in 2015.
- **Pre-registration of 16/17-year-olds**: ~20 states allow registration before voting age.
- **Online registration**: 42 states + D.C. allow online registration for in-state residents.

Registration purges remove inactive or ineligible voters from the rolls. Standards vary; this is one of the most contested administrative dimensions of U.S. elections.

## Mail ballots

The categories:

- **No-excuse absentee voting**: any registered voter can request a mail ballot. ~36 states + D.C. as of 2024.
- **Excuse-required absentee voting**: voters need a specific reason (illness, travel, etc.). Mostly in the South and a few other states.
- **All-mail elections**: ballots are mailed to all registered voters automatically. 8 states use this for some or all elections (Oregon, Washington, Colorado, Hawaii, Utah, Vermont, California — California for some elections, Nevada).

Mail ballots have been growing as a share of total votes for decades. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated adoption: in 2020, ~46% of ballots were cast by mail, up from ~25% in 2016.

The empirical record on mail ballots:
- **Turnout effect**: small positive effect (~2-4 percentage points), strongest for groups that historically have lower turnout.
- **Fraud rate**: extremely low. The Brennan Center's analysis found documented fraud rates around 0.0009% in mail-ballot elections — far lower than rates for in-person voting.
- **Partisan effect**: mixed and contested. Mail ballots historically favored Democrats slightly; recent evidence is more mixed.

## The empirical record on fraud

Multiple comprehensive studies converge on the same finding: U.S. voter fraud, by any plausible measure, is rare. Brennan Center, Heritage Foundation (which maintains a fraud database favoring restrictionist positions), and academic studies all find:

- **Voter impersonation** (the type of fraud voter-ID requirements specifically address) is the rarest form. Roughly 31 documented cases out of more than 1 billion ballots cast (Brennan Center).
- **Mail-ballot fraud** is documented at roughly 0.0009% rate.
- **Registration-list errors** are common (people registered in multiple states, deceased voters not removed) but rarely translate to actual fraudulent votes.
- **In-person fraud** (impersonation, multiple voting) is documented at very low rates because the costs of attempting it (criminal penalties, hard to scale) are high.

The most-common election-administration problems are not fraud but errors: misallocated polling places, undertrained poll workers, voter rolls not properly maintained, mail ballots not received in time. These are administrative problems, not security problems.

The Heritage Foundation's database, which has been used to support voter-ID arguments, contains roughly 1,400 documented cases over many decades — a small number by any large-scale measure.

## What states actually control

The federal floor is set by NVRA + HAVA + the Voting Rights Act. Above this:

- **ID requirements** — entirely state law (within constitutional limits, post-Crawford).
- **Registration deadlines** — state law. Range from same-day (EDR states) to ~30 days before election.
- **Polling-place hours** — state law. Some states have early-voting hours; others don't.
- **Mail ballot rules** — state law, including who can request, what postmark/receipt deadlines apply, signature-verification standards.
- **Post-election audits** — state law. Some states use risk-limiting audits; others don't.

This decentralization is sometimes described as a feature (states experiment, federalism is preserved) and sometimes as a bug (election administration is uneven, voters have different rights depending on geography).

## Why this lesson is contested

Three perspectives the next lesson will steel-man:

- **Security-first advocates** argue that even small fraud rates matter for election legitimacy, that ID requirements have low costs and meaningful benefits, and that mail-ballot expansion has accelerated faster than security infrastructure has kept pace.
- **Access-first advocates** argue that voter-ID and registration requirements suppress turnout disproportionately among low-income, minority, and elderly voters, and that the documented fraud rates don't justify the documented disenfranchisement.
- **Standards-based reformers** argue both sides overstate their case, and that the right approach is uniform national standards (free voter ID provided to all eligible voters; uniform mail-ballot rules; risk-limiting audits) that combine security and access without favoring either side.

A defensible position takes one of these views, says why, and identifies what evidence would change it. Lesson 2 sharpens each.
