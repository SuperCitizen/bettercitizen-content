## 905 votes

In November 2018, North Carolina's 9th Congressional District appeared to elect Mark Harris by 905 votes. In Bladen County, investigators found an operative, McCrae Dowless, whose crew had collected absentee ballots, filled in some, discarded others, and forged witness signatures. The state board refused to certify, and ordered a new election.

- Bladen County, 2018: a new election ordered

It was mail-ballot fraud, the channel photo ID laws do not touch. And it was caught by the system's own statistics.

> The fraud was real, and it ran through the mail, not the polling place.

## The security-first case

Security-first advocates argue that elections must be demonstrably honest, because legitimacy rests on the loser accepting defeat. They point to the Carter–Baker Commission of 2005, which recommended a uniform photo ID requirement, and to proven fraud in Bladen County. Deterrence, they argue, is not refuted by the rarity of the crime it deters.

> The security-first case: rarity is what successful deterrence looks like.

## The access-first case

Access-first advocates argue that the franchise is a right, and that the measurable harm runs the other way. They point to Justin Levitt's count of 31 credible incidents of in-person impersonation, out of more than 1 billion ballots, and to a Texas trial record that found about 608,000 registered voters without an acceptable ID. The burdens, they argue, fall disproportionately on poor, Black, Latino, elderly, young and disabled voters.

> The access-first case: the measured harms run one way.

## The standards-based case

Standards-based reformers argue that both camps overclaim, and that security and access can be satisfied together. They point to Carter–Baker itself, whose ID recommendation came bundled with free IDs and outreach, and to audits of paper ballots, which verify outcomes statistically. Neutral procedure, they argue, is what both sides can trust.

> The standards-based case: verify identity cheaply, and verify outcomes statistically.

## What the evidence does

A 2021 study by Cantoni and Pons followed 1.6 billion voter-file observations. It found that strict ID laws caused no significant change in registration or turnout, for any racial group. It also found no detectable reduction in fraud, and no improvement in confidence.

- No significant change in turnout
- No detectable reduction in fraud
- No improvement in confidence

The evidence rules out the cartoon version of each position. It cannot say which error a self-governing people should fear more: wrongful exclusion, or wrongful inclusion.

- Wrongful exclusion, or wrongful inclusion
