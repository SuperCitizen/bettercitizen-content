## Two states, one bridge

Stand on the bridge over the Snake River between Ontario, Oregon and Fruitland, Idaho, and you are standing between two election systems. Behind you, Oregonians get their ballots in the mail automatically, with no request and no ID shown at any counter. Ahead of you, Idahoans mostly vote in person, after showing a photo ID.

- Oregon: ballots mailed automatically
- Idaho: in person, with photo ID

> America has no single way to vote. It has fifty-one systems.

## A thin federal floor

Congress has set few rules for every state. The Help America Vote Act of 2002 holds the only federal ID rule in American law. A first-time voter who registered by mail without verifiable identification must show some form of ID, photo or not, the first time they vote.

- One federal ID rule: first-time mail registrants

Everything else is state law: what ID counts, who may vote by mail, whether a ballot with a problem can be fixed.

> Above the federal floor, the rules are state law.

## The voter without the document

36 states now request or require some document at the polls, and 23 of them ask for a photo ID. What matters most is what happens to a voter who does not have it.

- 36 states ask for a document

In a strict state, that voter casts a provisional ballot, which counts only if they return with ID by a deadline. In a non-strict state there is a fallback at the polls, such as signing an affidavit, and the ballot counts without a return trip.

- Strict: provisional ballot, return with ID
- Non-strict: a fallback, and the ballot counts

Ten states ask for photo ID on strict terms. Fourteen ask for it with a fallback.

## The envelope, not the mailbox

A mail ballot is checked after the voter signs the envelope. Most states compare that signature against registration records. In roughly two-thirds of states, officials must notify a voter whose envelope fails, and give them a window to fix it. That is called cure. In the rest, a flagged ballot is simply rejected.

- Cure: notice, then a window to fix it

In 2024, about 1.2 percent of returned mail ballots were rejected.

- Rejected in 2024: about 1.2%

> Mail-ballot security lives at the envelope check, not the mailbox.

## Feature or bug

Defenders of this arrangement see fifty laboratories and no single point of failure. Critics see that your ability to vote by mail, or without a driver's license, depends on which side of the river you live on. The lesson says both descriptions are true.

- Fifty laboratories, or a matter of address
