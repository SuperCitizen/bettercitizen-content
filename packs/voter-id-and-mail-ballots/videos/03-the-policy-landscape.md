## One law, two victories

On March 25, 2021, Georgia's legislature passed Senate Bill 202, ninety-eight pages rewriting the state's election code. Within days the president had called it Jim Crow in the 21st century. Eighteen months later, Georgia's 2022 midterm set state records for early-voting turnout, and both camps declared total vindication.

- Georgia SB 202, 2021: ninety-eight pages

> Both camps declared total vindication, from the same turnout numbers.

## Read the statute

SB 202 replaced the signature match with ID numbers for absentee voting, shortened the absentee request window, and capped drop boxes. It also added a second mandatory Saturday of early voting.

- Tightened: absentee ID numbers, drop boxes
- Added: a second Saturday of early voting

The whole country moved both ways at once. In 2021, one standard count recorded 34 laws restricting voting access in 19 states, and 62 expansive laws in 25 states.

- 2021: 34 restrictive laws, 62 expansive

## First in effect, then in court

Before 2013, covered states needed federal sign-off before changing voting rules. Shelby County against Holder retired the formula behind that requirement. Since then, a new law operates immediately and is challenged afterward. In 2021, Brnovich raised the bar for those challenges.

- Shelby County, 2013: no sign-off first
- Brnovich, 2021: a higher bar afterward

> After Shelby County, laws take effect first and are challenged later.

## Short of sixty

Each party has a signature federal bill, and each has failed the same way: in the Senate, short of sixty votes. The Republican bill, the SAVE Act, would require documentary proof of citizenship to register. It has passed the House more than once. In March 2026, the motion to take it up drew 51 of the 60 votes needed.

- SAVE Act: 51 of 60 votes needed

> As of mid-2026, the SAVE Act is not law.

## Three readings of one record

Security-first advocates read mid-2026 as overdue consolidation, with citizenship documentation the unfinished item.

- Security-first: overdue consolidation

Access-first advocates read the same record as burden-shifting, with rules that operate first and answer questions later.

- Access-first: burden-shifting

Standards-based reformers read it as two parties entrenching opposite regimes, so that the patchwork itself is now the problem.

- Standards-based: the patchwork is the problem
