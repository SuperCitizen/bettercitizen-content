November 2018. North Carolina's Ninth Congressional District appears to elect a Republican, Mark Harris, by 905 votes.

Then the absentee numbers start to look wrong.

In rural Bladen County, Harris has won 61 percent of the mail ballots. But registered Republicans account for only 19 percent of the mail ballots returned. [beat] Sixty-one, against nineteen. A statistical thumbprint.

It led investigators to a local operative named McCrae Dowless. His crew had collected blank and unsealed absentee ballots from voters, by the hundred. They filled in some. They discarded others. They forged witness signatures. All for pay.

The State Board of Elections refused to certify, with bipartisan unanimity, and ordered a new election. Dowless was indicted.

[pause]

Hold that episode up to the light, because every argument in this lecture is inside it.

The fraud was real, organized, and large enough to decide the outcome.

It was mail-ballot fraud: the channel that photo-ID laws do not touch.

It was detected by the system's own statistics, and stopped before a single illegitimate term was served.

And the operative, by his own account, had worked in earlier cycles too. [beat] Undetected.

> All three positions on voting rules cite Bladen County. All three are entitled to.

Last time, the machinery. This time, the argument. By the end, you should be able to state each of three positions the way its smartest adherent would, and say what the evidence settles and what it cannot.

Four stops. First, the security-first case. Second, the access-first case. Third, the research, which embarrasses both sides a little. And last, the standards-based reformer's case.

Each gets the same treatment: what it protects, what it argues, the evidence it points to, what it concedes, and what would change it.

## The security-first case

Begin with the security-first advocate, and with what this advocate is protecting: institutional trustworthiness. On this view, the harm of fraud is not arithmetic. It is corrosive. [beat] One Bladen County licenses a million suspicions.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Elections must not only be honest. [beat] They must be demonstrably honest. Legitimacy is the loser's reasoned acceptance of defeat, and that is the whole point of voting.

So a system that answers fraud concerns with "trust us, it's rare" fails the stress test of the closest elections, even if the trust is warranted. Verification rules, like documentary ID, are cheap insurance on the institution that underwrites everything else.

> The security-first case: elections must be demonstrably honest, not only honest.

Now the evidence this advocate points to. Three things.

First, a bipartisan commission. The Carter–Baker Commission of 2005 was co-chaired by a Democratic former president and a Republican former secretary of state. It recommended a uniform photo-ID requirement, paired with free IDs and outreach. And it warned that "absentee ballots remain the largest source of potential voter fraud."

Second, the proven cases, clustered exactly where that commission predicted. Bladen County. Paterson, New Jersey, in 2020, where a judge voided a city council race after roughly one in five mail ballots were disqualified. And Bridgeport, Connecticut, in 2023, where video of ballot-stuffing at drop boxes led a judge to order the mayoral primary run again.

Third, a justice and the public. In 2008, in Crawford v. Marion County, Justice Stevens, who was no conservative, wrote that "flagrant examples of such fraud" had been "documented throughout this Nation's history." A state's interest in deterrence and public confidence, he wrote, justified Indiana's photo rule.

And he wrote that while conceding this: "the record contains no evidence of any such fraud actually occurring in Indiana at any time in its history."

[pause]

On this view, deterrence is not refuted by the rarity of the crime it deters. Rarity is what successful deterrence looks like.

[pause]

And the public agrees, across party lines, as it does on almost nothing else. In Gallup's survey of October 2024, 84 percent of Americans favored photo-ID requirements.

Let's test that evidence. Think back to how this lecture opened. A security-first advocate cites Bladen County to argue for a strict photo-ID law. What is the mismatch? And what would have addressed the Dowless operation? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Bladen County was absentee-ballot fraud: the collection, completion and forgery of mail ballots. A photo-ID requirement at polling places would not have touched it.

What fits is control of the mail channel. Signature and witness verification. Limits on third-party ballot collection. Envelope tracking. And the statistical anomaly detection that actually caught Dowless.

A serious security-first advocate concedes exactly this. The marquee policy and the documented threat are mismatched: photo ID at the polls addresses in-person impersonation, the rarest documented fraud.

So the careful advocate cites Bladen for the general proposition that organized fraud happens, and then matches remedies to vectors. The advocate also notes that North Carolina did have limits on ballot collection on the books. Dowless broke them. [beat] Enforcement was the gap, not rules.

And the advocate concedes one thing more. The best study, which we will reach in a few minutes, found no detectable reduction in fraud from strict ID laws, and no improvement in voter confidence. Insurance, so far, with no measurable payout.

And what would change this position? Evidence of concentrated, uncured disenfranchisement, at magnitudes beyond any plausible deterrence benefit. Or sustained evidence that verification rules fail even to move public confidence.

## The access-first case

Now the access-first advocate, built the same way. What this advocate is protecting is the franchise as a right, not a privilege to be re-earned at a DMV counter. On this view, rights analysis weighs burdens on the burdened, not averages across everyone.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. The measurable harm runs the other way. Documented fraud is vanishingly rare. The burdens of documentary-ID rules and mail restrictions fall measurably, and disproportionately, on poor, Black, Latino, elderly, young and disabled voters.

So the trade is a real, skewed cost for a speculative benefit. That is bad policy arithmetic. And given who bears the cost, it is voter suppression in effect, whatever its intent.

> The access-first case: the measured harms run one way.

Now the evidence this advocate points to. Three things.

First, the denominators, the heart of the case. A comprehensive search by Justin Levitt looked for in-person impersonation, the crime that photo ID prevents. From 2000 to 2014, it found 31 credible incidents. [beat] Out of more than 1 billion ballots cast.

And the Heritage Foundation's own fraud database, built to demonstrate the problem, recorded more than 1,600 proven instances as of December 2025. That covers all fraud types, all elections, and more than four decades. [beat] As a rate, it rounds to zero.

Second, the burdens, in hard numbers. The trial record in Texas's Veasey litigation found that about 608,000 registered Texans lacked an ID acceptable under the 2011 law.

A "free ID," on this view, covers the card and not the cost of getting it. The birth certificate that costs money. The DMV two counties away, with weekday hours. The name mismatch, which falls hardest on married women.

Third, the quiet parallel in the mail. Hundreds of thousands of ballots are rejected each presidential cycle for technical defects on the envelope. Studies in Georgia and Florida found the rates two to four times higher for young and nonwhite voters. To this advocate, that is disenfranchisement that requires no fraudster. [beat] Only a signature that an algorithm or a clerk doesn't like.

Let's test that evidence too. Take the Texas number: about 608,000 registered voters without an acceptable ID. Taken alone, what does that number measure? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It measures exposure to the burden: how many registered voters lacked the documents. It does not measure how many were kept from voting.

That depends on the fallbacks, on the chances to cure, and on whether the affected voters mobilize in response. So a large possession gap and a small average turnout effect can both be true. And the number itself is a court-tested factual finding, not advocacy arithmetic.

A serious access-first advocate concedes this. The predicted turnout catastrophes keep failing to appear. The best-identified national study finds average turnout effects statistically indistinguishable from zero, even for minority voters. And the record turnout of 2020 arrived amid the very rules that access advocates call suppressive.

So the careful advocate answers that averages are the wrong test. A null average is compatible with real, concentrated harm, to subgroups too small for national panels to resolve. It is compatible with a costly countermobilization, which should not have to be paid. And tight elections are decided at margins far smaller than the confidence intervals of any of these studies.

And what would change this position? Replicated evidence that ID possession is near-universal across demographic groups, and that cure processes catch rejected ballots equitably. Or documented fraud at rates within an order of magnitude of the rejection rates the rules themselves produce.

## What the research finds

Two cases, each with its numbers. Now lay the best research under both. It is better than the public fight.

Start with ID laws and turnout. The famous alarm was a 2017 study by Hajnal and two colleagues, which found that strict ID laws sharply depressed minority turnout. The next year, Grimmer and four colleagues showed that the result rested on inaccurate survey data and fragile specifications. Correct the errors, and one can recover positive, negative, or null effects at will.

Then the heavyweight: Cantoni and Pons, in 2021. Their panel held 1.6 billion voter-file observations, from 2008 to 2018. Listen for two findings.

Strict ID laws caused no significant change in registration or turnout, overall or for any racial group.

[beat]

And they caused no detectable reduction in fraud, and no improvement in confidence.

[pause]

A double null.

Now mail voting. In 2020, Thompson and three colleagues studied decades of county rollouts of all-mail voting. They found that it raised overall turnout modestly, by about two percentage points. [beat] And it had no effect on either party's share of the vote.

The pattern across both bodies of research is the same, and it is strange.

> On the best evidence, the most fought-over election policies move very few votes.

Now sort what is settled from what is not.

Settled: in-person impersonation is vanishingly rare, and universal mail voting helps neither party.

Settled in direction: mail-ballot fraud is rare but the dominant documented vector, and mail-ballot rejections run higher for first-time, young and nonwhite voters.

Contested: whether strict ID laws cut turnout for particular subgroups. There is no large average effect.

[pause]

Now put the central study to work. Cantoni and Pons found no significant effect on turnout, and none on fraud or confidence. Which side of the debate does that help? Before you answer, build the strongest use of it for both sides.

[pause 5s]

It helps both. [beat] And therefore neither cleanly.

The security-first advocate argues that the disenfranchisement objection is empirically bankrupt. The laws cost nothing, so the rationale of deterrence and legitimacy stands unopposed.

The access-first advocate argues that the laws also detect nothing and deter nothing, and do not even raise confidence. That is dead-weight burden, imposed for theater. And null averages can hide concentrated harm.

What the study does is narrow the debate. Catastrophic suppression is off the table. So is meaningful fraud reduction. That moves the argument from consequences to principles: who should bear administrative friction, and what legitimacy requires.

> The deeper fight: which error to fear more, wrongful exclusion or wrongful inclusion.

The evidence constrains the empirical claims. It cannot answer that.

## The standards-based case

That move, from consequences to principles, is the opening for the standards-based reformer. Same treatment.

What this advocate is protecting is institutional design, over moral combat. On this view, a rule that requires one tribe to lose the argument will never stabilize a system both tribes must trust.

Here is the argument, in its own terms. Both camps overclaim from thin evidence, and the binary is false. Security and access are engineering constraints, to be satisfied jointly. They are not values to trade off.

So verify identity cheaply and universally, with free, automatically issued ID and modern registration. Verify outcomes statistically, with risk-limiting audits of paper ballots, which Colorado pioneered statewide in 2017. And standardize the mail-ballot pipeline, so that neither fraud nor wrongful rejection depends on county of residence.

> The standards-based case: satisfy security and access together. Do not trade them.

Now the evidence this advocate points to. Three things.

First, Carter–Baker itself. The ID recommendation that everyone quotes came bundled with universal free IDs, with government-funded outreach to people who lack them, and with interoperable registration. [beat] The package, not the paywall.

Second, the data on trust. Confidence in elections now swings with which party lost, more than with how elections are run. Republican confidence collapsed after 2020 while Democratic confidence rose: the mirror image of 2016. To the reformer, that suggests rules alone cannot buy legitimacy, and that visibly neutral, auditable procedures are the only currency available.

Third, the null results you have just heard. If ID laws neither suppress nor secure at scale, both parties are spending enormous political capital on a near-empty box. Meanwhile the measures with demonstrable payoffs go underfunded: paper trails, audits, cure processes, list-maintenance hygiene. Neither base is angry about them.

Let's test that evidence too. The reformer's program assumes that distrust is caused by procedure. Now set that beside the reformer's own second exhibit: confidence swings with which party lost. What does that suggest about where distrust comes from? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It suggests that distrust may come less from procedure than from elite rhetoric about procedure.

A serious reformer concedes this. On the trust data, the assumption that procedure causes the distrust is probably backwards. And "both sides should compromise" is itself a position with losers. National standards override federalism, and the settled practice of fifty states.

So the careful advocate has something to explain. The grand bargain has been available since 2005. [beat] Nobody with power has ever bought it. A theory of politics that requires politicians to disarm their best mobilization issues should explain why they would.

And what would change this position? Evidence that combined designs fail in practice: free-ID programs that still exclude at scale, or audits that do not move measured trust. Or continued proof that the bargain is politically unbuyable. [beat] At that point, talk of standards is a description of a nicer world, not a program.

## Three bets

Now hold the three side by side. Each one ends in a bet.

The security-first bet: the mail channel can be secured, and confidence will eventually follow visibly secure rules.

[beat]

The access-first bet: null national averages conceal real, concentrated exclusion, and tight elections are decided inside those subgroup margins.

[beat]

The standards-based bet: neutral, auditable procedure is the only currency that can buy legitimacy from both tribes at once.

[pause]

Now choose the position you find least sympathetic. State its best case, the way its smartest adherent would. Take your time.

[pause 5s]

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The security-first case: elections must be demonstrably honest, not only honest. Its evidence: Carter–Baker, the proven mail-ballot cases, and deterrence. Its concession: photo ID at the polls does not match the documented threat.

[beat]

Two. The access-first case: the measured harms run one way. Its evidence: 31 impersonation cases in a billion ballots, against 608,000 Texans without a qualifying ID. Its concession: the predicted turnout catastrophes keep failing to appear.

[beat]

Three. The standards-based case: satisfy security and access together. Its evidence: the whole Carter–Baker package, the trust data, and the null results. Its concession: the bargain has been available since 2005, and nobody with power has bought it.

[beat]

And four. On the best evidence, the most fought-over election policies move very few votes. Strict ID laws: no significant change in turnout, and no detectable reduction in fraud. All-mail voting: no gain for either party.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not a verdict. The record rules out the cartoon versions of both major positions. It cannot choose which error to minimize: wrongful exclusion, or wrongful inclusion. And it cannot say whether legitimacy is built by rules, or merely narrated about them.

The next lecture turns to what lawmakers and courts have done with that open question since 2020: the statutes, the litigation, and the fight over proof of citizenship.

For now, go back to Bladen County. One operative, one district, one election run again. [beat] Three positions hold that story up to the light, and each finds its own case in it. You can now state all three.
