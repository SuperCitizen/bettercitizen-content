Stand on the bridge over the Snake River. Behind you is Ontario, Oregon. Ahead of you is Fruitland, Idaho. [beat] You are standing between two election systems.

The Oregonians behind you will not go to a polling place this year. Their ballots arrive in the mail, automatically, two to three weeks before Election Day. No request needed. No ID shown at any counter. The signature on the envelope is checked against the registration file.

The Idahoans ahead of you will mostly vote the way their grandparents did: in person, at a neighborhood polling place, after showing a photo ID.

[pause]

Neither state is an outlier. Neither system is new. And the voters of each state express high confidence in their own arrangements.

Five minutes apart. Two complete answers to the same question.

> There is no single American way of voting. There are fifty-one systems.

The differences among those systems are what the argument over voter ID and mail ballots is about. So before the argument, the machinery. By the end, you will be able to take any state's rules and say what kind of system you are looking at.

We will take it in four steps.

First, the floor: the short list of things federal law requires of every state.

Second, the rolls: how voters get on them, and how they come off.

Third, showing ID at the polls.

And last, the mail ballot, from the day it goes out to the day it is counted.

## One thin federal floor

Start with who is in charge. The Constitution's Elections Clause hands the "Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections to the state legislatures, and it lets Congress override them.

Congress has used that override sparingly. What it has built is a floor, with three main planks.

Plank one is the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, the NVRA. Its nickname is Motor Voter. It requires states to offer registration at the DMV and at public-assistance offices. Less famously, it also regulates how voters may be removed from the rolls. We will come back to that half.

Plank two is the Help America Vote Act of 2002, known as HAVA, passed after the Florida meltdown of 2000. It required every state to build a single statewide registration database. And it created the provisional ballot: you vote, and the question of your eligibility gets resolved afterward.

It also imposed the only federal ID rule in American law. Listen for how narrow it is.

A first-time voter, who registered by mail without verifiable identification, must show some form of ID the first time they vote. [beat] Photo or not.

[pause]

Plank three is the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Its Section Two bans racially discriminatory voting practices, and that ban applies to every rule you are about to hear.

One change to that act shapes everything after it. States with histories of discrimination once had to clear their changes with Washington before making them. In 2013, in a case called Shelby County v. Holder, the Supreme Court ended that requirement.

[beat]

So the waves of state lawmaking after 2013, and again after 2020, took effect first and were litigated later. That holds for the laws that tightened the rules, and for the laws that loosened them.

[pause]

That is the floor. Everything else is state law: whether you need an excuse to vote by mail, what ID counts, how early voting runs, whether a misdelivered ballot can be fixed. And in practice, all of it is administered by roughly 8,000 county and municipal election offices.

> Federal law sets the floor. States write the rules. Counties run the machinery.

How much room does that leave a state? North Dakota has no voter registration at all. [beat] It abolished registration in 1951. You show up with proof of who you are and where you live, and you vote.

## Getting on the rolls, and coming off

So much for the floor. Now the rolls.

For a decade, registration policy has been moving, and mostly toward automation. Automatic voter registration signs up eligible citizens from DMV records, unless they decline. It began with Oregon in 2015, and it has spread to about half the states.

Same-day registration lets a citizen register and vote in one trip. About two dozen states have it. And the political science literature consistently credits it with the largest turnout effect of any registration reform: on the order of three to five percentage points.

The unglamorous counterpart is keeping the rolls accurate, while tens of millions of Americans move every year. States check death records and change-of-address data. About two dozen share data through a compact called ERIC, which flags duplicate registrations and voters who have moved.

And here the other half of Motor Voter comes in. A voter may not be purged merely for not voting.

Picture a state election director who wants to delete from the rolls everyone who skipped the last election. A federal lawyer objects, and the lawyer has the law right. First, the state must send a confirmation notice. Then, only if the voter fails to respond, and sits out two more federal general elections, may the registration be canceled.

[beat]

Notice, then wait. In 2018, in a case called Husted, the Supreme Court held that Ohio's version of exactly that process complies.

So purges are legal, regulated, and routine. The fights are over how aggressively the triggers are pulled.

And the rolls do carry errors: the voter who has died and is still listed, the student registered in two states. Errors like those are common. They are the kernel of truth inside most viral claims about "dirty rolls."

So let's weigh that kernel. An audit finds 1,500 deceased people still listed on a county's voter rolls. By itself, what is that finding evidence of? Take a moment.

[long pause]

It is evidence of lag in list maintenance. It is not, by itself, evidence of a single fraudulent vote. An obsolete registration is a bookkeeping artifact, and it matters only if someone actually votes on it.

> An obsolete entry on the rolls is not a ballot.

Put another way, fraud requires a vote cast on that entry. There are documented instances of exactly that, and they are the subject of the next lecture.

## Showing ID at the polls

Third stop: ID at the polls.

Start on ground nobody disputes. Every state verifies identity somehow. The argument is over documents.

The useful way to sort the states is a grid, kept by the National Conference of State Legislatures. The grid asks two questions.

One. Does the state ask for a photo ID, specifically?

Two. Is the rule strict?

"Strict" has an exact meaning here. In a strict state, a voter without the document casts only a provisional ballot, and it counts only if the voter returns with ID before a deadline.

In a non-strict state, there is some fallback at the polls: an affidavit, a poll worker who vouches, a signature match. The ballot counts, with no return trip.

> Two questions sort a state's ID rule: photo or not, strict or not.

Now the head-count, as of April 2026.

Today, 36 states request or require some document at the polls.

[beat]

Of those, about two dozen ask for a photo ID.

[beat]

And of those, 10 are strict.

[pause]

The other fourteen states require no document, and neither does D.C. They verify identity by signature, by attestation, or by the poll book.

Those numbers have ratcheted steadily upward since 2008, when the Supreme Court upheld Indiana's pioneering photo-ID law, in a case called Crawford. And the boundaries move almost every year. In April 2025, Wisconsin's voters wrote an ID requirement into their state constitution, with about 63 percent in favor.

Now notice what the grid quietly shows. [beat] "Photo ID state" does not mean "no ID, no vote." In the non-strict photo states, a voter without ID can sign a sworn statement and cast a ballot that counts. Texas has worked that way since 2017.

And every strict state offers a free state ID to voters who lack one. How many people that paper guarantee actually reaches is a contested question, and the next lecture takes it up.

Let's put the grid to work on two states.

State A requests photo ID, but a voter without one can sign an affidavit and cast a regular ballot.

State B accepts any of six listed documents, and none of them has to carry a photo. But a voter with none of the six casts a provisional ballot, and it counts only if she brings a document to the county office by Friday.

Classify both. Photo, or not. Strict, or not.

[pause 5s]

State A is non-strict photo ID. The affidavit means no return trip is required. It sits with Florida, Michigan and Texas.

State B is strict non-photo ID. The list of documents is flexible, but the consequence is strict. It sits with Arizona.

And of the two questions, the second matters more for the study of turnout, because it asks what happens to the voter without the document. That is why careful studies code laws this way, and do not just count "ID states."

## The mail ballot

Last stop: the mail.

Mail voting is older than the argument about it. Civil War soldiers voted absentee from the field, and every state has run absentee ballots for well over a century.

What states disagree about is who may use it. As of mid-2026, there are three regimes.

The first is the all-mail election. Every active registered voter is mailed a ballot automatically, and voting in person stays available. Eight states work this way, and so does D.C.

The second is no-excuse absentee voting. Any voter may request a mail ballot, with no reason needed. About 27 more states work this way. Add the all-mail states, and you have roughly two-thirds of the country.

The third is excuse-required absentee voting. A mail ballot is available only for listed reasons: illness, disability, absence, age. About 15 states work this way, concentrated in the South and the Northeast.

[beat]

All-mail. No-excuse. Excuse-required.

Oregon went first, by ballot initiative in 1998, and has run every election by mail since 2000. And the pattern is politically scrambled. Utah, a deep-red state, has been all-mail since 2019, though a 2025 law will move it to an opt-in system by 2029.

How much of the national vote travels this way? In 2016, about one ballot in four. [beat] In the pandemic year of 2020, 43 percent. [beat] In 2024, about 30 percent.

[pause]

Now the part of the system that almost nobody can describe: what happens to a mail ballot after the voter signs the envelope. Walk it with me, in six steps.

First, the ballot goes out: automatically in the all-mail states, on request in the others.

Second, the voter marks it and signs the return envelope.

Third, it is returned: by mail, by drop box, or in person.

Fourth, the envelope is verified.

Fifth, if there is a problem, the voter gets notice, and a deadline to fix it. That fix is called a cure.

Sixth, the ballot is counted, and the count is checked in post-election audits.

[pause]

The policy argument sits mostly on steps four and five, and three details carry the weight.

Verification. Most states compare the signature on the envelope against registration records. Some require an ID number, instead or in addition, as Georgia and Texas have since 2021. And some require a witness's signature.

Deadlines. More than half the states require a ballot to be received by Election Day. The rest count a ballot postmarked by Election Day that arrives within a grace window.

And cure. In roughly two-thirds of states, officials must notify a voter whose envelope fails verification, and give them a window to fix it. In the rest, a flagged ballot is rejected outright.

> Mail-ballot security operates at verification and cure, not at the mailbox.

How many ballots fail? Nationally, in 2024, about 1.2 percent of returned mail ballots were rejected. [beat] That is roughly 584,000 ballots. And studies in Georgia and Florida have found rejection falling disproportionately on younger and nonwhite voters.

Hold on to that finding. Every position in the coming argument has to account for it.

One more test. Two states both require mail ballots "by Election Day." In State A, a ballot postmarked Monday and delivered Thursday counts. In State B, it doesn't. A campaign lawyer says one of these states is "counting late votes."

Is the lawyer right? Think it through.

[long pause]

No. [beat] Both ballots were cast on time. The states differ on the deadline: postmark, or receipt. State A counts ballots postmarked by Election Day that arrive within its grace period. State B requires physical receipt by the end of voting.

Neither state counts a vote cast after Election Day. The phrase "counting late votes" runs together two moments: when a ballot is cast, and when the Postal Service delivers it. And the choice between the two rules is pure state law.

## Feature, bug, or problem

Now stack the layers. And before I do it for you, try it yourself. Near the start, I gave you three federal laws: the three planks of the floor. Can you still name them, and what each one guarantees?

[long pause]

Motor Voter guarantees access to registration. The Help America Vote Act guarantees minimum infrastructure and the provisional ballot. The Voting Rights Act guarantees nondiscrimination. Above that floor, state law decides the rest, and counties run the machinery.

That division of labor is federalism. And there are two descriptions of what it does here. Hear both at full strength.

Its defenders describe it this way. Fifty laboratories. No single point of failure for an attacker to compromise. Rules adapted to the place: to a state spread across six time zones, or to one you can drive across in an hour.

> Federalism's defenders: fifty laboratories, and no single point of failure.

Its critics describe it this way. Your practical ability to vote by mail depends on which side of the Snake River you live on. So does your practical ability to vote without a driver's license. Five minutes apart, and the answer changes.

> Federalism's critics: your access depends on which side of the river you live on.

[pause]

Both descriptions are true. This is federalism doing exactly what its defenders say, and exactly what its critics say.

So is the variation a feature, a bug, or a problem that requires national standards? [beat] That is not a factual question.

You may already lean one way. So take the answer you find least convincing. State the best case for it, the way someone who holds it would.

[pause 5s]

That question stays open. The next lecture takes it up, as the three-way argument it is.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Five things to keep.

One. There is no single American way of voting. There are fifty-one systems. Federal law sets the floor, states write the rules, and counties run the machinery.

[beat]

Two. The floor has three planks: Motor Voter, the Help America Vote Act, and the Voting Rights Act. And the waves of state law since Shelby County, in 2013, took effect first and were litigated later.

[beat]

Three. A state may remove a voter for not voting only after a notice and a wait. And an obsolete entry on the rolls is not a ballot.

[beat]

Four. Two questions sort a state's ID rule: photo or not, strict or not. 36 states ask for a document. About two dozen ask for a photo. 10 are strict.

[beat]

And five. Mail voting comes in three regimes: all-mail, no-excuse, and excuse-required. And mail-ballot security operates at verification and cure, not at the mailbox.

[pause]

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not yet an argument. It has not settled whether documented fraud is large enough to justify documentary ID. Or whether ID rules and mail restrictions measurably suppress turnout, and whose. Or who should bear the cost when security and access trade off.

Those are the contested questions. And the research on them is better, and stranger, than either party's talking points admit.

The next lecture sets three positions against that evidence: the security-first advocate, the access-first advocate, and the standards-based reformer. Each one gets its strongest case.

For now, go back to the bridge. Oregon behind you. Idaho ahead. [beat] Five minutes apart, and you can now say exactly how the two systems differ. Whether they should differ is the argument still to come.
