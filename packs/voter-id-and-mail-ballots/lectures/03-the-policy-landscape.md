March 25, 2021. Georgia's legislature passes Senate Bill 202: ninety-eight pages, rewriting the state's election code. Governor Brian Kemp signs it the same evening, behind closed doors, in front of a painting of a plantation, flanked by six white legislators.

A Black state representative, Park Cannon, knocks on the office door during the signing. She is arrested.

Within days, the president has called the law "Jim Crow in the 21st century." Major League Baseball has moved the All-Star Game out of Atlanta. And the state's largest corporations are issuing dueling statements.

[pause]

Now move ahead eighteen months. Georgia's 2022 midterm sets state records for early-voting turnout. [beat] And both camps declare total vindication.

One camp says the turnout proved the law suppressed no one. The other says that turnout despite the law proves nothing about the burden, only about the countermobilization.

[pause]

That episode is the era after 2020 in miniature. Legislation moving at maximum speed. Rhetoric outrunning the provisions. And a scoreboard that everyone reads through their own lens.

Last time, three positions and the evidence under them. This time, the map: what actually changed, through mid-2026. By the end, you should be able to check a headline about voting law against the record itself.

Four stops. First, the states. Second, the courts. Third, Congress. And last, the front where the fight is now: proof of citizenship.

## The states: both directions at once

Start in the states, in 2021. That year's sessions produced the largest burst of state election lawmaking since the years right after Shelby County. And it ran in both directions.

The standard count is the Brennan Center's. In 2021, it recorded 34 laws restricting voting access.

[beat]

And in the same year, 62 expansive laws.

> The 2021 wave of state election laws ran in both directions at once.

Each side's story tends to leave out half of that. And the pattern tracks partisan control almost perfectly. Since then, it has deepened, not converged.

The marquee laws reward a precise reading, because friend and foe alike routinely misstate what they do.

Take SB 202, the Georgia law from the opening. It replaced the signature match with ID numbers, on absentee applications and ballots. It shortened the window to request an absentee ballot. It capped drop boxes. It barred anyone but poll workers from giving food or water to voters in line. And it let the State Election Board take over county election boards.

[beat]

The same law added a second mandatory Saturday of early voting.

[pause]

Mostly tightening, then, with an expansion of access inside it.

Texas passed SB 1 that year. It banned the 24-hour voting and the drive-thru voting that Harris County pioneered in 2020. And it required a driver's-license number or a Social Security number on mail-ballot applications and envelopes.

Now the other direction. Vermont adopted universal mail ballots for all general elections. Nevada and California made universal mail ballots permanent. Michigan's voters, in 2022, put nine days of early voting into their constitution. And New York, in 2023, let any voter request an early mail ballot.

Two things come out of that list.

The first is about labels. "Voter suppression law" and "election integrity law" each do rhetorical work that the texts do not fully support. SB 202 expanded mandatory weekend early voting, even as it constrained the mail channel. And the expanding states were making permanent exactly the mail practices that the tightening states were banning.

The second is about where the action is. Almost every contested provision, in both directions, concerns the path of the mail ballot: who gets one, how envelopes are verified, where they can be returned.

The fight of the 2010s, over photo ID at the polling place, is largely settled ground. 36 states have ID laws, and the public approves of them by huge margins.

> The 2020s fight is about the kitchen-table ballot.

Let's test the point about labels. An ad says SB 202 "banned giving water to voters." A rebuttal says it "expanded voting access." What does each claim refer to? And what does each leave out? Take a moment.

[long pause]

The first refers to the rule against private individuals handing food or water to voters near a polling place or a line. It leaves out that poll workers may provide self-serve water, and that the rule targets line-warming as a campaign tactic.

The second refers to the added Saturday of early voting. It leaves out the compressed absentee window, the caps on drop boxes, and the state-takeover provision, the one that alarmed county officials most.

Both claims are technically true. [beat] Neither is an honest summary of a ninety-eight-page statute. In this field, that gap between provisions and rhetoric is the norm.

## The courts: a high bar, getting higher

Second stop: the courts. Three Supreme Court decisions structure all of this litigation. Listen for what each one gives a state that is defending its law.

First, Crawford, in 2008. The Court upheld Indiana's pioneering photo-ID law, six to three. Justice Stevens's controlling opinion balanced the state's interests in integrity and confidence against a burden the record left abstract. The posture that resulted has governed ID litigation since: challengers must show concrete, weighty burdens, not hypothetical ones.

Second, Shelby County v. Holder, in 2013. It retired the Voting Rights Act's preclearance formula. With it went the requirement that covered states get federal sign-off before changing their voting rules.

Texas announced that its strict ID law would take effect, within hours of the ruling. North Carolina enacted an omnibus bill weeks later. In 2016, an appeals court, the Fourth Circuit, struck that bill down, in the most quoted line of the era: its provisions "target African Americans with almost surgical precision."

[pause]

The structural point survives the anecdote.

> After Shelby, a restrictive law operates first and is challenged afterward.

Put another way, Shelby flipped the burden of inertia. The law is in force while the challenge runs.

Third, Brnovich, in 2021. It raised the bar for those after-the-fact challenges. The Court set out five guideposts, friendly to states, for claims under Section Two of the Voting Rights Act. Among them: the size of the disparity, how common the rule was in 1982, and the state's legitimate interest in preventing fraud. [beat] With no need to prove fraud inside the state.

Lower courts got the message. In Texas, challengers to SB 1 had won major rulings at trial. The Fifth Circuit reversed them.

In August 2025, it held that requiring ID numbers on mail-ballot applications and envelopes "fully complies" with the Civil Rights Act's materiality provision. In February 2026, it reversed the injunction against the law's ban on paid ballot collection. As of mid-2026, SB 1 stands largely intact.

State constitutions cut both ways, and increasingly they decide outcomes. Missouri's supreme court, and later a Pennsylvania state court, struck down photo-ID laws under their state constitutions. North Carolina's requirement survived its state courts, and has applied statewide since 2023.

And in April 2025, Wisconsin's voters wrote photo ID into their constitution, with about 63 percent in favor. That took the question away from courts entirely.

So as federal doctrine narrows, the fight migrates: to state constitutional law, and to the ballot initiative. There, as Wisconsin shows, the security side usually wins on ID questions.

Now a recall, with a use for it. It is 2027. A plaintiff challenges a new state law that requires a driver's-license number on mail-ballot envelopes. She argues that many eligible voters will have ballots rejected over clerical mismatches. The state will cite three precedents you have just heard. Which three, and for what?

[long pause]

Crawford: integrity and confidence interests justify identification rules, without proof of fraud inside the state.

Brnovich: modest disparities do not condemn a rule under Section Two, ordinary election burdens are acceptable, and fraud prevention is a legitimate interest.

And the Fifth Circuit's rulings on SB 1: an ID-number requirement "fully complies" with the materiality provision.

[beat]

Not Shelby. That case changed when a voting law gets reviewed, not whether it survives.

The plaintiff still has two lanes. A claim under the state constitution. Or evidence that rejection rates are large enough, and demographically skewed enough, to satisfy even Brnovich. That is why rejection-rate data matters in doctrine, and not only in morals.

## Congress: two stalled agendas

Third stop: Congress.

> Each party has a signature bill. Each stalled in the Senate, short of sixty.

Take the Democratic package first. It is two bills. The Freedom to Vote Act would bring, nationwide, automatic and same-day registration, two weeks of early voting, and no-excuse mail voting. The John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act would write a new preclearance formula, to replace what Shelby struck down.

Both passed the House in 2021. In January 2022, the Senate majority tried to carve an exception to the filibuster for them. It failed, 52 to 48, with two Democrats joining all Republicans. Both bills return each Congress as messaging votes. Neither has moved since.

[pause]

Now the Republican package: the SAVE Act. It would require documentary proof of citizenship, presented in person, to register or re-register for federal elections. A passport, a birth certificate, or the equivalent. That would replace the NVRA's system of sworn attestation.

The SAVE Act passed the House in July 2024, and again in April 2025. A broader successor, the SAVE America Act, added a federal photo-ID mandate. It passed the House in February 2026.

In the Senate, it met the same wall. On March 17, 2026, the motion to take it up drew 51 votes. [beat] It needed 60. An attempt in early April to fold the bill into a budget-reconciliation package failed as well. As of mid-2026, it is not law.

[pause]

One more piece runs alongside. A March 2025 executive order directed documentary-citizenship requirements onto the federal registration form. Federal courts preliminarily enjoined key parts of it, and appeals were pending as of mid-2026. The constitutional objection: the Elections Clause gives the power to regulate elections to the states and to Congress, not to the presidency.

## The citizenship front

Last stop: the front where the fight is now. Proof of citizenship.

Begin on ground nobody disputes. Registration by noncitizens is illegal everywhere. And the documented instances are rare.

Here is one count. In 2024, Georgia ran a citizenship audit of its rolls. It covered 8.2 million registrants. It found 20 noncitizens. [beat] Nine of them had voted.

And the burden question is real on the other side. Several million eligible citizens lack easy access to the qualifying documents. They are disproportionately women whose legal names changed at marriage, the elderly, and the poor.

So test yourself on that audit. As evidence, what does it most directly support: that noncitizen voting is widespread, that documentary proof is pointless, or something narrower than either? Take a moment.

[long pause]

Something narrower. Noncitizen registration occurs, at a rate of roughly 1 in 400,000 registrants.

> Georgia's audit supplies a numerator and a denominator. Nothing more.

What follows from that number is a matter of weight, and each position weighs it differently.

The security reading: the right number is zero, at any cost, and only documentation guarantees it.

[beat]

The access reading: burdening several million eligible citizens outweighs twenty registrations.

And the audit cannot carry more than it holds. It says nothing about how those twenty registered, or whether a documentary requirement would have stopped them.

Arizona has run a natural experiment since 2004. Its state form requires proof of citizenship. That produced a parallel class of "federal-only" voters: registrants who attested to citizenship but did not document it, numbering in the tens of thousands. Whether that class is a security gap or an artifact of federalism depends on which lens you hold.

And with the federal bill stalled, the action has moved to the states. New Hampshire's documentary-proof law came first. Wyoming followed in 2025. In 2026 the wave accelerated: South Dakota and Utah enacted versions, and Florida passed its own in March.

From here, there are four wires to watch.

One: the endgame for the SAVE bills. Three things could change their fate: a change in Senate rules, a reconciliation maneuver, or a shift in the 2026 elections. Any one would turn a House messaging bill into the largest federal change to registration since the NVRA.

Two: the executive-order litigation. If appellate courts uphold presidential authority over the federal form, the settlement under the Elections Clause moves.

Three: the state wave. Watch whether new laws repeat Arizona's split rolls, or the experience of Kansas. Its rule, in force from 2013 to 2016, suspended about 30,000 registrations before federal courts struck it down.

Four: the quiet bipartisan layer. Post-election audits, list hygiene, and the standardizing of cure keep advancing, without cameras.

## What to keep

Let's gather it up. Four things to keep.

One. The states. The 2021 wave of state election laws ran in both directions at once: 34 restrictive laws, 62 expansive ones. And the fight has moved to the kitchen-table ballot.

[beat]

Two. The courts. Crawford: a state may defend an ID rule without proof of fraud inside the state. Shelby: a restrictive law operates first and is challenged afterward. Brnovich: a higher bar for those challenges. So the fight migrates, to state constitutions and to the ballot.

[beat]

Three. Congress. Each party has a signature bill. Each stalled in the Senate, short of sixty. As of mid-2026, the SAVE Act is not law.

[beat]

Four. The citizenship front. Georgia's audit supplies a numerator and a denominator: 20 noncitizens among 8.2 million registrants. On the other side, several million eligible citizens lack easy access to the documents.

## Three readings of one record

Here is what this lecture has not settled. It is not a forecast. And it is not a verdict on the three positions. It is the board they now argue over. So hear how each one reads the same record.

The security-first advocate reads it as overdue consolidation. This advocate points to the ID consensus, locked in constitutionally where voters got the question directly. To mail channels, finally getting the verification that Carter–Baker urged two decades ago. And to citizenship documentation: the unfinished item on the 2005 list.

> The security-first reading: mid-2026 is overdue consolidation.

The access-first advocate reads it as burden-shifting, after Shelby and Brnovich. This advocate points to rules that operate first and answer questions later. To rules aimed disproportionately at the channels that disadvantaged voters use most: mail ballots, registration drives, drop boxes. And to the courthouse door, narrowing behind them.

> The access-first reading: burden-shifting, as the courthouse door narrows.

The standards-based reformer reads it as two parties entrenching opposite regimes, state by state, so that the patchwork itself is now the problem. This advocate points to the measures with demonstrable payoffs: audits, cure standardization, list hygiene. And to how those measures advance: quietly, precisely because neither base is angry about them.

> The standards-based reading: the patchwork itself is now the problem.

[pause]

The same statutes. The same cases. The same stalled bills. [beat] Three honest readings.

So go back to Georgia, where we began. One law. One turnout record. Two camps, each declaring total vindication. [beat] You now hold what they were arguing over: the statutes, the cases, the status of the bills, and the evidence.
